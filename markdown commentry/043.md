# Sūrah al-Zukhruf (Chapter 43) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Elitist Objection and the Gold Bracelets**

The historical climate of az-Zukhruf reflects the snobbish class prejudice of the Qurayshi aristocracy. The chieftains of Makkah could not understand why God would bestow prophetic revelation upon an orphaned, modestly circumstanced man like Muḥammad ﷺ, rather than upon the multi-millionaire oligarchs of the two commercial metropolises of Arabia. Verse 31 preserves their petulant objection: *“And they said: 'Why was this Qur'an not sent down to a great man of the two cities [Makkah and al-Ṭā'if]?'”* and, they echoed the ancient imperial prejudice of Pharaoh, who had mocked Moses' rough clothing and stammering tongue, sneering: *“Why have bracelets of gold not been cast down upon him, or angels accompanied him in procession?”* (43:53). Sūrah az-Zukhruf responds with devastating divine sarcasm: *“Do they distribute the mercy of your Lord? It is We who have apportioned between them their livelihood in the life of this world and raised some of them above others in degrees, so that they may employ one another in service; but the mercy of your Lord is better than what they amass”* (43:32).

**The Structural Anatomy of the Sūrah**

The movement of Sūrah az-Zukhruf unfolds in five distinct thematic movements. The opening movement (1–14) exalts the Qur'an as a clear Arabic scripture originating from the *Umm al-Kitāb* (the Mother of the Book) in the Divine Presence, reminders of how past nations were destroyed for mocking messengers, and presents the foundational prayer of travel when mounting beasts or vehicles: *“Exalted is He who has subjected this to us, and we could not have [otherwise] subdued it. And indeed, to our Lord we will return”* (43:13–14). The second movement (15–25) exposes the grotesque hypocrisy of the pagan Arabs who attributed daughters to God—claiming angels were female deities—while if one of them was given news of the birth of a female child, his face darkened with suppressed rage (43:17). It exposes the intellectual bankruptcy of their universal excuse: *“Indeed, we found our forefathers upon a path, and we are guided in their footsteps”* (43:22). The third movement (26–56) reviews sacred prophetic history: Abraham renouncing the idols of his father, making monotheism an enduring word among his posterity (*kalimatan bāqiyatan fī ʿaqibih*); the divine warning that whoever turns away from the remembrance of the Most Compassionate has a devil assigned to him as an inseparable, intimate companion (*qarīn*); and Moses confronting Pharaoh, whose contempt for Moses and reliance on his irrigation canals ended in Pharaoh and his hosts being drowned into a precedent and an example for future generations. The fourth movement (57–67) addresses the controversy surrounding Jesus: clarifying that ʿĪsā was not a divine being to be worshipped beside God. A noble servant favored with signs (*in huwa illā ʿabdun anʿamnā ʿalayh*), warning of the Hour, and stating the immortal law of human relationships in verse 67: *“Close friends, that Day, will be enemies to each other, except for the righteous.”* The closing movement (68–89) contrasts the joy of the believers in Paradise, served with golden trays and cups containing whatever the soul desires and the eyes delight in (*wa-fīhā mā tashtahīhi al-anfusu wa-taladhdhu al-aʿyun*), against the damned criminals in Hell pleading with the angelic warden, Mālik, to let their Lord end their existence, only to be answered: *“Indeed, you are to remain!”* (43:77). It concludes with the Prophet's pained sigh: *“O my Lord, indeed these are a people who do not believe!”* and the divine parting instruction: *“So turn aside from them and say: 'Peace!' For they will soon know.”*

Had God not feared that all humanity would collapse into a single monolithic culture of disbelief, He would have made the roofs, doors, stairs, and beds of the wicked out of pure silver and gold (43:33–35)—because in the sight of God, all the gold in the universe has less value than the wing of a mosquito.

## Verse 43:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm

**The Letters and the Family of Sūrahs**

The chapter opens with ḥā mīm, the pair of letters that begins six other sūrahs of this part of the Book: the Forgiver, the Fussilat, the Consultation, the Smoke, the Kneeling and the Sand-Dunes (40:1; 41:1; 42:1; 44:1; 45:1; 46:1). Readers of the Qur'an meet them as a family, and the scholars gave the group its name from the letters themselves. In each of the seven, the letters are followed by a statement about the Book, and this sūrah follows the same order: the letters here, the oath by the clear Book two verses down (43:2), and the statement that it was made an Arabic Qur'an so that the hearers might understand (43:3). The men who first heard these letters were the masters of the language, which is why the letters were the most economical way of raising the question the whole family of sūrahs presses: if the Book is made of your own alphabet, where is the speech like it?

**What the Letters Do at the Head of the Chapter**

The early teachers took two positions on the separated letters, and both have stayed. One reads them as something whose meaning is left with God, placing them with the verses the Book itself calls unclear in meaning, whose interpretation is reserved for God (3:7). The other reads them as a device that draws the ear before it delivers the content, which is what the letters do wherever they appear: they stop the recitation for a moment, and then the message arrives. Al-Ṭabarī collected both and held that the letters do have a meaning that God knows, without guessing at it; al-Qurṭubī gathered the readings that take ḥā mīm as an abbreviation of two of God's names. The reader is left where the sūrah intended to leave him: not with a puzzle, but with an attention that has been caught.

## Verse 43:2

> By the clear Book

**An Oath by the Book Itself**

The verse swears by the clear Book, and the Book swears by itself for a reason. The Qur'an's oaths usually call on something in creation — the sky, the dawn, the fig, the pen — and this one names the scripture, which places the revelation in the same class as the witnesses it calls on in other sūrahs. The sūrah of Qāf opens with the same oath by the glorious Qur'an (50:1), and the sūrah of Ṣād opens with an oath by the Qur'an that carries a reminder (38:1). What the three verses have in common is the claim that follows them: something is being denied by the men who hear the Book, and the Book is the witness against them.

**Why Clarity Is the Point**

The word for clarity here is the same one the sūrah of Fussilat uses when it describes a Book whose verses have been explained in detail, an Arabic Qur'an for people who know (41:3), and the sūrah of Yūsuf opens with the statement that the Book was sent down as an Arabic Qur'an so that the audience might understand (12:2). The clarity is not only a description of the language; it is the answer to the charge that the Qur'an is a riddle that only the Prophet's party can explain. The Book keeps claiming that its meaning is reachable, that the warnings have been varied inside it so that people will be mindful (20:113), and that it was revealed in Arabic without any crookedness (39:28). The oath in front of the reader puts the claim at the head of the sūrah, before the arguments about the idols and the resurrection that fill the rest of it.

## Verse 43:3
> Certainly, We have made it a Quran in Arabic so perhaps you will understand

**The Arabic Book and the People It Was Sent To**

The verse says that God has made the Book an Arabic Qur'an so that the hearers might use their minds. The Book states the same fact in several places and always ties it to the audience: the sūrah of Yūsuf says the Qur'an came down in Arabic so that the people addressed might understand it (12:2), the sūrah of Ṭā Hā says the warnings have been varied inside it so that people will turn away from evil or be reminded (20:113), and the sūrah of the Troops adds that the Book carries no crookedness in it so that people may be mindful of God (39:28).

The sūrah of Fussilat closes the argument when it says that the Book was made an Arabic Qur'an for people who know (41:3), and the sūrah of the Smoke ends with a verse that shows what the language was for: God made the Qur'an easy in the tongue of the Prophet so that the people might be mindful (44:58). The language was the door and not the wall.

**Most of the Sūrahs the Prophet Recited**

The Book never avoided the fact that the language of the revelation was the language of its first audience, and it never treated the Arabic tongue as a boundary around the message. It calls the Prophet a warner to all of humanity (34:28; 7:158), and it says that the Book was sent so that he might warn the Mother of the Cities and everyone around it (42:7). The Arab whose recitation is a proof to the Arab is also, by the Book's own account, a mercy to everyone else through the translation of meaning that the community has carried out since. The verse's last two words are the sūrah's invitation to the hearer: the language is fitted to the mind that will use it, and the mind is what the verse asks for.

## Verse 43:4

> And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom

**The Mother of the Book**

The verse says that the revelation stands in the Mother of the Book, with God, and the Book uses the same term elsewhere for the source from which revelation comes. The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān describes the clear verses of the Book as the Mother of the Book and the unclear ones as a trial (3:7), and the sūrah of the Thunder says that God eliminates and confirms what He wills, and that with Him is the Mother of the Book (13:39). The Book names the same reality in two other sūrahs when it says that the Qur'an is a glorious recitation recorded in a Preserved Tablet (85:21-22) and a noble Qur'an in a well-preserved Record (56:77-78). The commentators took the three images together and understood the Mother of the Book as the heavenly original from which the earthly revelation comes, and the sūrah of Ornaments of Gold is stating here what the other passages state in their own words.

**What the Verse Does to the Charge of Invention**

The verse answers the accusation the sūrah will report later, that the Qur'an was something the Prophet had assembled or copied from those around him. If the revelation's true home is with God before it comes down, then the earthly Arabic text is a sending rather than a composition, and the men who were meeting it as news were meeting a copy of what had already been. The Book makes the same move at the head of the sūrah of the Cave, where God is praised for revealing the Book to His servant with no crookedness in it (18:1), and in the sūrah of the Bee, where the angels are sent down with the spirit of God's command to whom He wills of His servants (16:2). The verse's two names for the heavenly original — highly esteemed, and rich in wisdom — belong to the same argument: the Book that men were mocking in the streets of Makkah was held in honour where it was kept, and its wisdom was not something the quarrel could reach.

## Verse 43:5

> Should We then turn the ˹Quranic˺ Reminder away from you ˹simply˺ because you have been a transgressing people

**The Question That Shows the Reminder Was Not Withdrawn**

The verse asks whether God should have held the Reminder back from the people because they were a transgressing community, and the question carries its own answer. The answer is in the sūrah's own history: the Reminder arrived among them anyway, and it kept arriving. The Book states the same lesson in the sūrah of Fussilat, where those who deny the Reminder after it has come to them are told that it is a mighty Book, one that falsehood cannot approach from any side (41:41-42), and the sūrah of the Consultation tells the Prophet that because the communities before him divided, he was sent to invite the whole of humanity and to stand firm (42:15). The mercy that keeps sending the message to a people who have not earned it is the sūrah's first statement about the Book, and it is placed before any of the warnings that follow.

**What the Transgression Was**

The verse calls the community transgressing without describing the offence, because the sūrah will do that work in detail over its remaining pages. The Book elsewhere names the offence of Makkah against the message in terms the reader can measure: the men who turned away from the Reminder and followed their own whims, and the association of partners with God that the sūrah of Ornaments of Gold attacks at length (43:9-15). The Book draws the same picture of the same audience in the sūrah of the Children of Israel, where it says that veils have been placed over their hearts and deafness in their ears, and that when the Prophet mentions his Lord alone in the Qur'an they turn away in aversion (17:46). The verse's question is therefore the opening of the sūrah's patience: the reminder was not withdrawn, and the men reading it are the evidence.

## Verse 43:6

> ˹Imagine˺ how many prophets We sent to those ˹destroyed˺ before

**The Prophets Sent to the Nations Before**

The verse points back into history with a sentence of astonishment: how many prophets were sent to the peoples who have passed away. The Book records the list of those nations with their messengers in the sūrah of the Prophets and the sūrah of Hūd, and it gives the pattern in a single verse of the sūrah of the Believers, where the messengers were sent one after another and the people denied each one, and the Book says that they were destroyed and reduced to stories (23:44). The same sequence is stated in the sūrah of the Companies, where the community of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Abraham and the people of Lot are named, and the Book adds that each of them accused its messenger of lying, so the sentence of doom fell (38:12-14). The reader of this sūrah has met those accounts in the sūrahs before it, and the verse calls on what he already knows.

**Why the Sūrah Opens With the Past**

The sūrah is addressed to men who were telling the Prophet that his message was new and that their fathers had managed without it. The verse answers by naming the road that ran through the centuries before them: messenger after messenger, community after community, and the same reception each time. The Book's instruction to the Prophet in the sūrah of the Cattle is the conclusion this verse is aiming at: other messengers were ridiculed before you, but those who ridiculed them were overtaken by the very thing they mocked (6:10), and the same statement comes back in the Prophets (21:41). The verse therefore does two things at once for the men of Makkah: it tells them they are not the first to hear the message, and it warns them that they would not be the first to regret it.

## Verse 43:7

> But no prophet ever came to them without being mocked

**The Mockery That Every Messenger Met**

The verse says that no prophet came to them without being mocked, and the Book states the same fact in the same words in two other places. The sūrah of the Cave's forerunner, the Sūrah of the Rocky Tract, says that no messenger came to them without being mocked (15:11), and the sūrah of Yā Sīn puts the sentence in the middle of its account of the men of the town: how sorry the servants are, that no messenger came to them without being laughed at (36:30). The mockery in the Book is not an accident of a particular century. It is the standard reception of a message that asks people to change, and the Book's reports of it are full of the ordinary forms it takes: the demand for a different sign, the sneer at the messenger's followers, the claim that the whole thing is an old story.

**What the Mockery Was Doing in Makkah**

The sūrah will report the makkans' specific mockery later, where they treat the Qur'an as sorcery and poetry and ask why the Prophet was not given what Moses was given (43:48-49; 43:31). The verse in front of us supplies the answer before the charge is made. The mockery is the badge the messengers wore, and the Book tells the Prophet so at the moments when the pressure was heaviest: the sūrah of the Cattle says that messengers were rejected before him and that they endured the rejection and the persecution until God's help arrived (6:34), and the sūrah of the Consultation tells him to stand firm as he was commanded (42:15). The verse is therefore a word of comfort as much as a warning, and the sūrah of Yā Sīn states the other half of it: the mockers were the ones who were destroyed.

## Verse 43:8

> So We destroyed those who were far mightier than these ˹Meccans˺. The examples of ˹their˺ predecessors have ˹already˺ been related

**The Mighty Who Were Erased**

The verse says that God destroyed a people far greater in strength than the men of Makkah, and the Book repeats the comparison wherever it wants to deflate a community's confidence in its power. The sūrah of the Romans, the sūrah of the Originator and the sūrah of the Forgiver all use the same sequence: the reader is asked whether he has travelled and seen what became of those before him, and the answer given is that they were stronger, and that they cultivated the earth and built more than the men looking at their ruins (30:9; 35:44; 40:82). The Book names the examples of that strength in other places: the ʿĀd of Iram, whose like was not built in any land (89:6-8), and Korah, who was told that God had destroyed nations stronger than he was (28:78). The men of Makkah measured themselves against their neighbours; the verse measures them against the ruins of the nations.

**What the Sūrah Has Already Shown**

The verse closes the first movement of the chapter, and it does so with the sentence that the example of the former peoples has already been related. That is a reference to the sūrahs the reader has already heard, and the Book expects the reader to have them in mind: the Flood, the wind of ʿĀd, the blast of Thamūd, the drowning of Pharaoh's army. The sūrah will go on to tell the story of Moses and Pharaoh in its own words a few verses later (43:46-56), and it will close the account with the same lesson: the men who mocked were the ones who were made a lesson for those who came after them (43:56). The verse in front of us is the hinge between the general statement and the particular story, and the story is chosen for a reason: the Pharaoh who called himself the lord of a people is the strongest possible case of the argument the sūrah is making.

## Verse 43:9

> If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth, they will certainly say, “The Almighty, All-Knowing did.”

**The Confession They Made Without Noticing**

The verse tells the Prophet to ask the idolaters who created the heavens and the earth, and it predicts the answer: they will say the Almighty, the All-Knowing did. The Book puts the same question to the same men in the sūrah of the Spider, where they are asked who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon, and the verse records their answer and then asks how they can be deluded away from the truth (29:61). The sūrah of Luqmān records the same answer in one word, Allah, and follows it with the instruction to say that praise belongs to God and the observation that most of them do not know (31:25). The sūrah of the Believers records the same exchange in a series of questions — who owns the earth, who owns the heavens, who holds the sky and the earth — and each time the men answer that God does, and the passage closes with the question the whole argument has been building to: will you not then be mindful and guard yourselves against Him (23:85-89)? The men of Makkah were not atheists. They knew who had made the world, and they said so whenever they were asked without a partisan purpose, which is exactly why the Book uses their own answer as the lever for everything that follows.

**What the Admission Proves**

The verse does not need to argue the case for God's creation, because the men being addressed had already accepted it. What the sūrah builds on that admission is the charge of inconsistency, and it does so in the same way the Book does everywhere it handles idolatry: it takes the idolaters' own statement about creation and asks them what right they then have to hand over the prayer, the sacrifice, and the ruling to anyone else. The Book sums the argument up in the sūrah of the Companies with the sentence that the men who say God made everything then turn away from Him (39:3 states their worship of others as a means of drawing near), and the sūrah of Ornaments of Gold spends its remaining pages drawing the conclusion out. The confession at the head of the sūrah is the lever that moves every argument after it.

## Verse 43:10

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for you, and set in it pathways for you so that you may find your way

**A Ground Prepared for Travellers**

The verse names the next sign: God spread out the earth for people and set paths in it so that they could find their way. The Book joins the two facts again and again, and it always presents them as mercies rather than accidents. The sūrah of the Prophets says that God placed firm mountains on the earth so that it would not shake under its people, and made broad pathways in it so that they might find their way (21:31), and the sūrah of the Bee lists the same provisions in its catalogue of signs: mountains placed so that the earth does not shake under its people, and rivers, and pathways so that they may find their way (16:15). The sūrah of the Ant puts the same gifts in a single question, asking who made the earth a place of settlement and caused rivers to flow through it and placed firm mountains upon it (27:61). The reader who has ever followed a road through the mountains of the Hijaz knows what the verse is pointing at: a land that would be impassable without the paths cut through it.

**The Roads as Signs**

The Book's habit of naming roads as evidence is worth noticing, because it is a very concrete kind of proof. A road is not a natural object; it is a promise that somebody has arranged the world so that a person can get where he needs to be, and the verse names that arrangement as a gift from the One who owns both the road and the destination. The Book uses the same argument for the sea in the sūrah of the Children of Israel, where God is said to have carried the children of Adam on land and sea and given them good provision, and the verse ends with the statement that they have been privileged far above much of what He created (17:70). The sign in front of the reader is not abstract. It is the road under his feet and the fact that it goes somewhere.

## Verse 43:11
> And ˹He is the One˺ Who sends down rain from the sky in perfect measure, with which We give life to a lifeless land. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺

**Rain in Measure, and Life Out of Dead Ground**

The verse names the rain that comes down from the sky in a measured amount, the dead land it brings back to life, and then draws the conclusion the sūrah wants: that is how you will be brought out of your graves. The Book makes the same move in verse after verse, and it always uses the revival of the earth as its standing proof of the raising of the dead. The sūrah of the Believers says that God sends rain down in measure and stores it in the earth, and that He is able to take it away (23:18); the sūrah of Qāf describes the blessed rain and the gardens and harvests that come out of it (50:9); the sūrah of the News names the rainclouds pouring water and the grain and plants and gardens that follow (78:14-16). And the sūrah of the Romans draws the conclusion in one sentence: look at the traces of God's mercy, how He gives life to the earth after its death; surely He is the one who gives life to the dead, and He is able to do all things (30:50).

The sūrah of the Pilgrimage uses the same picture to answer doubters directly, describing the earth that sits barren until the water comes and it stirs and swells and grows every kind of plant (22:5).

**Why the Measure Matters**

The verse's word for the rain is that it comes in a fixed amount, and the detail is not decoration. A land that receives too little dies of thirst, and a land that receives too much is drowned or stripped of its soil; the rain that arrives in measure is a rain that someone has calculated. The Book uses the same word for the water it keeps underground (23:18) and for the provision measured out to each household (65:3 speaks of provision from where he does not expect; 42:27 of measured provision). The measurement is the argument. The rain is not generous by accident and not withheld by accident, and the men who watched the sky each year were being asked to notice that the amount that fell was exactly what their fields could take.

## Verse 43:12

> And ˹He is the One˺ Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs, and made for you ships and animals to ride

**Pairs, and the Animals Carried on**

The verse names two signs together: God created everything in pairs, and He made for people ships and cattle to ride. The pairing is one of the Book's largest statements about creation. The sūrah of Yā Sīn says, in the same spirit, glory to the One who created all things in pairs, whether it is what the earth produces, or their own kind, or what they do not yet know (36:36), and the sūrah of the Scatterers states it in a sentence the community kept as a verse of gratitude: We created pairs of all things so that you might be mindful (51:49). The sūrah of the Thunder lists the pairs inside the created world — the fruits, the day and the night — as part of its account of God's spreading out of the earth (13:3). The Book presents the pattern as a signature on the made world rather than as a fact about human marriage alone.

**The Ships and the Riding Animals**

The second half of the verse turns to the creatures people actually depend on, and the Book gives them the same treatment elsewhere. The sūrah of the Forgiver says that God made the cattle so that people may ride some of them and eat others, and that they carry them to places they could not otherwise reach (40:79-80); the sūrah of Yā Sīn makes the Ark and the ships like it a sign in the same breath as the riding animals (36:41-42); the sūrah of the Bee lists the cattle, the horses, the mules and the donkeys as provision for warmth, food, transport and display (16:5-8). The verse in front of us gathers the two — the pair woven through creation and the particular creatures a traveller rides — and it sets them down as one more piece of the evidence the sūrah is assembling. The reader is being asked to count what he has been carried by.

## Verse 43:13

> so that you may sit firmly on their backs, and remember your Lord’s blessings once you are settled on them, saying, “Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so ˹on our own˺

**The Prayer Said When the Foot Goes Up**

The verse gives the reason for the riding animals it has just named: so that a person may settle on their backs, and then remember the favour of his Lord and say the words the verse prints for him — glory be to the One who has subjected this to us, since we could never have done it ourselves. The Book's practice of putting a prayer in the mouth of the traveller is old, and the community kept this verse as its travelling supplication. The Prophet recited it when he mounted: the reports in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim preserve the supplication of travel with these words at its head, meaning that the verse became a phrase the community says when it sets off. The Book elsewhere says that God has subjected the ships and the cattle and the sea to people (16:14; 40:79-80; 22:65), and the phrase in the verse makes the subjection personal: the animal under the rider did not volunteer, and the rider did not tame it by his own strength.

**What the Words Admit**

The prayer is a short course in the Book's theology of use. The rider is carried by something he did not create, over ground he did not lay out, to a destination he would not reach by himself, and the words he says name the one who arranged all three. The Book's instruction is that the man who sees this says the glory formula at the moment of settling, which is the moment when a person is most likely to feel that his standing is his own achievement. The next verse completes the sentence with the last phrase of the traveler's prayer, and the two verses together are one movement: the man is carried, and the man is going back to the One who carries him.

## Verse 43:14

> And surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return.”

**The Return That Closes the Traveler's Prayer**

The verse adds the final clause of the words the traveller says: and to our Lord we will surely return. The Book gives the same sentence to the community in its most familiar form — those who, when a disaster comes to them, say that they belong to God and that they are returning to Him (2:156) — and it makes the return the frame of the whole religious life rather than the closing event of a journey. The man who says it on the back of an animal has put the two facts of his journey together: he is being carried, and he is on his way back to the one who carries him, whether the road ends at the next village or at the end of his life.

**Why the Sūrah Ends the Sign-Section Here**

The verse marks the end of the sūrah's catalogue of created signs, and the closing words are the hinge the chapter needs before it turns to the idolaters' beliefs. The Book's habit is to place the reminder of the return where it can do the most work: the sūrah of the Children of Israel names the carrying of the children of Adam on land and sea, the good provision they were given and the rank they were raised to (17:70), and this sūrah has just walked its reader through the earth, the pathways, the rain, the pairs and the riding animals (43:10-13). The reader of this sūrah has been led through the heavens, the earth, the paths, the rain, the pairs and the riding animals, and the last of them left him with the sentence that he is being carried home.

## Verse 43:15

> Still the pagans have made some of His creation out to be a part of Him. Indeed, humankind is clearly ungrateful

**The Charge of Making the Created a Part of God**

The verse turns from the signs to the doctrine of the men who ignored them: they have made some of God's creation a portion of Him, and the Book adds its verdict, that the human being is clearly ungrateful. The Book names the specific error in other places with more detail. The sūrah of the Cattle says that they attributed to God sons and daughters out of ignorance, while the jinn, whom He created, were associated with Him (6:100), and the sūrah of the Star says that the men who do not believe in the Hereafter give the angels female names (53:27). The Book's phrase for the offence is that they made a part of the servants a part of the Lord — the same charge the sūrah of the Companies answers when it says that God did not take a son and that there is no other god with Him (23:91).

**The Ingratitude Named in One Word**

The Book calls the act ingratitude rather than ignorance, and the pairing is exact. The men had just answered, in the previous verse, that the Almighty and All-Knowing made the heavens and the earth; they had been carried on animals and roads they did not make; and they had grown up under a rain they did not send. Then they handed a part of the worship back to creatures of their own imagining. The Book uses the same diagnosis in several places. The sūrah of the Bee names the practice exactly: they assign to idols that know nothing a share of the provision God has given them, and the verse warns that they will be questioned about what they fabricated (16:56). The same sūrah then counts the gifts of spouses, children and good provision, and asks whether the men believe in falsehood and deny the favours of God (16:72). The verse in front of the reader is the sūrah's shortest statement of the same charge, and it lands the harder because the signs have just been counted out.

---

## Verse 43:16

> Has He taken ˹angels as His˺ daughters from what He created, and favoured you ˹O pagans˺ with sons

**Daughters for God and Sons for Themselves**

The verse asks the men of Makkah whether God has taken daughters from among the angels He created, while favouring them with sons. The Book puts the same question three times in the same words, which tells the reader how much weight it carries: the sūrah of the Troops says, ask them whether your Lord has daughters while they have sons (37:149), and again whether God created the angels as females while they were watching (37:150); the sūrah of the Mount asks whether God has daughters while they have sons, and the sūrah of the Star puts the same preference in their mouths before naming the angels as females (52:39; 53:21). In every case the Book stops before the doctrine and lays out the arithmetic: the men of Quraysh counted daughters as a burden and sons as the point of a household, and then handed the burdens to God.

**What the Book Refuses**

The verse refuses the whole transaction, and the Book's later statement in the same sūrah gives the reason: the idols they describe are creatures that cannot help anyone (43:18-19). What makes the charge land is not an argument about the status of women in the abstract; it is that the men had measured the value of their own daughters at home, and then imagined that a God who had made the angels would accept the share they had decided to keep the least. The Book treats the claim as a slander against its Maker, and the sūrah of the Star names the goddesses the makkans had set up and says that they are mere names which the men and their forefathers invented, a practice God never authorized, and that the men follow nothing but assumption and their own desires (53:19-23).

---

## Verse 43:17

> Whenever one of them is given the good news of what they attribute to the Most Compassionate, his face grows gloomy, as he suppresses his rage

**The Gloomy Face**

The verse catches the idolater at the exact moment his own belief is applied to him: when one of them is told of the birth of what he assigns to the Most Merciful, his face darkens, and he holds down his anger. The Book describes the same reaction in the sūrah of the Bee in the same words — when the news of a girl comes to one of them, his face turns dark and he is filled with rage (16:58) — and it goes on there to describe what such a father does with the daughter he is ashamed of, hiding from people, wondering whether to keep her in humiliation or bury her in the dust (16:59). The sūrah of the Folding Up names the practice in its account of the day of judgment: when the baby girls buried alive are asked for what crime they were killed (81:8-9). The Book is not talking about an attitude. It is talking about a graveyard.

**What the Verse Exposes**

The verse's argument does not need a counter-argument, because the idolaters' bodies gave the reply. The men who said that the angels were daughters of God reacted to a daughter's birth with a face like a closed door, and the Book leaves them holding the contradiction in their own hands. The sūrah puts the question directly in the next verse: do they attribute to God what they bring up in finery and who cannot hold their own in a dispute (43:18), which is the voice of the very prejudice the men themselves harboured. The Book's handling of the whole matter is one of the clearest cases of it turning a community's own habits into an argument against its theology, and the readers the sūrah wanted to reach were being asked to hear how their own son-preference sounded when it was spoken about God.

---

## Verse 43:18

> ˹Do they attribute to Him˺ those who are brought up in fineries and are not commanding in disputes

**The Question That Quotes Their Own View**

The verse presses the accusation further: do they give God those who are raised in finery and who cannot make themselves clear in a dispute? The words are the sūrah stating the men's own valuation of women back to them — the women they adorned and then refused to listen to in the affairs that mattered. The Book uses the same technique in the sūrah of the Mount, where the question about daughters and sons is put directly (52:39), and in the sūrah of the Star, where the Book rejects the judgment of the men who preferred sons and called the angels female, and then says that such naming is a claim they have no knowledge of (53:21-23, 53:27-28). The sūrah of the Bee closes the case by describing what the same men did with their daughters when they arrived, and asking the reader to look at the arbiter they had chosen for themselves (16:58-59).

**What the Verse Is Not Saying**

The verse is not stating the Book's own estimate of women; it is stating the makkans' estimate and holding them to it. A reader who takes the words as the Qur'an's description of women has misread the passage entirely, because the Book's own teaching is stated a few verses later in the same sūrah: the men and women who believe and do good works enter the garden and are given provision without measure  — the sūrah of the Forgiver puts it in one sentence, that whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer will enter Paradise and be provided for without limit (40:40). The community heard the Book praise the mothers, the daughters and the women who carried the message — the Prophet's own daughters stood in the line of his household — and the sūrah's argument here is aimed precisely at the men who thought God could be given the share they did not want.

---

## Verse 43:19

> Still they have labelled the angels, who are servants of the Most Compassionate, as female. Did they witness their creation? Their statement will be recorded, and they will be questioned

**The Claim That the Angels Are God's Daughters**

The Meccan idolaters had built a whole family tree for the unseen world. The Book reports several versions of it in this sūrah and outside it: they made some of God's creatures a part of Him (43:15), they said the angels were His daughters while they kept the sons they preferred for themselves (43:16; 37:149), and they asked, as if it settled the matter, whether God had created the angels female while they were watching (37:150; 43:19). The sūrah of the Star says plainly where the idea came from: the men who do not believe in the Hereafter are the ones who give the angels female names, and they have no knowledge for it, only inherited assumptions (53:27-28). What the Book refuses is not the vocabulary of an argument but the habit of speech that assigns ranks in heaven on the basis of a rank the speakers had arranged for themselves on earth.

**"Did You Witness Their Creation?"**

The verse answers the claim with a question that no one in the audience can answer: did you watch Us create them? The question is one the Book uses against the same people whenever they invent a fact about God or about the unseen, and it puts the claim on the level of eyewitness testimony, which is the only thing that could support it. The Book makes the same demand for the claim that God has a son (43:20 answers the argument that the idols were worshipped because God allowed it: they have no knowledge, and they do nothing but lie), and the Prophet is told to say that he has no knowledge except what he has been taught (6:50; 7:188). The reader is left with the standard the verse sets: claims about God and His servants rest on what He has said, not on what a community finds convenient.

**Recorded and Questioned**

The verse's last sentence is the one with teeth: what they say is written down and they will be asked about it. The Book keeps the same record in view everywhere it deals with talk about God, and it names the two angels who write what a person does (50:17-18), and the sentence that no word is spoken without a watcher ready by him (50:18). The point of the recording is not a formality. The men of this verse were making statements about the Creator and His servants that they had invented, and the Book tells them that the statements are on the file. The sūrah has been showing the idols as creatures of decoration — silver, gold, ornaments — and the last word in this passage is that the men who had set up the ornaments will have to explain what they said about the Maker of the angels.

---

## Verse 43:20

> And they argue, “Had the Most Compassionate willed, we would have never worshipped them.” They have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this ˹claim˺. They do nothing but lie

**The Argument That God Could Have Stopped Us**

The verse reports the makkans' last defence of their idols: if the Most Compassionate had willed it, we would never have worshipped them. The Book answers the same argument in the sūrah of the Cattle, where the idolaters make it in almost the same words — had God willed, neither we nor our fathers would have associated partners with Him or declared anything unlawful — and the Book replies that the men before them made the same claim until they tasted the punishment, and asks whether the community has any knowledge it can produce (6:148). The sūrah of the Bee records the same argument from the polytheists' own mouths — that neither they nor their fathers would have worshipped anything besides God, or declared anything unlawful, had He not willed it — and the Book's answer there is that the people before them said the same thing, and that a messenger's only duty is to deliver the message clearly (16:35). The argument fails on its own logic. If a man's wrongdoing were God's will, then the punishment for it would be God's will too, and the men making the argument were not offering to accept that.

**"They Have No Knowledge"**

The verse's verdict is blunt: they have no knowledge of what they are saying, and they do nothing but lie. The Book refuses to treat the claim as a serious philosophical position, because the men were not reasoning from evidence; they were reaching for a sentence that would end the argument. The same refusal appears wherever the Book handles the excuse: it asks whether the men have any knowledge they can produce, and then says that the men before them made the same claim (6:148), and it warns the Prophet in the sūrah of the Consultation that the men who dispute about God after He has been acknowledged have an argument that is worthless before their Lord (42:16). What the verse does in the sūrah's flow is close the door on the objection before the sūrah moves to the other one the same men used, which was that their fathers had done it before them (43:22).

---

## Verse 43:21

> Or have We given them a Book ˹for proof˺, before this ˹Quran˺, to which they are holding firm

**The Book They Never Had**

The verse asks a question the men of Makkah could not answer: has God given them a Book from before this one, which they are holding on to? The Book makes the same demand of the idolaters' claims elsewhere, and the point of the question is to press them on their evidence. The sūrah of the Cattle presses them in the same way over the animals they had declared lawful and unlawful, asking whether they were present when God laid that down (6:143-144), and the sūrah of the Ant tells the Prophet to ask them who originates the creation and gives them provision from the sky and the earth, and whether there is another god besides God doing it (27:64). The sūrah of the Cow uses the same court with the people of the Book: show your proof if you are telling the truth (2:111). The demand is the Book's standing rule for anyone who claims to speak for God on the basis of an inheritance rather than a text.

**What the Question Reveals**

The verse exposes the actual basis of the makkans' religion, which was not a book and not a prophet but the practice of their town. The Book elsewhere names the documents they did have: nothing that could be read out as a divine warrant, only the customs attached to the House and the interests that had grown around them. The sūrah's next verse reports what they said when the argument was put to them, and it is not an answer to the question: they say that they found their fathers on a way and are following in their footsteps (43:22). The two verses belong together in the sūrah's argument, and the order matters — the absence of a book first, and the appeal to ancestry second, because the second is what a man says when he cannot produce the first.

---

## Verse 43:22

> In fact, they say, “We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are following in their footsteps.”

**The Argument From the Fathers**

The verse gives the makkans' answer in their own words: we found our fathers on a way, and we are walking in their footsteps. The Book records the same sentence from the same kind of audience in several sūrahs, and it always pairs it with the same reply. The sūrah of the Cow says that when they are told to follow what God has revealed they answer that they will follow what they found their fathers doing, and the verse asks whether they would do it even if their fathers understood nothing and were not guided (2:170). The sūrah of the Table has the same exchange with the phrase that the way of the fathers is good enough for them, and asks whether they would follow it even if the fathers knew nothing and were on no guidance (5:104). The sūrah of Luqmān repeats it once more and adds the worst possibility: would they follow it even if Satan were calling them to the punishment of the fire (31:21)?

**Why the Argument Could Not Stand**

The Book deals with inherited practice as a fact rather than a crime: the community of the Prophet did not ask the Arabs to forget their fathers or to despise their history, and the Book itself honours the ways of Abraham and Ishmael at the House. What it refuses is the substitution of ancestry for evidence. The verse in front of us is the second of the sūrah's three answers to the makkans' claim of legitimacy — no book (43:21), no independent thought (43:22), and, in the verses that follow, no willingness to listen when a warner comes with better guidance (43:24). The three together tell the reader what the religion of Makkah actually rested on.

---

## Verse 43:23

> Similarly, whenever We sent a warner to a society before you ˹O Prophet˺, its ˹spoiled˺ elite would say, “We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are walking in their footsteps.”

**The Pattern Across the Nations**

The verse widens the makkans' sentence into a law of history: whenever God sent a warner to a society before them, the well-off among them said the same thing — we found our fathers on a way and we are walking in their footsteps. The Book's reports of the earlier nations are full of the pattern. The sūrah of Ṣād lists the nations in order — the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Pharaoh, Thamūd, the people of Lot and the dwellers of the thicket — and closes the list with the sentence that every one of them rejected its messenger, so the punishment became due (38:12-14). The sūrah of the Cattle records the same excuse from the polytheists of the Prophet's own city and notes that the nations before them kept making it until they tasted the punishment (6:148). The makkans were not inventing a defence; they were repeating an old one.

**The Elite Who Said It**

The verse names the group that made the argument: the affluent among the people, the men whose standing was tied to the arrangement the messengers were asking them to change. The Book's reports note the same thing about the opponents of every messenger, and the scholars have often observed that the resistance to the message in Makkah came from the men who held the trade of the pilgrimage and the standing that came with the idols of the sanctuary. The Book's parable of the man with the two orchards puts the same confidence in a wealthy man's mouth, and its ending is the sentence that support on that day belongs to God alone (18:32-44), and the sūrah of the Night Journey states the general rule: when God intends to destroy a society, He gives its elite room to act, and when they go on rebelling the decree is carried out (17:16). The verse's phrase is therefore doing real work: it is not random that the men leading the resistance were the men with the most to lose.

---

## Verse 43:24

> Each ˹warner˺ asked, “Even if what I brought you is better guidance than what you found your forefathers practicing?” They replied, “We totally reject whatever you have been sent with.”

**The Question Every Warner Put to Them**

The verse reports what the warner said when he heard the argument: what if what I have brought you is better guidance than what you found your fathers practising? The Book puts the same question to the same audience in the sūrah of the Cow, where the verse asks whether the men would follow their fathers even if the fathers understood nothing and had no guidance (2:170), and in the sūrah of the Table, where the question is whether they would do it even if their fathers knew nothing and were guided by nobody (5:104). The question is the whole of the Book's method with inherited practice: it does not tell a man that his father was a fool; it asks him whether he has compared the two ways. The answer the makkans gave is recorded in the same verse and it can hardly be better evidence of the point: they said they reject whatever the warner was sent with.

**What the Refusal Does to the Argument**

The verse shows the makkans abandoning even the defence they had chosen. The appeal to the fathers was, on its face, an argument about authority; the reply in this verse shows that it was never about authority at all, because the men did not ask what the guidance was before rejecting it. The Book treats the refusal as decisive against them, and it states the consequence in the next verse: the punishment fell on the nations who answered this way, and the reader is told to look at what became of the men who denied. The sūrah of the Bee records the same exchange from the generations before the makkans, and the Book's comment there is that God's way with the nations who did this is the one the reader can still see (16:34-35).

---

## Verse 43:25
> So We inflicted punishment upon them. See then what was the fate of the deniers

**The Punishment That Followed the Argument**

The verse states the outcome of the exchange it has just reported: the punishment fell on them, and the reader is invited to look at what became of the men who denied. The Book closes its accounts of the earlier nations with the same invitation, and it gives the reader specific ruins to look at rather than an abstraction: the sūrah of the Ant points at the houses of the wrongdoers lying ruined for their wrongdoing and calls it a lesson for people of knowledge (27:52), the sūrah of the Pilgrimage tells the community to travel the earth so that their hearts may reason and their ears may listen, warning them that it is not the eyes that go blind but the hearts (22:46), and the sūrah of Abraham tells the Prophet that he passes by the ruins of the peoples who wronged themselves, and that God made clear to him how He dealt with them (14:45). The sūrah of the Spider gives the same instruction to travel the land and see how the creation was originated (29:20).

**The Lesson the Ruins Were For**

The verse's last phrase tells the reader what the ruins are for: they are a lesson, not an ancient scandal. The Book's habit is to bring the past into the present of the audience, and it does so in this sūrah by the sequence it has chosen — the question about the book (43:21), the appeal to the fathers (43:22), the refusal of better guidance (43:24), and now the punishment (43:25). The men hearing the sūrah were walking through a landscape where the earlier communities had left their houses and their wells behind, and the Book expects them to connect the two.

The same use of the ruins is made in the sūrah of the Ant, where the Book says that the dwellings of the wrongdoers were left desolate because they denied (27:52), and in the sūrah of the Spider, where the reader is told to travel and look (29:20).

---
## Verse 43:26
> ˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when Abraham declared to his father and his people, “I am totally free of whatever ˹gods˺ you worship

**Abraham's Break With His People**

The verse turns from the ruin of the nations to the man the makkans claimed as their own: Abraham, who declared to his father and his people that he was free of everything they worshipped. The Book tells the story at length in several sūrahs. In the sūrah of the Cattle, Abraham addresses his father Āzar and tells him that he and his people are clearly misguided (6:74), and then works his way through the star, the moon and the sun until he declares that he has turned his face to the One who originated the heavens and the earth, and that he is not one of those who associate partners with God (6:76-79). The sūrah of the Prophets records his argument with his people and his demolition of their idols, and it ends with the sentence he said to them: shame on you and on what you worship instead of God; do you not have any sense (21:67)? The sūrah of Mary tells the same story with the father's threat and Abraham's promise to ask forgiveness for him (19:41-47).

And the sūrah of the Tested makes Abraham's break a model for the community: there is an excellent example in Abraham and those with him, when they told their people they were free of them and of what they worshipped besides God (60:4).

**Why Abraham Is the Sūrah's Answer to the Fathers**

The makkans had appealed to the way of their fathers (43:22). The sūrah answers with the father of the ways they claimed to honour, and the answer is devastating: the man they called the founder of their House had begun his whole career by walking out on what his people worshipped. The Book makes the same point when it tells the Prophet to follow the faith of Abraham the upright and reminds the community that Abraham was not one of the polytheists (16:123; 3:95; 4:125). The Book describes him as a model of excellence, devoted and upright (16:120), and it protects him from any suggestion that he was attached to the practices of his father's generation. The verse in front of the reader is the first of the sūrah's three statements about him, and the two that follow complete the account: what he excepted from his declaration, and what he left behind for his descendants (43:27-28).

---
## Verse 43:27

> except the One Who originated me, and He will surely guide me.”

**The One He Did Not Disown**

The verse records the single exception in Abraham's break with his people: he declared himself free of everything they worshipped except the One who originated him, who, he said, would guide him. The Book's account of the same moment in the sūrah of the Cattle gives the fuller speech, where Abraham declares that he has turned his face towards the One who originated the heavens and the earth, and that he is not of the polytheists (6:79). What the verse in front of us adds is the reason Abraham gave for holding on to that One: he was the One who made him, and He would guide him. The Book records the guidance that followed throughout Abraham's account: God gave him sound judgment early on (21:51), He tested him with commands and made him an imam for the people (2:124), and He chose him as a close friend (4:125).

**The Distinction the Verse Draws**

The sentence shows the reader where the line falls for a man breaking with the religion of his family. Abraham disowned the worship of created things — the star, the moon, the sun and the idols — and he kept his attachment to the One who had made him, and the Book presents the two moves as a single act. The community the sūrah is addressing was being asked to leave behind the gods of the sanctuary at Makkah, and the Book tells them that the man whose House they served made exactly the same break. The verse's closing phrase, that God would guide him, is also the Book's promise to the reader who makes the same move: the sūrah of the Spider records his word to his people that God is sufficient as a witness (29:52), and the sūrah of the Bees names him as a model of excellence, devoted to God and upright, and tells the Prophet to follow his way (16:120, 16:123).

---

## Verse 43:28

> And he left this enduring declaration among his descendants, so they may ˹always˺ turn back ˹to Allah˺

**The Word He Left Behind**

The verse says that Abraham made this declaration a word that endured among his descendants, so that they might always come back. The commentators read the enduring word as the testimony of God's oneness that ran through the line of Abraham, and the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān states the same fact about him: he was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but one who submitted, and he was not a polytheist (3:67). The sūrah of the Cow makes the same point against the men who wanted the community to follow their own faith instead (2:135). The Book keeps the line in view in the sūrah of the Bees, where Abraham's way is named as the way of the community, and the Prophet is told to follow it (16:123), and in the sūrah of the Women, where the best in faith are those who submit to God, do good, and follow the way of Abraham (4:125).

**The Turning the Verse Keeps Open**

The verse gives the purpose of the legacy: so that they may turn back. The Book's phrase for the return runs through this sūrah and the sūrahs around it, and it presents the legacy as a door that stays open for the descendants whether or not they use it. The Book is honest about the descendants: Abraham's own advice to his children is recorded in the sūrah of the Cow, where he tells them that God has chosen this faith for them and that they should not die except in submission (2:132), and the sūrah of Ornaments of Gold itself says a few verses later that God let the makkans and their forefathers enjoy themselves until the truth came to them with a messenger making things clear — and when it came they called it magic and rejected it (43:29-30). The enduring word is therefore a gift that follows a family through its bad generations, and it is the reason the makkans, in the middle of their idolatry, still held Abraham's name as their own. The sūrah uses that fact against them: the man they venerated had disowned what they were doing, and the word he left them was the message they were now rejecting.

---

## Verse 43:29

> In fact, I had allowed enjoyment for these ˹Meccans˺ and their forefathers, until the truth came to them along with a messenger making things clear

**The Long Enjoyment Before the Truth Came**

The verse follows the account of Abraham with the case of the men the sūrah is addressing: God let the makkans and their forefathers enjoy themselves while the generations passed, until the truth came to them with a messenger who made matters clear. The Book names this delay in the same words elsewhere. The sūrah of the Prophets says that God allowed enjoyment for the men of Makkah and their forefathers for so long that they took it for granted, and asks whether they do not see the land shrinking around them (21:44), and the sūrah of the Thunder answers the mockers with the same sentence that God gave the earlier deniers a while and then seized them (13:32). The Book states what the delay was for in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān: the longer life of the deniers is not a good thing for them, since they are only being given more time to add to their sin, and a humiliating punishment awaits them (3:178). The respite in front of the reader is therefore not an oversight, and the verse marks the moment the respite ends.

**What the Messenger Brought**

The verse says the truth came with a messenger who made things clear, and the Book's phrase for that clarity is the same one it uses for the scripture itself, which the sūrah of Fussilat describes as a Book whose verses have been explained, an Arabic Qur'an for people who know (41:3). What arrived among the makkans was not a riddle and not a private experience; it was a man they knew, speaking in their own language, reciting verses that named the errors they were living in. The Book presents the coming of that clarity as the hinge of the whole history: the enjoyment lasted while the message had not yet arrived, and the accounting begins now that it has. The verses that follow show what the makkans did with it.

---

## Verse 43:30
> ˹But˺ when the truth came to them, they said, “This is magic, and we totally reject it.”

**"This Is Magic"**

The verse records the reception the messenger got: when the truth came to them, they called it magic and said they rejected it outright. The Book reports the same charge from the same men in several places, and the repeated reports show how important it was to the sūrah's argument. The sūrah of Jonah has the sentence almost word for word: when the truth came to them from God, they said this is plainly magic (10:76), and the sūrah of the Sand-Dunes records the same words about the truth as it arrived (46:7).

The sūrah of Sheba records the same men saying that the recitation is only a man trying to turn them away from what their fathers worshipped (34:43), and the sūrah of the Cleaving records it from al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah, who after hearing the recitation pronounced it magic handed down from the ancients (74:24), while the sūrah of the Prophets gives the longer version in which he called it confused dreams, or a fabrication, or poetry, and demanded a sign like the signs given to the earlier prophets (21:5).

**Why the Charge Was Made**

The word magic was the makkans' way of explaining an effect they could not deny. They had heard the Book divide families and pull men away from the religion of their fathers, and rather than account for it as guidance they named it an art that works on the mind. The Book answers the same charge in the sūrah of the Children of Israel by naming the signs the objectors demanded instead of the Qur'an — a spring of water, a garden of palms, a house of gold, a ladder into the sky (17:90-93) — and it shows that the objection was never really about evidence, because the men had watched signs and still used the word magic for the messenger's coming. The sūrah of the Bee states the general rule for a community that has made up its mind: they prefer the life of this world to the Hereafter, and God does not guide a people who refuse the truth (16:107).

---
## Verse 43:31

> And they exclaimed, “If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!”

**Why Not a Great Man?**

The verse reports the makkans' objection in their own words: if the Qur'an were to be revealed at all, it should have come down on a great man from one of the two cities. The sūrah does not name the cities, and the commentators identify them as Makkah and Ṭāʾif, the two settlements of the region with wealth and standing, with reports naming al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah of Makkah and ʿUrwah ibn Masʿūd of Ṭāʾif among the men the objectors had in mind. The Book records the same complaint in other words in several places. The sūrah of the Distinguisher reports the mockery that the messenger ate food and walked in the markets, and asks why no angel was sent down with him or a treasure thrown to him (25:7-8), and the sūrah of the Children of Israel lists the demands in the same spirit (17:90-93). The objection is always the same shape: the message was measured against the messenger's standing in the market.

**What the Objection Assumed**

The objection assumed that God's choice of a messenger would follow the ladder of wealth and clan that the objectors climbed themselves. The Book had already answered that assumption at the beginning of its own history of the community of Israel, where the people protested that Saul was not the most deserving of kingship since he had no great wealth, and their prophet answered that God had chosen him and given him what the others lacked (2:247). The sūrah's next verse takes the same argument and turns it into a statement about who distributes mercy at all (43:32), and the sūrah of the Women puts the question directly to the men who resented the Prophet's standing: do they envy the people for what God has given them of His bounty (4:54)? The verse in front of the reader is the makkans' own estimate of themselves, and the Book lets them say it.

---

## Verse 43:32

> Is it they who distribute your Lord’s mercy? We ˹alone˺ have distributed their ˹very˺ livelihood among them in this worldly life and raised some of them in rank above others so that some may employ others in service. ˹But˺ your Lord’s mercy is far better than whatever ˹wealth˺ they amass

**Who Distributes the Mercy**

The verse answers the objection with a question of its own: is it they who hand out the mercy of your Lord? The Book puts the same question in the sūrah of the Women, where the men who grudged the Prophet his standing are asked whether they envy people for God's bounty, and are reminded that God gave the family of Abraham the Book, the wisdom and great authority (4:54). The point is not that God chooses His messengers at random; it is that the choice belongs to the One who owns everything, and the men complaining had no share in the appointment. The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān states the same fact about every rank a person can hold: God gives authority to whom He wills and takes it from whom He wills, and He honours and humbles whom He wills (3:26).

**The Livelihood Divided on Purpose**

The verse then says that God has divided the livelihood of the objectors among themselves, raising some above others in rank so that some may put others to work. The Book uses the same fact in the sūrah of the Bee, where the favouring of some people over others in provision is named as one of God's arrangements (16:71), and it gives the reason in the sūrah of the Cattle: people have been made successors on the earth and raised above one another in ranks so that God may test them in what they have been given (6:165). The sūrah of the Children of Israel draws the lesson for the eye: look at how God has favoured some over others in this life, and know that the Hereafter is greater in rank and in favour (17:21). And the sūrah of the Cave gives the Prophet the instruction that follows from all of it: do not stretch your eyes at what God has allowed some of the deniers to enjoy, since the provision of the Lord is better and more lasting (20:131).

**Better Than What They Heap Up**

The verse ends with the sentence that closes the argument: the mercy of your Lord is better than everything they amass. The men of Makkah were measuring revelation by their own ledger, and the verse tells them that the thing they were calling greatness had been distributed by the God whose mercy was the prize they were refusing. The sūrah has already shown what the mercy looks like in practice: it is the guidance the messenger brought (43:31), and the verses that follow show the wealth the objectors valued handed out in this world to the men who denied the Most Compassionate, along with the sentence that the Hereafter belongs to the mindful (43:33-35).

---

## Verse 43:33

> Were it not that people might ˹be tempted to˺ become one community ˹of disbelievers˺, We would have supplied the homes of ˹only˺ those who disbelieve in the Most Compassionate with silver roofs and ˹silver˺ stairways to ascend

**The Silver Roofs That Were Not Given**

The verse sets out one of the sūrah's sharpest statements about wealth: if the people were not at risk of becoming one community of deniers, God would have given the houses of the men who reject the Most Compassionate silver roofs and silver stairways to climb. The argument is exact. Riches are not a sign of approval, and the Book says how easily the world could have been arranged so that no one could make that mistake. The sūrah of the Cave states the same principle at the head of its account of the man with the two orchards: what is on the earth is an adornment placed there to test which people do the best work (18:7). The sūrah of Iron describes the whole of it — play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, competition in wealth and children — and compares it to the rain that makes the crops look thick before they wither (57:20).

**What the Passage Does to the Value of Things**

The silver roofs in the verse are not a promise that wealth is bad in itself; they are a demonstration that wealth can be no evidence at all. The men of Makkah read their prosperity as God's approval of their religion, and the verse takes that reading apart by showing what God would have to do to make it true. The Book's report of the Prophet's own life is the other half of the lesson: he warned the community that his greatest fear for it was that the treasures of the world would be opened to them as they had been opened to the nations before them, a warning preserved in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, and the reports of his household describe the mat he slept on and the marks it left on his side. The verse in front of the reader says the goods of the world are the same goods in any hand, and the difference is what they do to the one holding them.

**Where the Passage Continues**

The verses after it finish the picture with the gates and the thrones and the ornaments of gold (43:34-35), and then the sūrah turns away from the wealthy entirely and names what happens to the people who turn their backs on the reminder of the Most Compassionate and are given a devil as a companion (43:36). The Book's purpose in the silver roofs is not to condemn wealth; it is to remove the proof that the men of Makkah were treating their wealth as. The reader is asked to compare the metal of the houses he has just been shown with the garden and the Hereafter the sūrah promises to the people who were mindful.

---

## Verse 43:34

> as well as ˹silver˺ gates and thrones to recline on

**Silver Gates and Couches**

The verse continues the sentence begun two verses earlier: along with the roofs and the stairways, the deniers would have been given silver gates for their houses and couches to recline on. The Book keeps the whole list in one sentence, which is why the commentators read verses 33 to 35 together as a single picture of a world arranged so that wealth would be proof of standing. The sūrah of the Cave supplies the counter-picture: a man with two gardens of vines, palms and a harvest, who walked into them and said he did not think any of it would perish, forgetting the One who had made it (18:32-40), and the sūrah of Yā Sīn puts the same lesson in a shorter scene, where a man argues that decayed bones cannot be brought back to life and is answered by the question of who produced him the first time (36:77-79). The Book's warnings about wealth are never aimed at the possessions; they are aimed at the reading the owners give them.

**The Doors That Would Have Been Opened**

The detail of the gates matters in the argument, because a gate is the thing wealth builds to separate its owner from the street. The Book's objection is to the separation the goods produce in the mind, and it states the case in its parable of the man whose garden was destroyed overnight, so that he wrung his hands over what he had spent on it while it lay in ruins, saying that he wished he had never associated anyone with his Lord (18:42). The verse in front of the reader moves through the same objects — roof, stairs, gate, couch, gold — and leaves the reader to notice that every one of them is a thing the man walks on or sits on, not a thing that carries him any closer to his Lord. The sūrah closes the sentence in the next verse by naming what all of it actually is.

---

## Verse 43:35

> and ornaments ˹of gold˺. Yet all this is no more than a ˹fleeting˺ enjoyment in this worldly life. ˹But˺ the Hereafter with your Lord is ˹only˺ for those mindful ˹of Him˺

**Ornaments of Gold**

The verse completes the inventory of the withheld splendor by adding ornaments of gold, and the gold crowns the sequence of silver that began with the roofs, because where silver marked the structure of the household, gold marks its decoration, the superfluous beauty that signals wealth without serving need. The movement from roof to stairway to gate to throne to ornament is a descending spiral into pure display, and the gold is its final, shimmering note, the thing that adorns but does nothing.
**The Hereafter With Your Lord**

The verse turns from the fleeting to the lasting with the declaration that the Hereafter with your Lord is only for those mindful of Him. The "but" marks the great reversal that the chapter has been preparing, where the wealth the could have been granted gives way to the wealth that cannot be taken. The Hereafter with the Lord is the true inheritance, and its description as "with your Lord" locates it in His presence rather than in any earthly store, so that the believer's hope is anchored in a relationship and not in a possession. This connects the verse to the chapter's portrait of the believers in the gardens, because the mindful one is the one who inherits the lasting good that the silver and gold only pretended to be. So the hearer is offered the exchange the scripture has been urging: surrender the fleeting for the lasting, and the Lord Himself becomes the portion.

**The Glitter and the Verdict**

*Wa-zukhruf* — and gold ornaments. The word covers the whole surface of a wealthy life, and the verse closes a passage in which God has said something startling: if it were not that people would become one community of disbelievers, He would have given the houses of those who disbelieve in the Merciful roofs of silver, and stairways, and doors, and ornaments of gold (43:33-35). The Book then states what all of it is worth: this is only the enjoyment of this life, and the Hereafter with your Lord is for those who are mindful of Him. The sentence removes the one conclusion people draw from wealth, which is that the person who has it has been approved.
**Why the World Is Furnished This Way**

The Sūrah of the Cave states the same doctrine from the other end, saying that what is on the earth was made as an adornment for it, so that people could be tested as to which of them does the best work (18:7). The Book also warns the community not to be taken in by the wealth of people who are fighting the truth, and it tells the Prophet not to let their money and their children impress him (9:55). The sūrah of the Ornament is therefore not saying that the ornaments are evil. It is saying that they are not evidence, and the passage about silver roofs shows how much of the world's arrangement is deliberately left as a test rather than a reward.
**What the Mindful Get Instead**

The verse names the condition for the better thing: the Hereafter belongs to the mindful of God. The Book gives the same promise in other words when it describes the gardens prepared for those who feared their Lord unseen, and the sūrah's next verses return to the argument about the messengers and the people who rejected them. The reader who has followed the passage is left with a question that is easy to ask and hard to answer: what would he do if God gave him the silver roof, and what does he do with the gold he already has?

## Verse 43:36

> And whoever turns a blind eye to the Reminder of the Most Compassionate, We place at the disposal of each a devilish one as their close associate

**The Companion Assigned to the One Who Turns Away**

The verse states a law of the moral life: whoever turns a blind eye to the reminder of the Most Compassionate, God assigns to him a devil as a close companion. The Book makes the same statement in the sūrah of Fussilat, where God says that He placed at the disposal of the men who turned away associates who made their past and future deeds look attractive to them, so that the sentence against them came due (41:25), The sūrah of Maryam gives the mechanism in a sentence: God sends the devils against the deniers to keep inciting them (19:83), and the sūrah of the Cattle names the partnership of the two kinds — the human and the jinn devils whispering elegant words of deception to one another (6:112).

**How the Companionship Works**

The Book does not describe the devil here as an outside attacker; it describes him as a companion, which is the word the verse chooses and the word the sūrah of Fussilat explains when it says the associates made the men's deeds look fair to them. The story the Book tells of the man to whom the signs were given and who threw them off says that Satan took hold of him after that and he became a deviant (7:175), and the sūrah of the Cave tells the Prophet not to obey the man whose heart God has made heedless of His remembrance and who follows his own desire (18:28). The verse in front of the reader puts the companionship at the end of a decision the man made himself: the turning away came first, and the companion was assigned to the man who turned.

---

## Verse 43:37

> who will certainly hinder them from the ˹Right˺ Way while they think they are ˹rightly˺ guided

**Hindered From the Way, and Sure They Are Right**

The verse says what the companion does: he turns the man away from the road, while the man thinks that he is properly guided. The Book gives the same picture in several places, and it is always the self-confidence of the lost man that makes the picture terrible. The sūrah of the Cave closes its account of the men whose efforts were wasted with the sentence that they were the ones who thought they were doing good work (18:104), and the sūrah of Fussilat says that the devils made the men's deeds look fair to them until the sentence came due (41:25). The sūrah of Qāf describes the man who comes before his Lord with an angel driving him and another testifying, and the veil being lifted from his sight, and the record of everything he did set down ready (50:21-23), and the sūrah of the Thunder states the rule the whole passage rests on: God does not withdraw a people's state of favour until they change what is in their own hearts (13:11).

**The Way the Companion Works**

The Book's devils do not usually appear with an argument against God; they work by making the man's own position look reasonable to him. The sūrah of the Cattle names the method in a sentence when it says that they inspire one another with finely polished speech as a deception (6:112), and the sūrah of the Children of Israel reports the makkans' own version of the confidence when they said that they would not believe until the signs they demanded arrived — the springs, the garden, the house of gold and the ladder into the sky they listed one after another in the sūrah of the Children of Israel (17:90-93). The verse before the reader is the answer to the question the sūrah has been pressing: the men who mocked the messenger were not lacking information, and the companion's part was to keep them pleased with where they stood.

---

## Verse 43:38

> But when such a person comes to Us, one will say ˹to their associate˺, “I wish you were as distant from me as the east is from the west! What an evil associate ˹you were˺!”

**The Encounter at the End**

The verse shifts to the Day of Reckoning, where the one who was hindered by his devilish associate now comes to God and speaks to that associate in a torrent of regret, and the scene is the dramatic payoff of the warning that began two verses earlier. The "when such a person comes to Us" places the moment at the threshold of the judgment, where the illusions of the world are stripped and the true nature of the bond is finally seen. This connects the verse to the chapter's Last-Day vision, because the regret spoken here is the inverse of the confidence the misguided one felt while he thought himself guided. In this the hearer is shown the end of the road the devilish associate prepared, not as a doctrine but as a dialogue, a final word exchanged between the deceiver and the deceived when the deception can no longer hold.

---

The verse follows the passage on the devil's hindrance with a scene from the Day of Reckoning, where the one who had been misled speaks to the one who misled him and wishes the distance of east from west between them. The Book records the same exchange in the sūrah of the Criterion, where the wrongdoer asks that he had taken such a one as a friend (25:27–29), and in the sūrah of the Cessation, where the followers ask for the leaders to be given a double punishment (33:67–68). The verse's note that both will be together in the punishment is the sūrah's answer to the excuse of following others, since the follower's regret does not remove his responsibility.

## Verse 43:39

> ˹It will be said to both,˺ “Since you all did wrong, sharing in the punishment will be of no benefit to you this Day.”

**The Word Spoken to Both of Them**

The verse records what will be said when the man and his companion are brought in: that sharing in the punishment will do neither of them any good, since the wrongdoing was theirs together. The Book keeps the two parties in the same sentence in its descriptions of the end. The sūrah of the Spoils shows the groups of jinn and humans entering the fire together, each generation cursing the one before it until the last of them blames the first for leading them astray, and the leaders answering their followers that they are no better than they are (7:38-39), and the sūrah of the Confederates records the followers turning on the leaders with the prayer that they should be given double punishment for the way they were misled (33:67-68). The sūrah of Ṣād gives the same request in another form, where the men in the fire ask that whoever brought them to it should have his punishment doubled (38:61).

**Why Sharing Makes No Difference**

The verse ends the discussion rather than starting one. The Book's habit in these scenes is to have the parties argue and then to state that the argument changes nothing: the followers who obeyed their leaders are told that the punishment is doubled for those who misled them and not removed from anyone (7:38), and the sūrah of the Cave, after describing the fire for the men who thought they were doing good, states that all of it is their recompense (18:105-106). What the verse leaves with the reader is the timing of the companionship. The devil was assigned in this life, and the shared punishment is what remains of the partnership after the whispering has stopped.

---

## Verse 43:40

> Can you make the deaf hear, or guide the blind or those clearly astray

**The Question About the Deaf and the Blind**

The verse puts a question to the Prophet: can you make the deaf hear, or guide the blind, or guide a man who is obviously astray? The Book gives the same question, and the same answer, in several places, because the pressure on the messenger was to take responsibility for a choice that belongs to the man. The sūrah of the Ants says that the Prophet cannot make the dead hear, nor make the deaf hear the call when they turn their backs and walk away, nor lead the blind out of their misguidance, and that only those who believe in the signs will hear (27:80-81). The sūrah of the Romans says the same thing in almost the same words (30:52-53), the sūrah of the Originator says that the living and the dead are not equal and that only God can make a person hear (35:22), and the sūrah of Jonah puts it as a plain question: can you make the deaf hear even though they do not understand (10:42)?

**The Limits the Book Draws for the Messenger**

The question is not a rebuke of the Prophet; it is the Book setting a limit on his office. The sūrah of the Overwhelming Event tells him that his duty is to remind and that he is not a compeller (88:21-22), and the sūrah of the Consultation says that he is not a keeper over the people and that his work is the delivery of the message (42:48). The sūrah of the Cattle puts the refusal of responsibility beside the consolation: whoever chooses to see does so for his own good, and whoever chooses to be blind does so to his own loss (6:104), and the sūrah of Yā Sīn tells the Prophet not to let the mockers' words grieve him, since God knows what they hide and what they show (36:76). The verse in front of the reader closes the sūrah's long argument with the men of Makkah by naming what was actually happening: a man was speaking clearly to people who had decided not to hear.

---

## Verse 43:41

> Even if We take you away ˹from this world˺, We will surely inflict punishment upon them

**Even If We Take You**

The verse addresses the Prophet with the hypothetical that even if God takes him away from this world, the punishment of the rejecters will still be carried out, and the hypothetical is a reassurance against the fear that the Messenger's death would leave the wrongdoers unaccountable. The "take you away" refers to the natural end of the Prophet's life, and the verse severs that event from the fate of the message, so that the hearer understands the revelation and its consequences do not depend on the messenger's continued presence. This joins with the chapter's defense of the Prophet against the despair of rejection, because the verse shows that his passing is not the end of the story the rejecters wish to close. The hearer is in this way given a perspective that transcends the person of the Messenger, locating the weight of the message in the God who sent it rather than in the one who conveys it.

---

The hypothetical that God may take the Prophet away before the punishment of the people is answered in the sūrah of the Spoils: whether you are shown part of what they are promised or God takes you, the return is to Him and the accounting is His (10:46), and the same promise is made to the Prophet in the sūrah of al-Raʿd (13:40). The point is stated in the Book's own terms for the Makkan mockery, that their punishment is not deferred by the messenger's death, since the record is kept and the reckoning is set. The verse's sequel, the promise that God will show him what they were warned of, gives the other half of the comfort: the message will prevail whether or not he lives to see it.

## Verse 43:42

> Or if We show you what We threaten them with, We certainly have full power over them

**The Threat and the Power Behind It**

The verse adds a second possibility to the one in the preceding verse: whether God shows the Prophet the punishment he has been warning them about, or whether the matter is settled after his own lifetime, the men are in God's hands. The Book repeats the sentence almost word for word in three other places, and each time it attaches a different instruction. The sūrah of Jonah says that whether the Prophet is shown part of what God threatens, or is taken to God first, the return of the men is to God, who is a witness over what they do (10:46). The sūrah of the Thunder puts the same words beside the limit of the office: the duty is delivery, and the judgment is God's (13:40). The sūrah of the Forgiver opens the sentence with the command to wait patiently, since God's promise is true, and then gives the same alternatives (40:77). The verse removes the reassurance the mockers were drawing from the Prophet's mortality.

**What the Verse Refuses to Let Them Think**

The men of Makkah were counting on time. The Book reports their reasoning in the sūrah of Sheba, where they asked for the punishment to be hurried if the warning was true (34:29 gives the dare), and it reports their confidence that the Prophet would die before anything happened. The verse in front of the reader closes that door in a sentence: full power over them already exists, and the timing of its use is not an open question. The Book's habit in these verses is to state the power without describing the punishment, which puts the reader's attention where the sūrah wants it, on the certainty rather than the schedule. The next verse turns from the threat to the Prophet himself and gives him the instruction that fits the situation: hold on to what has been revealed.

---

## Verse 43:43

> So hold firmly to what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺. You are truly on the Straight Path

**Hold Firmly to What Was Revealed**

The verse gives the Prophet a command and a statement: hold firmly to what has been revealed to you, and know that you are on a straight path. The Book marks the firmness as the mark of the guided man in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, where the question is put to the believers of how they could turn away when God's verses are recited to them, and the answer is that whoever holds firmly to God is guided to the straight path (3:101). The same sūrah tells the whole community to hold firmly to the rope of God and not to split into factions (3:103), and the sūrah of the Cattle closes a list of commands with the instruction to follow the path of God and not the other paths that lead away from it (6:153). The command in front of the reader is given to a man who had heard the mockery of his own people in the verses just before it.

**The Path the Sūrah Has Been Describing**

The verse calls the path of the Prophet straight, and the sūrah has already said what the path is: it is the road of the revelation that came by God's command, the light by which God guides whom He wills of His servants (42:52-53). Holding firmly is therefore not a pose of stubbornness; it is the response of a man who has been given a road and told to keep walking it while others pull at him from both sides. The Book's instruction to the same messenger in the sūrah of the Consultation is the fuller version of the same command: stand firm where you were commanded and follow no desire of the people around you (42:15). The verse fixes the Prophet's position in one sentence, and the verse after it states what he carries: a revelation that is a glory for him and his people, and for which they will be questioned.

---

## Verse 43:44

> Surely this ˹Quran˺ is a glory for you and your people. And you will ˹all˺ be questioned ˹about it˺

**A Book That Is a Glory for Its People**

The verse names the Qur'an as a glory for the Prophet and for his people, and it adds the sentence that they will all be questioned about it. The Book makes the same claim about the same Book in the sūrah of the Prophets, where it says that a Book has been revealed in which there is a reminder and an honour for the community, and asks whether they will not understand (21:10). The glory is not a decoration; the Book means that the community carrying the revelation has been given something that raises it, and the sūrah of the Cow states the position the community was raised to: it was made a middle community, a witness over humanity, with the Messenger a witness over it (2:143). What the verse gives with one hand it takes with the other, because the gift is also a trust.

**The Questioning the Verse Announces**

The Book's rule for the accounting is stated in the sūrah of the Prophets: God is not questioned about what He does, while His creatures will be questioned (21:23). The verse in front of the reader applies that rule to the community that has been given the Qur'an, and the sūrah of the Cattle makes the same point about every gift: people have been placed on the earth as successors and given ranks above one another so that God may test them in what they were given (6:165). The questioning named here is the one the community still reads as an obligation: the Book has been handed to a people who are answerable for what they did with it, not merely for what they memorised of it. The sūrah's next verse turns from the community to the question of what the earlier messengers taught, which is the standing proof of the message's continuity (43:45).

---

## Verse 43:45

> Ask ˹the followers of˺ the messengers that We already sent before you if We ˹ever˺ appointed ˹other˺ gods to be worshipped besides the Most Compassionate

**Ask Those Who Came Before**

The verse tells the Prophet to ask the messengers God sent before him whether any gods besides the Most Compassionate were ever appointed for worship. The question is rhetorical, and the Book supplies the answer in three places that make the objection worthless. The sūrah of the Prophets states the rule for the whole line of messengers: God never sent anyone before the Prophet without revealing to him that there is no god but Me, so worship Me alone (21:25). The sūrah of the Bee gives the message delivered to every community: God sent a messenger to each people telling them to worship God and turn away from false gods (16:36). And the sūrah of the Prophets also names what the messengers were: men inspired by God, and it tells the doubters to ask the people of knowledge if they did not know it themselves (21:7). The question in the verse is therefore not a request for information; it is the sūrah handing the objectors their own history.

**What the Answer Proves About the Idols**

The men of Makkah had inherited their gods from their fathers, and the sūrah has already dismantled the appeal to the fathers (43:22-24). This verse adds the other half of the case: the men who are claimed as authority for the idolatry were themselves the teachers of the opposite. The Book reports Abraham's break with his people's gods in this same sūrah a few verses earlier (43:26-28), and it reports Moses bringing the message of the Lord of all worlds to a king who called himself a god (43:46). The verse therefore asks the objectors to name a single messenger who ever told a people to worship anything besides the Most Compassionate, and the sūrah's answer to its own question is the silence that follows.

---

## Verse 43:46
> Indeed, We sent Moses with Our signs to Pharaoh and his chiefs, and he said: “I am a messenger of the Lord of all worlds.”

**Moses Sent to Pharaoh and His Chiefs**

The verse opens the sūrah's longest narrative: God sent Moses with His signs to Pharaoh and the chiefs around him, and Moses said he was a messenger from the Lord of all worlds. The Book gives the same scene in several sūrahs, and it never changes the words. The sūrah of the Heights has the opening almost word for word: Moses said, O Pharaoh, I am truly a messenger from the Lord of all worlds (7:104). The sūrah of the Poets gives the command to Moses and Aaron to go to Pharaoh and say that they are messengers from the Lord of all worlds (26:16), the sūrah of Ṭā Hā gives the instruction to go to Pharaoh because he has crossed every limit (20:24), and the sūrah of the Snatchers repeats it in the same terms (79:17).

The sūrah of the Stories says that God narrates the story of Moses and Pharaoh in truth for people who believe, and it begins with the state of the land before the message came: Pharaoh had raised himself above the people and split them into groups, persecuting one of them (28:3-4).

**Why the Sūrah Tells This Story Here**

The makkans had asked why the Qur'an was not revealed to a great man of the two cities (43:31) and had treated the Prophet as a nobody who could hardly express himself, as the sūrah will report in Pharaoh's own mouth (43:52). The story answers the whole complaint in a single scene: the messenger of God stood in front of the most powerful man of his age with nothing but signs, and the contest that followed ended with the throne in the water and the messenger leading a people out. The sūrah attaches the moral after the narrative, when the drowned king is made a lesson for those who come after him (43:56). The next verses follow the reception Moses got, which was laughter.

---
## Verse 43:47

> But as soon as he came to them with Our signs, they laughed at them

**They Laughed at the Signs**

The verse says that as soon as Moses came to them with the signs, they laughed at them. The Book records the laughter and the mockery elsewhere with the details that make the attitude concrete. The sūrah of Ṭā Hā says that God showed Pharaoh all of the signs and he denied them and refused to believe (20:56), and the sūrah of the Heights records the chiefs saying that no sign the messenger produced would make them believe, whatever it was (7:132). The sūrah of the Stories gives their verdict on the signs themselves: they called them conjured magic and said they had never heard of such a thing among their ancestors (28:36), and the sūrah of the Believers records the chiefs' question about the two men standing in front of them, that they were only humans like themselves whose people were their slaves (23:47). The laughter was the reaction of men who had already decided that the messengers were beneath their notice.

**The Pattern the Sūrah Has Already Named**

The sūrah said earlier that no prophet came to a people without being mocked (43:7), and the verse in front of the reader shows the same reception at the court of Egypt. The Book presents the laughter as evidence about the laughers rather than about the message: the signs were plain and growing, and the men who saw them went on laughing until the water arrived. The Book states the general rule in the sūrah of the Cattle when it says that the men who mocked the messengers were overtaken by the very thing they ridiculed (6:10). The verse in front of the reader is the first stage of that process in Egypt, and the verses that follow trace the stages up to the drowning.

---

## Verse 43:48

> although every sign We showed them was greater than the one before. Ultimately, We seized them with torments so that they might return ˹to the Right Path˺

**Every Sign Greater Than the One Before**

The verse says that every sign God showed them was greater than the one that preceded it, and that the end of the matter was the seizure with punishment, which came so that they might turn back. The Book names the signs in the sūrah of the Heights: the flood, the locusts, the lice, the frogs and the blood, sent as clear signs, while the men went on in their arrogance (7:133). The same sūrah describes the pattern of the warnings: God first afflicted the people of Pharaoh with famine and crop failure so that they might come to their senses (7:130), and it records how they read their own condition — in good times saying that they deserved it, in bad times blaming Moses and those with him (7:131). The growing size of the signs is a mercy in itself: each one was a fresh chance presented in a plainer form.

**The Purpose Named at the End of the Verse**

The verse states the aim of the punishment in a single phrase: so that they might return. The Book uses the same phrasing for the famine that opened the sequence in Egypt (7:130), and the pattern is repeated throughout its accounts of the nations: the warnings arrive in stages because the door of return stays open until the last moment. The sūrah of Jonah names the one community among all the others that turned back in time and had its punishment lifted, the people of Jonah, and says that they were given their enjoyment for a while (10:98), and the sūrah of the Prostration says that God gives the warning in a lesser punishment before the greater one, so that people may return (32:21). The verse in front of the reader therefore describes a punishment with a purpose, and the next verses show what the Egyptians did with the chance: they asked Moses to pray for them, and then broke their word the moment the pressure lifted.

---

## Verse 43:49

> ˹Then˺ they pleaded, “O ˹mighty˺ magician! Pray to your Lord on our behalf, by virtue of the covenant He made with you. We will certainly accept guidance.”

**"O Magician, Pray for Us"**

The verse records the chiefs' plea when the signs had them cornered: O magician, pray to your Lord for us, by the covenant He has with you, and we will accept guidance. The Book gives the same scene in the sūrah of the Heights, where they cry to Moses to pray to his Lord on their behalf by the covenant He made with him, and promise to believe and to let the Israelites go if the torment is removed (7:134). The title they use is remarkable: the men who had dismissed the signs as magic (43:47) now address Moses by the word magic as a compliment, because they had nothing else to explain what was happening to them. The Book catches the same contradiction in the sūrah of Ṭā Hā, where Pharaoh looks at the signs and then asks whether Moses has come to drive his people out of their land with magic (20:57).

**The Promise Made Under Pressure**

The verse's ending gives the terms of the bargain: if the torment is lifted, they will follow the guidance. The Book treats promises of this kind with attention, because it states what such a plea is worth when it is made by men who are not repenting but negotiating. The sūrah of the Smoke states the outcome in the makkans' own case: God will remove the punishment for a while, and they will go back to what they were doing (44:15). The verse in front of the reader stands at the point where the persuasion has already been tried and has failed, and the only thing left is the pressure of the signs on a people who had spent their whole history refusing them.

---

## Verse 43:50

> But as soon as We removed the torments from them, they broke their promise

**The Promise Broken as Soon as the Pressure Lifted**

The verse says that the moment the punishment was removed from them, they broke their word. The Book reports the same sequence for the same people in the sūrah of the Heights with the same phrase about the lifting of the torment, and adds the sentence that they went on until they met their end (7:135). The sūrah of the Smoke puts the same fact in God's own words about the makkans: the punishment will be lifted for a while, and they will go back (44:15). The Book treats treaty-breaking as a character rather than an accident: the sūrah of the Spoils describes the men who break their agreements every time without any fear of the consequences (8:56), and the sūrah of the Bee warns the believers against treating their oaths as a means of deceiving one another, with the picture of a foot that slips after it had been firm (16:94).

**What the Verse Shows About Pressure and Belief**

The verse is the Book's clearest statement that a promise made under pressure is not the same as a change of heart. The Egyptians wanted the frogs and the lice and the blood to stop, and they were willing to say anything while the plague was on them; with the plague gone, the words went with it. The Book's account of Pharaoh's court is the case study for every later reading of the same pattern, which is why the sūrah keeps the sequence in order: the demand for signs, the signs, the mockery, the plea, the relief, the betrayal. The next verse moves to the king and his great boast, and the sūrah leaves the reader to see how quickly a man who has just been bargaining with his Maker goes back to calling himself a god.

---

## Verse 43:51

> And Pharaoh called out to his people, boasting, “O my people! Am I not sovereign over Egypt as well as ˹all˺ these streams flowing at my feet? Can you not see

**Pharaoh's Boast Over the Rivers of Egypt**

The verse gives Pharaoh's proclamation to his people: am I not the sovereign of Egypt, with these streams flowing beneath me? The Book records the same self-elevation in the sūrah of the Snatchers, where Pharaoh gathers his people and says that he is their lord most high (79:23-24), and in the sūrah of the Stories, where he announces that he knows of no other god for them but himself (28:38). The same sūrah describes the foundation of the whole claim: Pharaoh had raised himself above the land and divided its people into groups, persecuting one of them (28:4), and it records his threat to Moses in the sūrah of the Poets, that any other god would land Moses in prison (26:29). The rivers of the verse are the lifeline of the country, and the king holds them up as the proof of his authority.

**What the Claim Ignores**

The Book's answer to the boast is the history of the man who made it, and the sūrah has been building the contrast since it named the sending of Moses. The king who controls rivers and cities was standing in front of a messenger with a staff, and the Book has already stated where the power over the water actually sits: the same sūrah of the Stories narrates how the sea received him and his army (28:40). The Book makes the same point in the sūrah of the Ant when the letter of Solomon to the Queen of Sheba is read out, and it opens with the name of God rather than the name of the king (27:30), and the sūrah of the Cave closes the story of the man with the two gardens with the sentence that support on that day belongs to the true God (18:44). The Book's own verdict on the kingdoms of this world is stated at the end of the same passage: the wealth of the man who forgot his Lord was ruined overnight, and he was left wringing his hands over what he had spent (18:42). The verse leaves the boast standing at its height, and the next two verses let the king continue.

---

## Verse 43:52

> Am I not better than this nobody who can hardly express himself

**"Better Than This Nobody"**

The verse continues Pharaoh's speech with the insult that shows what the whole objection had been about: am I not better than this nobody who can hardly express himself? The Book records the same belittling in the sūrah of the Believers, where the chiefs ask whether they should believe in two men like themselves while their people serve them (23:47), and it records the defect the king is exploiting. Moses had asked God to loosen the knot on his tongue so that the people would understand his speech (20:27-28), and he had told God that his heart would be heavy and his tongue tied, which is why he asked for Aaron to be sent with him (26:13; 28:34). The king seized on a difficulty of speech and treated it as proof that the messenger could not be from God.

**The Measure the King Was Really Using**

The verse exposes the standard by which the king was judging the message, and it is the standard the makkans had used about the Prophet when they asked for a great man of the two cities (43:31). The Book has answered that standard twice in this sūrah already, first by naming who distributes mercy (43:32), and then by showing the silver roofs and stairways that could have been handed out to the men who denied the Most Compassionate, and the reason they were not (43:33-35). The king's sentence in front of the reader is the same mistake in its purest form: a man who had just been through plagues and relief and broken promises was still measuring God's messenger by the fluency of his tongue and the finery of his court.

---

## Verse 43:53

> Why then have no golden bracelets ˹of kingship˺ been granted to him or angels come with him as escorts!”

**The Golden Bracelets He Wanted**

The verse finishes Pharaoh's speech with the demand for proof of a particular kind: if this man had been sent from God, why were no golden bracelets granted to him, or angels sent down with him as an escort? The Book records the same appetite in the makkans' demands, and it lists them in full in the sūrah of the Children of Israel, where they ask for a spring to gush from the earth, a garden of palms with rivers running through it, a house of gold, a ladder into the sky (17:90-93), and in the sūrah of the Distinguisher, where the mockers ask why no angel came down with the messenger or a treasure was not thrown to him (25:7-8). The bracelets in this verse are the ornament an Egyptian nobleman would wear, and the king is asking for a diploma of office from God in the style of the court.

**The Ornament the Book Puts in the Garden**

The Book answers the demand in its own way, because it does hand out the bracelets — in the world the king was not building. The sūrah of the Cave describes the people of the eternal gardens with rivers flowing under them, adorned with bracelets of gold and wearing green garments of silk (18:31), and the sūrah of the Human Being says that the righteous will be dressed in green silk and brocade and adorned with bracelets of silver, and that their Lord will give them a pure drink (76:21). The verse in front of the reader shows the king asking for the sign of office in the middle of a history of plagues, and the sūrah lets the reader compare the metal he wanted with the garden the sūrah has already promised to the people who were mindful.

---

## Verse 43:54

> And so he fooled his people, and they obeyed him. They were truly a rebellious people

**He Fooled His People and They Obeyed**

The verse says that Pharaoh made light of his people and they obeyed him, and it adds the verdict: they were a people in rebellion. The Book reports the king's method and the people's response in the sūrah of the Snatchers, where he denied the truth, turned his back on it, gathered his people and proclaimed himself their highest lord (79:21-24), and in the sūrah of the Stories, where the announcement of his divinity is followed by the order to build a tower so that he might climb up to inspect the God of Moses (28:38). The verse's phrasing is exact: he made the people light — he treated them as small, and they accepted the treatment. The Book describes the same deformation in the sūrah of the Forgiving, where a believing man from Pharaoh's own household asks his people who would help them against the punishment of God if it came, while the king goes on telling them that he is only showing them the right way (40:29).

**The Obedience the Book Treats as the Crime**

The verse does not blame the people for being weak; it names them as rebellious, because obedience to a man who claims divinity is itself the refusal of God. The Book's account of the same crowd at the crossing gives the outcome: they were carried into the sea behind their king, and the Book says in the same passage that God made the king and his army leaders who invite others to the fire and that they will not be helped on the day of judgement (28:40-41). The verse in front of the reader is the hinge between the boasting and the drowning, and it puts the responsibility on both parties: the king who made the people small and the people who let themselves be made small.

---

## Verse 43:55

> So when they enraged Us, We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them all

**The Drowning**

The verse says that when they enraged God, He took revenge on them and drowned them all. The Book narrates the same end in several sūrahs, and each one keeps a detail that matters. The sūrah of the Heights says that God inflicted punishment on them by drowning them in the sea because they denied the signs and were heedless of them (7:136), the sūrah of the Stories says that God seized the king and his soldiers and cast them into the sea, and invites the reader to see what became of the wrongdoers (28:40), and the sūrah of Ṭā Hā describes the pursuit and the water that submerged them (20:78). The sūrah of the Poets gives the sentence in three words: then God drowned the others (26:66). The Book uses the same end for the refusal of the messenger in every account, and the detail of the sea is the fitting one: the king who had claimed the rivers as the proof of his power died in the water.

**What the Word "Enraged" Is Doing**

The verse describes the trigger as their enraging God, and the Book's language of anger is not a description of a mood but of a position: the men had gone on denying, mocking, breaking their promises and hunting the messenger's people, and the verse records that the account ran out. The Book states the same sequence in its account of the cities it destroyed: the people were warned, they went on as they were, and then the sentence arrived. The Book also states the mercy that held it back, in the sūrah of the Originator's account of the punishment of the past, where it says that if God held people to what they earned nothing on the earth would be left alive (35:45). The verse in front of the reader records the moment that delay ended for the men of Egypt, and the next verse says what God did with the memory of them.

---

## Verse 43:56

> And We made them an example and a lesson for those after them

**A Lesson for Those Who Came After**

The verse says that God made the drowned men an example and a lesson for the generations that followed. The Book attaches the same purpose to the ruin of the nations in every account, and it gives the reader the sites to look at. The sūrah of the Dawn names the people of ʿĀd with their tall buildings, Thamūd who carved their homes into the rock, and the Pharaoh of the structures, and it says that all of them crossed every limit in the land and spread corruption in it (89:9-12). The sūrah of the Stories says that God made the king and his army leaders who invite others to the fire, and that on the day of judgement they will not be helped (28:41), and the sūrah of the Heights records the same end for the people of Hūd, saying that God saved him and those with him by His mercy and uprooted the men who had denied the signs (7:72).

**How the Book Uses the Memory of Egypt**

The Book returns to Egypt again and again when it wants a picture of a power that thought itself answerable to no one, and the sūrah of the Smoke gives the reader the aftermath of the drowning: gardens and springs and treasure and a fine position left behind, and another people inheriting it all (44:25-28). The verse in front of the reader closes the sūrah's Moses narrative with the sentence that the whole scene was staged as a lesson, which is the connection the sūrah draws for the men of Makkah who were following the same road with less power and the same confidence. The Book's own instruction to the Prophet stands over the whole account: the stories of the messengers are related to steady the heart, and the sūrah says that there has come in it the truth, a warning and a reminder for the believers (11:120).

---

## Verse 43:57

> When the son of Mary was cited as an example ˹in argument˺, your people ˹O Prophet˺ broke into ˹joyful˺ applause

**Jesus Used as a Debating Point**

The verse reports the moment the makkans heard Jesus mentioned: they broke into noisy laughter and applause. The Book explains what they were doing with his name in the next verse, and the short of it is that they had found a text they thought they could use. The Christians around them honoured Jesus as a divine figure, and the makkans seized on the argument in the other direction: if a servant of God could be honoured in that way, then the honour they gave to their own gods was defensible too. The sūrah's answer is placed three verses later, where Jesus himself is described as a servant whom God favoured and made an example for the Children of Israel (43:59), and the same description is given in the sūrah of the Prophets, where the angels are named as honoured servants who do not speak until God has spoken (21:26-27), and in the sūrah of Mary, where Jesus speaks from the cradle and calls himself the servant of God (19:30).

**What the Episode Shows About the Argument**

The verse is not a report of a serious theological discussion; it is the record of a crowd that had found a name it could wave. The Book's answer to the whole exchange comes from Jesus himself in two verses of this sūrah, where he says that God is his Lord and the Lord of his hearers and that they should worship Him alone (43:64), which is the same testimony the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān records from him (3:51) and the same line the sūrah of the Table draws when it says that the men who call the Messiah God have denied the truth (5:72). The verses in front of the reader are the makkans' last attempt to turn a prophet's name into a licence for idolatry, and the sūrah answers it by letting that prophet speak for himself.

---

## Verse 43:58

> They exclaimed, “Which is better: our gods or Jesus?” They cite him only to argue. In fact, they are a people prone to dispute

**"Which Is Better, Our Gods or Him?"**

The verse gives the makkans' question in their own words: which is better, our gods or Jesus? The Book adds its verdict on the question itself, that they raised it only to argue, and that they were a people given to quarrelling. The sūrah has already shown what kind of argument this was, because the men asking the question did not believe in Jesus any more than they believed in the messenger sent to them; his name was a tool they picked up. The Book's answer is the verse that follows, where Jesus is named as a servant of God and not a partner in the divine (43:59), and the sūrah of the Prophets states the same about the whole class of beings the idolaters placed beside God, that they are honoured servants who act only by His command (21:26-27). The question collapses the moment the comparison is examined, because Jesus himself was a worshipper.

**The Quarrelling the Book Names**

The verse's closing words describe the makkans as a disputatious people, and the Book makes the same observation in the sūrah of the Cave, where it says that every kind of example has been set out in the Qur'an and that the human being is the most argumentative of creatures (18:54). The Book never treats argument as the road to faith; it treats it as a habit that can be used to avoid one. The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān gives the Prophet the instruction for the case once the argument had been made and refused: after the knowledge has come, the matter is settled by submission rather than by further talking, and the verse that follows gives the Prophet the words of that decisive call (3:61). The verse in front of the reader closes the makkans' case with the diagnosis rather than the reply, because the reply has already been given by the man whose name they were using.

---

## Verse 43:59

> He was only a servant We showed favour to, and made as an example for the Children of Israel

**He Was Only a Servant**

The verse corrects the Meccan misunderstanding of Jesus by declaring that he was only a servant whom God favored. The "only" is the corrective that dismantles the divinization the opponents had exploited, because it places Jesus firmly in the category of the creature who is honored, not the Creator who is worshiped. The word for servant carries the full dignity of one in right relation to God, so the verse does not demean Jesus but defines him truly, as the one who, like all the prophets, stood before the Lord in obedience. This ties into the chapter's defense of the strict oneness of God, because the verse removes Jesus from the frame of the debate the Meccans had constructed and relocates him in the line of the warners. The reader is invited to see that the highest honor of a human is to be a servant of God, and that the Meccan try to make him a god was a demotion dressed as exaltation.

---

The verse is one of the clearest statements in the Book on the nature of Jesus, and the Book states it repeatedly: he was a servant of God given favour, and he is the sign of the Hour (43:59, 61). The sūrah of Maryam records his own words at birth — that he is a servant of God, given the Book and made a prophet (19:30) — and the sūrah of the Women gives the same correction against the claims made of him (4:171–172). The phrase about being an example for the Children of Israel is read with the tradition that he spoke in the cradle and worked the signs among them, which the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān narrates in full (3:46–49).

## Verse 43:60

> Had We willed, We could have easily replaced you ˹all˺ with angels, succeeding one another on earth

**Angels Could Have Taken Your Place**

The verse says that if God had willed, He could have made angels succeed one another on the earth in place of the human beings He was addressing. The Book states the same possibility in several places to show that the human being is not a necessity for God. The sūrah of Repentance warns the community that if it does not go out for the cause, God will replace it with another people who will not be like it (9:39), and the sūrah of Muḥammad states the same readiness in plainer terms: if the community turns away, God will bring a people in its place, and they will not be like it (47:38). The sūrah of the Table describes the kind of replacement the Book has in mind: God will bring a people whom He loves and who love Him, humble with the believers and firm against the deniers (5:54). The verse in front of the reader puts the angelic alternative in the middle of the same argument.

**The Successors Named Elsewhere**

The verse uses the word for successors, which is the word the Book applies to human beings on the earth in general. The sūrah of the Originator says that God made the people successors on the earth, and goes on to describe what follows from the trust (35:39), the sūrah of the Cattle says that God placed the community as successors and raised some above others in ranks to test them (6:165), and the sūrah of the Cow opens the story of the human being with God's announcement that He is placing a successor on the earth (2:30). The succession the verse is talking about is therefore not a privilege the makkans could rely on; it is an arrangement that God can change, and the verse states that the arrangement could have been given to the angels. The sentence is a warning and a mercy in one: the men being addressed were replaceable, and they had not been replaced.

---

## Verse 43:61

> And his ˹second˺ coming is truly a sign for the Hour. So have no doubt about it, and follow me. This is the Straight Path

**The Second Coming as Sign**

The verse declares that the second coming of Jesus is a true sign for the Hour, and the declaration places the return of the favored servant within the Last-Day horizon that the chapter has been invoking since its opening warnings about the Day. The "sign for the Hour" means that the event is among the signs that mark the approach of the final reckoning, so that the career of Jesus bridges the present age and the end of time. This ties into the chapter's theme of the return of all matters to God for judgment, because the second coming is the hinge on which the human history and the divine accounting turn, and the verse names it as a certainty rather than a speculation. The reader is invited to see the sweep of salvation history, in which the servant honored in the previous verse is also the figure whose return signals the close of the world, so that the Christology and the teaching about the Last Day meet in a single line.

---

The verse is read in two ways that the commentators set out: that his coming, meaning his return before the Day, is a sign of the Hour, which the traditions and many tafsīrs support, or that the knowledge of the Hour is given through him, since he announced it to his people. Either way the verse's command is the same, and the sūrah states the doctrine that fixes it a few verses later: the servant of God is not a son of God, and the community is told to follow the straight path. The Book makes the same connection between Jesus and the Hour in the sūrah of the Women (4:159) and in the sūrah of al-Zukhruf's own account of his birth and speech, so the verse gathers the sūrah's christology into one sentence.

## Verse 43:62

> And do not let Satan hinder you, ˹for˺ he is certainly your sworn enemy

**Do Not Let Satan Hinder**

The verse turns from the christological heights to a practical warning, instructing the hearer not to let Satan hinder him, and the instruction assumes that the adversary is actively working to obstruct the very following of the Straight Path that the previous verse commended. The "do not let" is a call to vigilance rather than a prediction, because it places the responsibility for resistance in the hearer's hands, implying that the hindrance can be refused. This ties into the chapter's earlier portrait of the devilish associate given to the one who turns a blind eye to the Reminder, because the verse now addresses the believer directly, warning him that the same adversary who attaches to the rejecter is also the one who would divert the follower. The reader is invited to take the warning as a description of the spiritual terrain, in which the path is contested and the traveler must guard his step.

---

The warning against being hindered by Satan follows directly from the command to follow the straight path in the previous verse, and the Book states the same instruction in several places: do not let Satan turn you aside (43:62; and 17:53), and he is the enemy who should not be obeyed (36:60). The Book describes the danger in the sūrah of the Cave, where the devil's hindrance is a matter of making the truth seem distant (18:63), and in the sūrah of the Women, where Satan promises falsehood and misleads (4:120). The verse's phrase about the sworn enemy is the same description used at the beginning of the human story (2:168, 7:22).

## Verse 43:63

> When Jesus came with clear proofs, he declared, “I have come to you with wisdom, and to clarify to you some of what you differ about. So fear Allah, and obey me

**Jesus Came With Clear Proofs**

The verse returns to the voice of Jesus, recounting that when he came with clear proofs, he declared the message that the chapter has been building toward. The "clear proofs" echo the signs that marked the prophets throughout the surah, so that Jesus is placed firmly in the line of those who bore divine evidence. The proofs are the counterpart of the signs of creation and the signs of Moses, because the scripture consistently portrays the true messenger as accompanied by the evidence of his commission. This ties into the chapter's theology of the sign, because the clarity of the proofs is the mercy that removes the excuse of ignorance, so that the hearer of Jesus, like the hearer of the Qur'an, is without cover for refusal. The reader is invited to see the continuity of the prophetic witness, in which each messenger comes with the demonstrations that correspond to the declaration he makes.

---

**The Words Jesus Spoke**

The verse gives his message in his own voice: *I have come to you with wisdom, and to make clear to you some of what you disagree about; so be mindful of God and obey me*. Two things are claimed. He brings *ḥikmah*, and he comes to clarify the disputed points among his people — which is what the Qur'an says of him everywhere, a messenger sent to the children of Israel to confirm what was in the Torah and to settle what they had argued about. And then he asks for what every messenger asks: the fear of God and obedience to the man sent with the message.
**Where the Verse Leads**

The next verses complete the declaration and show the result. Jesus says: God is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him; this is a straight path (43:64). And then the sūrah reports what happened to that straight path: the factions disagreed among themselves, and woe follows (43:65). The disagreement about Jesus is not an afterthought in this sūrah; it is the thing the sūrah keeps returning to, from the cradle speech to the dismissal of the claim to sonship. The reader is asked to hold two facts side by side — a servant who asked for worship of God alone, and a body of followers who divided over exactly that. His earlier mention in the sūrah as a sign of the coming Hour (43:61) keeps the same company: he is a servant whose case will be settled by the One who sent him.

## Verse 43:64

> Surely Allah ˹alone˺ is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.”

**The Words of Jesus in the Sūrah**

The verse gives Jesus's declaration: God is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him alone; this is the straight path. The Book records the same sentence from him in three other places, and each one is set in an argument with the people who claimed him. The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān gives the words as his message to the Children of Israel, closing with the same phrase about the straight path (3:51), the sūrah of Mary records the same declaration in the same short form (19:36), and the sūrah of the Table puts the matter on the day of judgement, when Jesus is asked whether he told people to take him and his mother as gods, and he answers that he never said anything but what he was commanded to say: worship God, my Lord and your Lord (5:116-117). The verse in front of the reader is the creed of the man the makkans were waving around as an argument.

**What the Words Do to the Dispute**

The declaration removes Jesus from the debate in the plainest way available: the man whose name was being used to defend the idols is recorded as commanding the worship of God alone. The sūrah of the Women states the same limit from the other side when it tells the People of the Book not to go beyond the bounds in their religion and not to say anything about God except the truth (4:171), and the sūrah of Mary records the cradle speech in which Jesus calls himself the servant of God before he says anything else (19:30). The verse leaves the reader with the contrast the sūrah has been building since the laughter broke out a few lines earlier: the makkans debated about a servant who had already told everyone what he was, and the sūrah gives them his own words as the answer to their quarrel.

---

## Verse 43:65

> Yet their ˹various˺ groups have differed among themselves ˹about him˺, so woe to the wrongdoers when they face the torment of a painful Day

**Their Groups Differed**

The verse turns from the clear confession of Jesus to the lamentable fact that his followers split into various groups and differed among themselves about him, and the division is the historical sequel to the unity he preached, the tragic fracture of a community that could not hold to the Straight Path. The "various groups" evokes the sects that formed around the figure of Jesus, each claiming him while departing from the worship of the one God he commanded, and the verse names the discord as the predictable outcome of losing the center. This ties into the chapter's condemnation of the people prone to dispute, because the division among the followers is the institutional form of the disputatiousness the surah has been exposing, so that the verse shows the fruit of the contention in the fragmentation of a community. The reader is invited to see that the disputes about Jesus did not begin with the Meccans but continued among those who claimed to honor him.

---

The division the verse names is the division the Book condemns in several places: the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān records the groups differing about Jesus (3:55 fold), the sūrah of Maryam describes the dispute over him (19:34–36), and the sūrah of the Cow states the general warning to those who split their religion into sects (30:32). The sūrah's own method is to state the plain fact of his servanthood, as the verse before it does, and then to lament the division that followed the clear report. The verse's closing warning belongs to the wrongdoers of every group, so the passage applies to the sūrah's first audience as well.

## Verse 43:66

> Are they waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺

**Waiting for the Hour**

The verse poses a pointed question to the mockers, asking whether they are waiting for the Hour to arrive and take them unawares, and the question is the chapter's return to the theme of the scoffers who dared the Prophet to hasten the Day, now turned back on them as a reproach. The "waiting" is the posture of those who treat the reckoning as a distant rumor rather than a present reality, and the verse exposes that posture as both arrogant and foolish. This ties into the chapter's opening preoccupation with the Hour and the disbelievers' demand to see it hastened, because the verse shows that the same people who pretended to want it are in fact merely waiting for it to ambush them. The reader is invited to see the inconsistency of the scoffer, who dares the Day while living as though it will never come, and the question is the scripture's way of naming the contradiction.

---

The question of what the mockers are waiting for is repeated in the sūrah of the Bees and in the sūrah of the Spoils, where the answer is that the Hour comes on them while they are unaware, or the punishment arrives first: the sūrah of the Cattle says they will be seized by it while they perceive nothing (6:44), and the sūrah of the Bee gives the same warning for the people who asked about the timing of it (16:77). The verse's closing picture, that it will come upon them suddenly while they do not sense it, is the Book's standard description of the Day for those who denied it (12:107).

## Verse 43:67

> Close friends will be enemies to one another on that Day, except the righteous

**Friends on That Day**

The verse states what the judgement does to human relationships: the close friends will be enemies to one another on that day, except the righteous. The Book keeps the same bond in view in other places. The sūrah of the Distinguisher shows the wrongdoer on that day biting his hands and crying that he wishes he had taken a path with the messenger, and then naming the friend who made him stray after the reminder had reached him (25:27-29), and the sūrah of Ibrāhīm records Satan telling his followers, once the judgement has been passed, that he cannot help them and they cannot help him (14:22). The sūrah of the Cow gives the same scene from the other side, where the men who were followed disown the men who followed them as the two groups see the punishment together (2:166).

**Who the Exception Is**

The verse makes one exception, and it is the people the sūrah has just been describing: those who believed in the signs and submitted. The Book's friendships of this world are built on interest, kinship and shared advantage, and its report of the last day is that all three of those bonds are cut. The friendship that survives is the one formed for God's sake, and the Book's description of the people of the garden at the end of the sūrah is entirely about a company that has come through together: the servants told that they have nothing to fear and nothing to grieve (43:68), those who believed in the signs (43:69), and the spouses rejoicing (43:70). The verse in front of the reader is the line drawn through every friendship a person carries in this life, and the sūrah's answer to it is that the ones built on the truth are standing on the other side of it.

---

## Verse 43:68

> ˹who will be told,˺ “O My servants! There is no fear for you Today, nor will you grieve—

**The Servants With Nothing to Fear**

The verse gives the words spoken to the people who come through: O My servants, today there is no fear for you and nothing to grieve over. The Book uses the same pair of promises to mark out the people who are safe at the end: the sūrah of Jonah says that God's close servants have nothing to fear and nothing to grieve about (10:62), and the sūrah of the Cow records the promise given to Adam's descendants that whoever follows the guidance and puts right what is in him has the same assurance (2:38). The two words cover the two directions a person's anxiety runs: fear is about what is coming, and grief is about what is behind. The Book names both here and cancels both.

**Whom the Address Reaches**

The verse does not name the people being welcomed; the titles arrive in the two verses after it, and the Book often holds the description back in this way to make the reader measure himself against the words. The sūrah of the Troops uses the same order in its account of the souls that are told to enter the garden with the greeting of peace (39:73-74), and the Book's account of the men who believed and stayed firm attaches the same two promises to them (46:13). The address in front of the reader is spoken to servants, which is the title the Book gives to the men who accepted the position rather than claiming more, and the sūrah has just finished describing the creation of the human being from a drop as the reason no one should expect to be consulted about the appointment of the messengers (43:60-62).

---

## Verse 43:69

> ˹those˺ who believed in Our signs and ˹fully˺ submitted ˹to Us˺

**Those Who Believed in the Signs**

The verse names the people being addressed: those who believed in the signs of God and submitted themselves to Him. The Book attaches the same description to the reward in every account of the garden, and it keeps the two parts of it together. The sūrah of the Cow describes the people of the garden as those who believed and did good work (2:25), the sūrah of the Believers lists what the successful do and closes with the promise that they are the inheritors of paradise (23:1-11), and the sūrah of the Bee states the promise in a sentence: the men and women who do good work and believe will be given a good life in this world and a reward better than what they did (16:97). The two halves of the description in the verse in front of the reader cover the inner and the outer life: the belief that holds to the signs, and the submission that hands the rest over.

**The Submission the Verse Names**

The word the verse uses is the one the Book applies to the whole religion: the acceptance of God's authority without argument. The sūrah of the Cow calls Abraham a man who submitted and was not a polytheist (3:67), and the sūrah of the Women describes the best in faith as the man who submits himself to God, does good, and follows the way of Abraham (4:125). The men of Makkah had spent the sūrah arguing, mocking and asking for a great man from the two cities (43:31), and the verse in front of the reader describes the opposite posture, the one that has stopped asking for credentials and accepted the message. The Book's picture of the end is arranged so that the two groups the sūrah has been following — the ones who laughed and the ones who believed — are named in the same terms they were given in this life.

---

## Verse 43:70

> Enter Paradise, you and your spouses, rejoicing.”

**Enter the Garden With Your Spouses**

The verse gives the word of welcome: enter paradise, you and your spouses, rejoicing. The Book's accounts of the garden usually include the company of the people who enter it, and the details are consistent across the sūrahs. The sūrah of Yā Sīn describes the people of paradise on that day busy in their joy, lying in the shade on raised couches with their spouses (36:55-56), and the sūrah of the Mount describes the people reclining on thrones facing one another, joined with companions of lovely eyes (52:20). The Book's inclusion of spouses in the welcome is its way of saying that the garden is not a solitary exception to the life the people lived; the relationships formed for God's sake come through with them.

**The Rejoicing Named in the Verse**

The verse's last word describes the state of the people entering: full of joy. The Book uses the same word for the reward in the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, which closes with the promise that the people who do good have forgiveness and a great reward, and it names the joy of the men who are given the record in their right hand (69:19 records the man who says, take and read my record). The sūrah has spent its length on men who laughed at the messengers (43:47), applauded when a prophet was named in mockery (43:57), and quarrelled over the truth (43:58), and its picture of the end gives the last word of joy to the people they were laughing at. The verse in front of the reader turns from the argument to the door, and everyone the sūrah has described has already decided which side of it he will be standing on.

---

## Verse 43:71

> Golden trays and cups will be passed around to them. There will be whatever the souls desire and the eyes delight in. And you will be there forever

**Whatever Souls Desire**

The verse declares that there will be whatever the souls desire, and the comprehensiveness of the promise is its point, because the Paradise is not a place of fixed rations but of fulfilled longing, where the deepest wants of the heart are met by the Giver who knows them. This joins with the chapter's teaching that what is with God is far better and more lasting, because the verse shows that the "whatever" of the soul is supplied in the garden, so that the believer's truest desires are not denied but answered. One is invited to trust that the God who created the soul knows its hunger, and that the Paradise is the place where the hunger is filled rather than forbidden, so that the restraint of the world is answered by the abundance of the end. The desire is the chapter's way of honoring the human heart's deepest reach.

**Gold Here, Silver There, and the Promise Kept Open**

The vessels in this verse are of gold; the Book serves the same table in another sūrah in silver, with trays of silver and cups of crystal, measured to the exact measure of the drink (76:15-16), and the cup that is passed among the men of the garden in the Sūrah of the Event is the cup of a drink that never runs out (56:18). The variety is the point of the picture: the honour is not one material but a whole table with no shortage and no end. The verse's promise — whatever the souls desire and the eyes delight in — rests on reports that keep it open. The Prophet said that God has prepared for the righteous what no eye has seen, no ear has heard and no human heart has imagined (al-Bukhārī; Muslim), and the Book states it as a reward kept hidden from view (32:17). The verse's final words, that they will be there for ever, are the gift after the gift: the table is not a pause before a departure.

**The Garden That Is Inherited**

The next verses finish what this one begins: this is the garden you have been made to inherit for what you used to do, and in it there is fruit in plenty, of which you eat (43:72-73). The Book's word for entering it is inheritance, and it uses the word elsewhere: the men of Firdaws will inherit it and stay for ever (23:11), the garden is given as an inheritance to the servants of God who were righteous (19:63), and the people of the garden in the Sūrah of the Heights call out that they have found what their Lord promised them to be true (7:44). A few verses further on the sūrah sets the other table: the tree of Zaqqūm, the food of the sinner, like molten brass boiling in the belly, and a punishment they will never leave (44:43-46; 43:74-77). The reader is shown both tables, and the Book leaves the choice as plain as the verse states it.

---

## Verse 43:72

> That is the Paradise which you will be awarded for what you used to do

**The Garden Given for What They Did**

The verse says that the garden the people are entering is what they are given for what they used to do. The Book ties the reward to the deeds in every account, and it makes clear that the goodness is a gift as well as a consequence. The sūrah of the Heights describes the people of the garden saying that praise belongs to God who guided them to this, and adding that they would not have been guided had God not guided them (7:43), and the sūrah of the Troops records the same speech in the garden, that their Lord fulfilled His promise and made them inherit the land to settle wherever they wished (39:74). The Book's habit is to put the reward first and the praise second, because the people who arrive know that they did not build the house they were given.

**What the Verse Does With the Past**

The verse's phrase for what the people did is in the past tense, which is where the whole sūrah's argument has been pointing. The men of Makkah had spent their lives arranging the world according to their own standing, and the sūrah has met them at every turn with the Book's answer: the mercy is distributed by God (43:32), the silver roofs were held back (43:33), the quarrelling proved nothing (43:58), and the record of everything said in secret was being written the whole time (43:80). The verse in front of the reader closes the account with the sentence that the garden is the fruit of what the people did while the arguing was still going on. The next verse adds what the garden holds, and it is a longer list than the wealth the makkans were measuring themselves by.

---

## Verse 43:73

> There you will have abundant fruit to eat from

**Fruit in Abundance**

The verse says that the garden will hold abundant fruit for its people to eat. The Book's descriptions of the garden return to fruit again and again, and each passage supplies a different note. The sūrah of the Event says the people of the right hand will be served any fruit they choose (56:20), the sūrah of Muḥammad describes the paradise promised to the mindful as gardens with rivers of water, milk, wine and honey, and it adds that they will have fruit of every kind there (47:15), and the sūrah of the Cow describes the people given a fruit in the garden saying that this is what they were given before, since they are given fruit resembling one another in goodness rather than in identity (2:25). The Book's word for the abundance matches the generosity of its other promises: what grows in the garden does not run out.

**The Contrast With the Silver Roofs**

The sūrah's argument about wealth ran through the middle of it, and the fruit is the closing answer to the silver roofs and the couches and the ornaments of gold. The men who measured themselves by the metal they owned were told that the whole arrangement could have been handed to them in greater measure, and that the Hereafter belongs to the mindful (43:35), and the men who asked why the messenger had no golden bracelets (43:53) have already been answered by the garden the sūrah is describing. The verse in front of the reader names food rather than metal, and the Book's reward is always described in the terms of the thing a person actually needs: the fruit of the garden, the shade, the water, and the company of the people he loves. The last three verses of the chapter turn to the other end of the arrangement.

---

## Verse 43:74

> Indeed, the wicked will be in the torment of Hell forever

**The Wicked in the Torment**

The verse turns from the garden to the men who come to the other end: the wicked will be in the punishment of the fire, and they will stay there. The Book's descriptions of the fire keep the same note of permanence, and it supplies the details elsewhere. The sūrah of the Prostration says that the fire is the home of the rebellious, and that whenever they try to escape from it they are driven back into it with the words that they should taste the punishment they used to deny (32:20), and the sūrah of the Women describes the cycles of the punishment, where the skin is replaced as it burns so that the people go on tasting it (4:56). The sūrah of the News states the same permanence in the shortest form: they will be told to taste it, since all they will get from God is more (78:30).

**The Contrast the Sūrah Draws**

The sūrah has been pairing the two ends of the account since its opening verses, and this verse closes the pairing: the men who laughed at the signs and quarrelled over the truth are placed in a punishment that does not end, while the men who believed and submitted are in a garden with everything they wish for. The Book never presents the fire as an arbitrary sentence; it presents it as the state the refusal produces, and the verses that follow in this sūrah state the principle in God's own words, that He did not wrong the people but that they wronged themselves (43:76). The verse in front of the reader is short and its work is to fix the two destinations before the sūrah makes its final appeal to the men who were still listening.

---

## Verse 43:75

> It will never be lightened for them, and there they will be overwhelmed with despair

**A Punishment That Never Eases**

The verse says two things about the punishment of the men in the fire: it will not be lightened for them, and they will be left in despair. The Book states the same permanence in the sūrah of the Originator, where the disbelievers in the fire are not allowed to be finished by death and the torment is not lightened for them (35:36), and it gives the same picture in the sūrah of the News, where the deniers are told to taste and that nothing awaits them except more torment (78:30). The Book adds the scene that shows the despair in the sūrah of Qāf, where the fire is asked on the day of judgement whether it is full, and it answers by asking whether there are any more to come (50:30), and in the sūrah of the News, where the disbeliever cries out that he wishes he were dust (78:40).

**What Despair Means in the Book**

The word the verse uses for the people's condition is the loss of every hope, and the Book presents that state as the thing the punishment does to a person as much as the pain. The sūrah of the Troops records the men in the fire asking to be brought out so they may do good works, and the answer that they were given their chance and did nothing with it (35:37), and the sūrah of the Prostration pictures them driven back whenever they try to climb out (32:20). The men of Makkah were warned in this life about the day when the door would close, and the verse in front of the reader shows them on the other side of it. The next verse states the principle that governs the whole scene, and it is the one the sūrah wants its reader to carry away.

---

## Verse 43:76

> We did not wrong them, but it was they who were the wrongdoers

**The Wrong Was Theirs**

The verse states the justice of the whole punishment in a sentence: God did not wrong them, but they were the wrongdoers. The Book makes the same statement wherever it explains the destruction of a nation. The sūrah of Hūd says of the ruined towns that God did not wrong them but that they wronged themselves, and that the gods they called on beside God were no help when the command came (11:101), and the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān puts the same sentence in the account of the people who were given the Book and then went wrong (3:117). The Book's rule is stated at the start of its accounts of judgement: no soul is burdened with the load of another (6:164), and God does not wrong anyone by so much as a speck (4:40).

**Why the Sentence Is Needed at the End of the Sūrah**

The makkans had spent the sūrah arguing that their position was the reasonable one, and one of their arguments was that if God had disapproved He would have prevented them (43:20). The verse answers that claim in the plainest terms by placing the responsibility where it belongs. The Book's accounts always include this sentence at the end of the punishment, because the point of showing the fire is not to display power but to state the truth about who chose what. The reader of the sūrah has watched the whole sequence — the respite, the messenger, the mockery, the signs, the promises broken and the arguments answered — and the verse in front of him says that nothing in it was done to the men of Makkah against their will. The sūrah's next verses return to the same men while they are still alive, and to the things they were saying.

---

## Verse 43:77

> They will cry, “O Mâlik! Let your Lord finish us off.” He will answer, “You are definitely here to stay.”

**The Cry to Mālik**

The verse records the men in the fire calling out to the keeper of the fire: O Mālik, let your Lord finish us off, so that the punishment would at least end; and the answer comes back that they are staying. The Book names the keepers of the fire in the sūrah of the Covered One, where it says the fire is overseen by nineteen watchers (74:30-31 explains the number and its purpose), and the sūrah of the Prohibition describes them as formidable and severe angels who never disobey what God commands them and who do exactly what they are told (66:6). The request itself shows what the men had come to: after mocking the messenger and asking for signs and arguing over the truth, their last request is for their own destruction, and even that is refused.

**The Door That Stays Shut**

The Book keeps the same request in the record of the people of the fire in other sūrahs. The sūrah of the Originator says they will call out for the keeper to let their Lord finish them, and the answer will be that they are staying (43:77 is the passage, and 35:36 states the same for the people of the fire who are given neither death nor relief), and the sūrah of the News ends with the disbeliever wishing that he were dust (78:40). The Book's picture is that the punishment of the next life has no exit built into it, which is why the sūrah's message to its own readers is concentrated in the verses they are still alive to read. The verse after this one gives the reason the men were there, and it is the sentence the sūrah has been leading up to from its opening letters.

---

## Verse 43:78

> We certainly brought the truth to you, but most of you were resentful of the truth

**The Truth They Resented**

The verse states God's complaint against the men who had heard the message: the truth was brought to them, and most of them were resentful of it. The Book makes the same observation about the same audience in the sūrah of the Believers, where the question of the Prophet's sanity is answered with the sentence that he came to them with the truth and that most of them resent the truth (23:70). The sūrah has shown what the resentment looked like in practice: the men called the revelation magic and rejected it outright (43:30), asked why it was not revealed to a great man from one of the two cities (43:31), argued over Jesus when his name was mentioned (43:58), and plotted while the messenger kept reciting. The verse names the attitude behind all of it in one word.

**What the Resentment Was Made Of**

The Book does not treat the makkans' position as a search for answers that had not arrived. The sūrah has already recorded their admission that God created the heavens and the earth (43:9; 43:87), so the objection was never really about who made the world. What the men resented was a message that levelled them with the people they looked down on: the slaves, the poor and the men with no clan to protect them who accepted it before they did. The Book states the same diagnosis about the same men in the sūrah of the Cave, when it reports their demand that the Prophet send away the poor believers who sat with him (18:28). The verse in front of the reader ends the account of their reasoning and turns to the plans they were making.

---

## Verse 43:79

> Or have they mastered some ˹evil˺ plan? Then We ˹too˺ are surely planning

**Plans Against Plans**

The verse asks whether the men had settled on some plan of their own, and answers with the sentence that God is also planning. The Book uses the same exchange in the sūrah of the Spoils, where it recalls the night the disbelievers conspired to capture the Prophet, kill him or drive him out, and says that they were planning while God was planning, and that God is the best of planners (8:30). The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān states it in one line, that they plotted and God plotted (3:54), and the sūrah of the Ants gives the same scene with the detail that the men never knew another plan was in motion: they made their plan and God made His, without their perceiving it (27:50). The sūrah of the Morning Star gives the shortest version of the whole exchange, that they are devising a plan and God is planning too (86:15-16).

**The Plans the Book Names**

The makkans' plans against the Prophet are recorded in detail elsewhere: the meeting at Dār al-Nadwa, the attempt to confine him, the decision to send a group from different clans so that the responsibility would be spread, and the migration that took him out of their reach. The verse in front of the reader states the theological fact that governs all of it, and the sūrah of the Morning Star closes the point with the sentence that the plot of the plotters returns on their own heads (86:17). The verse turns the reader's attention from the political manoeuvring in Makkah to the One whose plan was already running, which is the same comfort the Book gives the Prophet in the sūrah of the Spoils: the men had decided, and the decision was already in other hands.

---

## Verse 43:80

> Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺

**The Secrets That Are Being Written**

The verse asks whether the plotters imagined that God does not hear their secret thoughts and their private conversations, and then answers with the strongest possible terms: God hears it all, and the angels who are with them are writing it down. The Book states the same fact in the sūrah of Qāf, where it says that not a word does a person utter without an observer standing ready to write it (50:18), and the sūrah of the Splitting Open describes the scribes in detail: vigilant watchers, honourable angels who record everything, who know what people do (82:10-12). The same knowledge is stated in the sūrah of Repentance, where the Book asks whether the plotters know that God is fully aware of their secret thoughts and their private talk and knows everything unseen (9:78), and the sūrah of the Iron describes the One who established Himself upon the Throne and knows everything that enters the earth and everything that comes out of it (57:4).

**What the Verse Does to the Plotters**

The sentence turns the makkans' meeting into an open session. The Book's point is not that God is watching for evidence; it is that the plans people make in the dark were never secret in the first place, and that the record was being kept while the plan was still being drawn. The sūrah of the Jinn quotes the jinn themselves on the impossibility of hiding from God, saying that they had learned that they could not frustrate God on the earth or escape Him into the sky (72:12). The verse in front of the reader leaves the plotters with the knowledge that everything they said in those meetings was filed, which is the same fact the sūrah has already stated about the men who argued over the truth in order to discredit it, whose statements are recorded and who will be questioned about them (43:19).

---

## Verse 43:81

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the Most Compassionate ˹really˺ had offspring, I would be the first worshipper.”

**"If He Had Offspring, I Would Be the First Worshipper"**

The verse gives the Prophet a sentence to say: if the Most Compassionate had offspring, he would be the first of those who worship. The Book handles the claim of offspring in several places and always with the same intensity. The sūrah of the Prophets reports the claim and answers it in two lines, that God is glorified above what they say and that the beings they are describing are honoured servants who act only by His command (21:26-27), and the sūrah of Mary describes the claim in the strongest terms: the heavens are almost torn apart, the earth split and the mountains crumbled at the statement that the Most Compassionate has a child (19:88-91). The sūrah of the Believers states the impossibility from the other side, that God has never had offspring and that there is no god besides Him (23:91).

**How the Commentators Read the Sentence**

The teachers of the community read the sentence in the verse in two ways that point in the same direction. On one reading it is an impossible condition of the kind the Book uses to make a proposition untenable: if God had a son — which He does not — the Prophet would be the first to worship the One he serves, since worship is what the messenger was sent for. On the other reading, the sentence is a plain declaration that the Prophet is the first servant of God and that no offspring of God exists to be worshipped. The Book's own answer to the whole matter is stated in the sūrah of the Cattle: God is the originator of the heavens and the earth, and how could He have a child when He has no consort (6:101)? The verse in front of the reader closes the sūrah's argument about the family the idolaters assigned to God, and the next verse states the glory that belongs to Him instead.

---

## Verse 43:82

> Glorified is the Lord of the heavens and the earth, the Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim

**The Lord of the Throne, Above What They Claim**

The verse answers the claim of offspring with the words of praise: glorified is the Lord of the heavens and the earth and the Lord of the Throne, far above what they describe. The Book uses the same formula in the same argument in several places, and it names the throne each time. The sūrah of the Prophets says that if there had been other gods besides God in the heavens or the earth, both realms would have been corrupted, and it closes with the words that God is glorified as the Lord of the Throne, above what they attribute to Him (21:22). The sūrah of the Troops answers the same claim with the statement that God has taken no son, that there is no god beside Him, and the sūrah of the Ranks ends its description of the idolaters' claims with the declaration that God is far above what they describe (37:159).

**The Throne in the Book's Language**

The Book names the throne as the seat of God's authority over everything, and it uses it whenever it wants to state the distance between the Creator and the created. The sūrah of the Throne describes the Lord of the heavens and earth whose care never tires (2:255), and the sūrah of the Heights names the throne in its opening praise (7:54). The verse in front of the reader uses the title to end the argument about offspring: the men who assigned children to God were talking about a throne they had never seen and a kingdom whose affairs none of them attended. The sūrah's next verse turns from the answer to the men themselves, and it gives them a brief space of time to enjoy while the answer stands.

---

## Verse 43:83

> So let them indulge ˹in falsehood˺ and amuse ˹themselves˺ until they face their Day, which they have been warned of

**Leave Them to Their Enjoyment**

The verse says: let them indulge in idle talk and amuse themselves until they meet the day they were promised. The Book uses the same sentence in the sūrah of the Heights, where the instruction is to let the deniers eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by false hopes, since they will soon know (15:3), and the sūrah of the Ascending Stairways repeats it in nearly the same words: let them indulge and amuse themselves until they face the day they were threatened with (70:42). The book of the Book's patience is stated in the same form in the sūrah of the Scatterers, where it ends its warnings with woe to the deniers when they meet the day they are warned of (51:60).

**What the Instruction Means**

The verse is not a licence for the deniers; it is the Book turning away from argument to time. The men had been given the messenger, the Book, the signs and the warning, and the sūrah has recorded every answer they gave to all of it. At a certain point the Book stops pressing the case and leaves the men with their own amusement, because the next thing they will meet is the day itself. The Book's instruction to the Prophet in the sūrah of the Overwhelming Event follows the same logic: his duty is to remind, and he is not to compel anyone (88:21-22). The verse in front of the reader closes the sūrah's argument with the men of Makkah, and the two verses after it return to the description of God that the whole chapter has been building.

---

## Verse 43:84

> It is He Who is ˹the only˺ God in the heavens and ˹the only˺ God on the earth. For He is the All-Wise, All-Knowing

**God in the Heavens and on the Earth**

The verse states that God is the only God in the heavens and the only God on the earth, and it closes with two of the names: the All-Wise, the All-Knowing. The Book uses the same statement in the sūrah of the Cattle, where it says that God is the only true God in the heavens and the earth, that He knows what people conceal and reveal, and that He knows what they do (6:3). The sūrah of the Cow states the same ownership from the other direction, that to God belong the east and the west, so that wherever a person turns he is facing towards God, who is all-encompassing and all-knowing (2:115). The verse leaves no room for the local deities the makkans kept around their sanctuary, because it names both realms and gives each of them one God.

**The Two Names at the End of the Verse**

The names the verse chooses are the answer to the two errors it has been handling. The wisdom answers the objections the men had raised about the distribution of mercy and wealth, since the sūrah has already said that God distributes provision and raises some above others as a test (43:32; 6:165). The knowledge answers the plotting that the previous verses described, since the plotters were being recorded while they drew their plans (43:80). The sūrah of the Cattle pairs the same two names with the same content when it says that God knows the secret and the hidden and what a person earns (6:3). The verse in front of the reader gives the men of Makkah the full statement of what they were dealing with, and the two verses that follow turn to the hour and the intercession.

---

## Verse 43:85

> And Blessed is the One to Whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and everything in between! With Him ˹alone˺ is the knowledge of the Hour. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned

**Blessed Is the One**

The verse opens a blessing upon the One to whom the kingdom belongs, and the blessing is the worshipful counterpart of the doctrinal affirmation just made, because the chapter moves from the confession that He is the only God to the praise that He is the blessed Giver of all dominion. The "blessed" is the ascription of all good to the Giver, the recognition that the kingdom is not a bare fact but a gift that fills the creation with beneficence, so that the verse joins the earlier doxology in lifting the hearer's heart. This ties into the surah's opening and closing theme of the kingdom, because the verse returns to the sovereignty with a tone of gratitude rather than argument, showing that the doctrine ends in praise. The reader is invited to say the blessing with the scripture, because the One who holds the kingdom is the One whom the heart is made to bless, and the verse is the chapter's way of turning the creed into the doxology.

**What the Verse Says and Where It Stands**

The verse names the One to whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, and it closes with the statement that He is the One known in the earlier scriptures, since the Torah and the Gospel had announced Him. The sūrah has been answering the claims of the men who assigned daughters to God and sons to themselves, and it has dealt with the men who made the angels female, and with those who served what they had been told not to. The verse in front of us ends that passage with a blessing on the One who holds the kingdom, and the blessing is the answer to all of it.
**The Two Sentences That Close the Sūrah**

The verse after this one is the sūrah's last: if you ask them who created them, they will say God, so how are they turned away? The Book uses the same tactic in the Sūrah of the Crowds, where the argument is closed by the confession the deniers were making with their own mouths, and in the Sūrah of the Ant, where the question about the creation is answered by the list of what God made. The sūrah ends with the men admitting the creator and refusing the worship, and the verse in front of us states the praise of the One they admitted.
**The Word for the Knowledge of Him**

The verse says that He is known in the earlier books, and the commentators read that as the promise of the messenger whose coming the earlier scriptures announced. The Book makes the same claim in three other places, where the Prophet is described as written in the Torah and the Gospel (7:157), and the Sūrah of the Cow has the same announcement to the People of the Book (2:146). The passage tells the men of Makkah that the God they were ignoring was the One their own books had named, and the closing question of the sūrah is built on it.

---

## Verse 43:86

> ˹But˺ those ˹objects of worship˺ they invoke besides Him have no power to intercede, except those who testify to the truth knowingly

**The Intercession That Belongs to God**

The verse says that the beings the idolaters call on besides God have no power to intercede, except for those who testify to the truth knowingly. The Book states the rule of intercession in several places, and it keeps the permission in God's hand each time. The sūrah of the Prophets says of the angels that they do not intercede except for whom God approves, and that they stand in awe of Him (21:28), the sūrah of the Throne asks who could intercede with God except by His permission (2:255), and the sūrah of Mary states the same limit with a condition: none will have any right to intercede except the one who has taken a covenant from the Most Compassionate (19:87). The sūrah of the Troops puts the whole matter in a sentence placed in the Prophet's mouth: all intercession belongs to God alone, to whom the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs, and to whom everyone returns (39:44).

**What the Verse Does to the Idols**

The men of Makkah were not claiming that their idols created anything; the sūrah has already recorded their admission that God made the heavens and the earth (43:87; 43:9). Their hope was that the idols would speak for them on the day of the account, which is the position the Book reports and answers in the sūrah of the Companies, where the men who took protectors besides God say they worship them only to bring them closer to Him (39:3). The verse in front of the reader removes the foundation of that hope in one sentence: the intercession the makkans were counting on belongs to the One they were refusing, and it is granted only on His terms. The next verse takes the admission of creation and turns it into the closing argument of the sūrah.

---

## Verse 43:87

> If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created them, they will certainly say, “Allah!” How can they then be deluded ˹from the truth˺

**The Admission That Closes the Case**

The verse points to what the men of Makkah said whenever they were asked a plain question: if you ask them who created them, they will say God; and then it asks how they can be deluded away from the truth. The sūrah has used the same admission twice before, first when it named the answer they give about the heavens and the earth (43:9), and again when it asked whether the men who answer that way could be turned from the truth (43:9-10). The Book returns to the same question in the sūrah of the Spider, where the men who are asked who created the heavens and the earth and subjected the sun and the moon answer with God's name, and the verse asks how they can be deluded (29:61), and in the sūrah of Luqmān, where the question is put again and the Book tells the Prophet to say that praise belongs to God while most of them do not know (31:25).

**The Contradiction the Sūrah Leaves Standing**

The verse is the sūrah's closing argument against the religion of Makkah, and its force is that it needs no evidence beyond what the men themselves said. The sūrah of Sheba records the same question and the same answer, and adds the instruction to ask them who provides for them from the sky and the earth, before stating that God is the truth and that what they call on besides Him is falsehood (34:24). The sūrah of Yūnus runs the same questions through a list — who provides from heaven and earth, who owns the hearing and sight, who brings out the living from the dead — and then asks whether they will not be mindful (10:31). The sūrah of the Companies gives the answer they gave about creation and then asks whether they will not remember God (39:38). The verse in front of the reader leaves the men of Makkah with their own statement in their hands.

---

## Verse 43:88

> ˹Allah is Aware of˺ the Prophet’s cry: “O my Lord! Indeed, these are a people who persist in disbelief.”

**The Prophet's Cry**

The verse records the Prophet's complaint to his Lord: O my Lord, these are a people who will not believe. The Book records the same cry from him in the sūrah of the Distinguisher, where the Messenger says that his people have treated this Qur'an as something to be abandoned (25:30), and the sūrahs describe the grief out of which the cry came. The sūrah of the Poets asks whether the Prophet will destroy himself with sorrow because the people do not believe (26:3), and the sūrah of the Cave puts the same question in a longer form, asking whether he will grieve himself to death over their denial if they continue to disbelieve (18:6). The Book records the mockery, the plans and the refusal in the verses before this one, and the verse gives the reader the one sentence that came out of all of it.

**Why the Complaint Is Recorded**

The Book does not hide the difficulty of the Prophet's position, and it does not present him as a man who was unaffected by the refusal of his own people. The complaint in front of the reader is the last word of the sūrah's narrative before its closing instruction, and the sūrah of the Distinguisher uses the same cry as the opening of God's answer about the Day. The Book's account of the Prophet's own life records the same weight in the year of sorrow and in the persecution of his companions, and the sūrahs that console him always answer with the same two things: the messenger's duty is to deliver, and the account is God's. The verse in front of the reader states the grief plainly, and the last verse of the sūrah answers it.

---

## Verse 43:89

> So bear with them and respond with peace. They will soon come to know

**Bear With Them and Say Peace**

The sūrah closes with the instruction to the Prophet: turn away from them and say peace, for they will come to know. The Book gives the same response as the mark of the servants of the Most Compassionate in the sūrah of the Distinguisher, where they are described as people who walk the earth humbly and who answer the ignorant with a word of peace (25:63), and the sūrah of the Stories records the same practice from the men who heard idle talk, who turned away from it with the words that they had their deeds and the others had theirs, and that they wished no quarrel (28:55). The sūrah of the Cow gives the same instruction at a moment of provocation, telling the believers to pardon and bear with the People of the Book's envy until God delivers His decision (2:109).

**What the Closing Words Look Like in Practice**

The Book's final word in the sūrah is not a curse and not a threat from the messenger; it is a greeting and a statement that the day of knowledge is coming. The reports of the Prophet's life describe the same policy in action: the men who insulted him in the streets of Makkah were answered with the peace, and the city that had driven him out was entered years later with a general pardon. The sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān states the same instruction in the passage that describes how the Prophet's gentleness held his companions together (3:159), and the Book's own summary of the messenger's character is the verse in the sūrah of the Pen that names him as a man of tremendous character (68:4). The sūrah ends with the greeting, and the last word in it is that the men who said the things it has recorded will come to know what they were dealing with.
