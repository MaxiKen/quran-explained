# Sūrah al-Zukhruf (Chapter 43) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Elitist Objection and the Gold Bracelets**

The historical climate of az-Zukhruf reflects the snobbish class prejudice of the Qurayshi aristocracy. The chieftains of Makkah could not understand why God would bestow prophetic revelation upon an orphaned, modestly circumstanced man like Muḥammad ﷺ, rather than upon the multi-millionaire oligarchs of the two commercial metropolises of Arabia. Verse 31 preserves their petulant objection: *“And they said: 'Why was this Qur'an not sent down to a great man of the two cities [Makkah and al-Ṭā'if]?'”* and, they echoed the ancient imperial prejudice of Pharaoh, who had mocked Moses' rough clothing and stammering tongue, sneering: *“Why have bracelets of gold not been cast down upon him, or angels accompanied him in procession?”* (43:53). Sūrah az-Zukhruf responds with devastating divine sarcasm: *“Do they distribute the mercy of your Lord? It is We who have apportioned between them their livelihood in the life of this world and raised some of them above others in degrees, so that they may employ one another in service; but the mercy of your Lord is better than what they amass”* (43:32).

**The Structural Anatomy of the Sūrah**

The movement of Sūrah az-Zukhruf unfolds in five distinct thematic movements. The opening movement (1–14) exalts the Qur'an as a clear Arabic scripture originating from the *Umm al-Kitāb* (the Mother of the Book) in the Divine Presence, reminders of how past nations were destroyed for mocking messengers, and presents the foundational prayer of travel when mounting beasts or vehicles: *“Exalted is He who has subjected this to us, and we could not have [otherwise] subdued it. And indeed, to our Lord we will return”* (43:13–14). The second movement (15–25) exposes the grotesque hypocrisy of the pagan Arabs who attributed daughters to God—claiming angels were female deities—while if one of them was given news of the birth of a female child, his face darkened with suppressed rage (43:17). It exposes the intellectual bankruptcy of their universal excuse: *“Indeed, we found our forefathers upon a path, and we are guided in their footsteps”* (43:22). The third movement (26–56) reviews sacred prophetic history: Abraham renouncing the idols of his father, making monotheism an enduring word among his posterity (*kalimatan bāqiyatan fī ʿaqibih*); the divine warning that whoever turns away from the remembrance of the Most Compassionate has a devil assigned to him as an inseparable, intimate companion (*qarīn*); and Moses confronting Pharaoh, whose contempt for Moses and reliance on his irrigation canals ended in Pharaoh and his hosts being drowned into a precedent and an example for future generations. The fourth movement (57–67) addresses the controversy surrounding Jesus: clarifying that ʿĪsā was not a divine being to be worshipped beside God. A noble servant favored with signs (*in huwa illā ʿabdun anʿamnā ʿalayh*), warning of the Hour, and stating the immortal law of human relationships in verse 67: *“Close friends, that Day, will be enemies to each other, except for the righteous.”* The closing movement (68–89) contrasts the joy of the believers in Paradise, served with golden trays and cups containing whatever the soul desires and the eyes delight in (*wa-fīhā mā tashtahīhi al-anfusu wa-taladhdhu al-aʿyun*), against the damned criminals in Hell pleading with the angelic warden, Mālik, to let their Lord end their existence, only to be answered: *“Indeed, you are to remain!”* (43:77). It concludes with the Prophet's pained sigh: *“O my Lord, indeed these are a people who do not believe!”* and the divine parting instruction: *“So turn aside from them and say: 'Peace!' For they will soon know.”*

Had God not feared that all humanity would collapse into a single monolithic culture of disbelief, He would have made the roofs, doors, stairs, and beds of the wicked out of pure silver and gold (43:33–35)—because in the sight of God, all the gold in the universe has less value than the wing of a mosquito.

## Verse 43:1

> Ḥâ-Mĩm

**The Fourth of the Hawamim Opens**

This verse is the opening of chapter 43 with the two letters ḥāʾ and mīm, and it places the sūrah within the same family of seven consecutive chapters, from 40 through 46, that begin with this pair and are called the Ḥawāmīm. Being the fourth of that group, the letters here carry a cumulative weight: a listener who has already heard them three times knows the threshold he is crossing and expects the same combination of mystery and majesty that marked the earlier members. The repetition is not monotony but a signature, a way of telling the audience that this stretch of the Book belongs to one continuous address even as each sūrah develops its own argument. The letters therefore function as a recurring door, and chapter 43 walks through it into a theme about false worship, the status of Jesus, and the certainty of the Hereafter.

**Why This Surah Carries the Same Opening**

The question arises why chapter 43 repeats an opening already used by its neighbors rather than striking out on its own. The answer lies in the unity of the Ḥawāmīm as a single sequence heard in recitation; the shared front binds the seven into one literary and religious block, so that the distinct arguments of each sūrah are heard as variations on a shared theme. The repetition also builds expectation: by the fourth occurrence, the hearer knows to brace for majesty and to set aside the demand for immediate sense. The sameness is a cue, like a musical key, telling the audience how to listen. The sūrah is free to develop its particular concerns because the opening has already done the work of establishing who is speaking and on what authority.

## Verse 43:2

> By the clear Book

**A Book That Needs No Decoding**

The verse swears by the clear Book, and the clarity stands in deliberate contrast to the unreadable letters that opened the sūrah. Where the letters withheld their sense, the Book declares its own transparency, so that the two openings together make a single point: the mystery is only at the threshold, and what lies inside is plain. The oath by the Book rather than by something in creation is unusual and significant; scripture swears by itself, as if to say that its own existence is the surest evidence available. The clarity claimed is not superficial but salvific, a message a person can understand and upon which he can act. The verse therefore reassures the hearer that the unopenable front was not a promise of more obscurity to come, but a frame around a teaching meant to be grasped.

**The Oath That Frames the Surah**

The formula "By the clear Book" is an oath, and oaths in the Qur'an typically introduce a weighty claim that follows. Here the oath frames the sūrah's entire argument, lending the force of a vow to what comes after: the refutation of false gods, the account of Moses and Pharaoh, and the description of the Hereafter. By swearing by the Book's clarity, the verse commits the hearer to take seriously the message that the Book contains, because the thing sworn by is presented as self-evidently true. The oath also connects this sūrah to others that open with oaths, building a rhythm in which the scripture vouches for itself before it speaks. The clarity is not argued for here; it is asserted as the ground on which the rest of the chapter will stand.

## Verse 43:3

> Certainly, We have made it a Quran in Arabic so perhaps you will understand

**The Word "Perhaps" and Human Freedom**

The verse says the Arabic was given "so perhaps you will understand," and the word "perhaps" is theologically loaded. It preserves the freedom of the hearer by refusing to guarantee the result; understanding is made possible, not forced. A stronger word would have implied that the clear Arabic automatically produces belief, but the sūrah knows that clarity can be met with rejection, as its later accusations against those who turned away confirm. The "perhaps" therefore honors the moral agency of the audience, presenting the scripture as an offer that requires a response. The softness of the word is not weakness but respect for the human being, who must cross the distance between hearing and understanding by his own assent. The verse gives every facility and then leaves the rest to the will of the hearer.

**The Gentleness of "So Perhaps"**

The tone of the verse is notably gentle, offering the Arabic scripture as a means rather than wielding it as a threat. The combination of certainty about the act ("Certainly, We have made it") with uncertainty about the reception ("so perhaps you will understand") models the patience the sūrah will later urge on the Prophet toward his opponents. The gentleness is strategic as well as merciful, because a hearer addressed with respect is more likely to consider the message than one addressed with contempt. The verse so embodies the daʿwa, the invitation, that the sūrah practices throughout, and it does so in its first explanatory clause. The Arabic is given not to overwhelm but to enable, and the "perhaps" keeps the door open for the hearer to step through it.

## Verse 43:4

> And indeed, it is—in the Master Record with Us—highly esteemed, rich in wisdom

**The Master Record Above the Earth**

The verse locates the Qur'an in a higher archetype, the Master Record kept "with Us," that is, with God, before it ever descended to the earth. The phrase points to the teaching about the Preserved Tablet, the heavenly original of all revelation of which the earthly scripture is a copy sent down. By asserting this higher residence, the verse protects the Qur'an from the charge that it is a merely human production; its true home is the divine court, and its earthly appearance is a sending-forth rather than an invention. The "with Us" stresses intimacy and security, the Book resting in the custody of the One who authored it. The verse so gives the scripture a double location, earthly in Arabic and heavenly in the Record, and it draws its authority from the second.

**Esteem and Wisdom in the Highest Copy**

The verse describes the Master Record as "highly esteemed, rich in wisdom," and both attributes belong to the original rather than only to the revealed copy. The esteem signals the value placed on the Book in the highest assembly, so that its earthly reception by mocking humans does not touch its standing above. The wisdom signals its content, a message saturated with sound judgment about creation, worship, and the end of things. By attributing these qualities to the Record, the verse insists that what the Meccans dismissed was in fact honored in the place that matters most. The pairing also answers the sūrah's later portrayal of the opponents as resentful of the truth; their low estimation is contrasted with the high esteem the Book enjoys with its Author. The wisdom is the substance that makes the esteem deserved.

## Verse 43:5

> Should We then turn the ˹Quranic˺ Reminder away from you ˹simply˺ because you have been a transgressing people

**A Rhetorical Question That Defends the Gift**

The verse asks whether God should withdraw the Reminder from the people simply because they have transgressed, and the question defends the persistence of revelation against the logic of desert. The form is rhetorical; the implied answer is that withdrawal would be contrary to the mercy that sent the Reminder in the first place. The opponents might have reasoned that their sinfulness forfeited the message, or that a just God would abandon a rebellious community, and the verse rejects that reasoning by treating the Reminder as a mercy that outlasts the offense. The question by this protects the hearer from the despair of thinking the door has closed, while also implicitly reproaching the transgression that made the question necessary. The defense of the gift is at the same time an indictment of the ingratitude.

**The Transgression That Might Have Forfeited It**

The verse names the people "a transgressing people," and the label acknowledges the real fault without letting it cancel the mercy. To call them transgressors is to admit that the warning is warranted and that the community has earned reproach, which makes the continued sending of the Reminder all the more striking. The sūrah will later describe their persistence in disbelief and their resentment of the truth, so the label here is not an one-off accusation but a description of a settled pattern. The verse so holds two truths together: the people are genuinely rebellious, and the Reminder is genuinely given despite that rebellion. The combination is the definition of grace, a gift extended across the very boundary that might have justified its withdrawal, and the question forces the hearer to feel the weight of receiving what he has not earned.

## Verse 43:6

> ˹Imagine˺ how many prophets We sent to those ˹destroyed˺ before

**Those Destroyed as a Warning to the Present**

The nations "destroyed before" are presented not as ancient trivia but as living warnings to the present hearers. The sūrah's method throughout is to recite the fate of past rejecters so that the current audience might take heed, and this verse opens that method by naming the destroyed as the object of attention. The destruction is the consequence the Meccans risk by following the same pattern, and the verse asks them to "imagine" it so that the consequence becomes vivid rather than abstract. The warning is so built into the grammar of the verse, which turns memory into a mirror. The destroyed are not only gone; they are sent, as the sūrah later says of some, as examples and lessons for those after them, and this verse begins that pedagogic use of the past.

**The Prophets Sent and the Nations Lost**

The verse balances two elements, the prophets sent and the nations lost, and the balance is the core of its argument. Together they display both divine patience and divine justice, the two attributes the sūrah will keep in view. The "how many" applies to the prophets, implying a corresponding multitude of chances given and refused, so that the destroyed are not victims of silence but of persistence in rejection. The verse so frames the coming narratives of Moses, Jesus, and the mocked messengers by establishing the pattern they will illustrate: warning given abundantly, refusal answered finally. The command to imagine the many prophets sent to the destroyed nations is a moral act disguised as a cognitive one, because to picture those scenes rightly is to feel the weight of one's own position in the same pattern. The "how many" also quietly indicts the hearer's forgetfulness, since the long history of warning and ruin was available to him and yet he stands on its edge unchanged.

## Verse 43:7

> But no prophet ever came to them without being mocked

**Mockery as the Constant Reception**

The verse states a stark pattern: no prophet ever came to a rejecting people without being mocked, and the "but" connects it to the preceding memory of the many prophets sent to the destroyed nations. The mockery is presented as the near-universal reception of warning, so consistent that its absence would be the surprise. The sūrah uses the pattern to normalize the Prophet's own experience, because the Meccans' ridicule of him is not a refutation of his claim but the predictable response of a resistant audience. The verse by this drains the mockery of its power to discourage, reframing it as the badge of a long and honored line rather than a mark of failure. The constancy also serves as a warning, since the nations that mocked were the nations that were destroyed, and the Meccans are being shown their likely end.

**The Pattern That Mecca Inherits**

By asserting that every prophet was mocked, the verse places Mecca squarely inside an established pattern rather than outside it as a special case. The Meccans might have thought their ridicule disqualified the Prophet, but the verse turns the argument around: their ridicule qualifies him as a genuine messenger, since mockery is what genuine messengers receive. The sūrah's later account of how the Meccans mocked the Qur'an as magic or poetry is so anticipated and interpreted in advance, shown to be the standard reaction of a hard-hearted people. The inheritance is double-edged, because to inherit the pattern of mockery is also to inherit the pattern of destruction that followed it. The verse therefore closes the gap between the hearer's present conduct and the fate of those destroyed before, making the parallel unavoidable.

## Verse 43:8

> So We destroyed those who were far mightier than these ˹Meccans˺. The examples of ˹their˺ predecessors have ˹already˺ been related

**The Mightier Ones Who Perished**

The verse follows the account of mocked prophets with the destruction of those who mocked them, and it stresses that the destroyed were "far mightier" than the Meccans, so that military or numerical strength offered no protection against the judgment. The comparison is calculated to humble an audience that prided itself on its power and its position as the custodian of the Kaaba. The sūrah by this removes the false security of might, showing that the nations erased from the earth were, by the Meccans' own standards, superior to them. The destruction is presented as a settled fact of history rather than a threat alone, which gives the warning the weight of precedent. The verse so turns the Meccans' confidence in their own strength into the very ground of their vulnerability, because the stronger have already fallen.

**The Relation of Past to Present**

The verse establishes the relation between the destroyed predecessors and the current hearers as one of typology, the past as the pattern the present is in danger of completing. The sūrah's whole use of history rests on this relation, in which the ruin of one people becomes the warning of the next. The "already related" examples are therefore not closed cases but open mirrors, and the verse keeps them open by applying them to the Meccans in the same breath. The relation also protects the sūrah from being merely retrospective, because the past is invoked precisely to act on the present. The hearer who recognizes the type is given the chance to break it, and the hearer who does not is silently enrolled in the line of the destroyed. The verse so makes memory a live, decision-shaping force rather than a museum.

## Verse 43:9

> If you ask them ˹O Prophet˺ who created the heavens and the earth, they will certainly say, “The Almighty, All-Knowing did.”

**The Test That Exposes Contradiction**

The verse proposes a simple test: ask the idolaters who created the heavens and the earth, and they will answer correctly, attributing the creation to the Almighty, All-Knowing. The test is devastating because it exposes the contradiction between the Meccans' stated theology and their actual worship, since the same people who confess God as Creator still assign Him partners. The sūrah uses the test to show that the opponents are not ignorant of the truth but inconsistent with it, which makes their idolatry a matter of practice rather than knowledge. The verse by this shifts the burden onto the hearer, who cannot plead unawareness of God's creative sole dominion. The test is also a rhetorical trap, because the honest answer condemns the behavior that follows it, and the sūrah lets the hearer Spring it on himself.

**The Answer They Cannot Avoid**

The verse predicts the answer with certainty, "they will certainly say," because the Meccans' own tradition acknowledged God as the maker of the cosmos even while they worshipped others alongside Him. The confidence reflects the shared semitic heritage in which the one high God was never denied as Creator, only supplemented by lesser beings. The sūrah exploits this consensus to corner the opponent, since a people who agree on creation cannot coherently defend their polytheism. The unavoidable answer is the wedge the sūrah drives into the idolater's position, using his own confession against his practice. The verse so shows that the Qur'an's argument often works by drawing out what the opponent already believes and holding it up against what he does, rather than by importing a foreign premise.

## Verse 43:10

> ˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for you, and set in it pathways for you so that you may find your way

**The Earth Made Habitable**

The verse turns from the creation of the heavens and the earth, confessed by the idolaters in the previous verse, to the specific preparation of the earth for human life, and the shift is from magnitude to mercy. To "lay out" the earth is to spread it as a stable, hospitable surface amid the waters and the void, a dwelling rather than a chaos, and the verse presents this ordering as a deliberate act for "you," the hearer. The sūrah by this moves from the generality of creation to the intimacy of provision, showing that the same God who made the cosmos made the ground beneath the hearer's feet fit for his life. The laying out is not only physical but accommodating, the shaping of a world that receives and sustains the creature, and the verse ties that accommodation directly to the One the Meccans already named as Creator.

**Finding the Way as a Double Meaning**

The phrase "so that you may find your way" carries a double sense, the physical finding of routes across the earth and the moral finding of the right path through life, and the sūrah exploits both. The literal pathways prepare the hearer to understand the greater guidance, because the God who lays routes on the ground is the one who lays the straight path for the soul. The sūrah's later urging to hold firmly to what was revealed because it is the straight path depends on this earlier echo, in which "finding the way" is already established as a divine gift. The double meaning also warns, because a people given physical pathways but refusing the moral one would be inconsistent with the very God who made both. The verse so plants the image of guidance in the soil of ordinary travel.

## Verse 43:11

> And ˹He is the One˺ Who sends down rain from the sky in perfect measure, with which We give life to a lifeless land. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺

**Rain Measured From the Sky**

The verse continues the sequence of signs by naming God as the one who sends down rain from the sky in perfect measure, and the emphasis on measure answers the idolater's tendency to see provision as random or as the work of lesser powers. The "perfect measure" means the rain is neither too little nor too much for the life it sustains, a precision that bespeaks a knowing distributor rather than a chance atmosphere. The sūrah's earlier confession by the Meccans that God is All-Knowing gains concrete illustration here, because measured rain is the visible execution of knowing care. The verse by this turns a familiar weather event into a proof, inviting the hearer to see in every downpour the same hand that laid the pathways of the earth. The sky, already confessed as God's creation, is here shown as His instrument of sustenance, and the connection tightens the monotheistic case.

---

The Book uses the same measured rain as the ground of the resurrection in the sūrah of the Ranged: the water sent down revives the earth, and the same God revives the dead (43:11; and 50:9–11, where the same proof ends with the raising of the dead). The mention of measure is the detail the commentators dwelt on: the water comes in a quantity fitted to the need of the land, and the sūrah of the Believers states the same measure in its account of the sky (23:18). The verse's closing phrase, that the dead land comes alive and so will you be brought out, is one of the sūrah's clearest statements of the resurrection from the commonest of sights.

## Verse 43:12

> And ˹He is the One˺ Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs, and made for you ships and animals to ride

**All Things Created in Pairs**

The verse continues the catalog of signs by stating that God created all things in pairs, and the claim reaches beyond the human and animal mates mentioned earlier to the broad duality woven through creation. The sūrah's interest in pairs is both descriptive and religious, because duality is a signature of the one Creator who originates contrast and complement without being Himself divided. The "all things" extends the sign to the widest possible scope, inviting the hearer to notice pairing everywhere, in the sexes, in day and night, in the balancing of the cosmos. The verse by this uses a pervasive feature of experience as a pointer to its source, and it does so in a way the idolater cannot easily contest, since the pairing of things is obvious to anyone who looks. The sign prepares the later, more specific instances of ships and animals as particular outworkings of the general principle.

## Verse 43:13

> so that you may sit firmly on their backs, and remember your Lord’s blessings once you are settled on them, saying, “Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so ˹on our own˺

**Sitting Firmly on Their Backs**

The verse continues the catalog of signs by describing the rider settled firmly on the backs of the animals and ships, and the firmness is both literal stability and a figure for the security God provides in travel. The sūrah's audience, a trading people of caravans, would recognize the relief of a steady mount and a sound vessel, and the verse attributes that stability to the Creator who subjected the creatures for human use. The "sit firmly" also implies a command of the situation that the rider did not achieve by his own strength, since the beast's submission is a gift. The sūrah by this turns the ordinary experience of riding into a sign of divine provision, and it does so by emphasizing the security that the hearer enjoys without having earned it. The firmness is the visible result of the unseen act of subjection the verse will name.

**The Glory Formula Spoken**

The verse puts a specific prayer on the rider's lips, the glorification of the One who subjected these creatures, and the formula is a compact acknowledgement that the subjection was His act and not the rider's. The sūrah later repeats this glorification as the proper response of the traveler, showing that the sign is meant to produce worship rather than mere convenience. The "Glory be" is a way of returning the creature's dependence to its source, refusing to let the benefit end in the self. The verse by this encodes the correct interpretation of the sign directly into the act of travel, so that the hearer who rides rightly is also praying rightly. The formula is the bridge between the physical gift and the spiritual recognition the sūrah seeks.

---

## Verse 43:14

> And surely to our Lord we will ˹all˺ return.”

**The Return Stated**

The verse states the return of all to the Lord as the conclusion of the traveler's prayer, and the return is the Last-Day horizon that gives the preceding gratitude its weight. The sūrah's sign-sequence, having moved from the subjected creatures to the glorification of their subjecter, now lifts the hearer's eyes to the end, where every journey ends before God. The "all" is total, sparing no one the return, which equalizes the rider and the rider's Lord in the single fact of accountability. The verse by this ties the daily act of travel to the final act of judgment, so that the mounted believer is already en route to the Gathering. The return is stated as certainty rather than hope, because the sūrah treats the resurrection as the settled structure of reality, attested by the signs the hearer has just been shown.

**The Subheading "Allah's Daughters?"**

The text of the verse as transmitted carries the appended phrase "Allah's Daughters?," which functions as the title of the section that follows and signals the sūrah's next argumentative turn. The placement of this heading at the close of the return-clause is deliberate, because the confession that all return to the one Lord is the exact premise that the coming accusation about divine daughters will test. The sūrah by this uses the believer's own prayer as the threshold into the critique of idolatry, so that the hearer who affirms the return is primed to see the incoherence of assigning offspring to the God he returns to. The appended title is not part of the revelation's sense but a marker of the topic, and its position here shows the chapter's architecture, moving from the sign to the scandal of the idolaters' claim.

## Verse 43:15

> Still the pagans have made some of His creation out to be a part of Him. Indeed, humankind is clearly ungrateful

**The Pagans' Attribution of Partners**

The verse accuses the pagans of making some of God's creation out to be a part of Him, and the charge names the core error of idolatry, the assimilation of the creature into the Creator. The sūrah's signs have just shown that the creation, from the earth to the mounts, is subjected by God and distinct from Him, so the pagans' move to make parts of it "part of Him" directly contradicts the lessons of the signs. The verse by this connects the argument to the preceding argument, using the traveler's gratitude as the foil for the idolater's confusion. The phrasing "a part of Him" captures the logic of association, in which deities are treated as limbs or offspring of the divine rather than as separate beings, and the sūrah exposes this as a category error at the heart of worship.

**Why Making Creation Part of Him**

The verse's specific formulation, that creation is made "part of Him," targets the Meccan habit of treating idols, angels, and jinn asextensions of the divine rather than as separate servants, and the sūrah's later questions about daughters and intercessors develop this same point. The error is to collapse the distance the signs established, between the subjecter and the subjected, and to pretend that the creature shares in the essence of the Creator. The sūrah's earlier assertion that there is nothing like Him is the direct negation of this move, because a God unlike anything cannot have parts drawn from His creation. The verse therefore names the precise metaphysical mistake and ties it to the observable behavior of the pagans, making the abstract error concrete in their cult.

## Verse 43:16

> Has He taken ˹angels as His˺ daughters from what He created, and favoured you ˹O pagans˺ with sons

**Daughters From the Angels**

The verse asks whether God took daughters from among the angels He created, and the question targets the Meccan belief that the angels were the daughters of God, a belief the sūrah presents as both theologically incoherent and personally telling. The sūrah's previous verse accused the pagans of making creation part of God, and this verse specifies that the part they claimed was daughters drawn from the angelic creation. The question implies the answer is plainly no, because the angels are themselves created beings who cannot be the offspring of their Creator without collapsing the distinction the signs established. The verse by this takes the idolaters' own claim and holds it up for inspection, letting its absurdity speak, and it does so with a sarcasm that the sūrah will sustain through the daughters argument.

**Sons Favoured for the Pagans**

The verse pairs the absurd assignment of daughters to God with the observation that the pagans themselves were favored with sons, and the pairing exposes the inconsistency of their values. The Meccans preferred sons and regarded daughters as a source of shame, yet they assigned to God what they despised for themselves, a contradiction the sūrah delights in highlighting. The "favoured you with sons" is a factual description of the gender preference among the Arabs, who celebrated male births and buried female ones, and the verse uses that preference as the wedge against their theology. The sūrah by this shows that the idolaters' doctrine about God's daughters is not reverence but projection, the dumping of what they disvalued onto the divine while keeping what they prized for themselves. The inconsistency is the argument.

---

## Verse 43:17

> Whenever one of them is given the good news of what they attribute to the Most Compassionate, his face grows gloomy, as he suppresses his rage

**The Gloomy Face at the News**

The verse depicts the idolater's reaction when told the news of what they themselves attribute to the Most Compassionate, namely a daughter, and the gloom on his face is the physical betrayal of his true feelings. The sūrah's previous verse exposed the inconsistency of assigning daughters to God while preferring sons for themselves, and this verse supplies the lived evidence, the man's downcast look when the same fate he scorns is announced to him. The "good news" is used with bitter irony, because what the idolater calls good news for God is bad news for himself, and the verse lets the irony land in the contortion of his expression. The gloom is the body's testimony against the creed, a confession the hearer cannot retract once observed.

**Suppressed Rage at a Daughter**

The verse says the man suppresses his rage, and the rage reveals that the announcement of a daughter is experienced as an insult rather than a joy, even when the daughter is nominally attributed to God. The suppression is significant, because the idolater cannot openly resent the divine without abandoning his pretense of reverence, so the anger is forced underground where it shows only in the gloom. The sūrah by this exposes the insincerity of the daughters doctrine, since the emotional reaction belies the verbal honor, and it shows that the idolater's worship is a performance he does not feel. The rage also confirms the cultural shame about daughters that the previous verse diagnosed, now visible as a felt response rather than a stated preference.

---

## Verse 43:18

> ˹Do they attribute to Him˺ those who are brought up in fineries and are not commanding in disputes

**Those Brought Up in Fineries**

The verse asks whether the idolaters attribute to God those who are brought up in fineries, and the "those" refers to the female idols or the angels they honored, described with mocking precision as creatures of ornament and luxury. The sūrah's argument about the daughters claim reaches a sarcastic peak here, as it pictures the alleged divine offspring as pampered, bejeweled beings unfit for the dignity of divinity. The fineries expose the idolaters' own contribution to the cult, because the idols were dressed and adorned by human hands, so the "offspring of God" was in fact a product of human vanity. The verse by this turns the idolaters' own devotion into evidence against them, showing that the beings they exalted were projections of their aesthetic and social tastes rather than revelations of the divine.

**Not Commanding in Disputes**

The verse adds that these beings are not commanding in disputes, meaning they lack the authority and cogency to settle arguments, which is exactly what one would expect of idols fashioned and dressed by humans. The sūrah uses the phrase to underline the weakness of the alleged divine offspring, who cannot even speak with force in a controversy, let alone govern creation. The "disputes" also echoes the sūrah's earlier theme of argument and contention, suggesting that the idols are silent and helpless where the true God is the decisive judge of every dispute. The verse by this contrasts the impotence of the finery-clad beings with the sovereignty of the One who settles all matters, and it does so with a sneer that the hearer is meant to share. The lack of command is the tell of their fraudulence.

---

## Verse 43:19

> Still they have labelled the angels, who are servants of the Most Compassionate, as female. Did they witness their creation? Their statement will be recorded, and they will be questioned

**The Idolaters and the Angels**

This verse returns to a strand that runs through Sūrah al-Zukhruf, the Meccan idolaters' confused beliefs about the unseen world. Having described how they assigned daughters to God and decorated their idols with finery, the chapter now turns to their treatment of the angels, whom they had come to regard as female beings belonging to the divine household. The word "still" carries a tone of exasperation, as if the scripture is recounting a series of errors one after another, each more revealing than the last. The angels were, in the Meccan imagination, elevated to a status that bordered on divinity, and the verse's task is to pull them back down to their true rank. This matters because the entire argument of the chapter rests on preserving the absolute oneness of God, and any promotion of a creature into the divine circle is a breach of that oneness.

**Angels as Servants, Not Offspring**

The verse sharply corrects the idolaters' error by naming the angels exactly what they are: servants of the Most Compassionate. In the Qur'anic scheme, the angel is a creature of pure obedience, a messenger who executes the command of God without will of its own, let alone kinship to Him. To call them female and by this imply they are His daughters is to commit a double error: it manufactures a family relationship that does not exist and it demotes the angel from servant to offspring, a category shift that the verse refuses. The title "Most Compassionate," al-Rahman, is loaded, because it is the very name the idolaters themselves used and even revered; the verse reminds them that the Compassionate has servants, not sons and daughters. The correction is not only semantic but religious: the creature, however exalted, remains a creature.

---

## Verse 43:20

> And they argue, “Had the Most Compassionate willed, we would have never worshipped them.” They have no knowledge ˹in support˺ of this ˹claim˺. They do nothing but lie

**The Argument From God's Will**

The specific argument is that "Had the Most Compassionate willed, we would have never worshipped them." On the surface this appeals to the omnipotence of God: nothing occurs except by His will, therefore our worship, occurring, must be within His will. But the premise is a misunderstanding of what divine will means. The Qur'an teaches that God wills the existence of human freedom, including the freedom to go astray, while simultaneously willing the guidance that calls the stray back. That a wrong choice happens does not mean it is approved; it means it is permitted within a larger economy of testing. The idolaters conflated permission with approval and by this excused their own rebellion. The verse refuses the conflation, because to accept it would make God the author of His own dishonor, a conclusion the scripture will not allow. The argument so reveals a confused theology dressed in the language of submission.

---

## Verse 43:21

> Or have We given them a Book ˹for proof˺, before this ˹Quran˺, to which they are holding firm

**The Challenge About a Prior Book**

This verse poses a pointed question to the idolaters: has God given them, before the Qur'an, a book to which they are now holding firm? The challenge is rhetorical, and its force depends on what the question implies. If the Meccans were truly people of a revealed scripture, then their objection to the Qur'an would have a basis; but if they have no such book, then their confidence in their ancestral ways is unfounded. The verse by this turns the tables on those who treated the new revelation as an unwelcome innovation, asking whether their own position rests on any revelation at all. The word "before this" explicitly contrasts the Qur'an with whatever the idolaters might claim as prior authority, and the chapter has already shown they had no warrant even for their angelology.

**Why the Verse Undermines Their Claim**

Taken together, the components of this verse dismantle the idolaters' self-image as a people already in possession of the truth. If they had no prior book, then their rejection of the Qur'an was not the defense of an existing faith but the protection of a habit. If they "hold firm" to nothing written, then their confidence is misplaced; if their authority was customary rather than revealed, then the Prophet's call is not an intrusion but a fulfillment of what they lacked. The verse undermines their claim not by mocking them but by calmly asking for the evidence they cannot produce, and the request itself becomes the refutation. In the flow of Sūrah al-Zukhruf, this is the hinge between the exposure of their bad theology and the exposure of their bad genealogy, because it shows that neither their picture of the unseen nor their appeal to the past can survive scrutiny.

---

## Verse 43:22

> In fact, they say, “We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are following in their footsteps.”

**The Argument From Ancestry**

The verse quotes the idolaters stating their real reason for rejecting the Prophet: they found their forefathers following a particular way, and they are walking in their footsteps. This is the candid core of their resistance, stripped of the religious justifications they offered elsewhere. Where they had appealed to the will of God or to the status of the angels, here they admit that their loyalty is to the path of their ancestors. The Qur'an treats this admission as decisive evidence, because it reveals that the obstacle to faith was not intellectual doubt but traditional attachment. The phrase "in fact" signals that this is the underlying truth beneath the more elaborate defenses, the thing the idolaters really mean when they object to the revelation. By quoting them directly, the verse lets the opponents indict themselves, showing that their commitment was to a heritage, not to the truth.

**"We Found Our Fathers Upon a Way"**

The image of finding one's fathers upon a way is vivid and telling. "A way," sira or milla, is a path of life and belief, and the idolaters describe themselves as inheriting a road already worn smooth by those before them. There is a certain comfort in this picture, the comfort of a well-trodden path and a known destination, and the verse does not deny that inheritance can carry wisdom. What it questions is the assumption that the mere antiquity of a path certifies its rightness. To find one's fathers upon a way is to inherit a fact, not to inherit a justification; the verse will press exactly this distinction. The candidness of the quote also exposes the circularity of the position: they follow the way because their fathers did, and they trust the fathers because that is the way, a loop that never reaches a foundation outside itself.

---

## Verse 43:23

> Similarly, whenever We sent a warner to a society before you ˹O Prophet˺, its ˹spoiled˺ elite would say, “We found our forefathers following a ˹particular˺ way, and we are walking in their footsteps.”

**The Pattern Repeated Across Nations**

This verse widens the lens from the Meccans alone to the whole sweep of salvation history, showing that the excuse quoted in the previous verse was not a local quirk but a recurring human pattern. Whenever God sent a warner to a society before the Prophet Muhammad, the response was the same: the people appealed to the way of their forefathers. By using the word "similarly," the Qur'an draws a direct line between Mecca and every prior community that rejected its messenger, turning the Meccans' resistance from an unique defiance into a familiar repetition of error. This is a double-edged move: it comforts the Prophet by showing he is not facing anything new, and it warns the Meccans by showing where their repetition leads. The pattern, once named, becomes a mirror in which the present audience can see its own reflection in the ruined nations of the past.

**The Prophet's Place in the Sequence**

By saying "before you," the verse places the Prophet Muhammad explicitly within the lineage of warners, the latest in an unbroken line that stretches back through every revealed community. This is both a honor and a burden: he is confirmed as a true messenger because he meets the same reception as the true messengers before him. The chapter has already insisted that the Qur'an upholds the faith of Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, and this verse adds the experiential proof, the similarity of the rejection. For the Prophet, the knowledge that his suffering was shared by his predecessors would have been a deep consolation, and for the Meccans, the knowledge that they were repeating a doomed pattern was a summons to break it. The verse so situates the present conflict within salvation history, where it belongs.

---

## Verse 43:24

> Each ˹warner˺ asked, “Even if what I brought you is better guidance than what you found your forefathers practicing?” They replied, “We totally reject whatever you have been sent with.”

**"Better Guidance Than Your Fathers"**

The phrase "better guidance" is the heart of the warner's case, because it reframes the dispute completely. The idolaters had treated the forefathers' way as authoritative simply because it was old; the messenger asks them to consider whether it was actually good, whether it led aright. Guidance, huda, is not a property of age but of direction toward God, and the new revelation claims to embody it more fully. The question also gently exposes the absurdity of preferring a worse path to a better one merely because it is familiar; no one would choose a darker road over a lit one for the sake of the darkness. By casting the matter in guidance rather than inheritance, the verse makes clear that religion is about being led, not about belonging, and that the only legitimate reason to keep an old way is that it is the right way.

**"We Totally Reject"**

The word for "totally reject" carries the sense of dismissing something completely, casting it aside with finality. There is no hedging in the reply; the elite and their followers do not say the message is partly wrong or in need of revision, they say they reject it outright. This totalism is itself a sign of the stakes: a message this threatening to the old order could only be met with total rejection or total acceptance, and they chose rejection. The verse records the words with grim precision, because that finality is what seals a people's fate. For the reader, the bluntness is a warning about the cost of hardening: once the heart decides to reject without examination, the nuances fall away and only the wall remains. The warner had offered a question; the response was a closed door, and the chapter will show where closed doors lead.

---

## Verse 43:25

> So We inflicted punishment upon them. See then what was the fate of the deniers

**"Them" — Who Were Punished**

The "them" of the verse refers to the destroyed communities of the earlier narratives, the peoples who received messengers, preferred their own ways, and were warned until the appointed term arrived. The chapter has named no single nation in this stretch so much as a type, the rejecter of warners, and the pronoun gathers all such peoples into one judgment. This is important because it shows the punishment was not an one-off event but the recurring end of a recurring refusal, the settled consequence that meets every community that hardens against the truth. The believer reading the verse is meant to see that the "them" is a category with an open membership, and the only way out of it is to not be among those who deny. The pronoun is both a record and a threat.

**See the Fate of the Deniers**

The second clause turns from the act of punishment to the act of looking, commanding the hearer to "see then what was the fate of the deniers." The word "see" is an invitation to contemplation, a call to study the ruins and the stories of the perished as a lesson rather than a curiosity. The Qur'an repeatedly points the hearer to the ends of former nations as visible evidence of divine justice, and this verse makes that pointing explicit. The "then" carries a tone of conclusion, as if to say that after all the warnings and refusals, this is what remained, and the hearer is invited to draw the obvious inference about his own case. The fate of the deniers is not hidden; it is displayed, so that the living may learn from the dead.

---

## Verse 43:26

> ˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when Abraham declared to his father and his people, “I am totally free of whatever ˹gods˺ you worship

**The Shift to Abraham**

The chapter turns from the litany of destroyed nations to the figure of Abraham, the archetypal monotheist whose break with his people becomes a template for the Prophet Muhammad and for every believer after him. The address "O Prophet" pulls the Messenger directly into the narrative, so that the story of Abraham is not a detached history but a word spoken to the hearer's own messenger. This shift signals a change in the chapter's strategy, from warning the Meccans by the fate of others to encouraging the Messenger by the example of the greatest of the earlier warners. Abraham is introduced as the proof that separation from idolatry is both possible and commanded, and his appearance here gathers the chapter's argument into a living model of how a single man faced a whole society of idolaters.

**The Scope of the Renunciation**

The phrase "whatever you worship" leaves nothing outside the scope of Abraham's freedom, because the objects of idolatry were many and varied, from the gods of the temple to the angels the Meccans had mislabeled. By renouncing "whatever," Abraham refuses to rank the false gods or to retain the respectable ones, and the verse by this insists on the totality of the break. This matters for the Meccans, who might have been willing to concede some deities while keeping others, because Abraham's example shows that partial monotheism is not monotheism at all. The scope of the renunciation is the measure of its sincerity, and the verse presents a model in which no idol, however honored, survives the declaration of freedom from it.

---

## Verse 43:27

> except the One Who originated me, and He will surely guide me.”

**The God Who Originated Abraham**

The title given to the true God here is the One who originated Abraham, and the choice of "originated" rather than merely "created" stresses that God is the source not only of Abraham's body but of his very being and line. To originate is to begin, to be the first cause on which everything else depends, and the verse presents this as the defining attribute of the deity worth worshipping. This connects the verse to the chapter's broader argument, in which the Creator of the heavens and the earth is shown to be the only fit object of worship. By naming God as his originator, Abraham is also naming God as his owner and sustainer, which is why the worship follows naturally; one worships the source of one's own existence. The title is a compact theology of creation and devotion in two words.

**Origination and Guidance Linked**

The verse's pairing of origination and guidance reveals a single truth in two aspects: the God who begins a life is the God who should govern it. There is a moral logic here, because to worship a creature while ignoring the Creator is to honor the made and neglect the Maker, whereas Abraham's sequence of being originated and then guided restores the proper order. The link also answers the Meccans' practical objection that abandoning the gods would leave them without help, because Abraham's example shows that the Originator is also the Guide, the one help that cannot fail. The verse by this turns a metaphysical claim into a practical assurance, and it does so by letting Abraham speak from experience rather than theory. The two words, originated and guided, are the alphabet of monotheism.

---

## Verse 43:28

> And he left this enduring declaration among his descendants, so they may ˹always˺ turn back ˹to Allah˺

**The Enduring Declaration**

The verse tells how Abraham left his declaration of monotheism among his descendants, and the word "enduring" signals that this was not a private conviction that died with him but a legacy meant to outlast its speaker. A declaration that endures is one that is passed down, taught, and recalled by those who come after, and the verse presents Abraham's faith as a gift to his line rather than a solitary stance. For the chapter, this is important because it connects the Arabs, who traced part of their lineage to Abraham through Ishmael, to the pure monotheism of their forefather, and it implies that the idolatry of Mecca was a corruption of an inheritance they were meant to guard. The endurance of the declaration is the measure of its truth, because a lie does not survive transmission as a call to return.

**A Heritage of Return**

The purpose of leaving the declaration among the descendants was "so they may turn back to Allah," which reveals that the legacy was not a proud genealogy but an invitation to repentance. Abraham's concern for his line was not that they boast of him but that they return to the God he worshipped, and the verse frames the heritage as a repeated opportunity rather than a fixed status. This corrects the Meccans' reliance on their ancestry, because the value of being Abraham's descendants lies not in the blood but in the return his declaration calls for. The verse by this turns lineage into a summons, and it offers the Meccans the chance to become true heirs of Abraham by doing what his declaration asks. The heritage is a door, not a trophy.

---

## Verse 43:29

> In fact, I had allowed enjoyment for these ˹Meccans˺ and their forefathers, until the truth came to them along with a messenger making things clear

**The Divine Permission to Enjoy**

The verse shifts from the model of Abraham to the situation of the Meccans, declaring that God had allowed them and their forefathers a period of enjoyment before the truth arrived. The word "allowed" or "given respite" carries the sense of a deliberate delay, a time granted in which the people lived and prospered without the clarifying message. This is a significant admission, because it acknowledges that the Meccans' present comfort was not a sign of divine approval but a gift of patience, a space in which they were left to themselves. For a people who might have taken their prosperity as evidence that God was pleased with their idolatry, the verse corrects the interpretation, showing that the enjoyment was a respite with an expiration, not an endorsement without end.

**The Truth With a Clear Messenger**

The truth is said to have come "along with a messenger making things clear," and the pairing of truth and clarification is the answer to any excuse that the Meccans were left in darkness. The Messenger's function is precisely to make clear what was obscure, to remove the ambiguities in which the idolaters had sheltered, so that their rejection cannot plead ignorance. The verse by this completes the picture of the respite: God gave them ease, and then He gave them clarity, which means they are without defense when they refuse. For the Prophet, the description of himself as the one who makes things clear is both a commission and a consolation, because it defines his role as the light that ends the excuse of the dark. The clarity is the mercy that also becomes the judge.

---

## Verse 43:30

> ˹But˺ when the truth came to them, they said, “This is magic, and we totally reject it.”

**The Arrival and the Refusal**

The verse records the Meccans' response to the truth they had been given a respite to receive, and the response is a flat refusal, which completes the pattern the chapter has been building across its historical examples. God gave them enjoyment, sent them a clear Messenger, and they answered with rejection, which means the respite was wasted and the clarity was scorned. The "but" that opens the verse marks the tragic turn, the moment when the long patience meets the hard heart, and the chapter's whole argument about the destroyed nations finds its application in the living audience. The verse is therefore a verdict in miniature, showing that the Meccans have stepped into the very role the former nations played, and that the outcome they have been warned about is now their own.

**The Pattern Completed**

With this verse, the chapter's long comparison between the former nations and the Meccans reaches its completion, because the Meccans have now said the same words and made the same choice as the peoples who were destroyed. The pattern that the verse "Imagine how many prophets" invited them to see is now mirrored in their own mouths, and the mirror is exact. The completion is sobering, because it means the warnings the chapter issued have been fulfilled by the very people warned, and the historical examples have become the present reality. The verse by this closes the gap between past and present that the chapter opened, and it does so by showing the hearer that he has stepped into the story he was shown, speaking the lines of the ruined.

---

## Verse 43:31

> And they exclaimed, “If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from ˹one of˺ the two cities!”

**The Exclamation of the Elite**

The verse records the Meccans' reaction to the Qur'an in the form of a wistful objection, as if the problem were not the message but the messenger, and the exclamation reveals the social pride that lay beneath their rejection. Instead of engaging the content of the revelation, they lamented that it had not been given to a "great man" from one of the two cities, which shows that their resistance was filtered through concerns of status rather than truth. The chapter has already shown the elite leading the rejection, and this verse captures their tone, a blend of conceding the Qur'an's impressiveness and resenting its bearer. For the reader, the exclamation is a window into the psychology of unbelief, where the messenger's background becomes the excuse for refusing the message.

**The Two Cities**

The "two cities" refers to Mecca and the nearby Ta'if, the urban centers of the region whose notables the Meccans thought worthy of such a revelation, and the specification shows that the objection was parochial, concerned with local prestige rather than with the message's universal claim. By limiting their wish to the great men of these two towns, the Meccans revealed that their loyalty was to their own society's hierarchy, and that a messenger from outside it, or from within it but without status, was unacceptable to them. The verse by this exposes the tribal and civic narrowness of the rejection, and it shows that the Qur'an's address to all was resisted because it did not flatter the few. The two cities become a symbol of the smallness the revelation came to enlarge.

---

## Verse 43:32

> Is it they who distribute your Lord’s mercy? We ˹alone˺ have distributed their ˹very˺ livelihood among them in this worldly life and raised some of them in rank above others so that some may employ others in service. ˹But˺ your Lord’s mercy is far better than whatever ˹wealth˺ they amass

**God Alone Distributes**

The verse answers its own question by declaring that God alone has distributed the livelihood of the people, and the "alone" is the decisive word, excluding every human claimant from the role of dispenser of grace. This is a direct correction of the Meccans' assumption that the great men of the cities should be the channels of revelation, because it asserts that the true distribution of all good, including provision, is God's and His only. The verse by this relocates the source of every blessing from the human hierarchy to the divine will, and it does so in language that leaves no room for partnership. For the believer, the affirmation is a foundation of trust, because the Giver of livelihood is the same Giver of guidance, and His distribution is wise even when it baffles the proud.

**The Better Mercy**

The verse turns from the distribution of livelihood to the distribution of mercy, declaring that the Lord's mercy is far better than whatever wealth the people amass, and the comparison is the heart of the answer to the objection. The Meccans wanted the Qur'an tied to wealth and rank; the verse replies that the mercy of God, which includes the guidance they are rejecting, is infinitely superior to the riches they hoard. This connects the verse to the chapter's recurring contrast between the fleeting and the lasting, because the wealth is transient while the mercy is the true good. The "far better" is a quantitative and qualitative judgment, asserting that the thing the Meccans prized is worthless next to the thing they scorned, and it reverses their valuation with a single phrase.

---

## Verse 43:33

> Were it not that people might ˹be tempted to˺ become one community ˹of disbelievers˺, We would have supplied the homes of ˹only˺ those who disbelieve in the Most Compassionate with silver roofs and ˹silver˺ stairways to ascend

**The Hypothetical of Silver**

The verse opens with a counterfactual, declaring that were it not for a certain risk, God would have supplied the homes of the disbelievers with silver roofs and stairways to ascend, and the hypothetical is one of the Qur'an's most striking reflections on the purpose of material inequality. The "were it not" frames the present distribution of wealth as a deliberate restraint, because God could have lavished silver on the rejecters but chose not to for a reason tied to the moral economy of humanity. This joins with the chapter's earlier teaching that provision is sent in measure and that abundance would lead to transgression, because here the same logic is applied to the specific luxury of the disbelievers' houses. The verse by this reveals that even the withholding of splendor is an act of wisdom rather than a sign of neglect, and it invites the hearer to see the poverty of the righteous and the plainness of their dwellings as part of a larger design.

---

## Verse 43:34

> as well as ˹silver˺ gates and thrones to recline on

**Continuation of the Hypothetical**

Grammatically and theologically, this verse is the continuation of the preceding counterfactual, and it must be read as part of a single sentence that runs from the silver roofs through the stairways to the gates and thrones, so that the force of the "were it not" carries across all of them. The continuation shows that the restraint God exercises is not partial but total, that He withholds the entire edifice of superfluous splendor rather than merely its parts. This connects the verse to the unity of the chapter's argument, because the single counterfactual binds the images into one lesson about the purpose of poverty and wealth. The hearer who reads the verses separately misses the rhetorical sweep, and the verse's placement as a fragment is itself a cue to remember that it belongs to the longer sentence that began with the silver roofs.

**The Worldly Nature of the Gift**

The silver gates and thrones are, in the end, thoroughly worldly, bound to the near life and powerless in the face of the Hereafter, and the verse's placement just before the conclusion of the counterfactual prepares the hearer for the stark contrast that follows. The luxury is real but terminal, a glory that ends where the world ends, and the chapter will immediately pivot from these silver things to the gold ornaments and then to the declaration that all of it is fleeting. This connects the verse to the overarching rhythm of Sūrah al-Zukhruf, which moves from the signs of creation, through the exposure of idolatry, to the contrast between the passing world and the lasting Hereafter. The gates and thrones are the last items in the inventory of the passing, and their silver shines only until the light of the final verse diminishes it.

---

## Verse 43:35

> and ornaments ˹of gold˺. Yet all this is no more than a ˹fleeting˺ enjoyment in this worldly life. ˹But˺ the Hereafter with your Lord is ˹only˺ for those mindful ˹of Him˺

**Ornaments of Gold**
The verse completes the inventory of the withheld splendor by adding ornaments of gold, and the gold crowns the sequence of silver that began with the roofs, because where silver marked the structure of the household, gold marks its decoration, the superfluous beauty that signals wealth without serving need. The movement from roof to stairway to gate to throne to ornament is a descending spiral into pure display, and the gold is its final, shimmering note, the thing that adorns but does nothing.
**The Hereafter With Your Lord**
The verse turns from the fleeting to the lasting with the declaration that the Hereafter with your Lord is only for those mindful of Him. The "but" marks the great reversal that the chapter has been preparing, where the wealth the could have been granted gives way to the wealth that cannot be taken. The Hereafter with the Lord is the true inheritance, and its description as "with your Lord" locates it in His presence rather than in any earthly store, so that the believer's hope is anchored in a relationship and not in a possession. This connects the verse to the chapter's portrait of the believers in the gardens, because the mindful one is the one who inherits the lasting good that the silver and gold only pretended to be. so the hearer is offered the exchange the scripture has been urging: surrender the fleeting for the lasting, and the Lord Himself becomes the portion.

**The Glitter and the Verdict**
*Wa-zukhruf* — and gold ornaments. The word covers the whole surface of a wealthy life, and the verse closes a passage in which God has said something startling: if it were not that people would become one community of disbelievers, He would have given the houses of those who disbelieve in the Merciful roofs of silver, and stairways, and doors, and ornaments of gold (43:33-35). The Book then states what all of it is worth: this is only the enjoyment of this life, and the Hereafter with your Lord is for those who are mindful of Him. The sentence removes the one conclusion people draw from wealth, which is that the person who has it has been approved.
**Why the World Is Furnished This Way**
The Sūrah of the Cave states the same doctrine from the other end, saying that what is on the earth was made as an adornment for it, so that people could be tested as to which of them does the best work (18:7). The Book also warns the community not to be taken in by the wealth of people who are fighting the truth, and it tells the Prophet not to let their money and their children impress him (9:55). The sūrah of the Ornament is therefore not saying that the ornaments are evil. It is saying that they are not evidence, and the passage about silver roofs shows how much of the world's arrangement is deliberately left as a test rather than a reward.
**What the Mindful Get Instead**
The verse names the condition for the better thing: the Hereafter belongs to the mindful of God. The Book gives the same promise in other words when it describes the gardens prepared for those who feared their Lord unseen, and the sūrah's next verses return to the argument about the messengers and the people who rejected them. The reader who has followed the passage is left with a question that is easy to ask and hard to answer: what would he do if God gave him the silver roof, and what does he do with the gold he already has?

## Verse 43:36

> And whoever turns a blind eye to the Reminder of the Most Compassionate, We place at the disposal of each a devilish one as their close associate

**A Devilish One as Associate**

The verse declares that God places at the disposal of the rejecter a devilish one, a shaytan, as a close associate. The gift is the terrible counterpart to the mercy refused, because the one who will not have the Compassionate as companion is given the devil as the one who stays near. The "close associate" is a chilling echo of intimacy, because the word suggests the constant presence that should belong to God or to the believer's own better self, now filled by the adversary. This connects the verse to the broader Qur'anic theme that the rejecter is gradually handed over to the very thing he chose, so that the devil becomes the companion of the one who preferred the world. The hearer then is shown the mechanics of spiritual ruin, in which the refusal of the good opens the door to the evil that fills the vacancy.

**The Place at the Disposal**

The phrase "We place at the disposal of each" indicates that the devilish associate is not a conqueror who seizes but a gift permitted by God in response to the human choice, and the permissive grammar is crucial, because it preserves both divine sovereignty and human responsibility. God does not force the devil on anyone; He places him at the disposal of the one who has already turned away, so that the association is the fruit of the prior blind eye. This connects the verse to the chapter's recurring theme that God leaves to stray whoever persists in refusal, because the devilish companion is the form that the leaving-to-stray takes in the social and inward life. In this the hearer is warned that the company he keeps is the consequence of the gaze he chooses, and that the blind eye to the Reminder is what summons the wrong companion.

## Verse 43:37

> who will certainly hinder them from the ˹Right˺ Way while they think they are ˹rightly˺ guided

**While They Think They Are Guided**

The most chilling phrase is the clause "while they think they are rightly guided," because it exposes the deception at the heart of the association, that the blinded one is confident in his direction even as he is led astray. The thinking, not the reality, is what he has, and the gap between the two is the devil's victory, because a person who knows he is lost may yet turn, but a person who is sure he is right will not. This echoes in the chapter's exposure of the Meccans' self-confidence in their fathers' way, because the same delusion that afflicted them is here named as the devil's fruit, the conviction of being guided while walking the wrong road. The hearer is in this way warned that assurance is not evidence of correctness, and that the most dangerous state is the one in which the lost believe themselves found.

**The Verse's Warning**

As a verse, this one is the warning that completes the threat of the preceding line, showing that the devilish associate does not merely keep company but actively diverts, and that he does so most lethally when the victim is sure of his own righteousness. In the flow of Sūrah al-Zukhruf, it sits between the grant of the associate and the scene of regret at the end, and it supplies the mechanism by which the blind eye becomes the lost road. The verse is therefore a guardrail for the believer, who is called to maintain the open gaze upon the Reminder precisely so that the hindrance cannot take hold, and it is a verdict for the rejecter, who is shown the true nature of the confidence he mistakes for guidance. The warning is clear: the Way is real, the hindrance is real, and the gaze is the difference.

## Verse 43:38

> But when such a person comes to Us, one will say ˹to their associate˺, “I wish you were as distant from me as the east is from the west! What an evil associate ˹you were˺!”

**The Encounter at the End**

The verse shifts to the Day of Reckoning, where the one who was hindered by his devilish associate now comes to God and speaks to that associate in a torrent of regret, and the scene is the dramatic payoff of the warning that began two verses earlier. The "when such a person comes to Us" places the moment at the threshold of the judgment, where the illusions of the world are stripped and the true nature of the bond is finally seen. This connects the verse to the chapter's Last-Day vision, because the regret spoken here is the inverse of the confidence the misguided one felt while he thought himself guided. In this the hearer is shown the end of the road the devilish associate prepared, not as a doctrine but as a dialogue, a final word exchanged between the deceiver and the deceived when the deception can no longer hold.

---

The verse follows the passage on the devil's hindrance with a scene from the Day of Reckoning, where the one who had been misled speaks to the one who misled him and wishes the distance of east from west between them. The Book records the same exchange in the sūrah of the Criterion, where the wrongdoer asks that he had taken such a one as a friend (25:27–29), and in the sūrah of the Cessation, where the followers ask for the leaders to be given a double punishment (33:67–68). The verse's note that both will be together in the punishment is the sūrah's answer to the excuse of following others, since the follower's regret does not remove his responsibility.

## Verse 43:39

> ˹It will be said to both,˺ “Since you all did wrong, sharing in the punishment will be of no benefit to you this Day.”

**The Word to Both**

The verse records the divine word spoken to both the misguided person and his devilish associate, and the inclusion of both as addressees is significant, because it shows that the association was a shared enterprise in wrongdoing, not an one-sided deception alone. The "both" refuses the excuse that the human was merely a victim, because the word addresses him and the devil together as partners in the evil that was done.

**No Benefit This Day**

The qualifier "this Day" anchors the statement in the Day of Judgment, where the economies of the world are overturned and the benefits the misguided one hoped for from his association are shown to be null. The Day is the time when the Reminder he ignored would have helped and the associate he embraced does not, so the verse marks the contrast between the false benefit and the true, which was the Reminder he refused. This joins with the chapter's Last-Day urgency, because the "this Day" is the day the chapter has been warning about since its opening, and the verse uses it to settle the account of the blind eye and the devil. The hearer then is brought to the moment where choices become consequences, and where the sharing that once seemed harmless is exposed as the sharing of ruin.

## Verse 43:40

> Can you make the deaf hear, or guide the blind or those clearly astray

**Can You Make Them Hear?**

The question "can you make the deaf hear" is rhetorical, and its answer is no, because the power to open the inward ear belongs to God alone, and the Messenger's task is to deliver, not to compel the hearing. This matches the chapter's earlier release of the Prophet from being a keeper over the people, because the verse now gives the religious reason for that release, which is that the keeper's function would require a power the Messenger does not have. So the hearer is relieved of the expectation that the Prophet should succeed with everyone, and the Prophet is protected from the despair of failure, because the deafness is not his to cure. The question is a boundary marker, drawing the line between conveyance and conversion, and assigning each to its proper source.

**The Blindness That Persists**

The blindness the verse names is not physical but moral, the inability to see the truth that has been placed before the eyes, and the persistence of this blindness is what makes guidance impossible for the one who will not look. This runs through the chapter's imagery of the Reminder and the blind eye, because the blind of this verse are the ones who averted their gaze, and their blindness is the consequence rather than the cause of their refusal. so the hearer is taught that the inability to be guided is often self-induced, the hardening that follows upon repeated neglect, so that the "clearly astray" are not innocently lost but wilfully so. The verse so refuses to let the rejecter plead incapacity, because the incapacity is the fruit of the choice he made when he could still see.

## Verse 43:41

> Even if We take you away ˹from this world˺, We will surely inflict punishment upon them

**Even If We Take You**

The verse addresses the Prophet with the hypothetical that even if God takes him away from this world, the punishment of the rejecters will still be carried out, and the hypothetical is a reassurance against the fear that the Messenger's death would leave the wrongdoers unaccountable. The "take you away" refers to the natural end of the Prophet's life, and the verse severs that event from the fate of the message, so that the hearer understands the revelation and its consequences do not depend on the messenger's continued presence. This joins with the chapter's defense of the Prophet against the despair of rejection, because the verse shows that his passing is not the end of the story the rejecters wish to close. The hearer is in this way given a perspective that transcends the person of the Messenger, locating the weight of the message in the God who sent it rather than in the one who conveys it.

---

The hypothetical that God may take the Prophet away before the punishment of the people is answered in the sūrah of the Spoils: whether you are shown part of what they are promised or God takes you, the return is to Him and the accounting is His (10:46), and the same promise is made to the Prophet in the sūrah of al-Raʿd (13:40). The point is stated in the Book's own terms for the Makkan mockery, that their punishment is not deferred by the messenger's death, since the record is kept and the reckoning is set. The verse's sequel, the promise that God will show him what they were warned of, gives the other half of the comfort: the message will prevail whether or not he lives to see it.

## Verse 43:42

> Or if We show you what We threaten them with, We certainly have full power over them

**The Threat Shown**

The verse offers a second hypothetical, that even if God were to show the Prophet the very punishment He threatens the rejecters with, the display would not exhaust His power, because the threat and the power behind it are one. The "or if" builds on the preceding verse about the Prophet's passing, extending the reassurance from the sphere of time to the sphere of vision, so that whether the Messenger dies or is shown the doom, the outcome for the rejecters is settled by God. This connects the verse to the chapter's concern with the certainty of the consequence, because the showing would be a mere preview of what God already has full capacity to enact. So the hearer is assured that the threats spoken in the Qur'an are not empty rhetoric but the announced exercise of a power that is already complete, and the conditional merely dramatizes what is true in any case.

## Verse 43:43

**Hold Firmly to the Revelation**

The verse commands the Prophet to hold firmly to what has been revealed to him. The "hold firmly" suggests a grip that does not loosen under pressure, and the pressure in view is the rejection and the mockery the chapter has documented, so the command is a strengthening for the warner in the face of hostility. This connects the verse to the chapter's defense of the Messenger, because the instruction to cling to the revelation is the antidote to the despair that the deaf and blind might induce. The hearer is in this way shown that the Prophet's task is not to force hearing but to retain the message, and that his stability lies in his attachment to what he has been given rather than to the response he receives.

## Verse 43:44

> Surely this ˹Quran˺ is a glory for you and your people. And you will ˹all˺ be questioned ˹about it˺

**For You and Your People**

The verse extends the glory to the Prophet's people, so that the honor is not a private possession of the Messenger but a communal inheritance, shared by the community that gathers around the book. This ties into the chapter's portrait of the believers as a people defined by their response to the revelation, because the glory is the collective identity of those who hold to the Straight Path. The "your people" also answers the Meccan objection that the Qur'an should have come to a great man of the two cities, because the verse shows that the glory attaches to the Prophet and his own people regardless of the status the elite craved. Here the hearer is drawn into the honored community, invited to share in the glory by sharing in the book, and the verse makes the scripture the bond that constitutes the people as a people.

**You Will Be Questioned**

The verse adds that the Prophet and his people will all be questioned about the Qur'an, and the questioning is the necessary counterpart to the glory, because a gift so great carries a responsibility proportionate to its worth. The "about it" indicates that the account will concern what they did with the revelation, whether they held it firmly as commanded or turned from it as the rejecters did. This joins with the chapter's theme of the return of all matters to God for judgment, because the questioning is the moment when the glory becomes a test, and the hearer is reminded that honor is not automatic but answerable. The verse by this balances the comfort of the glory with the seriousness of the reckoning, so that the community that boasts in the book is also the community held to it.

## Verse 43:45

> Ask ˹the followers of˺ the messengers that We already sent before you if We ˹ever˺ appointed ˹other˺ gods to be worshipped besides the Most Compassionate

**The Question of Other Gods**

The question itself is whether God ever appointed other gods to be worshipped alongside the Most Compassionate, and the answer the verse expects is a resounding no, because the Consistent Witness of the prophets is the exclusive worship of the one God. The "other gods" are the idols and the partners the Meccans had set beside God, and the verse challenges the opponents by asking whether the revelation they claim to respect ever sanctioned such a thing. This runs through the chapter's sustained argument against the attribution of offspring and partners to God, because the verse frames the question as one the prior communities would answer in the negative, by this exposing the Meccan practice as a deviation from the very tradition they invoke. In this the hearer is shown that the idolatry is not ancient truth but a rupture of the ancient truth.

**The Consistency of the Message**

The verse rests on the teaching about the consistency of revelation, the teaching that the one God sent one message through many messengers, so that the Qur'an's monotheism is the same as Moses' and Jesus'. This ties into the chapter's broader concern with the unity of the scriptures, because the question assumes that the followers of the prior messengers would recognize the Qur'an as their own message restated. So the hearer is drawn into a catholic vision of prophecy, in which the apparent novelty of the Arabian revelation is revealed as the ancient truth returning, and the verse uses the question to dissolve the objection that the Prophet invented a new religion. The consistency is the shield against the charge of fabrication.

## Verse 43:46

> Indeed, We sent Moses with Our signs to Pharaoh and his chiefs, and he said: “I am a messenger of the Lord of all worlds.”

**Moses Sent to Pharaoh**

The verse begins the narrative of Moses with the emphatic "indeed, We sent," and the emphasis establishes the historical reality of the mission as a fact the hearer is to receive, not a legend to doubt. Moses is sent with the signs, the clear proofs of his commission, to Pharaoh and his chiefs, and the pairing of the tyrant and his court mirrors the situation of the Prophet facing the Meccan elite, so that the story is presented as a precedent for the present conflict. This matches the chapter's method of using the destroyed nations and the former prophets as mirrors, because the Moses-Pharaoh encounter is the archetype of the warner versus the arrogant power. Here the hearer is drawn into a known story that now functions as a commentary on his own circumstance, and the verse's "indeed" is the scripture's pledge that the parallel is real.

**The Lord of All Worlds**

The name "Lord of all worlds" is significant, because it asserts God's sovereignty over every realm, in contrast to Pharaoh's claim to lordship over Egypt, and the verse by this sets the true Lord against the pretender in a single phrase. This runs through the chapter's theme of divine kingdom versus human arrogance, because the Lord of all worlds is the One whose signs Moses bears, while Pharaoh is a chief whose power is lent and limited. One is invited to feel the disproportion between the two claims, the infinite Lord and the finite tyrant, and to recognize that the outcome of the confrontation is foreordained by that disproportion. The name is the religious anchor of the story, the ground on which Moses stands and Pharaoh falls.

## Verse 43:47

> But as soon as he came to them with Our signs, they laughed at them

**The Arrival With Signs**

The verse notes that Moses came "with Our signs," so the laughter met not a bare claim but a showed proof, which makes the mockery all the more revealing, because it shows that the rejection was not due to a lack of evidence. This matches the chapter's theology of the sign, because the signs were the enacted counterparts of the cosmic evidences, and their presence removes the excuse of ignorance. In this the hearer is shown that the problem was never the insufficiency of the proof but the sufficiency of the pride, because the chiefs who laughed had seen and still scorned. The arrival with signs is therefore a double-edged moment, the offer of proof and the exposure of the refusal, and the verse captures both in a single breath.

**The Verse's Ominous Calm**

As a verse, this one is strikingly calm in its reportage, stating the laughter without commentary, because the calm itself is ominous, hinting that the jeering court is already standing on the edge of the torment the next verses will describe. In the flow of Sūrah al-Zukhruf, it stands as the opening beat of the Mosaic narrative, the moment that sets the pattern of rejection whose consequences will unfold in the seizure and the pleas that follow. The verse is therefore a quiet trigger, because the laughter it records is the first note of a story that ends in the drowning of the proud, and the hearer who recognizes the pattern in his own moment is given the chance to stop laughing before the consequence arrives.

## Verse 43:48

> although every sign We showed them was greater than the one before. Ultimately, We seized them with torments so that they might return ˹to the Right Path˺

**Seized With Torments**

The verse declares that God ultimately seized them with torments, and the "ultimately" marks the end of the patience, the point at which the accumulating refusal exhausted the time of grace and the consequence arrived. The torments are the historical plagues and the final drowning that the Qur'an recounts elsewhere, and here they are named as the divinely wrought answer to the hardened scorn. This echoes in the chapter's theme of the destroyed nations, because Pharaoh's court becomes the exhibit of what happens when the signs are laughed at, and the seizure is the fulfillment of the warning the surah has been issuing. The hearer is in this way shown that the laughter of the previous verse was the prelude to the torment of this one, and the pattern is the mirror of the Meccan fate the chapter threatens.

**The Mercy in the Torment**

The idea that the torments were meant to induce return reveals the mercy woven into the severity, because the affliction is presented as a pedagogy rather than a mere venting of wrath, and the verse by this protects the justice of God from the charge of cruelty. This ties into the chapter's balance of mercy and might, because the same God who sends the signs sends the torments, and both are aimed at the same end, the turning of the creature to the Right Path. One is invited to read the severity as love in its harsh register, the tearing away of the illusion that blocks the return, and the verse's "so that they might return" is the quiet heartbeat beneath the dramatic seizure. The mercy is the reason the torment is not the end.

## Verse 43:49

> ˹Then˺ they pleaded, “O ˹mighty˺ magician! Pray to your Lord on our behalf, by virtue of the covenant He made with you. We will certainly accept guidance.”

**Then They Pleaded**

The verse records the moment when the tormented chiefs reversed themselves and pleaded with Moses, and the "then" marks the collapse of the scorn into supplication, because the laughter of the court gives way to the begging of the afflicted. This belongs to the chapter's pattern of the wrongdoers at the torment, because the same reversal appeared in the scene of the Meccans pleading for a way back to the world, and here it is enacted in the Pharaoh narrative. The reader is invited to see the consistency of the human response to pain, which is to abandon the mockery the moment the cost arrives, and the verse uses the plea to show that the conviction was always present but suppressed by pride. The plea is the truth the laughter was hiding.

**We Will Accept Guidance**

The chiefs promise "we will certainly accept guidance," and the promise is the crux of the scene, because it is the profession of the repentance that the torments were meant to elicit, yet its sincerity is undercut by everything the narrative has shown. This ties into the chapter's concern with the heart's true turning, because the verse presents the words of conversion without the reality, a verbal submission that the next verse will show to be hollow. The hearer is warned that the promise made under duress is not the same as the return made in freedom, and the chapter uses the chiefs' words to distinguish the lips that say "we accept" from the life that confirms it. The promise is the test the relief will expose.

## Verse 43:50

> But as soon as We removed the torments from them, they broke their promise

**The Torment Removed**

The verse records the moment the torments were lifted and the chiefs immediately broke the promise they had made under duress, and the "as soon as" captures the speed of the relapse, because the relief was the trigger of the betrayal rather than the confirmation of the repentance. This belongs to the preceding scene of the plea, because the promise to accept guidance was contingent on the pain, and the removal of the pain revealed that the promise was never rooted in a changed heart. The hearer is shown that the affliction was a test that exposed the hollowness of the confession, because the relief that should have been met with gratitude was met instead with the resumption of the refusal. The verse by this completes the portrait of the insincere conversion, showing that the words spoken in terror evaporated the instant the terror lifted.

**The Verse as a Caution**

As a verse, this one is a caution against the repentance that is really a negotiation, because it shows that the promise made to escape the pain is the promise most likely to be broken when the pain is gone. In the flow of Sūrah al-Zukhruf, it stands between the plea and Pharaoh's boast, and it serves to show that the chiefs' apparent submission was a sham, so that the ensuing narrative of pride is not a contradiction of the plea but its exposure. The verse is therefore a diagnostic for the hearer, who is invited to test his own return by its durability under relief, and to ask whether his promises to God would hold if the affliction that prompted them were removed. The caution is the chapter's mercy, because it names the danger before the hearer walks into it.

## Verse 43:51

> And Pharaoh called out to his people, boasting, “O my people! Am I not sovereign over Egypt as well as ˹all˺ these streams flowing at my feet? Can you not see

**Sovereign Over Egypt**

The claim to be sovereign over Egypt is the assertion of absolute earthly rule, and the verse presents it as the foundation of Pharaoh's self-exaltation, because he grounds his divinity in his governance of the land. This matches the chapter's contrast between the Lord of all worlds and the lord of a single country, because the verse exposes the disproportion between Pharaoh's claim and the true Lord's, so that the sovereignty over Egypt is shown to be a borrowed and bounded thing next to the sovereignty over all. One is invited to see the idolatry of power, in which the ruler equates his office with the divine, and the verse uses Pharaoh's own words to display the equation as the essence of tyranny. The sovereignty is the lie the narrative will drown.

**The False Lordship**

The deeper claim of the verse is the false lordship that Pharaoh asserts, because to call himself sovereign over Egypt is to set himself in the place of the Lord of all worlds, and the verse presents this as the political form of the idolatry the chapter has been attacking. This joins with the earlier question about whether God ever appointed other gods, because Pharaoh is the answer in flesh, the human who claims the title of god and demands the worship that belongs to the Creator. The believer is invited to see that the Meccan idolatry and the Egyptian tyranny are one disease, the erection of a creature in the place of the Creator, whether as a statue or as a king. The false lordship is the chapter's central target, and Pharaoh is its clearest face.

## Verse 43:52

> Am I not better than this nobody who can hardly express himself

**The Insult to Moses**

The insult to Moses as a "nobody" is the outward form of the rejection of his Lord, because to call the messenger insignificant is to imply that the message he bears is not worth heeding, and the verse exposes the conflation as the tactic of the proud. This matches the chapter's earlier defense of the Prophet against the complaint that the Qur'an should have come to a great man, because both Pharaoh and the Meccans make the messenger the measure of the message, and both are corrected by the scripture's insistence that the message stands on its own. The hearer is shown that the insult to the warner is never really about the man but about the God he represents, and that the contempt is the shield of the threatened power. The "nobody" is the chapter's emblem of how pride reads the prophet.

**The Standard of Worth**

The verse reveals the false standard by which Pharaoh measures worth, which is worldly power and eloquence, and the scripture repeatedly subverts this standard by choosing the lowly and the weak to carry the greatest messages. This runs through the chapter's contrast between the fleeting ornaments of this life and the lasting glory of the Hereafter, because Pharaoh's standard is completely of the fleeting, measuring a man by his throne and his tongue. The believer is invited to recognize that the true measure is the relation to the Reminder, so that the "nobody" who holds the sign is greater than the king who holds the streams, and the verse uses the boast to invert the world's scale of value. The standard is the chapter's quiet correction of every hierarchy the proud erect.

## Verse 43:53

**The Verse as Climax of Boast**

As the climax of Pharaoh's boast, this verse gathers the pride of the king into a single, revealing question, because the demand for bracelets and escorts is the clearest statement of where the tyrant's heart rests, on the splendor of this world and the validation of the powerful. The verse is therefore a pivot, because after the boast the narrative will move toward the confrontation and the fall, and the hearer is left with the image of a king measuring the prophet by his jewelry, a measure the chapter has spent its length overturning.

As the verse that follows the long boast of Pharaoh, this one is the verdict on that boast, showing that the king who exalted himself succeeded only in fooling his people into rebellion and by this sealing their destruction. ## Verse 43:55

> So when they enraged Us, We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them all

**Drowning Them All**

The hearer is warned that the king and the crowd sink together, and that the obedience which felt like safety was in fact the bond of a common doom. The totality is the scripture's way of showing that the rebellion was total and the response matched it.

**Why the Punishment Came**

The punishment came because the enraging exhausted the patience that had been extended through signs and torments, so that the drowning is the last step of a long arc that began with the sending of Moses and ended with the closing of the term. This echoes in the surah's theology of the decree that fixes the term of every warning, because the "when they enraged Us" is the temporal clause that triggers what had been held in reserve, and the verse shows that the delay was mercy, not weakness. In this the hearer is taught that the apparent slowness of the divine response is the space in which repentance is still possible, and that the punishment arrives only when the space is refused, so that the drowning is the sinner's own closing of the door.

## Verse 43:56

> And We made them an example and a lesson for those after them

**Made an Example**

The verse declares that God made the drowned Egyptians an example and a lesson for those who came after them, and the making is the deliberate conversion of a historical catastrophe into a pedagogic instrument, so that the ruin of one people becomes the warning of another. **The Subheading on Worship**

The appended heading "All Objects of Worship in Hell?" turns the verse toward the argumentative question of idolatry, asking whether the gods the Meccans worship will save them when the judgment comes, and the question is the natural inference from the example just named. If Pharaoh and his people were drowned despite their power, what will become of the idols that have no power at all, and the verse poses the query as a challenge to the worshiper of the false. This belongs to the chapter's sustained attack on the idols and the angels, because the example of Egypt is the preface to the exposure of the helplessness of the so-called deities. ## Verse 43:57

## Verse 43:58

> They exclaimed, “Which is better: our gods or Jesus?” They cite him only to argue. In fact, they are a people prone to dispute

**Our Gods or Jesus**

The pairing of "our gods" with Jesus reveals the Meccan theology in a single phrase, because it shows that they had collapsed the distinction between the revered prophet and the manufactured idols, treating both as competitors in the same arena of divinity. This joins with the chapter's treatment of the angels and the daughters, because the same levelling of the creature and the deity appears here with Jesus, so that the verse exposes a consistent pattern of confusing the honored with the worshipful. The hearer is shown that the Meccans' reverence was not discriminating but promiscuous, lavishing the name of god on anything that served their scheme, and the verse uses the pairing to display the confusion at the heart of their argument. This echoes in the chapter's opening and closing affirmations of the sole kingdom of God, because the verse's refutation rests on the impossibility of the category the Meccans assumed, so that the "which is better" question collapses before it starts. The believer is invited to see that many disputes are generated by unstated false premises, and that the task of the warner is to expose the premise rather than to answer within it. The futility is the chapter's way of showing that the Meccan objection was not a puzzle to solve but a confusion to dissolve.

## Verse 43:59

> He was only a servant We showed favour to, and made as an example for the Children of Israel

**He Was Only a Servant**

The verse corrects the Meccan misunderstanding of Jesus by declaring that he was only a servant whom God favored. The "only" is the corrective that dismantles the divinization the opponents had exploited, because it places Jesus firmly in the category of the creature who is honored, not the Creator who is worshiped. The word for servant carries the full dignity of one in right relation to God, so the verse does not demean Jesus but defines him truly, as the one who, like all the prophets, stood before the Lord in obedience. This ties into the chapter's defense of the strict oneness of God, because the verse removes Jesus from the frame of the debate the Meccans had constructed and relocates him in the line of the warners. The reader is invited to see that the highest honor of a human is to be a servant of God, and that the Meccan try to make him a god was a demotion dressed as exaltation.

---

The verse is one of the clearest statements in the Book on the nature of Jesus, and the Book states it repeatedly: he was a servant of God given favour, and he is the sign of the Hour (43:59, 61). The sūrah of Maryam records his own words at birth — that he is a servant of God, given the Book and made a prophet (19:30) — and the sūrah of the Women gives the same correction against the claims made of him (4:171–172). The phrase about being an example for the Children of Israel is read with the tradition that he spoke in the cradle and worked the signs among them, which the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān narrates in full (3:46–49).

## Verse 43:60

> Had We willed, We could have easily replaced you ˹all˺ with angels, succeeding one another on earth

**The Hypothetical of Angels**

The verse opens a counterfactual, declaring that God could have replaced the human messengers, including the Prophet and his community, with angels succeeding one another on earth, and the hypothetical is a striking reminder of the divine freedom that stands behind the choice of human warners. The "had We willed" frames the entire mission of prophecy as a deliberate act of God rather than a necessity, because the Creator was under no compulsion to use frail human beings to carry His word. This ties into the chapter's high theology of revelation, where the sending of the messenger by command was shown to be a free decision of the Most High, and the verse now extends that freedom to the very substance of the messengers, who could have been angels had God so chosen. The reader is invited to see the humility of the divine economy, in which the infinite condescends to the finite not because it must but because it wills.

---

## Verse 43:61

> And his ˹second˺ coming is truly a sign for the Hour. So have no doubt about it, and follow me. This is the Straight Path

**The Second Coming as Sign**

The verse declares that the second coming of Jesus is a true sign for the Hour, and the declaration places the return of the favored servant within the Last-Day horizon that the chapter has been invoking since its opening warnings about the Day. The "sign for the Hour" means that the event is among the signs that mark the approach of the final reckoning, so that the career of Jesus bridges the present age and the end of time. This ties into the chapter's theme of the return of all matters to God for judgment, because the second coming is the hinge on which the human history and the divine accounting turn, and the verse names it as a certainty rather than a speculation. The reader is invited to see the sweep of salvation history, in which the servant honored in the previous verse is also the figure whose return signals the close of the world, so that the Christology and the teaching about the Last Day meet in a single line.

---

The verse is read in two ways that the commentators set out: that his coming, meaning his return before the Day, is a sign of the Hour, which the traditions and many tafsīrs support, or that the knowledge of the Hour is given through him, since he announced it to his people. Either way the verse's command is the same, and the sūrah states the doctrine that fixes it a few verses later: the servant of God is not a son of God, and the community is told to follow the straight path. The Book makes the same connection between Jesus and the Hour in the sūrah of the Women (4:159) and in the sūrah of al-Zukhruf's own account of his birth and speech, so the verse gathers the sūrah's christology into one sentence.

## Verse 43:62

> And do not let Satan hinder you, ˹for˺ he is certainly your sworn enemy

**Do Not Let Satan Hinder**

The verse turns from the christological heights to a practical warning, instructing the hearer not to let Satan hinder him, and the instruction assumes that the adversary is actively working to obstruct the very following of the Straight Path that the previous verse commended. The "do not let" is a call to vigilance rather than a prediction, because it places the responsibility for resistance in the hearer's hands, implying that the hindrance can be refused. This ties into the chapter's earlier portrait of the devilish associate given to the one who turns a blind eye to the Reminder, because the verse now addresses the believer directly, warning him that the same adversary who attaches to the rejecter is also the one who would divert the follower. The reader is invited to take the warning as a description of the spiritual terrain, in which the path is contested and the traveler must guard his step.

---

The warning against being hindered by Satan follows directly from the command to follow the straight path in the previous verse, and the Book states the same instruction in several places: do not let Satan turn you aside (43:62; and 17:53), and he is the enemy who should not be obeyed (36:60). The Book describes the danger in the sūrah of the Cave, where the devil's hindrance is a matter of making the truth seem distant (18:63), and in the sūrah of the Women, where Satan promises falsehood and misleads (4:120). The verse's phrase about the sworn enemy is the same description used at the beginning of the human story (2:168, 7:22).

## Verse 43:63

> When Jesus came with clear proofs, he declared, “I have come to you with wisdom, and to clarify to you some of what you differ about. So fear Allah, and obey me

**Jesus Came With Clear Proofs**

The verse returns to the voice of Jesus, recounting that when he came with clear proofs, he declared the message that the chapter has been building toward. The "clear proofs" echo the signs that marked the prophets throughout the surah, so that Jesus is placed firmly in the line of those who bore divine evidence. The proofs are the counterpart of the signs of creation and the signs of Moses, because the scripture consistently portrays the true messenger as accompanied by the evidence of his commission. This ties into the chapter's theology of the sign, because the clarity of the proofs is the mercy that removes the excuse of ignorance, so that the hearer of Jesus, like the hearer of the Qur'an, is without cover for refusal. The reader is invited to see the continuity of the prophetic witness, in which each messenger comes with the demonstrations that correspond to the declaration he makes.

---

**The Words Jesus Spoke**
The verse gives his message in his own voice: *I have come to you with wisdom, and to make clear to you some of what you disagree about; so be mindful of God and obey me*. Two things are claimed. He brings *ḥikmah*, and he comes to clarify the disputed points among his people — which is what the Qur'an says of him everywhere, a messenger sent to the children of Israel to confirm what was in the Torah and to settle what they had argued about. And then he asks for what every messenger asks: the fear of God and obedience to the man sent with the message.
**Where the Verse Leads**
The next verses complete the declaration and show the result. Jesus says: God is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him; this is a straight path (43:64). And then the sūrah reports what happened to that straight path: the factions disagreed among themselves, and woe follows (43:65). The disagreement about Jesus is not an afterthought in this sūrah; it is the thing the sūrah keeps returning to, from the cradle speech to the dismissal of the claim to sonship. The reader is asked to hold two facts side by side — a servant who asked for worship of God alone, and a body of followers who divided over exactly that. His earlier mention in the sūrah as a sign of the coming Hour (43:61) keeps the same company: he is a servant whose case will be settled by the One who sent him.

## Verse 43:64

> Surely Allah ˹alone˺ is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.”

**Allah Alone Is My Lord**

The verse places in the mouth of Jesus the central confession of the oneness of God, that Allah alone is his Lord and the hearer's Lord. The confession is the climax of the christological correction, because it has the revered figure himself deny any share in divinity by affirming the sole lordship of the Creator. The "alone" is the decisive qualifier, excluding every partner and every pretender, so that the words of Jesus become the scriptural proof that the one the Meccans misused was a monotheist who pointed to the one God. This ties into the chapter's unrelenting defense of the divine unity, because the verse lets the very figure cited in the dispute speak the truth that dissolves the dispute, and the hearer is invited to hear in Jesus' own voice the doctrine the Qur'an has been arguing for. The lordship confessed is shared, "my Lord and your Lord," so that the servant and the hearer stand together before the single Sovereign.

---

## Verse 43:65

> Yet their ˹various˺ groups have differed among themselves ˹about him˺, so woe to the wrongdoers when they face the torment of a painful Day

**Their Groups Differed**

The verse turns from the clear confession of Jesus to the lamentable fact that his followers split into various groups and differed among themselves about him, and the division is the historical sequel to the unity he preached, the tragic fracture of a community that could not hold to the Straight Path. The "various groups" evokes the sects that formed around the figure of Jesus, each claiming him while departing from the worship of the one God he commanded, and the verse names the discord as the predictable outcome of losing the center. This ties into the chapter's condemnation of the people prone to dispute, because the division among the followers is the institutional form of the disputatiousness the surah has been exposing, so that the verse shows the fruit of the contention in the fragmentation of a community. The reader is invited to see that the disputes about Jesus did not begin with the Meccans but continued among those who claimed to honor him.

---

The division the verse names is the division the Book condemns in several places: the sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān records the groups differing about Jesus (3:55 fold), the sūrah of Maryam describes the dispute over him (19:34–36), and the sūrah of the Cow states the general warning to those who split their religion into sects (30:32). The sūrah's own method is to state the plain fact of his servanthood, as the verse before it does, and then to lament the division that followed the clear report. The verse's closing warning belongs to the wrongdoers of every group, so the passage applies to the sūrah's first audience as well.

## Verse 43:66

> Are they waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise when they least expect ˹it˺

**Waiting for the Hour**

The verse poses a pointed question to the mockers, asking whether they are waiting for the Hour to arrive and take them unawares, and the question is the chapter's return to the theme of the scoffers who dared the Prophet to hasten the Day, now turned back on them as a reproach. The "waiting" is the posture of those who treat the reckoning as a distant rumor rather than a present reality, and the verse exposes that posture as both arrogant and foolish. This ties into the chapter's opening preoccupation with the Hour and the disbelievers' demand to see it hastened, because the verse shows that the same people who pretended to want it are in fact merely waiting for it to ambush them. The reader is invited to see the inconsistency of the scoffer, who dares the Day while living as though it will never come, and the question is the scripture's way of naming the contradiction.

---

The question of what the mockers are waiting for is repeated in the sūrah of the Bees and in the sūrah of the Spoils, where the answer is that the Hour comes on them while they are unaware, or the punishment arrives first: the sūrah of the Cattle says they will be seized by it while they perceive nothing (6:44), and the sūrah of the Bee gives the same warning for the people who asked about the timing of it (16:77). The verse's closing picture, that it will come upon them suddenly while they do not sense it, is the Book's standard description of the Day for those who denied it (12:107).

## Verse 43:67

> Close friends will be enemies to one another on that Day, except the righteous

**Close Friends as Enemies**

The verse declares that close friends will become enemies to one another on the Day. The reversal is one of the most piercing images of the Qur'an's the teaching about the Last Day, because it takes the deepest human bond of this world and shows it sundered by the reality of the reckoning. The "close friends" are those whose loyalty and affection were the texture of the near life, and the verse asserts that the bond born of the world will not survive the world's ending, because the friendship was anchored in the passing rather than the lasting. This ties into the chapter's contrast between the fleeting and the enduring, because the friendship is among the fleeting ornaments that the Hour exposes as temporary, and the verse uses the intimacy of the bond to heighten the drama of its rupture. The reader is invited to see that the dearest relation of this life is contingent, and that only the bond with God outlasts the Day.

---

## Verse 43:68

> ˹who will be told,˺ “O My servants! There is no fear for you Today, nor will you grieve—

**The Word to the Servants**

The verse records the word spoken to the righteous on the Day, "O My servants! There is no fear for you Today, nor will you grieve," and the address is the divine welcome that answers the terror of the reckoning with a personal reassurance reserved for those who feared God in the world. The title "My servants" is the honorific the chapter has been moving toward, because the ones who submitted are claimed by the Lord as His own, and the word undoes the enmity of the Day described in the previous verse by replacing it with intimacy. This ties into the surah's portrait of the believers who responded to their Lord, because the address gathers them under the name that the Straight Path was meant to earn, so that the servant who walked the path is met by the Master who called him. The reader is invited to hear in the word the goal of all the urging, the moment the fear of the Hour is transformed into the safety of the addressed.

---

## Verse 43:69

> ˹those˺ who believed in Our signs and ˹fully˺ submitted ˹to Us˺

**In Our Signs**

The phrase "in Our signs" specifies the object of the belief. The "Our" is the personal claim of God over the evidences. That the signs are not impersonal facts but the self-disclosures of the Lord who will later say "My servants." This joins with the surah's method of moving from the signs to the Sign-giver, because the verse shows that to believe in the signs is to believe in the One who placed them. The comfort of the Day is the fruition of that belief. One is invited to treat every sign, from the rain to the ships to the scripture, as a personal word from the God who will address him, so that the belief is not abstract but relational. The "Our" is the chapter's hint that the signs and the servants belong to the same Giver.

**Fully Submitted**

The verse adds that the servants fully submitted to God, and the submission is the practical outflow of the belief, because the assent of the mind becomes the surrender of the life, and the "fully" marks the completeness of the yielding that the chapter has praised throughout. This belongs to the portrait of the believers who put their trust in their Lord and conduct their affairs by consultation, because the submission is the character the surah has been sketching, the habitual turning of the will toward God. The believer is invited to see that the welcome of the Day is the reward of a life bent in obedience, so that the "fully submitted" are the ones whose path was the Straight Path walked to its end. The submission is the chapter's name for the life that earns the address.

---

## Verse 43:70

> Enter Paradise, you and your spouses, rejoicing.”

**Enter Paradise**

The verse issues the command that is also the supreme invitation, "Enter Paradise," and the imperative is the moment the long journey of the surah arrives at its destination, because the Paradise toward which the signs and the Straight Path pointed is now thrown open to the believer. The command is not a suggestion but a royal admission, the Lord's own word granting entry to those who believed and submitted. That the hearer hears the gate swing wide in the single word "enter." This ties into the chapter's recurring promise of the gardens to the believers, because the verse is the fulfillment of that promise in the second person, addressed to the very servants who were welcomed in the previous verses. The reader is invited to receive the word as spoken to him, the summons from the threshold of the world into the garden of the end, and the verse by this turns the abstract hope into a personal command.

---

## Verse 43:71

> Golden trays and cups will be passed around to them. There will be whatever the souls desire and the eyes delight in. And you will be there forever

**Whatever Souls Desire**

The verse declares that there will be whatever the souls desire, and the comprehensiveness of the promise is its point, because the Paradise is not a place of fixed rations but of fulfilled longing, where the deepest wants of the heart are met by the Giver who knows them. This joins with the chapter's teaching that what is with God is far better and more lasting, because the verse shows that the "whatever" of the soul is supplied in the garden, so that the believer's truest desires are not denied but answered. One is invited to trust that the God who created the soul knows its hunger, and that the Paradise is the place where the hunger is filled rather than forbidden, so that the restraint of the world is answered by the abundance of the end. The desire is the chapter's way of honoring the human heart's deepest reach.

---

## Verse 43:72

> That is the Paradise which you will be awarded for what you used to do

**For What You Used to Do**

The clause "for what you used to do" specifies the ground of the award, and the "used to do" points to the habitual, this-worldly conduct that the surah has been describing, the mindfulness, the prayer, the consultation, the charity, and the trust that marked the believer's days. This joins with the chapter's ethic of the faithful, because the verse shows that the eternal reward corresponds to the temporal life, so that the believer's ordinary obedience is the seed of the everlasting joy. One is invited to recognize the dignity of the small acts, because the "what you used to do" includes the quiet habits of a godly life, and the verse assures him that none of it is lost. The clause is the chapter's way of honoring the lived faith as the cause of the promised rest.

**The Portrait of Grace**

Even as it insists on the correspondence of deed and reward, the verse also reveals grace, because the award is the Giver's own gift, the Paradise that no life could strictly earn but that the merciful Lord bestows on the believing life as its fitting crown. This runs through the chapter's balance of mercy and justice, because the "awarded" is both the wage and the gift, the earning and the giving held together in a single word. Here the hearer is to receive the Paradise as both the fruit of his obedience and the favor of his Lord, so that neither pride nor despair has a place at the gate, and the verse's double character is the chapter's way of keeping the believer humble and hopeful at once. The portrait is the chapter's final image of the saved as both earned and gifted.

---

## Verse 43:73

> There you will have abundant fruit to eat from

**To Eat From**

The phrase "to eat from" grounds the fruit in the bodily delight of the garden, because the eating is the intimate act of reception, the taking of the Giver's provision into oneself, and the verse by this honors the body as well as the soul in the promise of the end. This joins with the earlier description of the trays and the desire, because the fruit is the concrete form of the soul's contentment, the tangible nourishment that matches the visual delight, so that the Paradise engages the whole person. One is invited to receive the imagery not as a crudity but as the scripture's way of affirming that the resurrection includes the body and its joys, and the eating is the chapter's image of the fulfilled creature. The "from" suggests a tree of endless supply, the source that does not run dry.

**The Sustenance of the Garden**

The sustenance described is the answering of the hunger that the world could never fully satisfy, because the fruit of the garden is the provision that does not perish, so that the eating is the perpetual participation in the Giver's bounty. This belongs to the chapter's contrast between the fleeting and the lasting, because the fruit of the world ripens and rots while the fruit of the Paradise is the nourishment of the forever, and the verse uses the simple image to make the eternity tangible. The believer is invited to let the fruit stand for the whole provision of God, the daily bread of the earth become the eternal bread of the garden, and the sustenance is the chapter's way of showing that the Giver who fed him in the world feeds him without end. The garden is the home of the satisfied hunger.

---

## Verse 43:74

> Indeed, the wicked will be in the torment of Hell forever

**The Wicked in Torment**

The verse turns abruptly from the garden to the fire, declaring that the wicked will be in the torment of Hell forever. The turn is the chapter's stark presentation of the two ends, because the same Day that opens Paradise closes Hell, and the surah will not let the hearer linger in the comfort without facing the alternative. The "wicked" are the ones whose lives were the mirror image of the believers', turned from the signs and unsubmitted, and the verse names their end without softening it. This ties into the chapter's consistent contrast of the two communities, because the verse is the negative counterpart of the entrance into Paradise, so that the reader sees both destinations in a single turn of the page. The reader is invited to feel the chill of the word "torment" after the warmth of the fruit, because the chapter intends the contrast to be felt as well as understood, and the abruptness is the scripture's way of refusing to hide the cost of refusal.

---

## Verse 43:75

> It will never be lightened for them, and there they will be overwhelmed with despair

**Never Lightened**

The verse declares that the torment will never be lightened for the wicked, and the negation is the closing of the door that the wrongdoers had hoped might open, because the fire is not a discipline that eases but a consequence that remains, so that the relief they crave is forever withheld. The "never" answers the earlier pleas of the damned for a way back or a finishing off, because the verse shows that neither reprieve nor release is granted, and the unlightened pain is the substance of the eternal loss. This ties into the chapter's portrait of the wrongdoers who begged for return and were refused, because the verse is the religious ground of that refusal, the statement that the mercy has its boundary and the boundary, once crossed, is not reopened. The reader is invited to see the seriousness of the term, because the lightening that does not come is the proof that the Day's verdict is final, and the verse's "never" is the chapter's way of ending the hope of escape.

---

## Verse 43:76

> We did not wrong them, but it was they who were the wrongdoers

**We Did Not Wrong Them**

The verse enters a defense of God's justice against the implicit charge that the punishment was unfair, declaring that "We did not wrong them," and the defense is necessary because the very extremity of the torment might provoke the objection that the Creator was harsh beyond desert. The verse meets that objection head-on, asserting the innocence of God in the matter, so that the ruin of the wicked is not the overflow of divine cruelty but the consequence of their own deed. This ties into the chapter's concern with the wrongdoers who will suffer a painful punishment, because the verse now justifies that suffering by tracing it to its human source, and the hearer is invited to see that the fire is the sinner's own making, not the Giver's malice. The "We did not" is the scripture's way of guarding the character of God against the slander of the sufferer.

---

## Verse 43:77

> They will cry, “O Mâlik! Let your Lord finish us off.” He will answer, “You are definitely here to stay.”

**Let Your Lord Finish Us Off**

The request "let your Lord finish us off" is the starkest expression of the torment's weight, because the sufferers ask not for paradise but for non-existence, preferring the end of being to the continuation of the pain. The verse by this reveals the fire as a state so total that the creature wishes to be unmade. This joins with the earlier description of the overwhelming despair, because the cry is the spoken form of that despair, the moment the soul acknowledges there is no exit but the cessation of itself. One is invited to see the gravity of the loss, because the Giver made the creature for life and the creature now begs for its undoing, and the verse uses the cry to display the full cost of the refusal. The finish-us-off is the chapter's image of the final poverty.

**You Are Here to Stay**

The answer of Malik, "you are definitely here to stay," is the flat closure of the last hope, because the keeper confirms that the annihilation they beg for will not come. That the torment is not only unlightened but unending in the most literal sense, the continuation of the self in the pain. This belongs to the verse that said the torment would never be lightened, because the answer "here to stay" is the personal form of that never, the divine refusal of the only relief the damned can imagine. The believer is invited to feel the finality of the word, because the stay is the permanence of the loss, and the verse uses the keeper's voice to seal the doom that the Giver has decreed. The stay is the chapter's way of ending the dialogue of the damned.

---

## Verse 43:78

> We certainly brought the truth to you, but most of you were resentful of the truth

**We Brought the Truth**

The verse turns from the fate of the doomed to the address of the living, declaring that God certainly brought the truth to the hearer. The "certainly" is the firm assertion that the revelation was not ambiguous or lacking, so that the hearer stands before a clear delivery rather than a confusing one. The truth here is the Qur'an in its entirety, the message of the oneness of God and the certainty of the account, and the verse insists on its having been brought, not merely promised, so that the hearer cannot plead ignorance. This ties into the chapter's earlier assurance that the Qur'an is a glory and the Prophet was told to hold it firmly, because the verse now states the corollary that the truth was indeed delivered, and the hearer's response is the only open question. The reader is invited to receive the "certainly" as the ground of his accountability, because the clarity of the gift is the measure of the gravity of its refusal.

---

## Verse 43:79

> Or have they mastered some ˹evil˺ plan? Then We ˹too˺ are surely planning

**Have They Mastered a Plan**

The verse turns to a rhetorical question about the secret designs of the rejecters, asking whether they have mastered some evil plan against the Prophet and the message, and the question is the scripture's way of exposing the conspiracies that the opponents imagined would nullify the revelation. The "evil plan" is the scheming of the Meccan elite to silence or discredit the warner, and the verse names it without fear, because the very asking is the disclosure that the plots are known. This ties into the chapter's portrait of the elite who resisted from pride, because the verse now shows that their resistance included the crafting of schemes, so that the refusal was not only scorn but calculation. The reader is invited to see that the opposition to the truth has always included the hidden plot as well as the open mockery, and the question is the chapter's way of refusing to let the scheming remain hidden from view.

---

## Verse 43:80

> Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺

**Do They Think We Do Not Hear**

The verse poses a question about the secret unbelief of the opponents, asking whether they think God does not hear their evil thoughts and secret talks. The question exposes the folly of the one who dares in private what he hides in public, as if the hidden were outside the knowledge of the Knower. The "evil thoughts" are the schemes and the sneers that never reached the Prophet's ear, and the verse declares that they reached the Giver's, so that the privacy of the rejecter is no privacy at all. This ties into the chapter's theme that God is the All-Knowing, because the verse applies that attribute to the inner life of the opponent, showing that the secret malice is as audible to the Lord as the spoken word. The reader is invited to see that the hidden rebellion is the most foolish kind, because it is committed against the One who cannot be excluded from any chamber of the heart.

---

## Verse 43:81

> Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the Most Compassionate ˹really˺ had offspring, I would be the first worshipper.”

**Say, If the Compassionate Had Offspring**

The verse instructs the Prophet to address the Meccan claim with a reductio, saying that if the Most Compassionate truly had offspring, the Prophet himself would be the first to worship that offspring, and the statement is a logical trap that exposes the inconsistency of the objectors. The "if" is hypothetical, because the premise is false, but the Prophet's willingness to accept the consequence if it were true shows that his opposition to their claim is not stubbornness but conviction, since he would honor the offspring were it real. This ties into the chapter's defense of the oneness of God, because the verse turns the tables on the Meccans, who accused the Prophet of denying what they honored, by showing that his denial is precisely the refusal to worship a falsehood, not a refusal to honor the true. The reader is invited to see the logic, that the warner's resistance is the guard of the very monotheism the objectors claim to respect.

---

## Verse 43:82

> Glorified is the Lord of the heavens and the earth, the Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim

**Glorified Is the Lord**

The verse breaks into a doxology, declaring that the Lord of the heavens and the earth is glorified, and the glorification is the fitting response to the long dispute about His nature, because the chapter that has defended His oneness now crowns the defense with praise. The "glorified" is the exaltation that follows the vindication, the recognition that the God whose unity was argued is the God who is above all argument, worthy of the worship the disputers withheld. This ties into the surah's opening and closing affirmations of the sole kingdom, because the verse is the worshipful echo of the doctrine, turning the argument into the praise. The reader is invited to join the glorification, because the verse is not only a statement about God but an act of devotion addressed to the hearer's heart, the moment the chapter lifts from contention to adoration.

**The Lord of the Throne**

The addition "the Lord of the Throne" elevates the lordship to its sovereign seat, because the Throne is the Qur'an's image of the divine rule over all that exists, so that the verse places the disputed God on the throne cosmic He governs. This belongs to the chapter's high theology of the Most High, because the Throne is the symbol of the authority that the Meccans' idols could never approach, and the verse uses it to dwarf the pretensions of the creatures who claimed partnership. The believer is invited to feel the majesty, because the Lord of the heavens and the earth is also the Lord of the Throne, and the double title is the chapter's way of overwhelming the reader with the greatness of the One the disputers diminished. The Throne is the scripture's image of the unrivaled sovereignty.

---

## Verse 43:83

> So let them indulge ˹in falsehood˺ and amuse ˹themselves˺ until they face their Day, which they have been warned of

**Let Them Indulge**

The verse issues a striking permission, telling the Prophet to let the rejecters indulge in falsehood and amuse themselves. The permission is not a sanction of the falsehood but a relinquishing of the anxiety to convert by force, because the warner is released from the burden of compelling those who will not hear. The "let them" is the divine calm in the face of refusal, the assurance that the Giver's patience is not threatened by the opponent's play, so that the believer need not strive beyond the delivery. This ties into the chapter's earlier release of the Prophet from being a keeper, because the verse now extends that release to the attitude of the warner, who is told to step back and let the rejecter reap what he sows. ## Verse 43:84

This ties into the chapter's exposure of the angels mislabeled as daughters and the son of Mary misappropriated, because the verse now gathers those errors into the single statement that the heavens contain no deity but Him. The reader is invited to look up and see the unrivaled sovereignty, because the verse is the scriptural emptying of the sky of every partner, leaving the One who fills it alone. The "only" is the chapter's way of finishing the argument with a clean assertion of the truth the disputes had obscured.

**The Only God on the Earth**

The verse pairs the heavenly with the earthly, declaring Him the only God on the earth, and the pairing is the completion of the oneness, because the idols the Meccans set in the land are hereby dethroned, leaving the earth as exclusively His as the heavens. This joins with the surah's opening claim that to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, because the verse now applies that belonging to the question of deity, so that the ownership and the godhead coincide in the single Lord of both realms. One is invited to see that there is no pocket of the creation where a rival reigns, and the double "only" is the chapter's way of leaving the idolater without a foot of ground on which to stand his god.
