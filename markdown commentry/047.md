# Sūrah Muḥammad (Chapter 47) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Standard of the Messenger and the Annihilation of Falsehood**

Muḥammad is the forty-seventh chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 38 verses of early Medinan revelation. It is one of only three chapters in the entire sacred text titled with the personal name of a prophet, and it is universally unique as bearing the personal name of the Final Messenger of Allah, Muḥammad ﷺ (*“And those who believe and do righteous deeds and believe in what has been sent down upon Muḥammad—and it is the truth from their Lord—He will remove from them their misdeeds and amend their condition,”* 47:2). In classical exegesis, it is also widely designated as *Sūrat al-Qitāl* (The Chapter of Fighting), because it was revealed immediately following the *Hijrah* to Madinah (approx. 1 or 2 AH) to articulate the decisive rules of military engagement, the treatment of prisoners of war, and the moral fortitude required to defend the new commonwealth against pagan aggression. Characterized by explosive, razor-sharp contrasts between the radiant clarity of faith and the spiritual bankruptcy of denial, Sūrah Muḥammad is an uncompromising ideological manifesto: declaring that all the deeds, wealth, and strategic maneuvers of the disbelievers are destined to be completely neutralized and brought to absolute futility (*aḍalla aʿmālahum*).

**The Threshold of Statehood and the Inevitability of Conflict**

The historical canvas of Sūrah Muḥammad is the tense, dangerous interlude between the arrival of the Muslim refugees in Madinah and the outbreak of the Battle of Badr. For over a decade in Makkah, the believers had been subjected to physical torture, confiscation of wealth, and relentless boycott without being permitted to retaliate. Upon reaching Madinah, the community was no longer an underground devotional circle; it was a sovereign sanctuary facing imminent, total military assault from the Quraysh, while surrounded by treacherous factions of hypocrites (*al-munāfiqūn*) within the oasis who sought every opportunity to sabotage the state. Sūrah Muḥammad descended to steel the resolve of the believers. It stripped away all pacifist illusions: when oppressors wage war to extinguish the freedom of faith, the believers must not falter or cry out for humiliating peace while they have the upper hand (*“So do not weaken and call for peace while you are superior; and Allah is with you,”* 47:35).

**The Structural Anatomy of the Discourse**

The architecture of Sūrah Muḥammad moves between military jurisprudence, psychological radiography, and eschatological realities. The opening movement (1–3) establishes the grand ontological divide: those who disbelieve and obstruct the path of God have their deeds rendered completely void, while those who believe in what was revealed to Muḥammad have their sins expiated and their inner state rectified (*aṣlaḥa bālahum*), because the deniers follow falsehood while the believers follow the truth from their Lord. The second movement (4–19) codifies the laws of active warfare: when meeting the combatant enemy in battle, strike with decisive firmness until the threat is broken, then secure the bonds of captives, after which they may be released either by gracious generosity (*mannan*) or by ransom (*fidā'an*), until the war lays down its burdens (47:4). It honors the fallen martyrs, promising that God will never let their deeds be lost, but will guide them, amend their state, and admit them into the Paradise He has already made known to them. It delivers the celebrated covenantal formula of victory in verse 7: *“O you who have believed, if you support Allah, He will support you and plant firmly your feet.”* It contrasts the rivers of Paradise—pure water that never stagnates, milk whose taste never changes, wine delicious to the drinkers, and purified honey—against the inmates of Hell forced to drink scalding water that tears their bowels to pieces. The third movement (20–34) conducts an unsparing psychological autopsy of the hypocrites: when an ordinary peaceful sūrah is revealed, they appear compliant, but the moment a decisive chapter mandating military defense descends, *“you see those in whose hearts is disease looking at you with a look of one overcome by death!”* (47:20). It exposes their secret conspiracies with the enemies of Islam, warning them that their treachery is known and that the angels of death will strike their faces and their backs at the moment of expiration. The closing movement (35–38) summons the community to financial mobilization, reminding them that worldly life is but play and diversion, and warning them that stinginess harms only the miser's own soul: *“And Allah is the Free of need, while you are the needy. And if you turn away, He will replace you with another people; then they will not be the likes of you!”* (47:38).

**The Rectification of the Soul: Aṣlaḥa Bālahum**

The profound spiritual thesis of Sūrah Muḥammad is encapsulated in the exquisite divine phrase *Aṣlaḥa Bālahum* (verses 2 and 5)—the total rectification, peace, and soundness of the inner mind and heart. Worldly success, military strength, and financial abundance are meaningless if the inner soul (*al-bāl*) is diseased by hypocrisy, cowardice, and guilt. By aligning one's loyalty unreservedly with the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and standing firm in defense of justice, the believer experiences a sublime psychological integration: sins are expiated, fear is transformed into courage, and the heart is crowned with tranquil certainty.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:1

> Those who disbelieve and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, He will render their deeds void

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Verbs of Damage**

The sūrah opens its account with a pair, and the pairing is the diagnosis. First the private act: they disbelieve. Then the public one: they hinder others from the Way of Allah. The Arabic verb rendered "hinder" — ṣadda ʿan — carries both turning away oneself and pushing others off the road, and the verse intends the double weight: these are people who left the path and then built their lives across it. Sin in the Qur'an is rarely a private hobby for long; unbelief, once adopted, becomes an interest with a maintenance schedule — pressure on the wavering, mockery of the committed, gates kept and guarded. The verse's first lesson is structural: what a person does with the road behind him — block it or leave it open — counts as much as which way he himself walked.

**What It Means for Deeds to Go Void**

The sentence pronounced here is specific: God renders their deeds void — aḥbaṭa in Arabic, a verb whose concrete sense is to strip a thing of its weight so that it no longer registers on the scale. The image is the scale of the Hereafter, and the horror is precision, not cruelty. Nothing is said here about punishment added from outside; what the verse describes is the deeds themselves losing their standing — generosity done for show, courage spent on injustice, kindness practised without any allegiance to the Kind. The action existed; the fruit does not. A life of impressive works can arrive at the scale and register nothing, and the sūrah will spend its opening verses teaching exactly why.

**Deeds Dressed Up to Look Good**

The Qur'an explains the mechanism elsewhere, and the explanation belongs beside this verse. Of those who tamper with sacred months it says: "Their evil deeds have been made appealing to them." (9:37). The same diagnosis covers the people of this verse: they do not experience their obstruction as darkness. The blocked road looks to them like the preservation of order; the mocked messenger looks like a public menace; the whole programme feels like virtue. That is half of what the loss of deeds means — the other half arrives when the costume is removed at the scale, and what felt like service reads as sabotage. Self-deception is not a defence in that courtroom; it is merely the method by which the account was emptied while its owner slept.

**Ashes in the Wind**

The Qur'an gives this voiding a portrait, and the portrait is worth keeping beside the statute: "The parable of the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord is that of ashes fiercely blown away by wind on a stormy day. They will gain nothing from what they have earned. That is ˹truly˺ the farthest one can stray." (14:18). Note the materials. Ashes look like substance — they have mass, colour, a history of having been something. But they are what remains after combustion, and the first honest wind reveals them. The stormy day is the Day of Accounting, and the parable's promise is exact: they will gain nothing. Not a little; nothing. The striver without God has been labouring, in the parable's terms, at the manufacture of ash.

**The Exception the Same Sūrah Will State**

There is a second verdict in this chapter, placed like a pillar at the end of its battle verse, and the two belong in one view: "And those who are martyred in the cause of Allah, He will never render their deeds void." (47:4). The grammar of the sūrah's opening is thus bracketed: deeds voided for those who blocked the road; deeds sealed, never voided, for those who died opening it. The contrast teaches what the voiding is really about. Deeds lose their weight when they are aimed at a self that answers to nothing above it; deeds keep their weight when they are spent for the One who weighs. Direction, not volume, decides the reading on the scale.

**Why Hindering Others Weighs So Heavily**

A question the verse forces: why does blocking others get named in the first line of the sūrah, rather than the long list of private sins available? Because obstruction multiplies. The disbeliever damages one soul; the hinderer stands astride a road that thousands have not yet walked. Influence is the one genuinely renewable resource in moral life, and the verse treats its misuse as the capital crime of the ledger. The Quraysh chiefs who pressured clients away from the Prophet, who threatened the families of new Muslims, who organised the boycott that starved a clan for three years — they are the portrait behind the participle. And the principle survives them: any structure — a state, a family, a fashion — that makes faith costlier than it needs to be is running the obstruction trade, on the exact terms of this verse.

**A Past Tense With the Door Still Open**

One grammatical note, and it is a mercy. The verse states the voiding in the past tense — He has rendered their deeds void — although the accounting stands in the future. Arabic uses that tense for futures that cannot fail, and so the certainty is the point. But the tense describes a category, not a roll of names; as long as a hinderer breathes, the repentance that reverses everything remains on the table, and the sūrah's very next verse opens exactly that door. The God who voids deeds is, in His own names, the One who covers them again for whoever turns — and turning, for the people of this verse, begins with stepping off the road and letting others pass.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:2

> As for those who believe, do good, and have faith in what has been revealed to Muḥammad—which is the truth from their Lord—He will absolve them of their sins and improve their condition

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror Image of the Opening Verse**

The sūrah's second verse reverses its first with surgical symmetry. There: disbelieve and hinder — deeds voided. Here: believe and do good — sins covered and condition repaired. The same divine accounting stands behind both sentences, and the two verses together form a completed diptych: one panel shows the loss, the other the gain. Nothing in either sentence is arbitrary; the outcomes track the inputs with the neatness of a ledger, and the reader is being taught, from the sūrah's first breath, that the Day of Judgment is not a lottery but an audit with two possible findings.

**Three Conditions in One Sentence**

The verse lists the human side with care. They believe — the allegiance of the heart. They do good — the evidence in conduct; the Qur'an never allows the first without demanding the second. And they have faith in what has been revealed to Muḥammad — the specific, historical commitment. The naming is deliberate and rare; the Qur'an usually says the Messenger, but here the full name stands, because what is at stake is not a mood of spirituality but a particular revelation received by a particular man. Generic belief in a general god was the idolaters' own favourite doctrine; the verse asks for belief with an address and a date — this Book, this messenger, this lifetime.

**Kaffāra: Sins Covered as Though Never Done**

The first reward is a word built for the occasion: He absolves them — yukaffiru, from the same Arabic root as kufr, whose base sense is to cover. A farmer covers seed with soil; night covers the day; and God covers sin. The classical distinction is worth carrying: forgiveness can leave the deed on the record, known but not punished; covering, takfīr, is the erasure of the entry itself, so that on the Day the sin is not merely excused but absent. The believer's worst years are not waiting for him as a display; they have been buried like seed, and what grows instead is the repaired life. The Gospel-familiar word for this, atonement, is not the Qur'an's image; the Qur'an's image is a blanket drawn over what shames — thrown by the only One who had the right to display it instead.

**What Islam Wipes Out**

The Prophet stated the doctrine of this verse as an amnesty, and the report is one of the tradition's most quoted. ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ — a man who had spent years opposing the Muslims before his own conversion — came to pledge allegiance and asked that his pardon be written in as a condition. The Prophet replied: "Are you not aware of the fact that Islam wipes out all the previous (misdeeds)?" adding that emigration and pilgrimage do the same (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 121). The amnesty's breadth is the point. A convert does not drag his old file into his new life; the file is destroyed at the border. Men like ʿAmr, and later commanders who had once drawn swords against the Prophet, built the first Muslim state on exactly this clause — proof that the verse was understood, from the beginning, as a real transaction and not a metaphor.

**Evil Replaced by Good**

The Qur'an elsewhere reveals a second stage past covering, and it belongs in any full reading of this verse: "As for those who repent, believe, and do good deeds, they are the ones whose evil deeds Allah will change into good deeds." (25:70). Not merely cancelled — converted. The tradition has always read the conversion spiritually: the old sins become the raw material of the new humility, the testimony of the returned, the tenderness toward other strugglers that the never-fallen rarely learn. The man who wasted a decade can end it with a compassion that the decade itself paid for. Under this economy, a past is not a liability to be hidden but an asset that has changed hands; the believer's history is not deleted, it is refinanced.

**"Improve Their Condition": The Visible Half of Grace**

The second reward is quieter and easily missed: He will improve their condition — in Arabic, yuṣliḥa bālahum, set their state aright. The word covers both worlds. In this life, the repair is often visible: the chaotic life acquires order, the corrosive habits loosen, the relationships that sin was eating begin to heal. The scholars also read the phrase of the Hereafter — the final setting-right of every account — and both readings stand, because grace is not piecemeal. What God forgives, He also reorganises; the absolution of the past and the improvement of the present are one motion of the same hand. A religion that only buried sins would leave its converts standing in a graveyard; this verse promises a garden, and the offer is part of the evidence for it.

**The Truth From Their Lord**

The verse's middle clause states the object of faith once more, in its own register: what was revealed to Muḥammad "which is the truth from their Lord" (47:2). The clause does double work. It protects the faith from becoming a personality cult — the object is a revelation and its truth, not a man's charisma. And it locates the truth's origin: from their Lord — their Lord, the possessive that the believers had already internalised before the verse was spoken. Belief, on this description, is not the wager of a gambler but the allegiance of a creature who has recognised whose he is; the absolution and the repair are simply what that Lord does for His own once they have come home.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:3

> This is because the disbelievers follow falsehood, while the believers follow the truth from their Lord. This is how Allah shows people their true state ˹of faith˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Because That Goes to the Root**

The verse opens with a "because", and the logic it supplies runs deeper than a reason for the previous two sentences; it reaches the root of both verdicts. Deeds are voided or sins covered not by an arbitrary selection but because of what each group follows. The Qur'an grades a life, before anything else, by its direction of travel — by the thing behind the marching. Men are followers by constitution; everyone walks behind something: an idea, a hunger, an ancestor, a fear, a book. The verse's quiet anthropological claim is that the following is not in question — only the object is. Choose the voided object and the deeds go with it; choose the standing one and the same feet arrive somewhere permanent.

**Bāṭil and Ḥaqq: What Collapses and What Stands**

The two objects are named with the Qur'an's most fundamental pair. Falsehood is bāṭil in Arabic, from a root whose concrete sense is to fall in, to collapse — the word was used of a wall that gives way, a well that runs dry. Truth is ḥaqq, from a root of firmness and right: what stands, what is owed, what holds under load. The etymology is the argument. A life built on bāṭil is not slightly wrong; it is structurally unsound, and time is the demolition crew. A life built on ḥaqq is aligned with what cannot fall — the reality God made — and the same pressure that collapses the one presses the other into place. The two words occur across the Qur'an in every register, and this verse is the pair's cleanest one-line statement.

**How God Sets Forth the Likenesses**

The verse's second sentence names the Qur'an's teaching method: "This is how Allah shows people their true state" — literally, sets forth their likenesses, the mathal. God does not merely state verdicts; He draws portraits, and the whole Qur'an is a gallery of them: the spider's house for the flimsiness of false trust, the good tree and the rotten one, the two men, one of them dumb and powerless, the rain-gorged field that dries to chaff. The parable's power is diagnostic. A person can argue with a proposition indefinitely; he recognises himself in a likeness before he has time to mount a defence. That is why the Qur'an answers the abstract question — who are you, really? — with pictures, and lets the reader gasp on his own behalf.

**The Good Tree as This Verse's Exhibit**

The most famous of the likenings deserves a place here, because it is the portrait of the very people this verse calls the followers of ḥaqq: "Do you not see how Allah compares a good word to a good tree? Its root is firm and its branches reach the sky, yielding its fruit in every season by the Will of its Lord." (14:24). The correspondence with this verse is exact. The good word — the confession at the root of faith — is ḥaqq: rooted, load-bearing, standing. From it grows the doing of good, which is the fruit, and the fruit arrives "in every season", which is the Qur'an's way of saying that a rooted life produces across all weathers. The believer of this verse is that tree; the verse under commentary is the botanical label on the portrait.

**Truth "From Their Lord" — the Possessive That Decides**

The believers follow the truth "from their Lord" (47:3), and the possessive carries doctrine. The truth in question is not a discovery the believers own — an insight they generated, a system they can improve at will — but something sent to them by the One who owns them. The order of possession is reversed from the modern instinct: they do not have a Lord because they found a truth; they have a truth because they have a Lord, and He sent it. That is why the truth holds them up rather than the other way around. A manufactured truth collapses with its manufacturers — the fate of every ideology that promised permanence and delivered a decade — but a sent truth survives its followers' failures, because its structural support is not in them.

**Why the Verse Speaks of Following, Not Knowing**

The measure here is what people follow, not what they know, and the choice of verb is a judgment on knowledge's track record. The Qur'an's deniers are never described as short on information; the chiefs of Makka knew the Prophet's lifetime of honesty, and the scholars of earlier nations knew their own scriptures' testimony. Knowledge, held without obedience, becomes a possession — hoarded, traded, defended — rather than a road walked. Following is knowledge with legs. It exposes what a person actually accepts, because people will claim any belief and follow only what they trust. The verse therefore hands every reader a mirror cheaper than a library: look at what you follow — where your feet actually go when the cost arrives — and you will know your true state without a single further test.

**The Likeness Applied Before the Judgment Day**

"This is how Allah shows people their true state" — and the showing is not postponed until the Hereafter. The Qur'an itself performs it, and history performs it, and the events of this sūrah will perform it in a manner its first audience lived through: battle as an X-ray, revealing in a single afternoon who believed and who had been performing. God's likenings are merciful exactly here. A state of the heart that would otherwise be discovered too late to change is published early, in parable and in circumstance, so that whoever recognises the less flattering portrait still has time to sit for a better one. The verdict is certain; the sitting is still open.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:4

> So when you meet the disbelievers ˹in battle˺, strike ˹their˺ necks until you have thoroughly subdued them, then bind them firmly. Later ˹free them either as˺ an act of grace or by ransom until the war comes to an end. So will it be. Had Allah willed, He ˹Himself˺ could have inflicted punishment on them. But He does ˹this only to˺ test some of you by means of others. And those who are martyred in the cause of Allah, He will never render their deeds void

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Rulebook Revealed Inside a Battle**

This is the sūrah's weightiest verse, and its first character is restraint disguised as severity. It regulates what was already happening — the first battles between the Muslims and the Quraysh coalition — and its commands are boundaries drawn around an existing human activity, not a licence invented for a new one. The blow is aimed at the neck, the decisive act of close combat, until the enemy is thoroughly subdued — and the verse itself then removes the sword from the equation: what follows the subduing is binding, not killing. In the seventh century, a commander's options for a defeated enemy ran to slaughter, slavery, or ransom; this verse closes with two humane exits — release as a favour, or ransom — and confines detention to the duration of the war. Whatever a reader brings to it, the verse deserves to be read as what it is: conduct-of-war law, delivered mid-war, mostly in the direction of limiting what war was.

**The Sequence Keeps Captives Off the Battlefield**

The order of operations carries a doctrine: strike until thoroughly subdued, then bind. Prisoners are taken only after resistance has collapsed, not during it, and the Qur'an states the reason elsewhere in a verse revealed after the battle of Badr: "It is not fit for a prophet that he should take captives until he has thoroughly subdued the land." (8:67). The concern is practical and moral at once: captives taken mid-battle become leverage, hostages, and the temptation to fight for prisoners rather than for the end of the fighting. The thorough-subduing clause turns the fighter's mind from capture to conclusion — war's purpose, in this sūrah's grammar, is to end war, and the sequence serves the purpose.

**Grace or Ransom: The Only Two Exits**

Once the fighting is done and the captives bound, the verse sets the menu: release as an act of grace, or ransom, until the war lays down its burdens. No third category of reprisal killing is commanded; and the Prophet's practice, recorded across the campaigns, was precisely this menu — free releases for the poor and for those who could teach, ransom payments, and exchanges. After Badr, the Qur'an itself instructed him to speak to the captives: "O Prophet! Tell the captives in your custody, 'If Allah finds goodness in your hearts, He will give you better than what has been taken from you, and forgive you. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.'" (8:70). The jurists of the later tradition, reading this verse, kept its flexibility: the commander decides between the exits case by case, because the verse legislates outcomes, not a single procedure. Rigidity was read into it by nobody who had read it closely.

**"Until the War Lays Down Its Burdens"**

The closing formula of the custody clause is a metaphor worth slowing down for: war is pictured as a beast of burden, loaded with weights — the Qur'an elsewhere names the burdens: fear, hunger, loss of wealth and lives — and the verse speaks of the day the beast finally sets them down. Some commentators read it simply: until fighting ceases. Others read it teleologically: so that war ceases — custody itself is to be administered with its own end in view. Both readings agree on the essential: the verse does not permit a permanent institution of detention, and it does not imagine war as a career. Wars, in this grammar, are episodes that righteous conduct is designed to close; peace is not the interruption of the system but its completion.

**He Could Have Punished Them Without You**

Then the verse lifts the curtain on its own theology: had Allah willed, He could have inflicted the punishment Himself (47:4). The angels fought at Badr in the Qur'an's own account; armies could have been stopped by winds as ʿĀd's was; God needs no infantry. The fighting is therefore not God's necessity but the believers' test — and the Qur'an states the principle in the verse of the table-spread: "If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but ˹He willed otherwise˺, that He might test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you. So compete with one another in doing good." (5:48). This is the hardest and cleanest theodicy of war in the Qur'an: conflict exists so that what is in people may come out where it can be seen and repaid. Cowardice, courage, mercy, greed — battle is the exam hall of the hidden.

**Tested by Means of One Another**

The phrase "test some of you by means of others" deserves its own reading, because it makes every human being an instrument in someone else's trial. The strong are tested by the weak who need them; the weak by the strong who outrank them; the believers by the deniers who resist them; the deniers by the believers who forgive them. No one's test is private, because no one's life is private. The doctrine converts the whole of social existence into an examination with consequences — and it converts the believer's view of his opponents: the man across the field is not merely an enemy; he is, whether he knows it or not, a component of a trial God is running for both their sakes. That knowledge does not soften every duty; it does forbid contempt.

**The Martyrs' Deeds Never Lose Their Weight**

The verse ends where the sūrah began, closing its circle: those who are martyred in the cause of Allah — He will never render their deeds void (47:4). The same verb that opened the chapter over the hinderers now stands negated over the fallen. A martyr's account is not merely credited; it is frozen full — sealed against the voiding, the moth, and the thief, the Qur'an says elsewhere. For the community that had buried men at Badr and would bury more, the clause was not doctrine but medicine: grief met by ledger. And for the living it answered the calculation every recruit silently performs — what happens to everything I was becoming, if I die here? The verse's reply is the chapter's thesis in miniature: nothing is lost on God's side of the line. The loss is all on the side of the road not taken.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:5

> He will guide them ˹to their reward˺, improve their condition

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verse Whose Only Actor Is God**

Read the verse slowly and count the performers. He will guide them; He will improve their condition. The fallen are the only humans in the sentence, and they do nothing — they are carried. In a passage that began with commands to fighters, this little verse changes key entirely: the war verses are full of human verbs, and the reward verses have one subject only. The lesson is embedded in the grammar. Whatever the battle demanded of the living, what follows death is not another achievement but a reception; the martyr has finished acting, and the One who commanded the acting takes over every remaining step. Grace, in this verse, is not a mood; it is a series of divine actions with a definite object.

**Guidance That Continues Past the Grave**

The first of those actions is surprising: He will guide them. Guidance is the word the Qur'an uses for the whole of religion in this life — the pointing of the road, the correction of the feet. That God keeps guiding after death teaches that His relationship with a servant does not end at the graveside. The commentators read the clause specifically: He will guide them to their reward — lead them along the way, to the Garden, to their stations in it — the way a host walks honoured guests in from the gate. The image is procession, not post. For the person who fears death as an abrupt termination into darkness, the verse offers the opposite picture: a road that keeps being shown, by the same Guide, on the other side of the last wall.

**Alive, With Their Lord**

The Qur'an does not leave the martyrs' condition to inference. Elsewhere it states it outright, forbidding the living to call the fallen in God's cause dead: they are alive with their Lord, provided for, even as their families stood at the graveside (2:154); and it adds that they rejoice in what God has given them, and are glad on behalf of those who have not yet joined them (3:169). This is the doctrine behind the little word "them" in this verse — the objects of God's guiding and repairing are conscious, receiving beings, not memories. The Islamic tradition built on these verses its whole understanding of the shahīd: not a man deleted, but a man relocated, with the accounting of his deeds still open and accruing.

**The Same Repair Promised to the Living**

The second verb repeats, word for word, the promise made to living believers earlier in this sūrah: God "will absolve them of their sins and improve their condition" (47:2). The repetition is a stitch joining the two halves of the sūrah's opening: the believers at home and the believers in the grave receive the same repairing God. Nothing about dying for the faith changes the fundamental transaction — sins covered, state set aright — it only accelerates and perfects it. For the recruit weighing his life against his death, the verse levels the terror: both states are in the same hands, under the same promise, and the hands have a record. The improvement the martyr receives is simply the believer's improvement completed, without the interference of a remaining lifetime of temptation.

**The Order of the Welcome**

Put the verses in a row and a sequence appears: He will guide them, improve their condition, and admit them into Paradise (47:6). Three steps, all divine, in order: the showing of the road, the setting aright of the traveller, and then the entry. The order is ceremonial, like the reception of a returning envoy — met on the road, honoured, escorted, and finally brought inside. Nothing in the sequence is hurried, and nothing is done by the guest. The Qur'an could have compressed all three into one word, rewarded; it chose a procession, and the choice tells the reader what kind of God is doing the receiving: One who lingers over the homecoming.

**Consolation Written as Theology**

These verses were not revealed into a seminar; they were revealed to a community that had buried men it could not replace. The consolidation of grief into doctrine is one of the Qur'an's signature achievements: mourners are not told their dead are "in a better place" as a kindness of speech; they are given the mechanism — alive, provided for, guided, improved, admitted — with the divine actions itemised. Grief responds to itemisation better than to sentiment, because each item is a place to rest the mind. A widow of Badr or Uḥud reciting this verse held, in three lines, an answer to the only questions grief actually asks: where is he, who is with him, and what is being done for him. The verse answers all three without pausing.

**What It Asks of the Living**

The verse addresses the dead in its content and the living in its effect, and what it asks of the living is trust in the Guide of the dead. There is a boundary in every believer's grief past which his own love cannot follow the departed — the grave's door — and this verse stands at that boundary as a guarantee: the guiding that has not stopped will not stop. The ask is concrete. It rules out the two classic errors about the fallen: the despair that pictures them deleted, and the inflation that pictures them needing our ceremonies to progress. God needs neither our grief management nor our help; the men He guided into the field are not beyond His guiding now. The living owe them remembrance, and owe God the trust that the remembrance rests on.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:6

> and admit them into Paradise, having made it known to them

**Expanded Commentary**

**Admission as an Escort**

The verb is hospitality: He admits them — yudkhiluhum, causes them to enter. Not "grants them", not "allots them"; brings them in, the way a host brings a guest through his own door. The Qur'an's pictures of the last day are full of being driven, gathered, exposed; against that background, this word is almost domestic. The men who fell in battle — the sūrah's immediate subjects — are pictured not standing trial but entering a house, and the picture is the doctrine: the judgment they feared has already been settled by the sūrah's earlier verses; what remains is the welcome. The Arabic name for the destination carries its own picture: jannah, Paradise, comes from a root meaning to hide or to cover — a garden, in the oldest sense of the word, is growth so lush it conceals its own ground. Even the name, before any description begins, promises enclosure, shade, a world that wraps its residents rather than displaying them. And the verse's closing clause — having made it known to them — belongs to the same picture: a place whose residents arrive already familiar with it, greeted not as strangers at a gate but as heirs walking onto their own land.

**"Having Made It Known to Them"**

The closing clause is the verse's jewel, and the commentators open it carefully. The Arabic reads: and He will acquaint them with it — familiarise them with the Garden, teach them its places. The reading is not that they receive an orientation lecture; it is that each entrant is brought to what is already his, and knows it. Heaven, on this description, is not a reward chosen at random from a catalogue; it is a prepared place, and the preparation includes the resident's own recognition of it. The believers' own Book had promised them the Garden all their lives; the clause completes the promise at the only point it could be completed — not in the promising, but in the showing.

**The Recognition the Prophet Described**

The Prophet gave this verse a narrative, and it is one of the tradition's most vivid. He said that when the believers are saved from the Fire, they will be stopped at a bridge between the Garden and the Fire, and there they will settle the injustices done among them in the world; when they are purified, permission to enter is given — and then: "By Him in Whose Hands the life of Muhammad is, everybody will recognise his dwelling in the Paradise better than he recognises his dwelling in this world" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2440). Every reader feels the force of the comparison immediately. A man finds his own home in the dark without a lamp, by habit, by love, by ten thousand small memories. That is the familiarity described — earned residence, not assigned lodging.

**Purification Before the Gates**

The same report adds a step modern readers often skip: the settling of accounts among the believers at the bridge. The saved are not waved through with their grudges; wrongs done between them are dealt with before entry, until they are clean. The doctrine balances this verse's tenderness with severity, and the two are one design. A Garden entered by people still holding debts against each other would be a colony of old bitterness under new lighting; God purifies the entrants of their entanglements first, because mercy without justice would ruin the very home mercy built. The consolation of the verse is therefore not cheap: what the believers receive, they receive clean — and the cleaning is part of the gift.

**Recognition and the Continuity of the Self**

Beneath the hospitality lies a philosophical claim worth naming: the self that enters the Garden is the self that lived. Memory, attachment, the sense of one's own place — all survive, because the recognition the verse promises requires them. The Qur'an's afterlife is not the dissolution of a person into an ocean of bliss; it is that person, healed and continued, finding his own dwelling known to him. For believers who loved particular rooms, particular trees, particular faces, this is the doctrine that makes eternity inhabitable rather than merely endless. Heaven is not a different programme running on different beings; it is the same story, resumed past the point where death tried to end it, in a country where every arrival has a home already in view.

**Known Before Entry — and by Promise Before That**

Some commentators turn the clause toward this life: God had already made the Garden known to them — in promises, in glimpses of nearness, in the verses they recited — so that the entry is the reunion with a landscape they had memorised from description. The two readings, made-known-in-the-world and shown-at-entry, do not compete; they are the two halves of the same comfort. The promise trained the longing, and the entry satisfies it, with nothing alien in between. This is the Qur'an's deep answer to the fear that heaven cannot be anticipated without being invented: the map was sent with the promise, and at the gate the resident discovers the map was drawn by the Landlord, who does not err.

**The Verse That Finishes the Welcome**

Set in its sequence, this verse is the terminus of a movement begun three verses earlier: deeds never voided, guidance continued, condition improved, and now admission, with recognition, into the Garden. The whole itinerary of the martyr is divine from the second he falls. For the living, the sequence works as both comfort and summons: the same God who receives the fallen so completely has assigned the living their stretch of the road first. The sūrah now turns from the rewarded to the conditional that governs the rewarders — help God, and be helped — and the turn begins exactly here, at the Garden's open door.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:7

> O believers! If you stand up for Allah, He will help you and make your steps firm

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Conditional That Governs the Community**

After the battle rules and the rewards, the sūrah states its engine: if you stand up for Allah, He will help you. The sentence structure places the whole communal destiny inside one conditional, and the placement is the teaching. The victory verses before and the doom verses after are not fate; they are consequences, switched on and off by a clause that begins with the believers' own act. A community that knows its history is conditional is protected from both of history's diseases: the arrogance that credits victories to itself, and the despair that reads defeats as abandonment. The condition named here is the cure for both — and the address, O believers, makes sure the sentence lands on the people who assume it is about someone else.

**How Can Anyone Help God?**

The phrase startles until it is read as the Qur'an reads it. To stand up for Allah — to help Him, in the Arabic — cannot mean supplying a deficiency in the Almighty; it means serving His cause in the world. And the Qur'an defines that cause concretely: "˹They are˺ those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason other than proclaiming: 'Our Lord is Allah.' Had Allah not repelled ˹the aggression of˺ some people by means of others, destruction would have surely claimed monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques, in which Allah's Name is often mentioned. Allah will certainly help those who stand up for Him." (22:40). The verse reveals the method: God defends the houses of worship of all communities through people, and the people He uses are called His helpers. Standing up for God is standing between the violent and the vulnerable — the definition that this sūrah's own refugees, expelled from Makka for one sentence of faith, embodied.

**Two Gifts: Rescue and Firmness**

The promise has two clauses, and they answer two different fears. He will help you — that is rescue, the intervention when the matter is beyond the community's strength. And He will make your steps firm — thabbit aqdāmakum in Arabic, literally make your feet stand fast — that is stability: the refusal of panic, the holding of the line, the mind that stays clear when the charge comes. Victory needs both. Armies have been saved by help and lost by wobble; the Qur'an promises the pair. At Badr, the Qur'an says, the help was even assigned staff — the angels were told before the battle: "I am with you. So make the believers stand firm." (8:12) — the firmness distributed to the fighters' hearts while the fighting was done through them.

**Make Our Steps Firm: The Prayer the Verse Teaches**

The phrase became the believers' own prayer, and the Qur'an records them praying it in the middle of their hardest day. Of the prophets and the faithful who fought beside them, it says: "And all they said was, 'Our Lord! Forgive our sins and excesses, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the disbelieving people.'" (3:147). The prayer's order is its theology: pardon before firmness, firmness before victory. Nobody asks an army to stand on unforgiven sin, and nobody asks God for an outcome while refusing the footing. The tradition took the phrase into every crisis since — the prayer whispered before every trial, the phrase a believer inherits as his own portable clause of this verse: the help is God's to give, and the asking is ours.

**Firm Feet as a Portrait of the Heart**

The idiom deserves a last look, because Arabic — like the Qur'an's usage generally — lets the body part stand for the inward thing. Feet in the Qur'an are what a life walks on: the direction of conduct, the habits beneath the intentions. To make the steps firm is therefore more than a command about battle-lines; it is God steadying the whole apparatus by which a believer moves — conviction under the heart, resolve under the will, endurance under the feet. The commentators found the allegory everywhere in this sūrah's vocabulary, and ordinary believers have always felt it: the worst hours of trial are not when the enemy is strongest but when one's own steps go soft. The promise names the exact thing that goes soft, and promises its steadying.

**Why the Conditional Comes After the Rewards**

The verse's position is not decoration; it is pastoral strategy. Had the sūrah opened with conditions, the community would have heard the battle verse as a contract and the rewards as wages. Instead the sūrah first shows what God does for the fallen — guiding, improving, admitting, acquainting — and only then states the condition, so that the believers hear it as servants who already know the Employer's generosity. Trust established, duty can be stated plainly. It is the difference between a conscript reading a statute and a friend reading a request. The Qur'an's sequencing here is a standing lesson for anyone who teaches: the giving must be visible before the asking is credible.

**A Promise With an Edge**

The conditional that comforts also cuts, and the sūrah lets the edge show in what follows. Help is promised to those who stand up; by the cold logic of conditionals, standing up for something else forfeits the standing firm. The next verse turns the conditional over and reads its reverse side aloud, and the reading is as unsentimental as the promise was generous. The verse's final service to the believer is that it leaves him no third option to camp on: no arrangement in which God's help is subscribed by heritage while the feet walk their own way. The door is wide, but it is a door — open to those who pass through it in the direction it was cut for.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:8

> As for the disbelievers, may they be doomed and may He render their deeds void

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Reverse Side of the Conditional**

The previous verse opened a door; this one shows what lies on the other side of it. Those who disbelieve stand outside the promise, and the verse pronounces their state in two clauses: doomed, and deeds voided. The structure mirrors 47:7 exactly — two gifts for the helpers, two losses for the refusers — and the mirroring is the message. The God of this sūrah is not a seller of indulgences to one party and a tyrant to another; He is one Accountant with two findings, and the finding follows the audited life. A reader who felt the warmth of the promise must feel the chill of its negation, or he has not yet read the sūrah; he has only browsed it.

**A Word Too Heavy for One English Word**

The first clause of the Arabic is taʿsan lahum — a construction of unusual force that translators have rendered "may they be doomed", "woe to them", "ruined are they". The root from which it is built carries, in the classical lexicons, a thesaurus of loss: ruin, misfortune, sorrow, defeat, revilement, ignominy, deprivation. The form is intensive; the verse compresses the whole dictionary into two words and drops it on one subject. This is the Qur'an's method with words of warning — it does not describe the loser's state from a menu; it picks the word whose sound and sense are the state. No single English phrase can carry it, which is why the translation keeps the invocation's rhythm: may they be doomed.

**Verdict or Prayer?**

The grammar invites a question worth asking: is the clause a statement — their state is ruin — or a wish, an imprecation spoken over them? The construction can bear both, and the commentators divide. The stronger reading treats it as a verdict in the garb of a dirge: the Arabic idiom of taʿsā was used in wailing and in cursing, and the verse adopts that funeral register to pronounce what is already factually settled. The register does real work. A flat prediction — "they will be ruined" — informs; the funeral cadence makes the reader feel the sentence as the disbelievers themselves will feel it when it lands. The Qur'an chooses form the way it chooses words: for the weight. A reader who flinches at the register should also notice what it replaces: the verse offers no elaborate description of torments, no catalogue of punishments. What the sūrah spends on the disbelievers is two heavy words and a repeated legal clause. The restraint is itself a doctrine — this Book is more interested in the accounting than in the agony, and the fright it aims to produce is the kind that still turns a person around, not the kind that merely horrifies him.

**Deeds Voided a Second Time**

The second clause repeats the sūrah's opening verdict word for word: He renders their deeds void. The repetition is not carelessness; legal documents repeat their operative clause, and this sūrah is a verdict document. The first statement (47:1) established the law; this one applies it; and the sūrah will repeat it twice more before closing (47:9, 47:28), like a stamp pressed at each page's foot. Repetition also serves the reader's memory: a doctrine this hard — that human effort can be real, costly, and finally weightless — must be stated more than once before proud minds will hold it. The verse is the stamp falling a second time on the page it will seal.

**The Symmetry of the Two Households**

Read 47:7 and 47:8 side by side and the theology of the passage stands exposed in its architecture. For the believers: help and firmness. For the disbelievers: doom and voided deeds. Four outcomes, two per household, and nothing arbitrary anywhere in the frame. The Qur'an loves this diptych form in its early Madinan sūrahs precisely because the young community needed the frame: persecution had inverted worldly appearances, making the believers look doomed and their opponents flourishing. The verses teach the members of a battered minority to read the ledger beneath the news — appearances are instalments, the audit is the total — and the lesson has lost none of its usefulness for every community since that has looked, from the outside, like the losing side.

**The Doom Already Rehearsed**

The verse's first audience could check the category against events. The Quraysh had driven the believers from their homes, planned assassinations, and finally met them at Badr — and the battle the Quraysh expected to win became, in the Qur'an's account, a day of help and firmness for the side they outnumbered. Uhud, the year after, ran the other way, and the sūrah's conditional explained even that: setbacks read by the light of 47:7 are not verdicts but tests, while the opponents' gains are the instalments of a different account. The verse trains this double vision: when the wicked prosper, the believer does not revise his theology; he files the prosperity where the verse files it — on the doomed account, accruing.

**What the Verse Refuses to Close**

For all its funeral cadence, the verse — like the sūrah's opening — speaks in categories, not names. The category is disbelievers, defined by what its members do with the road; and the sūrah itself has already published, in its second verse, the transaction by which a person exits the category: believe, do good, and find the ledger reopened with sins covered and condition repaired. The imprecation, read inside the whole document, functions like the warning printed on medicine: severe, specific, and intended to prevent the very outcome it describes. The doom is real, and the door out of it is two verses back — that is the sūrah's own order of presentation, and it is not an accident.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:9

> That is because they detest what Allah has revealed, so He has rendered their deeds void

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Because of Detestation**

The verse supplies the root cause of the voiding, and the cause is not intellectual. They detest — kariha in Arabic, the verb of visceral aversion, the word for a thing the stomach turns from. The verse is not describing men who examined the revelation and found it unconvincing; it is describing men who hated it on arrival. That distinction organises the whole sūrah's psychology. Doubt asks questions; detestation mounts defences. The denier of this verse had reasons, arguments, charges — sorcery, forgery, ancient fables — but the verse strips the costume and shows the engine underneath: what was revealed was unwelcome, and what is unwelcome must be explained away at any cost. Unbelief, in this diagnosis, is not a conclusion; it is a allergy wearing a conclusion's clothes.

**What They Actually Hated**

What, concretely, was detestable in what Allah revealed? The Qur'an itself answers across its Makkan pages. It declared a God who sees the heart — detestable to men whose power ran on privacy. It threatened an accounting in which wealth and lineage weighed nothing — detestable to a city built on both. It seated slaves and chiefs in the same row of prayer — detestable to the proud. It asked honesty in trade, restraint in appetite, and spending on the poor — detestable to comfortable men in both directions. The objection to the messenger was always, at root, an objection to the message's bill; and the verse's phrasing — what Allah has revealed, not whom Allah has sent — makes the target explicit. Men rarely hate a prophet's face; they hate the invoice his Book delivers.

**The Third Stamp of the Same Verdict**

This is now the third time the sūrah has pronounced the voiding of deeds, and the repetition is structural, like the stamping of a document at every page. First the law was stated: those who disbelieve and hinder — deeds void (47:1). Then the verdict was read over the party: may they be doomed, deeds void (47:8). Now the cause is entered into the record: because they detested the revelation, deeds void (47:9). Each pass adds a layer — statute, sentence, motive — until the case is closed with the pathology documented. Repetition in the Qur'an is rarely redundancy; it is emphasis achieved through architecture, and the sūrah's architecture here teaches that the loss of deeds is not one punishment among many but the standing consequence, restated because the heart resists it.

**Deeds Wasted in Both Worlds**

The Qur'an is explicit that this voiding is not deferred to the Hereafter alone: "They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers." (3:22). The worldly half of the loss is visible in the pattern this sūrah keeps citing: schemes that collapse, alliances that betray, victories that rot on the branch. A project pursued against the grain of what God revealed carries its failure inside it from the start, like a building drawn on faulty geometry. The afterlife's half of the loss merely makes the ledger official. For the audience that watched the Muslim community grow while its own coalition corroded, the two halves of 3:22 were not a prophecy to await; they were the news.

**Detestation as a Diagnostic Instrument**

The verse quietly hands every reader an instrument for self-examination, and honest souls have always used it. The heart's unguarded reaction to truth is data. A person can note — privately, without ceremony — which commands of the Book produce in him not difficulty but distaste, which teachings he hopes are misinterpreted, which restrictions he describes, to friends, with a curled lip. Difficulty is human; the believer feels it and obeys anyway, and the struggle itself is honoured in the tradition. Distaste is different: it is the early detestation of this verse, at home size. Caught early, it can be confessed and unlearned; fed and rationalised, it becomes the settled aversion that one day files a person's deeds under void. The diagnostic is free, and the verse invites its use.

**Why Revelation Provokes Hatred at All**

A last question the verse provokes: why would revealed truth draw hatred rather than mere disagreement? Because revelation is not a proposal. It claims obedience, names its Author, and refuses the status of an opinion to be sampled. The person who wants spirituality as an accessory can tolerate any number of gods, provided none of them issues invoices. What Allah has revealed arrives as command and criterion — a standard above the self — and to a soul organised around its own autonomy, a standard is an insult before it is ever an argument. The history of hostility to prophets is thus not a history of intellectual failure; it is the biography of pride meeting a verdict. This verse writes the diagnosis over the whole file.

**The Mercy Inside a Motive Clause**

Yet even this severest of clauses carries a mercy in its form. By naming the motive — detestation — the verse makes the voiding conditional on something a living person can still inspect and change. A verdict attached to ancestry or fortune would be unchangeable; a verdict attached to what the heart does with revelation is a verdict attached to a choice. The sūrah's earlier door — believe, do good, and the ledger reopens — stands exactly beside this verse, as it stood beside every other. What God records as cause, He offers, in time, as warning; the file stamped "because they detested" is the same file a changed heart can petition to have reopened, while the stamped page is still being written.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:10

> Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those before them? Allah annihilated them, and a similar fate awaits the disbelievers

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command to Travel and See**

The Qur'an's recurring demand for evidence takes a surprising form here: travel. Not scripture, not argument — a field trip. The deniers are told to walk the land and inspect the endings of those who stood where they stand: confident, established, surrounded by power, and unimpressed by warnings. The demand assumes something remarkable about the world — that God has left His verdicts visible in it, that history's crime scenes are preserved as open files, and that any honest traveller can read them. Religion, in this verse, is not a retreat from evidence; it is an insistence on following the evidence farther than the comfortable want to go.

**Superior in Might, Richer in Monuments**

What the traveller will find, the Qur'an states elsewhere with the same vocabulary. Of the earlier peoples: "They were far superior in might; they cultivated the land and developed it more than these ˹Meccans˺ ever have. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs. Allah would have never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves." (30:9). And again, with the ruins itemised: "Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might and ˹richer in˺ monuments throughout the land. But Allah seized them for their sins, and they had no protector from Allah." (40:21). The repetition across sūrahs is deliberate. Every clause of the deniers' security — numbers, walls, works, legacy — is named in the record as having been tried already, by better resourced applicants, and the application was denied.

**Annihilation, Not Gradual Decay**

The verb of this verse is total: Allah annihilated them — dammara in Arabic, a word for destruction that leaves the thing ruined beyond repair, like a city flattened to its foundations. The verse does not describe the ordinary entropy by which all institutions fade; it names the ending that history knows as catastrophe — the empire that stopped mid-sentence, the civilisation whose ruins puzzled its heirs. The distinction matters to the argument. Gradual decline proves nothing; any materialist can read decay into economics. Catastrophic, patterned endings that follow the rejection of messengers — that is a signature, and the Qur'an invites its readers to check for it across every archive they can walk to: "Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers." (3:137).

**"A Similar Fate Awaits"**

Then the verse commits itself: a similar fate awaits the disbelievers. It is one thing to report the past; it is another to predict its repetition — to tell the current management of unbelief that the exhibit they are touring is a preview. The Qur'an makes that prediction repeatedly, and its first audience lived to see its opening instalments. The confidence of the prediction rests on the doctrine the whole sūrah builds: the endings of nations are not accidents of climate and luck but judgments with stable criteria, and criteria, by definition, repeat. A reader may reject the premise; what he cannot do is accuse the Book of vagueness. It staked its claim in public, in advance, with the ruins as its exhibits.

**Why the Caravans Saw Nothing**

Here lies the tragedy the verse presupposes: the Quraysh had travelled those routes all their lives. Their trade ran past the very ruins this verse cites, season after season, and they came home with profit margins and no questions. The failure was not in the eyes; it was in the asking. Travel educates only the traveller who carries a question; to everyone else it is mere transport. The verse's conditional — travel and see — conceals its own anthropology: seeing is a moral act before it is an optical one. The merchants of Makka could read a ruin's water table at a glance and stayed blind to its sermon; the same body of evidence lies before every age, waiting for the question that unlocks it.

**The Modern Traveller's Portfolio**

The invitation has not expired; it has industrialised. The ruins are now museums, digs, documentaries; the endings of mighty states are catalogued with a precision the seventh century could not match. What has not changed is the split this verse diagnoses: data without the question. A century can tour Assyria, read Rome's ledgers, walk the Dead Sea shore, and file the endings under economics, climate, mismanagement — every category except the one the Qur'an proposed. The verse stands over the archive offering its reading: every mightier predecessor ended, the endings clustered around the rejection of warners, and the repetition is the lesson. Travel and see, it says; the exhibits have improved. The seeing is still the traveller's to do.

**The Comfort in a Warning**

For the beleaguered community the sūrah first addressed, this verse of destruction was a shelter. They were the driven-out, the mocked, the bereaved; the political facts of their decade said they were the losing side. The verse relocates the verdict: the men with the fortresses are standing in the queue of ʿĀd and Pharaoh, and the queue moves. Patience — the sūrah's recurring prescription — is not stoicism here; it is a theory of history. The believer who has read the exhibits knows how this pattern ends, and he knows it while the sun is still shining on the people who doubt him. That knowledge has steadied embattled minorities of every generation since, and it was purchased, as this verse insists, by the price of a ticket: travel, and see.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:11

> This is because Allah is the Patron of the believers while the disbelievers have no patron

**Expanded Commentary**

**Mawlā: A Word With a Social History**

The verse turns on a single Arabic word, and the word arrives trailing a whole society. Mawlā — rendered "Patron" — meant the protector in a patronage system: the clansman who answered your feuds, the ally whose name made looters pass by, the patron of the client, the ex-master who remained a freedman's guardian after manumission. In seventh-century Arabia, your mawlā was your social skeleton; without one, you were not poor merely — you were exposed, naked in the fullest sense. The verse takes the most load-bearing word in the tribal vocabulary and reassigns it: Allah is the believers' mawlā. All the duties the word carried — defence, standing, answerability — are claimed for God, and claimed exclusively: the disbelievers have none. It is the tribe's map of reality, redrawn around one Patron.

**The Battle Cry That Illustrated the Verse**

The word was battle-tested in the sūrah's own decade. At Uḥud, when the idolaters had the upper hand, their commander Abū Sufyān called across the field: a day for a day — we have al-ʿUzzā and you have no ʿUzzā — naming the idol the goddess of victory and taunting the Muslims as patronless. The Prophet told his Companions to answer him: God is our mawlā, and you have no mawlā. The exchange is preserved in the sīrah literature and in Ibn Kathīr's commentary on the idols of the Quraysh, and it is this verse preached in shorthand — the same theology, shouted and written. Two patronage claims, one battlefield: an idol that could not hear, and a Lord whose help had already been seen at Badr a year before.

**The Emptiest Sentence in Arabic**

To an Arab ear, "they have no patron" was the emptiest sentence the language could build. Exile from every protection was the death that preceded dying. The verse lifts that social terror into the ledger of eternity and lets it stand as the disbelievers' permanent condition — no protector against Him, as the previous sūrah put the same doctrine. And the sentence carries a quiet irony the first audience could not miss: the disbelievers were not, socially speaking, patronless men. They had the finest patronage network in Arabia — clans, alliances, idols, wealth. The verse's "no patron" is not a census; it is a verdict on what all that patronage will be worth on the day it is invoked. Everything that cannot hear you is not protecting you.

**Patronage Transferred**

For the believers, the verse describes the transaction their conversion actually performed. To say "our Lord is Allah" — the sūrahs keep the confession formulaic and public — was to resign from one patronage system and enrol in another. In Makka the resignation cost: clan protection withdrawn, trade boycotted, exile to Madinah. The verse is God's receipt for the transfer: what you gave up is now what you have — the Patron Himself, the One whose patronage does not lapse when the politics do. Converts in every generation since have made the same trade, and the verse remains their certificate. It also explains the community's otherwise strange composure under loss: a people who have transferred their skeletons do not collapse when the old frame is kicked.

**An Inventory of the Patron's Services**

Read the sūrah around this verse and it becomes an itemised contract of what the Patron provides. He guides, improves, and admits the fallen into the Garden (47:5-6). He helps and makes firm the steps of those who stand up for Him (47:7). He removes the sins and repairs the condition of those who answer (47:2). This is patronage in the old full sense — defence of the person's interests in every court, this world's and the next's — and the sūrah's earlier verbs are the services listed. No human patron, however generous, ever offered a portfolio like it: the best of them could avenge your death; this One intends to reverse it. The verse's claim is not that God out-ranks the patrons; it is that He provides what they were always imitating.

**The Confidence and Its Condition**

Two cautions keep the verse from becoming a tribal licence. First, the patronage is of the believers — the ones who believe, a word the sūrah has already defined with conduct attached; it is not a birthright of the religiously labelled. Second, the sūrah has just published the conditional that governs the services: if you stand up for Allah, He will help you (47:7). The Patron is not an insurer of the idle; the protection extends to clients who act like clients. With those two cautions in place, the verse's confidence is total, and the tradition has always read it so: the believer's security is not in outcomes — he may lose the battle, the property, the life — but in the standing of his case, which is defended by the only Advocate whose cases do not end. Uḥud proved the point in the very battle that named it: the Muslims were mauled, the taunt was shouted, and the community that lost the field kept the Patron.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Explanation**

Notice how the verse begins: This is because. The destruction of the past nations, the voiding of deeds, the coming similar fate — all of it is now explained in one word of relationship. History's engine, the sūrah reveals, is not power, and not justice in the abstract, but patronage: who stands behind whom. The believers' survival and the deniers' endings are the same principle read from two sides. For a young community digesting both victory and defeat, the explanation did what doctrine exists to do — it made the news legible. The field of Uḥud was confusing; the ledger behind it was not. God is the believers' Patron, and the only protection in either world is the kind He extends.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:12

> Surely Allah will admit those who believe and do good into Gardens under which rivers flow. As for the disbelievers, they enjoy themselves and feed like cattle. But the Fire will be their home

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Diets**

The verse sorts humanity by what it eats — not by menu but by relationship to provision. The believers, doing good, are admitted to Gardens watered by rivers: provision received from a host, at a table, in a home. The disbelievers enjoy themselves and feed like cattle: provision grazed, self-served, head-down. The image is agricultural and unsparing. Cattle are not wicked; they are complete — complete at the level of intake, wanting nothing, aspiring to nothing, accountable for nothing. The verse's accusation is that a human being, gifted with an address from his Maker, can live entirely at the level of his own body and call that life. The two halves of the verse are not two punishments and rewards; they are two diets, and each is the natural destination of the appetite that chose it.

**The Cattle Comparison, Weighed Honestly**

The comparison is one the Qur'an makes more than once, and always with the same escalation. Of the heedless: "They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with. They are like cattle. In fact, they are even less guided!" (7:179). And in al-Furqān, after asking whether the masses listen or understand: "They are only like cattle—no, more than that, they are astray from the ˹Right˺ Way!" (25:44). The escalation is the argument's honesty. Cattle cannot stray; there is no right road for a cow to leave. A human being grazing his way through a lifetime is worse positioned than the animal, because his detour was chosen with equipment the animal does not own. The verse is not sneering at the poor or the simple — hunger and simplicity have no entry here. Its target is the reduction of a covenanted creature to a digestive system with plans.

**Why Cattle Are Not Blamed and Grazers Are**

The distinction deserves its own line, because it answers the common objection that the verse is merely contemptuous. Cattle feed without blame for a simple reason: they were never addressed. No revelation reached the herd; no caller stood in the pasture; no accounting awaits the herd's ledger. The human grazer was addressed — the Qur'an's word for revelation is precisely an address — and the whole difference between him and the animal is the address. To live as though one had never been called is therefore not innocence; it is the refusal of a summons that was verifiably delivered. The verse's severity is the severity reserved for missed appointments, not for ignorance. That is also why the image stings the comfortable rather than the crushed: the man with leisure, options, and information who still organises his existence around intake alone.

**Enjoyment Advanced, Once More**

The clause "they enjoy themselves" repeats a doctrine the previous sūrah stated in courtroom terms: the denier's good things are advances on a closed account. Nothing in this verse makes pleasure itself the crime, and the Qur'an has always defended lawful enjoyment against ascetic exaggeration. What is catalogued here is pleasure as a total programme — enjoyment that fills the horizon, leaves no room for the address, and is mistaken, because it feels like success, for a verdict. The believer receives the same sunshine and the same meat and is neither poorer nor sadder for it; he simply knows the table's Owner and eats as a guest. The difference between the two diets is not the food; it is whether anyone is thanked, and whether anyone is home.

**The Believer's Diet Is Also Provision**

Look carefully at the believer's half of the verse and notice what it does not say. It does not say the believers will earn Paradise; it says Allah will admit them. The verb of hospitality stands where a wage-verb might have stood, and the gardens come with rivers — running water, the desert's exact picture of wealth unstinted. Provision, in both halves of the verse, is exactly what each party wanted. The grazer wanted intake, and intake is what he is handed, all the way to the end of the verse. The believer wanted a Host and a home, and a Host and a home is what he is given. The verse's quiet horror and quiet hope are the same sentence: desire, in the long run, is destiny.

**A Saying the Commentaries Preserved**

The classical commentaries pass down a lapidary summary of the verse's anthropology: in this world the believer makes provision, the hypocrite preens, and the disbeliever revels. Three relationships to the same lifespan. The believer treats the world as travel-country — the Arabic word for provision, zād, is the traveller's pack — eating what he needs and packing what he can carry. The hypocrite treats it as a mirror, arranging its goods for the viewing of others. The disbeliever treats it as the destination itself, grazing in place. The saying's economy is its power: it locates all three postures not in creedal vocabulary but in the ordinary grammar of a day, and everyone who hears it knows, without instruction, which of the three he spent yesterday being.

**Home, at the End of Both Menus**

The verse closes with a word of terrible plainness: the Fire will be their home. Home — the same domestic word that gardens and rivers were building for the others. The ending is the diet's logic completed: a creature that treated this world as home will be given a home that matches the choice, permanent as every real home is, and fitted to what the resident loved. The verse performs no theatrical sentencing; it simply lets the two menus run to their ends. That is the Qur'an's deepest doctrine of justice — consequences as ripened desires — and it is why the verse ends without an exclamation. The tragedy needs no raising of the voice. It was written, quietly, into every head-down day.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:13

> ˹Imagine, O Prophet,˺ how many societies We destroyed that were far superior in might than your society—which drove you out—and there was none to help them

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse With a Wound in It**

Something is different here. The sūrah's history verses have spoken in the third person — those before them, the peoples of the past — and suddenly the address turns personal: your society, which drove you out. The verse names the Prophet's own injury, and in doing so reveals something about the Book's pastoral method. This is revelation pausing, mid-argument, to acknowledge that the man carrying it has a wound with an address. The expulsion from Makka was not an abstraction to him: the town of his birth, his people, his early life of standing among them — closed to him by force. God enters the detail into the argument itself. The God of the Qur'an does not counsel from above the grief; He argues from inside it.

**His Farewell to the City**

The memory the verse touches had a recorded moment. When the Prophet left Makka for the migration, he turned toward the city and said: "By Allah, you are the best land of Allah, and the most beloved of Allah's lands to Allah. Had I not been expelled from you, I would not have left" (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3925, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). Every word of that farewell belongs beside this verse. He loved the town that expelled him; he excuses his own leaving by naming its cause; he blames nothing of the valley itself. There is no curse in the sentence, no bitterness — and this from a man with a price on his head. The verse under commentary does what the farewell models: it names the injury without marinating in it, and puts the expellers on notice in the same breath.

**How Many — the Plural of Exhibits**

The verse's arithmetic is its argument: how many societies — the Arabic idiom kam min qarya glories in the uncounted plural. Not one cautionary example but a category; not a special judgment but a routine. And the members of the category are specified as far superior in might to the society being addressed. ʿĀd of the sand-hills, Egypt of the store-cities, the rock-cutters of Thamūd — the Qur'an's exhibits again, filed now under a single emphasised comparison: the expelled Prophet's expellers are the smallest military power ever to stand in this queue. The verse is mercy disguised as arithmetic. A man driven from his home by the mighty might conclude that power decides everything; the verse hands him the archives of the mighty and asks him to read their endings.

**"None to Help Them": the Sentence Inside the Sentence**

The verse's last clause is its quietest and worst: there was none to help them. Not "they lost slowly" or "they declined"; at the end, no help arrived — from the idols, from the allies, from the wealth. Every society has helpers on the payroll while the sun shines: treaty-partners, client tribes, gods with shrines and budgets. The verse reports the audit at the finish: the payroll dissolved exactly when it was invoked. That clause is addressed, with precision, to the expellers' theology. They drove out a man in the name of gods who could not keep a single one of their own worshippers standing when the wind came for ʿĀd. The history the verse cites is not generic doom; it is the specific failure of every patron except God.

**The Comfort Logic, Spelled Out**

For the driven-out community, the verse's logic ran with syllogistic neatness. Premise: societies mightier than Makka were destroyed for the rejection that Makka was now practising. Premise: Makka is not mightier than they. Conclusion: the fate cited in the previous verse — a similar fate awaits the disbelievers — is not poetry but schedule. And the Prophet's expulsion, the very wound the verse acknowledges, is the qualifying crime that enrols his city in the comparison class. The comfort is not that revenge is promised; it is that history has rules, the rules have a Judge, and the current suffering of the saints is not evidence of His absence but the occasion of His patience. The besieged community of every century has survived on exactly this arithmetic.

**Grief Given Proportion**

There is a second, softer ministry in the verse, and it deserves its own reading. By folding the personal loss into the pattern of nations, God gives the Prophet's grief a proportion. The expulsion is real, named, felt — and it is one instance of a war older than him: the powerful against the sent. He is not uniquely unlucky; he is in the finest possible company, the queue of Noah, Hūd, Ṣāliḥ, and the rest, and the queue's members are remembered while their expellers are ruins. Grief that sees itself as unique is crushing; grief that sees itself as membership is almost glory. That is the pastoral move inside the verse's warning: the wound is acknowledged, filed under the ancient pattern, and quietly reframed as a share in the most honoured lineage on earth.

**A Word for Every Driven-Out Believer**

The verse's application has never been confined to its first recipient. Believers have been driven out — from Makkas of their own in every century: homes, careers, families, countries — for no crime beyond the confession this sūrah keeps repeating. To each of them the verse offers its two-part gift: the injury is known by name, and the injury is inside a pattern with a Judge. What it refuses to offer is the poison that exile ferments — the hatred that eats the exiled before it ever reaches the expeller. The Prophet's farewell to the city remains the standing model: name the wound, love what was lovely in it, blame the expulsion and not the earth, and leave the schedule of the mighty to the One who has already read their endings.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:14

> Can those ˹believers˺ who stand on clear proof from their Lord be like those whose evil deeds are made appealing to them and ˹only˺ follow their desires

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Is Already a Verdict**

The verse's grammar does the arguing. In Arabic the question opens with the particle hal yastawī — can they be equal? — the Qur'an's standard formula for a comparison it wants laughed out of court. The device works because it makes the reader complete the syllogism himself: light and no-light, standing and stumbling — the equality is refused by the picture before the doctrine is stated. The Qur'an uses the formula across the Book for precisely its most important binaries — knowing and ignorant, seeing and blind, living and dead — and the formula's message is always the same: these two are not two opinions; they are two conditions. When a question mark carries a verdict, the reader is being asked to notice that he already knows the answer.

**Standing on Clear Proof**

The believers' condition is described architecturally: they stand — ʿalā in Arabic, upon — a clear proof from their Lord. The believer is pictured as a structure on a foundation; the proof is not in his hand as a trophy but under his feet as ground. And the proof is from their Lord — not mined by their own acumen, but sent down by the One it certifies. That is the Qur'an's consistent epistemology of faith: certainty is received before it is constructed. The believer did not ascend to the truth by introspection; revelation arrived, he tested it as this sūrah keeps inviting men to test it, and having found the ground sound, he built his life on it. Standing, in this verse, is a full-time posture: storm-tested, and still upright.

**Evil Made Beautiful**

The other party's condition is a workshop. Their evil deeds are made appealing to them — zuyyina lahum, the Qur'an's technical term for the decorating of sin. The verb is used of adornment, of a bride's preparation; the imagery is deliberate and uncomfortable. Sin, at the moment of choice, does not present itself as sin; it presents itself dressed, lit, and described in the vocabulary of the good. The Qur'an names the decorator elsewhere: "We have surely sent messengers to communities before you ˹O Prophet˺, but Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them. So he is their patron today." (16:63). The mechanism is less supernatural than it sounds — it runs on habit, appetite, and the mind's genius for rebranding — but the verse insists on the outcome: a man can live inside a gallery of his own evils, admiring them.

**Desire Enthroned**

The second half of the pair names the workshop's owner: they follow their desires. The Qur'an's sharpest single sentence on this condition is in al-Jāthiyah: "Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god?" (45:23). The formulation is exact anthropology. Whatever has the final say in a life is that life's god — and for the person of this verse, the last word always belongs to appetite: what he wants decides what he believes, whom he defends, what he calls right. This is why the verse pairs the decorated deeds with the following: the decorating is the service, the desire is the throne. And it is why the Qur'an treats desire-worship not as a minor weakness but as the primal idolatry — the shrine is internal, and it travels everywhere the man goes.

**The Road Out of the Question**

The tradition's commentators add a strand that deepens the verse's fairness. Following desire is not fixed at birth; the man whose evils are decorated is not beyond reach, because the decoration can crack. The verse itself implies the exit: those believers who stand on clear proof were once standing somewhere else, and what moved them was not the disappearance of desire but the arrival of ground under the feet. The Qur'an's most withering sentence about desire's destination — "And who could be more astray than those who follow their desires with no guidance from Allah?" (28:50) — contains its own cure: guidance from Allah, the thing this entire Book claims to be. The two conditions of the verse are therefore not castes; they are positions, and positions can be changed while the offer stands.

**The Reader Between Two Grounds**

Every reader of the verse stands somewhere, and the verse's question leaves no neutral gallery. The honest use of it is not to file one's neighbours into the second category — the reflex that ruins half of all moral teaching — but to run the audit the verse hands over: what has the final say in my life? When revelation and appetite disagree — on money, on desire, on pride, on an apology owed — which one blinks first? The believer's answer, on his worst days, embarrasses him; and the verse, honestly used, is designed for exactly those days: not to classify the other man, but to show the reader where his own feet actually are, while there is still floor left to move on.

**Why the Comparison Stands at the Sūrah's Centre**

Placed at the chapter's hinge, the verse gathers everything before it into two portraits. All the sūrah's verbs — believing, doing good, standing firm, following the truth from one's Lord — assemble the first figure. All its other verbs — detesting, grazing, decorating, following desire — assemble the second. The battle verse, the rivers, the ruins, the patronage — these are the biographies and destinations of the two figures, and the verse simply sets the portraits side by side and asks the question no honest eye has ever answered yes to. What remains, with the two figures standing in view, is the only decision a reader of this sūrah is ever asked to make: which portrait is his, and which one he will, starting now, sit for.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:15

> The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey. There they will ˹also˺ have all kinds of fruit, and forgiveness from their Lord. ˹Can they be˺ like those who will stay in the Fire forever, left to drink boiling water that will tear apart their insides

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Portrait Built to Be Compared**

The verse announces itself as a description — mathal, a likeness — and then paints with a deliberately heavy brush: four rivers, every fruit, and a forgiveness, set against a fire whose drink disembowels. The structure is the sūrah's recurring question put into imagery: can these be alike? The Garden half is lavish on purpose. This is not decoration for its own sake; it is an argument in produce. To a desert audience, the inventory is the exact inversion of their daily lack — water that does not fail, milk that does not sour, wine that does not harm, honey needing no hives, fruit without season. The message inside the menu is the message of the whole sūrah: what God gives is what the world only imitated.

**Four Rivers, Each One Repairing a Broken Copy**

Read the four rivers as a series and a pattern appears: each is this world's version of itself, with the defect removed. Water, everywhere prone to stagnation and taint, flows incorruptible. Milk, the most perishable of drinks, never changes its taste. Wine — forbidden in this life precisely for the harm and hostility it breeds — returns as the pleasure without the poison, for elsewhere the Qur'an sets the specification: a drink "that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication" (56:19). Honey arrives already pure, unstung by the filtering earth demands. The doctrine is quietly enormous: the next life is not a different kind of thing from this one; it is this one, matured — every legitimate pleasure of earth restored at full fidelity, minus the entropy.

**The Menu of the Night Journey**

Tradition preserves a scene that reads like this verse enacted. On the night of the Isrāʾ, the Prophet was presented at Jerusalem with two cups — one of wine, one of milk — and chose the milk. Gabriel told him: Praise be to Allah, Who guided you to the fiṭrah, the sound nature; had you taken the wine, your nation would have gone astray (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5576). The choice is the verse in miniature. Milk is the primordial drink — the first food of every human life — and the fiṭrah is the primordial constitution; the Prophet's instinct mapped the created order correctly. In the Garden, the tradition adds, the two drinks are reconciled: the milk that never sours and the wine that never harms, each river running past the same orchards — the repaired world, where nature and celebration are no longer rivals.

**Forgiveness Listed With the Fruit**

Then, at the inventory's summit, the catalogue changes register: and forgiveness from their Lord. The placement is the verse's deepest teaching. Rivers, fruit, wine, honey — every category of delight is named, and then the list culminates in a pardon. The arrangement answers the question every sophisticated reader asks of Paradise: is it not merely pleasure for pleasure's sake? The answer is in the order: the pleasures are real, but they are not the point. What the righteous were promised, at the bottom, was the restoration of the relationship — the Lord's own favour — and the rivers are simply the garden around the reconciliation. A homecoming feast laid by the offended party, at which the offence is formally retired: that is the verse's final item, listed last because it is the gift the rest of the table serves.

**The Other Table**

The verse's second portrait is drawn with equal economy: the Fire's residents, staying forever, drinking water that tears the insides apart. The Qur'an's images of that drink range wide — elsewhere, water "like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces" (18:29) — but the emphasis here is internal: the tearing apart of the insides. Intake, the body's most basic act of reception, becomes the organ of the punishment; what the damned drink destroys the drinker from within. There is an awful symmetry with the first half of the verse. The grazer of the earlier portrait lived by intake alone; his eternity is all intake, and nothing else arrives. The menu, in both houses, is the desire, served forever.

**Can They Be Like?**

The comparison returns — the same formula the sūrah has already used — and by now the reader hardly needs the question mark. Standing on proof against decorated evil; gardens against fire; forgiveness against the boiled drink: the two portraits have been built verse by verse since the sūrah opened, and the verse simply sets the completed canvases side by side. The Qur'an's method throughout has been to refuse abstraction — it never argues "outcomes differ" in the abstract; it paints the two outcomes until the difference is tasteable. That is why the verse spends its length on rivers and pain rather than on theory. The reader is not asked to compute the two conditions; he is asked to look at them — and then to notice which table he is currently seated at, while the seating can still be changed.

**Why Rivers, for This Audience**

One last look at the imagery's address. The first audience measured wealth in water: a spring was an estate, a running stream a fairy tale. For them, "gardens under which rivers flow" was not a generic paradise but the specific reversal of their landscape and their politics — the poor of Quraysh, mocked for their lack of patronage, promised an estate whose water never needed a treaty. The verse's imagery remains honest for every later reader, though his scarcity shifts: where wealth is time, the rivers run as rest; where it is love, as welcome; where it is safety, as permanence. The defects differ by century; the repair is the same. That is the mark of the verse's origin in the One who built every audience's lack: the description is ancient, and the thirst it answers has not aged a day.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:16

> There are some of them who listen to you ˹O Prophet˺, but when they depart from you, they say ˹mockingly˺ to those ˹believers˺ gifted with knowledge, “What did he just say?” These are the ones whose hearts Allah has sealed and who ˹only˺ follow their desires

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Scene Every Reader Can Stage**

The verse sketches a scene in three beats, and it is so exact that it can be staged in any century. A man attends the recitation — present, visible, counted in the assembly. He leaves. Away from the Prophet's presence, among the believers given knowledge, he asks his question with a curled lip: what did he just say? The scene's anatomy is the verse's lesson. Attendance is not attention; proximity is not reception; and the exit is where the truth about the session comes out. What a person does with the message after leaving its presence is the only evidence of what the message did to him.

**The Cruelty Engineered Into the Question**

The question deserves to be heard as it was asked. What did he just say? is not inquiry; it is demolition in three moves. It brands the recitation as noise — unintelligible, not worth tracking. It brands the attentive listeners as dupes — men who claimed to understand what no one could. And it flatters the asker as the only sane man in the room. A single sentence, addressed to the knowledgeable, mocks the speech, the speaker, and the students at once. The Qur'an's habit of quoting such lines verbatim is its own kind of answer: the mockery is preserved, and the preservation is the verdict — the words that were said in private are now recited, as exhibits, by every believer on earth, fourteen centuries after their author turned to dust.

**Hearts Sealed — the Anatomy of the Condition**

The verse then names the asker's condition with the Qur'an's most sobering image: hearts sealed. The sealing is not described as an arbitrary act that preceded the listening; the Qur'an's wider doctrine runs the other way — God's seal follows the self-sealing. Of another group of the same tribe the Book says: "There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears." (6:25). Pretended listening, veiled heart, deafened ear: the order is instructive. The performance of hearing is exactly what the hearing cannot survive; a heart in the room for politics must keep politics out of the recitation, and keeping politics in the ears is, over time, how an ear dies.

**Desire as the Remaining Compass**

The verse's closing clause completes the diagnosis: they follow their desires. With the heart sealed, navigation defaults to appetite — and the sūrah has already shown the reader what appetite-navigation looks like: decorated evil, grazing, the dismissal of whatever invoices the soul. The connection between inattentive listening and unruly desire is tighter than it first appears. Desire is a lobbyist; revelation is the law it must defeat. The man who will not let the Book reach his heart is not neutral — he is protecting a client. That is why the sealed hearts in this sūrah are never described as empty. They are occupied — full of the thing the sealing was purchased to protect, and run by it.

**The Four Obstacles, as al-Ghazzālī Counted Them**

The great jurist-mystic al-Ghazzālī, in his Revival of the Religious Sciences, turned this verse's problem into a checklist of what blocks understanding for listeners who are not hypocrites at all. First, reducing recitation to pronunciation — perfecting the letters while the meaning starves. Second, partisan attachment to a school or habit of interpretation, so the verse is heard only as ammunition. Third, persistence in sin and the pride of position, which harden the surface the meaning would land on. Fourth, deciding in advance that the Book holds only what earlier commentators transmitted, and stopping there — closing the door the recitation was opening. His list is uncomfortable precisely because it addresses the devout: the verse's hypocrite is the extreme case, but the obstacles are available to everyone.

**A Mirror Held Up to Believers**

The tradition has always read this verse with two audiences in view, and the second is the believing reader. Hearing without understanding is a believer's occupational hazard: the recitation that becomes background music, the prayer-speed reading of the Book, the lifetime of attendance that never once asks what the passage actually means or demands. The verse's mocked question — what did he say? — is, asked honestly, the most religious question in the world; asked dishonestly, it is the mocker's signature. The difference between the two askers is not the words but the heart's posture, and the verse stands as a permanent audit: after the session, after the recitation, after the page is closed — what did he say, and what did I do with it?

**The Question That Saves**

End where the verse's honest twin leads. The believers gifted with knowledge — the verse's own respectful phrase for the Companions who tracked the meaning — were not born tracking it; they asked, repeatedly and submissively, what the revelation meant, and the Prophet answered, and their questions became the seedbed of the entire commentary tradition. The Qur'an honours the question asked to learn and quotes the question asked to mock, and it keeps both in the Book so the reader can choose his company. Two men asked the same words in the same century; one of them left the assembly insured against nothing, and the other became a scholar the ummah prays behind. The words were never the difference. The heart was, and the verse was written so that hearts could still be moved.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:17

> As for those who are ˹rightly˺ guided, He increases them in guidance and blesses them with righteousness

**Expanded Commentary**

**Guidance as a Growing Asset**

The verse's first surprise is its verb: increase. Guidance, in much religious speech, is a status — one has it or lacks it, like a membership. The Qur'an treats it as capital — something one holds in quantities that grow or shrink. Those who are rightly guided receive, of the same gift they already have, more of it. The Qur'an states the principle as a universal law: "And Allah increases in guidance those who are ˹rightly˺ guided. And the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in outcome." (19:76). The doctrine changes the believer's self-understanding at the root: he is not standing on a line he crossed once, but walking a road whose surface extends under his feet as he walks it. Standing still, on that road, is not neutral; it is how the surface retracts.

**Two Trajectories From One Recitation**

Placed immediately after the verse of the sealed hearts, this verse completes the diagram. The same recitation, the same session, the same words in the air — and two exits: the mockers walk out with less than they came with, and the guided walk out with more. Nothing in the message differed; everything in the reception did. The Qur'an has a whole theology of this split, and states it in the verse the Muslims used to recite over every new revelation: "Whenever a sûrah is revealed, some of them ask ˹mockingly˺, 'Which of you has this increased in faith?' As for the believers, it has increased them in faith and they rejoice." (9:124). Revelation, sūrah by sūrah, was the sorting instrument of Madinah — increasing those who received it as news from their Lord, and exposing those who had been attending for other reasons. The mechanism has not retired; every honest congregation is still being sorted by the same Book.

**Righteousness Granted, Not Just Commanded**

The verse's second clause carries a quieter doctrine: God blesses them with righteousness — with taqwā, the mindful, protective reverence that the Qur'an everywhere commands. The commandments of taqwā, read alone, could suggest a climb the believer makes unaided; this verse returns the ladder to its Owner. The commentators read the clause both ways and hold both: God grants them the doing of taqwā — enabling, strengthening, making the reverent life possible — and God grants them its reward, the outcome of the reverence they were enabled to live. The two readings are one doctrine from two sides: in the Qur'an's economy, even the currency of obedience is supplied by the One who asks for it. The believer who finds, years in, that reverence has become second nature is not discovering his own achievement; he is receiving instalments of this verse.

**The Law of Use and Its Dark Twin**

Step back and the verse states a law that runs through the Qur'an's whole psychology of the heart: capacities respond to use. Guidance used is guidance increased; hearing refused is hearing withdrawn, until the ear the mocker protected becomes the ear God seals. The law is the same in both directions, and the Qur'an treats it as natural law rather than special punishment — the way muscle grows under load and withers in a cast. This is why the verse's parties cannot blame each other, or God, for their positions: every increment in both trajectories was self-participated. The guided man's advantage began as a small advantage and compounded; the sealed heart began as an afternoon of pretended listening and compounded too. Compound interest is the least remarked, most real, and most terrible doctrine in the Book.

**What Increase Actually Feels Like**

For the reader who imagines increase as visions or sweetness, the ground truth is humbler and better. Guidance increased is mostly noticing: the verse read for the twentieth time opening; the sin that used to be effortless becoming heavy; the prayer that used to be a task becoming a place; the decision that would once have been automatic now arriving with a question attached. The believers of Madinah described their increases in exactly such terms — faith that got heavier, not lighter, in the hand. The promise of this verse is therefore not for the spiritually spectacular; it is for the persistent. The one who keeps reading, keeps attending, keeps asking what the passage means and then does it, is enrolled in a programme whose gains are quiet, cumulative, and guaranteed by the verse itself — the increase is God's clause, not the believer's performance.

**A Word to the Person With Little**

Read devotionally, the verse is one of the Qur'an's kindest. Nobody's share of guidance is fixed; the one who has a little is holding the very asset this verse compounds. The beginner's awkward prayer, the new Muslim's stammering recitation, the believer returning after years away — each is standing at the head of a growth curve that this verse promises, and the size of the starting balance is irrelevant to the promise. What disqualifies is not smallness but stasis — the sealed ear, the protected appetite, the attendance without reception. The verse thus pairs with the previous one as offer beside warning: the same Book that hardens the refusing heart grows the asking one, and the asking heart, however small its portfolio, has God Himself as its investment manager.

**The Verse's Place in the Sūrah's Argument**

In the sūrah's architecture, this verse completes the sorting that began with the two portraits of guidance and desire. From here the chapter will move to the Hour, to the command of knowledge and forgiveness, to the wished-for sūrah with fighting in it — and in each episode the two trajectories will be visible again: hearts that increase and hearts that sicken, at the same revelation, in the same city. The verse is the chapter's hinge quietly stated: God is increasing some and letting others decay, and the reader, still free to choose his trajectory, is being shown the mechanics of both. Increase is real, decay is real, and the door between them — this sūrah's whole argument — is still standing open while the reader reads.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:18

> Are they only waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise? Yet ˹some of˺ its signs have already come. Once it actually befalls them, will it not be too late to be mindful

**Expanded Commentary**

**Waiting as a Strategy**

The verse opens on a posture and exposes it: are they only waiting? The people in view are not arguing anymore; the sūrah has moved past their arguments. They are simply deferring — treating the Hour as a distant theoretical event that no present behaviour needs to reckon with. Deferral is the most common religious posture on earth, and the verse names its hidden logic: the waiter is not a skeptic but a gambler, betting that the reckoning is far enough away that today can be spent without accounting. The verse's question dismantles the bet in one stroke — what exactly are you waiting for, and what will waiting have purchased when it arrives?

**The Signs Already Delivered**

The wager's fatal flaw is the verse's middle clause: some of the Hour's signs have already come. The tradition reads the clause chiefly of the Prophet's own sending, and he himself supplied the scale of the nearness. Anas reported: "The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, 'I and the Hour have been sent like these two' — and he held up his index and middle fingers together" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6139; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2951). Two fingers, held together: the entire gap between the last Messenger and the end of history — no new prophet, no new dispensation, one joined span of time. Elsewhere the Qur'an voices the same nearness in a single line of prophecy: "The approaching ˹Hour˺ has drawn near." (53:57). For an audience that could date the signs' arrival to a living man in their own city, waiting was not caution; it was ignoring the arrival that had already happened.

**Mindfulness With an Expiry Date**

The verse's closing question is its severest clause: once it befalls them, will it not be too late to be mindful? Faith, the Qur'an teaches again and again, is only faith while the matter is unseen; the Day itself will convert every denier into a believer, and that conversion will count for nothing. The transformation of the heart under direct sight is not moral change but visual acknowledgement — the difference between trusting a bridge and standing in the ravine under it. The verse therefore assigns mindfulness a window, and the window is the present life. That is why the Qur'an's invitations always carry the urgency they carry: not because God is impatient, but because the door being open is itself the doctrine, and doors on a schedule are not the same as doors without one.

**The Suddenness the Qur'an Keeps Insisting On**

The phrase "take them by surprise" belongs to a family of images the Qur'an uses for the Hour's arrival — sudden, unannounced, arriving in the middle of ordinary transactions. Elsewhere the Book asks whether they await the Hour coming upon them suddenly while they do not perceive (43:66), and pictures the moment with the markets still full and the cups still wet. The insistence is pastoral as much as forensic. Human beings plan around announced events and improvise around surprises; the Qur'an wants its readers to convert the surprise into an announced event — which is exactly what reciting its verses does. The believer who knows the appointment cannot be ambushed by it; the one who waits for it to ambush him has chosen the ambush.

**The Irony of the Waiting Itself**

There is an irony folded into the scene the verse implies. The same people waiting for the Hour have a Caller standing in their market, a Book being recited in their city, signs dated within the decade — and they are waiting. Waiting, in this verse, turns out to be a way of not-hearing what has already arrived. The modern form of the posture is familiar: the deferral of religion to a convenient season, the illness, the old age, the someday after the career — each a wager that the Hour's schedule accommodates the waiter's plans. The verse declines to condemn the posture with rhetoric; it simply states the two facts that dismantle it — the signs are here, and mindfulness expires — and lets the waiter do the arithmetic himself.

**What Nearness Does to a Life Today**

For the believer, the same nearness that alarms the waiter is the architecture of his discipline. A joined-finger span from the end of history is also a joined-finger span from his own exit from it, for no one is promised the full pageant. The tradition distilled the verse's practical teaching into the Prophet's counsel to live as a traveller — provisions light, accounts short, the long plans held loosely. This is not morbidity; it is proportion. The person who knows the meeting is soon treats the interval differently: the grudge gets settled faster, the prayer stops being postponable, the amends get made in daylight. The Hour, for the mindful, functions the way a deadline functions for the serious — it converts intention into schedule.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Clock**

In the sūrah's flow, the verse installs a clock. The sorting has been described — guided and sealed, increasing and decaying — and now the time in which the sorting happens is bounded: the Hour exists, its signs have begun, and the window of mindfulness is exactly as long as the reader's own share of the interval. The next verse will state, with startling directness, what the reader should be doing with the interval: know that there is no god worthy of worship except Allah, and seek forgiveness. The sūrah's clock, in other words, is not ticking toward mere catastrophe; it is ticking toward the one appointment for which preparation has been named, commanded, and kept on offer while the fingers are still joined.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:19

> So, know ˹well, O Prophet,˺ that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah. And seek forgiveness for your shortcomings and for ˹the sins of˺ the believing men and women. For Allah ˹fully˺ knows your movements and places of rest ˹O people˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Command Whose Verb Is "Know"**

Between a sūrah of battles and betrayals, this verse plants the quietest and largest command in the Book: know. Not fight, not fear, not strive — know that there is no god worthy of worship except Allah. The verb is cognitive before it is anything else, and the choice is doctrine. Islam's foundation is not a mood of devotion, a heritage of belonging, or a chant repeated without content; it is a piece of knowledge, held with the same certainty one holds the existence of the sun. The scholars drew the consequence the verse implies: the first obligation of every responsible person is to know this truth — and everything else in the religion, prayer and charity included, is built on the knowing, and collapses with its collapse.

**The Formula That Carries Everything**

The sentence the verse commands to be known is the confession itself: lā ilāha illā llāh — no deity deserving worship except Allah. Its structure is a negation followed by an affirmation, and the order is its power: first the world's crowded counter is cleared, then the one true Object stands alone. The tradition has always measured the formula's weight by a saying the Prophet uttered about its minimum: whoever dies knowing that there is no god except Allah enters Paradise (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 26). Knowing — not merely reciting; the hadith's verb is the verse's verb. A sentence that can carry a soul across the boundary of eternity when its last breath carries it is not a slogan; it is the keystone, and this verse commands the masonry of the whole life to hang from it.

**A Prophet Commanded to Seek Forgiveness**

The verse's second command has scandalised opponents and steadied believers for fourteen centuries: the Prophet himself is told to seek forgiveness for his shortcomings. For his accusers, it was ammunition — see, he admits fault. The sūrah's own logic reads it the opposite way. A messenger who models repentance makes the religion livable for the repentant; a sinless-seeming elite would produce either despair or pretence in everyone beneath it. The Prophet's asking pardon, and the community's imitating him, established the pattern the Qur'an praises: certainty of truth combined with consciousness of shortcoming, held together without contradiction. In this, as in everything else, he was uswa — an example to be walked after, not a statue to be gazed at.

**Prayer for the Believing Men and Women**

The forgiveness he is commanded to seek is not his alone: for his own shortcomings and for those of the believing men and women. The detail is a whole ecclesiology in a clause. The leader prays for his people by name and gender; the community's sins are carried upward by someone; and the ummah's membership is written here in the widest possible terms — men and women, side by side, equally the objects of the Prophet's intercession, equally in need of it. Later generations would build institutions on this foundation: the imam's prayer in the Friday assembly, the parent's prayer at the child's bedside, the believer's private mention of others by name. The verse is the origin-point of a culture in which being prayed for is a right of membership, not a favour.

**Movements and Places of Rest**

The verse closes with an omniscience stated in the most ordinary terms: God fully knows your movements and your places of rest. The commentators heard both a daily and a cosmic register in the phrase. Daily: the waking hours and the sleeping ones — the day of activity, the night of rest, nothing in either hidden. Cosmic: the movement through this life and the resting in the grave — the two stages of every soul's journey. Both readings teach the same thing from different ends. For the believer, it is comfort: no act of obedience, however unobserved, is unrecorded. For the heedless, it is the audit's small print: the secret counsel, the hidden deed, the interior motive — all movement, all rest, all known.

**Knowledge and Grace in a Single Verse**

Hold the verse whole and its balance is the balance of the religion. On one side: know — the demand for certainty, the clearing of the mind, the foundation laid as fact. On the other: seek forgiveness — the admission of shortcoming, the standing need for pardon, even at the religion's summit. Knowledge without the second becomes arrogance — a mind full of truth and a heart full of itself. Pardon-seeking without the first becomes anxiety — a soul begging without a foundation to beg from. The verse welds them: certainty beneath, humility above, and the believer's interior life is the weld. This is why the tradition placed this verse among the most recited of the Qur'an — it is the whole of religion compressed to two commands and a closing fact.

**Why the Verse Stands Where It Stands**

The placement completes the argument. The sūrah has sorted hearts, unmasked mockers, shown guidance increasing, and set the Hour's clock ticking; and the question such a chapter leaves standing is: very well — what, concretely, should a person do before the ambush arrives? The verse answers in order of priority: know the truth, and seek pardon — for yourself and everyone who shares the truth with you. Nothing about battle, nothing about politics, nothing about the hypocrites and their games; at the edge of the chapter's storm, the instruction is the one that fits every reader in every century. The war verses govern wars; this verse governs the hours between them, and the last hour most of all.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:20

> And the believers say, “If only a sûrah was revealed ˹allowing self-defence˺!” Yet when a precise sûrah is revealed, in which fighting is ˹explicitly˺ mentioned, you see those with sickness in their hearts staring at you like someone in the throes of death. It would have been better for them

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Wish of the Oppressed**

The verse preserves a prayer the believers used to make in Makka: if only a sûrah allowing selfdefence would come down. Behind the wish lie the years the Qur'an itself remembers — the boycott that starved a clan, the tortures, the migration that emptied the town of its weakest. The believers were not warmongers; they were the beaten asking their Lord for permission to swing back. The Qur'an records the exact shape of their earlier complaint in the verse of al-Nisāʾ: "Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who had been told, 'Do not fight! Rather, establish prayer and pay alms-tax.'? Then once the order came to fight, a group of them feared those ˹hostile˺ people as Allah should be feared—or even more." (4:77). One group wished; a group within it, when the wish was granted, wilted. The verse under commentary is the story of that wish's granting.

**Granted, and Instantly a Test**

The wish comes true, and the verse's plot turns on the mechanism: a precise sūrah is revealed — muḥkama, firmly established, unambiguous — and fighting is named in it explicitly. No metaphor, no esoteric reading, no retreat into interpretation. The very precision the believers asked for becomes the instrument that separates them: a clear command is the only kind of command that can be clearly disobeyed. The episode teaches the Qur'an's recurring lesson about prayer itself: God's answers expose as much as they give. A person asks for strength until the weights arrive, for provision until the job demands the hours, for guidance until it points somewhere expensive. The sūrah descended; the room divided; the wish was audited.

**Sickness With Symptoms**

The verse's clinical eye is its artistry. Those with sickness in their hearts — the Qur'an's standing diagnosis of hidden unbelief — are identified not by a creedal questionnaire but by a symptom: they stare at you, the Prophet, like a man in the throes of death. The look is the tell. A believer receiving a hard command may feel fear — the Qur'an is unembarrassed by fear — but his face is a man's face receiving marching orders. The sick hearts wear the face of a dying man, because for them the command is not an order but a sentence: their private reservations, their bargains with comfort, their real loyalties — all of it surfacing in the eyes. The verse turns the physiology of dread into evidence of the heart's condition, and it does so without a single word of interrogation.

**Revelation's Staged Method**

Behind the scene stands a method worth naming. Permission came in stages — first patience, then the defensive licence this sūrah sharpened — and the stages were not tactical adjustments but pedagogy. A community that had never fought under a revealed law was being taught what fighting under one meant: that it is bounded, purposeful, and answerable to God rather than to appetite. Had the command come at the beginning, the community would have dissolved between grief and war; coming as it did, after years of teaching, it found men ready — most of them. The verse's sober contribution to the doctrine of gradualism is its honesty about the residue: even a perfectly staged revelation cannot manufacture courage in a heart that has spent its years protecting its comfort. Stages train the willing; they only delay the exposure of the rest.

**The Sentence That Hangs**

The verse ends with its verdict half-spoken — It would have been better for them — and the sentence physically breaks off, to be completed at the head of the next. The suspension is not scribal accident; the Qur'an deploys the broken sentence the way a speaker trails into silence at the worst part. The reader leans forward into the gap, and the gap itself is the punishment's prologue: better for them — the reader must wait, as the sick hearts must wait, for the rest of the charge. What fills the gap, when it comes, is not torture imagery but a path not taken: obedience and honest speech. The dread was always optional; the better way was always on the table; and the verse's hanging clause lets the reader feel, before reading on, that the ending of the sentence could still, for him, be different.

**Wishing for Tests: A Permanent Human Problem**

The verse's most transferable lesson is about wishes themselves. The believers who asked for the sūrah of permission were sincere — and sincerity did not spare them the test the answer carried. Every generation of the devout repeats the pattern: we ask God for growth, for purpose, for the chance to matter — and the answers arrive looking like complications, demands, and costs. The spiritually mature learn to read their own prayers before making them; the community around the Prophet learned, through this very sūrah, that a granted request is the beginning of labour, not the end of it. The wish was right, the command was right, and the men who wilted were wrong — and all three facts are preserved together so that no later reader could romanticise either the wishing or the wilting.

**What the Verse Refuses to Say**

Notice, finally, what the verse does not say. It does not name the sick-hearted men, and the Prophet never hunted them out on the evidence of their stares. The diagnosis is God's, recorded in His Book; the community's business was to march with whoever marched. That reserve — God exposing hearts in His own Book, the community judging only conduct — became a constitutional principle for the polity the sūrah was building. Suspicion of interiors belongs to God; governance of actions belongs to law; and the believer reading this verse today is being trained in exactly that discipline, even as he is being asked the private question the verse was written to ask: which face would mine have worn, on the morning the precise sūrah came down?

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:21

> to obey and speak rightly. Then when fighting was ordained, it surely would have been better for them if they were true to Allah

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sentence Completed**

The previous verse broke off at its verdict — it would have been better for them — and this verse supplies the missing term: to obey and to speak rightly. The completion is the diagnosis in miniature. Two remedies for the sickness of the heart, and neither is mysterious: obedience, the alignment of action with command; and upright speech, the alignment of words with truth. Everything the sūrah has shown of the hypocrites — the wished-for sūrah, the death-stare, the later verses' secret bargains — is the negative image of these two virtues. The sick heart disobeys and talks around its disobedience; the healed heart does what it was told and says what it means. The gap between the verses, where the reader held his breath, turns out to contain the whole of moral life.

**Why Speech Gets Equal Billing**

It startles, at first, that speech stands beside obedience in the remedy — as though talk were half of religion. But the sūrah's own material explains the priority. Hypocrisy, as this chapter draws it, is not primarily a failure to act; it is a success at talking — professions made, questions asked mockingly, secret assurances given to the enemy. Before hypocrisy can misbehave, it must speak. The Qur'an therefore aims its remedy at the crime scene: the tongue, the most mobile limb and the hardest to police. Elsewhere the Book gathers the command into one line, telling believers to be mindful of God and to speak with upright, direct words (33:70) — the same pair as here, obedience in the fear of God, and speech that goes straight. A community whose speech runs straight is largely inoculated; the sūrah is writing the vaccine into its own exposed cells.

**"When Fighting Was Ordained"**

The verse's second sentence uses the register of decree: when fighting was ordained — kubita in the Arabic, the same verb the Qur'an uses for fasting, written upon you. The vocabulary does several things at once. It marks the moment as legal institution rather than ad hoc permission — fighting entered the law of the community, with rules, boundaries, and accountability. It echoes the language of duty — an obligation laid down, not an option exercised. And it dates the exposure: the ordinance was the X-ray, and the verse reports the film. The men who had wished for the sūrah were now standing inside the thing they had wished for, and the decree's formality left nowhere to hide — a written duty cannot be mistaken for a passing mood.

**True to Allah**

The closing condition names the quality at stake: if they were true to Allah — ṣadaqū llāh, truthfulness toward God. Truthfulness, ṣidq, is the Qur'an's word for the alignment of the inner person with his professions; its opposite, as the tradition counts the diseases of the heart, is the very parent of hypocrisy. The phrase implies a covenant picture: a person has, in professing faith, made claims before God, and truthfulness is the keeping of those claims. The men of the death-stare had made the claims and then, when the claims were called in, declined to honour them. The verse's closing logic is thus an audit language: better for them — if they had simply been what they said they were. The standard is not heroism, not even courage as a virtue; it is honesty, and the standard's very modesty is what makes the failure so damning.

**Better for Them: the Sūrah's Economics**

Twice now the chapter has used the phrase better for them — would have been better, it surely would have been better — and the repetition sketches an economic theology. God's commands are not presented as arbitrary taxes on human nature; they are presented as the terms on which benefit actually flows. Obedience and truth are better — that is, they pay — for the one who does them; the disobedience that protected comfort is the transaction that lost. The Qur'an argues this way throughout, pricing the alternatives side by side and letting the reader choose his own best interest with his eyes open. A legal system threatens; this Book quotes exchange rates. The believer who internalises the sūrah's arithmetic stops experiencing commands as costs and starts reading them as the inside information they were always claimed to be.

**The Hypocrite's Bargain, Priced at Both Ends**

Set the verse's two moments side by side and the bargain's stupidity becomes visible. Before the ordinance: professions of faith, community membership, safety, the convenience of belonging to a rising power, all purchased at the cost of occasional pretending. After the ordinance: the pretending suddenly had a price in blood, and the bargain's buyers wanted out. The verse prices the exit too — a life of disobedience and double-talk, an eternity of deeds voided — and the ledger's totals are published. This is the verse's lasting service to believers who will never see a battlefield: every compromise of convenience contains, somewhere down the schedule, an hour when the cost of the compromise comes due, and the person who made it will be found staring like a man in the throes of death, at the exact thing he was told would come.

**Speech Still Leads the List**

A closing return to the verse's first word of remedy: to obey and speak rightly. Order matters in the Qur'an's phrasing, and the tradition noticed that upright speech stands where one might expect a list of deeds. The community being formed here was to be a community of the word — recitation, testimony, teaching, proclamation — and in such a community, crooked speech is not a minor etiquette failure but a structural defect. Every institution of this religion — its law, its transmission, its scholarship — lives or dies on whether words mean what they say. The verse, by making truthful speech half of the remedy for a sick heart, built the vaccination into the foundations, and every believer who has ever corrected his own tongue mid-sentence has felt the medicine work.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:22

> Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Political Hypothesis the Verse Floats**

The verse poses its threat as a hypothesis: if you turn away — perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever the ties of kinship. The addressees are the hypocrites the sūrah has just exposed, and the hypothesis imagines their program should they ever gain the upper hand. The classical reading heard in the turning away the full weight of apostasy-plus-ambition: abandon obedience once the fighting costs too much, take power or side with its holders, and govern accordingly. The verse declines to assert that they will do this; it says perhaps — and the perhaps is more frightening than an accusation, because it makes the addressees confront a projection of their own trajectory. Sin, given command of a country, does not stay private; the verse sketches what the sick heart looks like with a treasury and an army.

**Corruption: the Qur'an's Word for Devastation**

The first projected crime is fasād — corruption, the word the Qur'an uses for the ruination of a whole ecosystem, moral and material at once. The Book's most sweeping statement of it pictures corruption appearing on land and sea because of what human hands have earned (30:41) — greed, exploitation, and injustice written into the physical world like poison into a watershed. Applied to the hypocrites, the projection is precise: these men, whose religion was convenience, would govern for convenience, and a state governed for the convenience of its governors is the definition of fasād — courts for sale, taxes as plunder, patronage for the loyal, ruin for the rest. The verse's warning is thus political science before it is theology: certain hearts, given power, produce certain countries. The corruption of the province begins in the corruption of the person.

**Why Kinship-Severing Stands Beside Corruption**

The second projected crime seems smaller beside the first — severed family ties, next to a corrupted land? — until the Qur'an's moral geography is remembered. In the world this Book addressed, kinship was the infrastructure: the network that fed orphans, funded ransoms, settled disputes, and held travellers. To sever the womb-ties — qaṭʿ al-arḥām — was to vandalise the social equivalent of roads and aqueducts. And the hypocrites had form: alliance was, for them, a market, and kinship a claim to be priced. A man who will bargain his blood-brother away for advantage will bargain anything, and the verse names the smallest betrayal as the proof of the largest program. Corruption in the land and severed tables at home are not two crimes; they are one appetite operating at two scales.

**The Womb That Seized the Merciful**

The Qur'an gives kinship a founding covenant, and the Prophet narrated it in words the tradition never forgot. He said: Allah created the creation, and when He finished, the womb — the bond of kinship itself — arose and seized hold of the Most Merciful, saying: at this place I seek refuge with You from all who sever me. And Allah answered: Yes — will you not be pleased that I keep good relations with the one who keeps good relations with you, and sever relations with the one who severs you? The womb said: Yes, O my Lord. And Allah said: Then that is for you (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4830). The report ends with the Prophet himself directing his Companions to this very passage of Sūrat Muḥammad as the verse to recite for it. The teaching is staggering: the social bond of kinship is underwritten by a divine covenant — those who honour it are allied with God, and those who cut it are cut, by name, from the Merciful.

**The Horizontal Covenant of the Single Soul**

The Qur'an had already grounded kinship in theology, at the opening of the women's sūrah: "O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties." (4:1). The sequence is the doctrine: one origin, therefore one family; and God's Name invoked as the seal on the appeals humans make to one another through their kin. The hypocrites' program reverses the sequence — treating people as instruments and the Name as a tool — and the verse's warning stands as the eternal veto on every politics of instrumentality. The believer's map of society is drawn from a single soul; the corrupter's map is drawn from what he can use.

**The Brotherhood That Replaces What Was Cut**

For those the hypocrites would sever from, the Qur'an builds a new weave. The believers are one brotherhood — "The believers are but one brotherhood, so make peace between your brothers. And be mindful of Allah so you may be shown mercy." (49:10) — and the sūrah's community, soon to absorb the hypocrites' defection and the war's losses, would need that weave. The verse's warning and this promise belong together: what the ambitious cut, God re-knits in a different fabric — not the blood-tie alone, but the tie of faith, which adopts the orphan, ransoms the captive, and inherits the believer who has no kin. Islam neither abolished the family nor absolutised it; it subordinated it to a covenant that includes every believing stranger as a brother, and it cursed only the cutting.

**A Reading for Every Person With Influence**

The verse's address travels well, because everyone holds some small province — a company, a parish, a family, a platform. The hypothesis it floats is permanent: what would this heart do with real power? The question is not hypothetical for God, who reads the trajectory already, and the verse lets the reader audit his own before the promotion arrives. The tell is in the small severs: the relative dropped because the relationship stopped paying, the truth bent because a client was watching. Corruption in the land is never sudden; it is the family tie priced and then cut, multiplied by every hand that ever held anything. The verse warns from inside the smallest decision, and its refuge is the same at every scale — the God who answered the womb, and Who does not change His answer.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:23

> These are the ones who Allah has condemned, deafening them and blinding their eyes

**Expanded Commentary**

**Condemned, and Then Senseless**

The verse carries two acts in sequence: God has condemned them — the Arabic laʿana, the solemn curse of exclusion — and then, as the outworking of the condemnation, deafening them and blinding their eyes (47:23). The order is doctrine. The curse is not a lightning strike of pain; it is a verdict of distance, removal from the circle of mercy's influence, and what follows it is deprivation. The punished senses of this verse are not seared; they are switched off. Hell's loudest punishments in the Qur'an are often of exactly this kind — not added agonies but withdrawn goods: light, hearing, the friendship of God. The worst thing that can happen to a soul is not suffering; it is being left to itself.

**Deaf and Blind With the Organs Intact**

The physical men of this verse could still hear a camel cough and spot a rider on the horizon. The deafness and blindness are moral, and the Qur'an removes all doubt about the organ involved: "Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind." (22:46). A heart gone blind is the sūrah's own diagnosis of the hypocrites, and this verse names the consequence formally: what the heart refuses long enough, the whole person forfeits. The ears keep working at concerts and councils; they simply no longer carry the recitation anywhere. The tragedy of this deafness is that no one else can detect it — the man hears himself being warned and registers nothing but noise.

**The Cattle Picture, Completed**

Earlier in this sūrah the disbelievers grazed like cattle; the tradition's commentators heard this verse as the completion of that portrait. Cattle hear without understanding and see without reasoning — but cattle were never addressed, and that is precisely why the comparison was an insult rather than a compliment. The men of this verse were both addressed and equipped, and they chose the animal's register of existence: intake, movement, avoidance. The senses were given for a purpose above grazing — to receive the Book, read the ruins, hear the caller — and senses repurposed away from their mandate eventually lose the mandate's benefits. The verse is not describing an arbitrary mutilation; it is describing a completed migration. The man has moved, by his own daily choices, into the class of beings for whom the address makes no sound.

**What a Curse Actually Is**

Laʿn — curse — needs rescuing from cartoon fire. In the Qur'an's usage, to curse is to banish from mercy, to place at a distance: the opposite of blessing, which draws near. The word was used of driving away, of expulsion from a group. When the verse says Allah has condemned these people, it is pronouncing the relational sentence: excluded, far, kept away from the mercy that was offered them at every dawn they ignored. The hellfire of the next verses is the address of that exclusion — a place, a state, a distance made permanent. The deepest reading of the curse is therefore not that God turned cruel, but that the relation the hypocrites spent their lives refusing was, at the last, formally recognised on both sides.

**Punishment Shaped Like the Choice**

Notice what the punishment shares with the crime. They would not hear the revelation — they are made deaf. They would not see the signs — they are made blind. The Qur'an's punishments keep this grammar of fit: the boaster is disgraced, the hoarder leashed by his hoard, the slanderer seized by the forelock. The fittingness is not poetic flourish; it is the doctrine that punishment is the sin, matured and made permanent. God does not import alien torments; He grants the self the full consequences of its own selections. A soul that spent its hearing on whispers and its sight on loopholes has, in the end, a world made entirely of whispers and loopholes — and the verse states the outcome in the same breath as the verdict, because between the choice and its echo, nothing external stands.

**The Order the Verse Refuses to Rush**

Readers conditioned by later fire-literature may notice how restrained the verse is: no flames, no instruments, no duration. Condemned, deafened, blinded — three words, and the silence around them does the frightening. The restraint is not squeamishness; it is confidence. The verse does not need to dramatise the loss of God, because the reader with any imagination has already felt it — everyone has known an hour of not being heard, a season of not being able to see what mattered — and the verse simply extends those hours into an address. The Qur'an's scariest verses are often its shortest, and this is one: the punishment of the insincere is the permanence of their own company.

**The Door the Verse Still Holds**

The tense of the verb has mercy folded into it. The condemnation is past and settled in the verse's grammar — but the verse exists, now, in front of readers who are not yet in it. Every warning in the Qur'an is addressed to the living, and its predictions are, until death, descriptions of trajectories rather than of persons. The hypocrite being described still breathes while he reads; the senses still function; the recitation is still arriving at ears that can, this very hour, choose to let something through. The verse's horror is thus its pastoral purpose — God publishes the far country precisely so that no one need emigrate to it. Deafness is still optional. So is hearing.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:24

> Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verb That Names the Method**

The question the verse poses is not have they read it? but something more demanding: do they not reflect on the Quran? The Arabic verb, yatadabbarūn, is built from a root meaning to walk behind something until its end — to track a matter through, the way a tracker follows a trail. Tadabbur is reading as pursuit: following the verse through its connections, its consequences, its demands, until the reader arrives where the verse was going. The Qur'an has no patience with the scanning eye. It asks for readers who will walk behind the text as a Bedouin walks behind a trail — patiently, alert to every bent twig — and the verse implies that the community's problem is not the Quran's clarity but the reader's gait.

**A Book That Invites Verification**

The demand for reflection is not a demand for reverence in a vacuum; the Qur'an ties tadabbur to testing: "Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies." (4:82). The invitation is startling by the standards of scripture. This Book asks to be read with its seams checked — inviting readers to hunt for contradiction as proof-reading, and betting its divine origin on the result. A forger would suppress the auditing instinct; the Quran commands it. Reflection, then, is not a devotional garnish; it is the Book's own evidentiary protocol, and the reader who will not run it has declined to participate in the very process the text proposes for establishing its truth.

**Locks on the Heart**

The verse's second clause supplies the alternative explanation for non-reflection: or are there locks upon their hearts? The image is architectural and exact. A lock does not mean the room is empty; it means the room is inaccessible. The heart of the non-reflector may be furnished with intelligence, learning, even affection for religion — and locked, so that the Book's contents remain in the corridor. The tradition explored the image in stages of severity: the locked heart beyond reach, the rusted heart dimmed by sins, the sealed heart gone dark — a progression of corrosion. And locks imply keys, and the tradition named them: sincerity of intention, asking God for opening, acting on what has already landed, keeping the company of those who pursue the text. The lock, in every telling, is engaged from the inside.

**How the First Readers Read**

The first generation's practice of this verse is preserved, and it reads like a different religion from the speed-reading of later centuries. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd — the shepherd's hireling who became one of the Qur'an's great transmitters — described the method: the Prophet would teach them ten verses, and they would not move to the next ten until they had learned what was in them — the knowledge and the deeds it commanded, together. Qur'an plus practice, in single units, learned to completion. The point was not literacy; it was transformation per verse. A community formed by that pedagogy produced, within a generation, judges, scholars, and souls of a quality the world still borrows from — which is the verse's argument made historical: the Book unlocks hearts precisely in the measure that hearts are walked through it, line by line, into obedience.

**The Best of You**

The Prophet compressed the entire economy of this verse into one sentence he made famous: "The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5027). Three stations in five words — learn, teach, best — and the middle term is the first's proof. Learning that never teaches curdles; teaching that outruns learning lies. The hadith's best is not the fastest reciter or the largest memoriser, but the one in whom the Book passes through — entering by reflection, exiting by transmission. For a community that would eventually build universities on this sentence, the verse under commentary was the charter: reflection on the Book is not one pious option among many; it is the engine of whatever excellence the community will ever have.

**Treasures With Covers On**

The early Muslims spoke of the Qur'an's depths with the reverence of men who had actually been down. One of them, ʿAlī Zayn al-ʿĀbidīn, the great-grandson of the Prophet, compared the verses to treasures with covers: whenever a servant opens one, he must look at what lies inside. The image answers a question every serious reader eventually asks — why does the Book yield so differently to different people? Because treasure is stored at depth, and covers are real, and the opening is labour. The same page that whispers to the distracted reader discloses to the tracker; the same verse preaches simply in one decade and detonates in another. This is not the Book hiding; it is treasure behaving like treasure — which is exactly what the verse promises the reflector and denies the locked.

**The Question Placed Before Every Reader**

The verse, like the sūrah's other diagnostic questions, is addressed to the one reading it — each of us in turn. Do I reflect on the Quran, or do I merely revisit it? The honest test is not the shelf of commentary beside the bed but the trail-walking: when did a verse last change a decision of mine, spend an hour with me, follow me into the week? The alternative clause — locks upon the hearts — was written about hypocrites, but locks are an equal-opportunity furniture, and the verse hands every believer the keymaker's question. The Book is the same Book it was on the morning it made a shepherd's hireful into a teacher of nations. What has changed is the reader's pace, and the reader's pace, this verse insists, is still the reader's to set.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:25
> Indeed, those who relapse ˹into disbelief˺ after ˹true˺ guidance has become clear to them, ˹it is˺ Satan ˹that˺ has tempted them, luring them with false hopes

**Expanded Commentary**

**Relapse After the Lights Came On**

The verse names a specific failure: not the ignorance of the never-taught, but the retreat of the once-guided. Relapse — irtadda in Arabic, to turn back on one's tracks — after guidance has become clear. The crime's precondition is clarity: these are people who saw where the road went, and walked it, and then reversed. The Qur'an treats this as a category of its own, more serious than simple unbelief, because it squanders knowledge rather than lacking it. And the medical register of the English word fits the Arabic exactly: the recovered patient who returns to the sickness, the freed man who walks back to the cell — the relapse is chosen in full knowledge of both addresses, which is what makes it a tragedy instead of a misstep.

**Who the Relapsers Were**

The commentators offered several identifications, each with its grounds. One reading points at the hypocrites who turned away when fighting was ordained — the sūrah's own immediate context; the battle-fatigued who discovered that faith with a price tag was not the product they had purchased. Another points at segments of the Madinan Jews who had accepted part of the revelation and refused the parts that cost — the Qur'an elsewhere asks them, in amazement, whether they believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part. A third reads the verse of the Makkan deniers who knew the Prophet's trustworthiness for decades and rejected him once the message touched their idols. The first reading fits the sūrah's flow best; the others fit the psychology, which is the same in every case — the retreat happens exactly where the guidance begins to invoice.

**Satan's Currency Is Time**

The verse then credits the operation to Satan — but notice the method named, because it is not fire or possession: luring them with false hopes. Satan's instrument, in this verse, is the future tense. Not now; later; after the season of enjoyment; when the odds improve; when the family comes round — hope deferred as an anaesthetic for conscience. The Qur'an elsewhere gives the classic statement of the disease's terminal phase: "Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment." (3:178). The false hope is always the same hope: that delay is harmless. Satan does not need a person to renounce God; he only needs the person to reschedule — indefinitely, comfortably, one season at a time.

**The Slide, Not the Leap**

Relapse in real life is rarely a leap; it is a gradient, and the verse's participles describe a process rather than an event. The chart runs familiar: a private reservation about a command; a friendship that feeds the reservation; an attendance that thins; a counsel that begins to serve the reservation; and one ordinary day, without drama, the person is simply on the other side of the line, describing the crossing as growth. The sūrah's earlier verses had the anatomy — the sick heart, the sealed senses, the locks — and this verse adds the motor: desire plus deferred reckoning. The practical teaching follows directly: relapse is arrested at its early stations, almost never at its late ones, which is why the tradition urges the believer to fight the first private reservation as though it were a burglary in progress.

**God's Delay, Misread as Safety**

The false hope works only because God's justice runs on a schedule the hopeful can ignore. The respite is real — the Qur'an never denies the disbeliever his years — but its purpose is the opposite of a pardon: the interval exists either for repentance or for the full ripening of what was chosen. The verse's Satan banking on this is the oldest confidence trick in the Book: he sells time as though it were an asset the buyer owns, when every hour is a loan with the ledger attached. And the trick never updates its script. Every generation's relapsers are sold the same three-word product — you have time — and every generation discovers, at the hour the verse describes in the next passage, that the product was never in stock.

**Two Hopes, Only One With Legs**

A final distinction, because the verse could be misread as an attack on hope itself. The Qur'an commands hope — hope in God's mercy is a duty so basic that despair of it is named a sin. The difference is in the object and the effect. Hope in mercy sends a person toward God — it fuels the return, the asking, the reform. The false hope sends a person away from God — it fuels the postponement, the softening of sin, the comfortable distance. Same emotional machinery, opposite directions, and the test is brutally simple: does this hope make me move toward what pleases Him, or does it fund my staying where I am? The believer's hope books travel; Satan's hope books delay. Both are sold as tickets, and only one has a destination.

**The Verse as a Rescue Notice**

Read at the right speed, the verse is not a report on the damned; it is a rescue notice posted inside the sliding zone. It tells the reader who has begun the retreat that the voice recommending it has a name, a method, and a product line — and that the product is counterfeit. Naming the mechanism is half the cure: the man who hears "later" and recognises the salesman can decline the sale. The sūrah's next verse will trace the relapse into its social stage — the secret assurances given to the enemies of revelation — and each stage named is a stage at which the slide can be stopped. The verse stands in the path of every reader who is mid-retreat, holding a lamp and asking the only question that matters: who sold you the schedule?

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:26

> That is because they said ˹privately˺ to those who ˹also˺ detest what Allah has revealed, “We will obey you in some matters.” But Allah ˹fully˺ knows what they are hiding

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Whisper, Quoted as Evidence**

The verse explains the relapse by quoting its social stage: a private message, delivered to the enemies of revelation — we will obey you in some matters (47:26). Consider what that sentence is. It is a declaration of allegiance with a limping qualifier; a treaty with a clause the other party is supposed not to read. The Qur'an preserves the actual words the way a prosecutor preserves a wiretap — because the wording convicts. The hypocrites' public record showed profession of Islam; their private record, here published, shows a competing subscription with a leakage clause: we will follow you — in some matters. The some is the whole person. Anyone whose obedience is sold in instalments has already priced the rest.

**The Arithmetic of Partial Obedience**

The qualifier deserves the mathematics. God's claim in the Qur'an is total — the word islam itself means surrender, and surrender knows no some. A soldier who obeys ninety percent of orders and sells the remaining ten to the enemy is not ninety percent loyal; he is a spy with a good attendance record. The hypocrisy described here works precisely on the confusion between the two: as long as the some stays hidden, the person holds community membership, marriage, inheritance, and safety — all the goods of the ninety — while running a private franchise on the side. The verse's quiet horror is arithmetic: the withheld share is not a fraction of the man's religion; it is the whole of his religion, because what a person keeps back from God is the exact measure of what he actually serves.

**Whose Ear the Whisper Sought**

To whom was the message sent? To those who also detest what Allah has revealed — the sūrah's earlier diagnosis of the deniers, now revealed as the hypocrites' true constituency. The Qur'an legislates about such private channels elsewhere in words that fit this scene exactly: "Have you not seen those who were forbidden from secret talks, yet they ˹always˺ return to what they were forbidden from, conspiring in sin, aggression, and disobedience to the Messenger?" (58:8). Secret counsel — najwā — is the habitat of this religion of some: it is where reservations become strategy, where the public self and the private self can be different products for different markets. The verse drags the channel into the open by the simple expedient of publishing its contents, and the publication is the punishment's first instalment.

**The Omniscience Clause**

Then the verse closes the exits: But Allah fully knows what they are hiding. The commentators noted a fine shade in the Arabic: the word can be read as their secrets — the hidden counsels — or as their persistence, the dogged holding-to of the hidden thing. Both readings agree on the substance: nothing in the private channel is private. For the hypocrites this clause was the death of the entire business model — an agency whose product is concealment, operating inside the hearing range of the Omniscient. For believers, the same clause is the bedrock of comfort: every unrecorded kindness, every suppressed temptation, every prayer nobody witnessed — fully known. One clause, two audiences; the omniscience of God is the terror of the insincere and the treasury of the sincere, and no third use exists.

**Whispers as Archived Documents**

There is a doctrine folded into the verse's method that deserves its own line: the Qur'an quotes the whisper. Not a summary of it — the words. The Book's habit of publishing the private speech of its opponents is its own kind of miracle claim: the revelation claims access to what was said behind closed doors, at night, in confidence, and stakes its authority on the accuracy of the quotation. The first audience could test the claims against what they knew of their own councils — the chiefs who had heard these sentiments in their own sitting rooms — and the Qur'an was recited, publicly, with the quotations intact, in the city where the whisperers still lived. No whisperer stood up to deny the quote. The archive, in other words, was checked by its own subjects, and the subjects went on reciting it.

**"Some Matters" in Modern Dress**

The verse's qualifier has a long afterlife. No generation has lacked the religion of some: the faith practised exactly up to the point where it invoices — generous in ritual, absent at the audit; devout in season, renegotiated at the bank; all of God that fits the calendar, the portfolio, the politics. The modern believer does not whisper it to a tribal chief; he whispers it to himself in the same grammar — I will, of course, in some matters. The verse's question to every reader is not about them, the long-dead whisperers; it is about the some in our own subscription: which command is currently filed under it, and to which private audience is the message addressed? The sūrah has already answered the only part that matters: the audience is heard.

**Relapse Explained, and the Door Held**

Set in its place, the verse completes the previous one's diagnosis. Relapse, it turns out, was not a private philosophical development; it had a constituency, a channel, and a sales team — the detesters of revelation, receptive to defectors, collecting assurances. Sin organises. That is the last thing the comfortable believer wants to learn from this passage, and the most useful: the private reservation, fed quietly, finds allies, and the allies send back encouragement in the same whisper frequency. The verse names the network so that the reader can cut the subscription at its cheapest point — the first whispered some — while the cutting still costs almost nothing, and the Knower of what is hidden is still accepting full returns.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:27

> Then how ˹horrible˺ will it be when the angels take their souls, beating their faces and backs

**Expanded Commentary**

**Taking in Full**

The verse turns from the hypocrites' secrets to the hour those secrets end: the angels take their souls. The Arabic verb, tatawaffā, is built on a root meaning completeness — to take in full, to recover entirely what was lent. The image behind the word is repossession: the soul was never the person's property, only a deposit, and the angels arrive as the authorised collectors, taking the deposit back with nothing withheld. For people whose entire strategy was the partial — some matters, some obedience, some visibility — the verb carries its own sentence: at the end, nothing partial remains. The some is collected like the all.

**Anatomy of the Beating**

Then comes the image that has made the verse famous and feared: the angels beating their faces and their backs. The Qur'an rarely specifies anatomy without purpose, and here the purpose is the sūrah's own grammar of fit. The face — what a person presents, the surface of pretence, the public mask the hypocrites spent their lives maintaining. The back — what they turned on the commands of God, for turning one's back is the Qur'an's idiom for contemptuous refusal; these were the people who reversed after guidance, showing their backs to the Book. The punishment lands on exactly the two surfaces the sin was performed on: the mask and the exit. The verse is not gratuitous; it is receipt-of-goods, delivered to the address on the package.

**The Twin Scene From the Battle Sūrah**

The Qur'an stages this death elsewhere in near-identical terms, and the twin belongs beside this verse: "If only you could see when the angels take the souls of the disbelievers, beating their faces and backs, ˹saying,˺ 'Taste the torment of burning!'" (8:50). The repetition across sūrahs does two things. It fixes the scene as doctrine rather than flourish — this is what the Qur'an teaches about the death of the insincere, stated twice, in war's sūrah and in hypocrisy's sūrah. And it attaches a spoken clause: taste. The sentence the angels deliver is not an argument, an explanation, or an invitation — the time for those is the years the person just spent. What remains at the exit is sensation, the account settling into the body that ran up the bill.

**Grammar of Dread**

Notice how the verse opens: Then how — kayfa in Arabic — how will it be. The verse does not describe the scene; it gestures at it and asks the reader to complete the picture. This is deliberate craft. A full description bounds the horror — the imagination measures it and moves on. The open question makes the reader the scene's cinematographer, and every reader supplies his own best rendering of what the departure of God's mercy looks like on a face. The Qur'an uses this interrogative of dread repeatedly — how will it be when… — and it is worth asking why: because the verse wants the reader inside the scene, feeling its weather, rather than outside it taking notes. A described terror can be filed away as somebody else's bad news; an unfinished how hands the reader the pen and waits, and what the reader writes with it is, invariably, the thing he most fears losing. The question mark, in other words, is a mirror, and it is aimed at whoever is holding the page. Fear that is merely read is forgotten; fear that is co-authored is not.

**The Believer's Death, Quietly Different**

For balance, the tradition always read this verse beside its mirror image. The Qur'an describes another death as well — the one the believers are promised: angels of mercy descending with the greeting, peace be upon you, enter the Garden for what you used to do. The same biological event, two entirely different receptions; the difference lies not in the manner of dying but in the reception committee. The verse of the beating is written in the horror register precisely so that the believer's death, when it comes, reads as the rescue it is. Every funeral of the righteous the community ever held was a quiet commentary on this verse: the same angels, a different errand, a face touched instead of struck.

**A Warning Aimed at the Living**

The verse's verb points at a future event, but its function is entirely present-tense. No reader of the verse is, at this moment, in the scene; the scene is scheduled, and the schedule is the point. The hypocrites of the sūrah lived for years after this verse was revealed — years in which the beating was not yet, and the door of repentance was still standing open, and some, the tradition records, walked through it. The horror is published so that it can be pre-empted; the receipt is shown early so that the account can still be settled differently. That is the Qur'an's settled method with its darkest material: the afterlife is described in detail precisely because description is mercy's last dialect — the photograph of the far country shown to the traveller who can still change his ticket.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader**

Sit under the verse long enough and it asks its question: which exit am I building? The faces and backs of the hypocrites were not assembled in an afternoon; they were the accumulated posture of years — the mask maintained, the commands turned upon. The believer cannot make himself sinless before the collectors arrive, and is not asked to; the Qur'an's own prescription, given in this very sūrah, is knowledge of the truth and the seeking of pardon, continually, to the last day. What the verse rules out is the one arrangement it was written against: the private clause, the some, the mask worn so long it becomes the face. The collectors take in full. The reader's whole task is to ensure that what they collect, when they come, is a whole man.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:28

> This is because they follow whatever displeases Allah and hate whatever pleases Him, so He has rendered their deeds void

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Final Because, All About Love**

The sūrah has now asked four times why the deeds are void, and this last answer is the deepest of the four. Not because they hindered (47:1), not because they disbelieved (47:8), not even because they detested (47:9) — but because their affections are inverted: they follow what angers God and hate what pleases Him. The diagnosis has moved from conduct through creed through emotion to the root system itself. In the Qur'an's anthropology, loves are not the decoration of a life; they are its engine and its identity. Deeds are what the loves wore in public. A person is, at the last analysis, not what he did but what he delighted in — and the voiding of the deeds follows from the verdict on the loves the deeds were serving.

**Anatomy of a Reversed Heart**

Hold the two halves of the condition up to the light. Following what displeases Allah: a life navigated by appetite, counsel, and custom that runs against the grain of revelation — the sūrah has catalogued it thoroughly. Hating what pleases Him: the subtler and more damning half — the actual aversion felt toward prayer, toward obedience, toward the company of the sincere, toward every good that once grazed the heart. The first half is behavior; the second is weather. And the second is the proof of the first, because behaviour can be coerced by circumstance while hatred cannot — hatred is the free testimony of what a man actually is. The reversed heart is thus not weak; it is consistent. It has simply answered the question loves exist to answer — what do you want? — in the wrong direction, on purpose, at length.

**The Fourth Stamp of the Verdict**

This is the fourth stamping of the verdict — He has rendered their deeds void (47:28) — and by now the refrain has taught its lesson the way refrains do. First stamp: the statute, with hindering named. Second: the sentence, at the sūrah's turn. Third: the cause, detestation of the revealed. Fourth: the root, the loves themselves. Each repetition added a deeper stratum, and the order of the strata is the sūrah's whole psychology of unbelief in miniature: act, allegiance, aversion, affection. The legal-document rhythm is not redundancy; it is drilling. A doctrine this costly to human pride — that impressive lives can be voided by what their owner loved — needs four statements before proud minds will hold it, and the sūrah spends them like a physician spending a long consultation on the one diagnosis the patient keeps refusing to hear.

**Anger and Pleasure: God's Real Categories**

The verse quietly establishes something the modern mind resists: that God's pleasure and displeasure are real features of the moral universe, not metaphors for human wellbeing. What angers Him and what pleases Him are the fixed poles between which every life moves — and the verse's tragedy is a life magnetised toward the wrong pole. This is the Qur'an's answer to the question of moral grounding: good is not a fashion, a consensus, or an evolutionary residue; it is what pleases the Person who made the creatures deciding. Remove the Person, and follow-what-angers becomes merely an alternative lifestyle; keep Him, and it is what this verse says — the forfeiting of everything, because the standard one ran against turns out to own the scales.

**The Diagnostic Applied at Home**

The tradition never allowed this verse to remain aimed at the dead hypocrites; it is aimed, by unanimous consent, at the living believer's mirror. And the test it provides is more honest than the usual ones. Not do I obey? — obedience can be staged, and is, by the best of us. But do I love what pleases Him? Does the command I keep at cost secretly repulse me? Does the good I applaud privately bore me? Loves can be trained — the tradition is full of the training: companionship with those who love the good, repeated exposure to it, asking God for the taste, doing the good until the doing warms the heart — because the Qur'an treats affection as a discipline, not a given. The verse's reader is not asked to have perfect loves today; he is asked to know which way his are currently pointing, and to start the retraining before the stamp falls.

**Why the Refrain Had to Repeat**

A last word on the repetition itself, because the sūrah's architecture is the argument. Four times voided — and the reader's own resistance is the reason. Every unreligious instinct in us agrees with the hypocrites' account: surely the deeds count; surely generosity, courage, and hard work accumulate something. The refrain keeps falling on that instinct like a stamp on a form that keeps coming back unsigned. The Qur'an is not saying works are worthless — the same Book promises the righteous gardens for what they used to do. It is saying that works are weightless when the loves behind them are inverted, because the loves are the signature on the account. A cheque is paper without a signature; the deeds voided in this sūrah were real cheques, drawn on a closed account, and the account closed when the loves turned.

**A Verse That Sets Up the Coming Question**

Placed in the sūrah's flow, this verse completes the indictment just in time for the prosecution to rise. The hypocrites have been described, their whispers published, their exit scheduled, their loves diagnosed — and now the next verse asks the question any defendant asks at this stage: do these people actually believe they will never be exposed? The sūrah, having laid out the evidence, turns to the dock. And the reader, who has been following the case with the comfortable distance of a spectator, discovers — as every reader of this sūrah eventually does — that the gallery is inside the courtroom, and the docket has his generation's name on it too.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:29

> Or do those with sickness in their hearts think that Allah will not ˹be able to˺ expose their malice

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Arithmetic of Hiding From the Knower**

The verse states the hypocrites' gamble as a question, and the gamble, laid bare, is absurd: they believe God will not expose what is hidden inside them. Consider the components. They believe in God — hypocrites must, for hypocrisy is meaningless without a God to perform before. They believe He sent a Book, raises the dead, and audits the worlds. And they simultaneously believe — this is the working assumption their entire strategy rests on — that the interior of a human heart is beyond Him. It is the most incoherent position in the catalogue of unbelief: omniscience acknowledged in the creed and deleted from the plans. The verse's question format is mercy; it lets the hearer do the arithmetic himself and watch it fail.

**The Fear That Already Told on Them**

The sūrah's God had a way of confirming this verse before their eyes. The Qur'an records the hypocrites' own tell elsewhere, and it is one of the Book's most psychological passages: "The hypocrites fear that a sûrah should be revealed about them, exposing what is in their hearts. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Keep mocking! Allah will definitely bring to light what you fear.'" (9:64). They feared exposure — and fear of exposure is itself the confession. A man with nothing in the vault does not flinch when the auditors are announced. Their dread of each new revelation was the receipt for what the revelations kept finding, and the Prophet was told to answer the dread with the guarantee this verse supplies: what you fear is scheduled. Malice that arranges itself for concealment has already conceded the standard it will be judged by.

**Malice, the Word for the Core**

The verse names the hidden content with a hard, exact word: malice — the settled grudge, the standing wish that the believers' project fail. The sūrah has been building toward the noun. It began with sickness in the hearts, progressed through the whispers and the some-matters obedience, and now the sickness is named by its fruit: not pain, not confusion — malice, the will against the good. Naming it matters, because the modern reader instinctively softens hypocrisy into weakness, as though the double-life were a failure of nerve. The verse refuses the softening. The man was not too weak to be sincere; he was sincere — sincerely committed to a grudge, around which his religion, his speech, and his friendships were all arranged. Weakness asks for help. Malice waits in back rooms for the light.

**Exposure Already Under Way**

And the exposure the verse guarantees had, in fact, already begun — in the most public way conceivable: the Qur'an itself. Verse after verse of this sūrah has been publishing the hypocrites' interior — the sickness, the whispers quoted verbatim, the some-matters clause, the malice — recited in the believers' prayers, in their assemblies, in the city where the subjects of the description still lived and prayed alongside the people they were betraying. God's method of exposure is not primarily the lightning of circumstance; it is His own Book, holding the mirror up in the hearing of the mirrored. History added the second instalments: the betrayals surfacing one by one, the alliances failing, the individual faces known to the community — until the Prophet's own generation could recite the diagnosis from memory. The verse's question — will God not expose? — was answered by the verse being recited.

**The Last Delusion of the Secret Life**

Every secret life runs on a piece of necessary fiction, and the verse names it: God will not bring it to light. Not that He cannot — the hypocrites were not theologians of a blind deity — but that He will not: too distant, too patient, too occupied. The fiction is load-bearing, because a person cannot maintain two selves while believing both are fully seen. This is why the omniscience of God is not, in the Qur'an, a piece of remote metaphysics; it is the single doctrine most disruptive to the machinery of sin. The tradition taught believers to recite this verse's fact — He knows what you conceal — as a discipline of the imagination, precisely because the fiction of the unwatched room is where every relapse begins. The exposed room is the honest one, and the honest one is the only one that can be furnished with anything lasting.

**The Mercy of Being Findable**

There is a gentler reading available, and the tradition always kept it open. Exposure, before death, is a mercy — the last mercy, sometimes, but a real one. The person whose malice is found out in this world has been given a diagnosis, and diagnosis precedes treatment; the hypocrite exposed at noon can be a penitent by evening, and the Qur'an's door for penitents never once closes on its own side. What the verse guarantees is not damnation but light — malice brought into the open, where it can finally be repented rather than maintained. The tragedy is reserved for the one who reads the verse about exposure and tightens the lid. Light is only cruel to what insists on staying dark.

**Why the Question Mark Is the Sermon**

The verse ends as a question, and the format is the last sermon in it. A statement — God will expose your malice — could be filed away as information, theology for another day. A question — do you think He will not? — follows the reader into the week, reassembling itself whenever a secret is next being arranged. The Qur'an uses this device sparingly and deliberately, and always at the load-bearing points: the question makes the reader his own preacher, delivering the sermon every time the relevant temptation calls. For the hypocrites of the sūrah, the question was the exposure beginning. For the reader since, it is the standing audit, issued in advance of the audit — a mercy, so long as it is answered honestly, while the answering can still change the answer to everything else.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:30
> Had We willed, We could have truly shown them to you ˹O Prophet˺, and you would have certainly recognized them by their appearance. But you will surely recognize them by their tone of speech. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows your doings ˹O people˺

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Miracle That Was Withheld**

The verse opens with a remarkable conditional: Had We willed, We could have truly shown them to you. The Prophet is told, in effect, that supernatural identification of the hypocrites was available — a divine labelling system, faces marked, files exposed — and God declined to install it. The withholding is the teaching. A community governed by revealed X-ray vision would obey for the wrong reasons and police the impossible: the interior of hearts. Instead, the verse points to the slower, humaner instruments — speech, conduct, time — and leaves certainty about hearts to the One who owns them. The refusal is a constitutional act: the Prophet's state was to judge what appears, not what hides, and every later Islamic jurisprudence of evidence descends from this restraint.

**Marks of a Different Kind, on a Different Day**

The marks exist — the verse says so — but their display is scheduled for the right court. The Qur'an describes that scheduling in the sūrah of the Most Merciful: "The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by ˹their˺ forelocks and feet." (55:41). What is concealed in this world — the malice of v. 29, the secrets of v. 26 — arrives at the resurrection already printed on its owners. The doctrine is two-edged. For the hidden hypocrite it is the exposure he assumed would never come; for the hidden believer — the sincere one whose goodness nobody recorded — it is the vindication he never sought. Both interior lives, the corrupt and the pure, are wearing their marks now, invisibly, and both sets will be legible on the Day the verse points toward.

**The Tell in the Tone**

Meanwhile, the Prophet is given a subtler instrument: you will surely recognize them by their tone of speech. The Arabic, laḥn al-qawl, is the tone beneath the text — what the voice does while the words pretend to be innocent. The hypocrites of the sūrah were fluent in profession; what betrayed them was the music: the enthusiasm that surfaced at the wrong moments, the hesitancy whenever God's pleasure was described, the qualifier that softened every commitment, the slight speed-up past the verse about spending. Anyone who has listened long to human speech knows the register — sincerity has a sound, and so does its imitation. The verse dignifies that knowledge: the Prophet, and every leader after him, is licensed to hear — while remaining unlicensed to convict on hearing alone.

**Ibn Ubayy's Sentence, Preserved as Evidence**

The Qur'an once caught the tone in an actual quotation, and it remains the masterclass of self-exposure. The leader of Madinah's hypocrites, ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, said of the Prophet and his Companions: "If we return to Medina, the honourable will definitely expel the inferior." (63:8). One sentence, and the tone has told on him ever after: the contempt, the self-appointment, the politics of expulsion in a mouth that publicly professed brotherhood. The Qur'an quotes the sentence verbatim — published in the Book, recited for eternity — which is the second exposure inside the first: not only the malice, but the very music of the malice, archived. The tradition drew the obvious lesson for readers: watch your own tone, because it is the part of your speech you least control and others hear first.

**Recognition Without a Witch-Hunt**

Now assemble the verse's quiet politics. The Prophet could recognise the hypocrites — by tone, by pattern — and the Qur'an's later verses show him informed of specific councils. What he did not do is what the verse withholds from him: hunt. No trials of the interior, no loyalty tribunals, no purge of the suspect — the hypocrites died in their beds, some forgiven later by the Prophet's own prayer at their funerals, and the community's unity was purchased with that patience. The constitutional rule that emerged — God manages hearts; the community manages deeds — was tested at its extreme by exactly these men, and it held. There is a standing temptation in every religious society to enforce the inside; this verse, with its declined miracle, is the permanent veto.

**The Closing Audit Changes Rooms**

Then the verse ends by turning the X-ray around: And Allah ˹fully˺ knows your doings ˹O people˺ (47:30). The pronoun shifts — your, plural, everyone in the room, the reader included. The instrument the Prophet was not given is running constantly in Someone's hands, and its field of view is total: not only the hypocrites' councils and the believers' battles, but the current reader's afternoon. The shift completes the verse's mercy for the sincere and its warning for the calculating: nothing you do is unobserved, and nothing you hide is unfiled. The believer rests his case there — his deeds, known in full, are the only portfolio he ever needed. The hypocrite's tragedy is that he knows the doctrine, and structures his entire life around betting against it.

**The Verse as Leadership Training**

Read last, the verse is a manual compressed to three moves for anyone who must lead communities of mixed hearts — which is every community. First, accept that full identification is not yours; God withheld it from a prophet, and no committee will improve on that ruling. Second, listen — the tone of speech is licensed evidence, and the wise leader hears what is said and how. Third, act on deeds, not on decoded interiors; the audit of hearts is fully staffed already. Between the paranoid state that reads minds and the naive one that reads nothing, this verse routes a third way: patient, observant, just — and leaves the ultimate unmasking, with its forelocks and its feet, to the Court that scheduled it.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:31
> We will certainly test you ˹believers˺ until We prove those of you who ˹truly˺ struggle ˹in Allah’s cause˺ and remain steadfast, and reveal how you conduct yourselves

**Expanded Commentary**

**Testing Until Proving — the Meaning of the Phrase**

The verse's boldest phrase is its most easily misread: We will certainly test you ˹believers˺ until We prove. God, in the Qur'an, is never the student learning the answer; His knowledge is complete before the exam is printed. The tradition resolved the phrase with precision: the proving is the making-manifest — God knows all things eternally, and the test brings that knowledge out of the hidden and into existence, where it can be witnessed, rewarded, and answered for. Creation is the courtroom in which what God already knows is performed. The Qur'an makes the same correction itself elsewhere, asking after the battle that staggered the community: "Do you think you will enter Paradise without Allah proving which of you ˹truly˺ struggled ˹for His cause˺ and patiently endured?" (3:142). Proving — the word of demonstration, not discovery. The exam is not for the Examiner; it is for the examined, and for a universe in which justice is done in the open.

**The Two Virtues on the Scorecard**

Notice which qualities the exam measures: those who struggle and remain steadfast. Struggle — mujāhada, the root of jihad — is effort in the direction of God's pleasure, with whatever it costs. Steadfastness — ṣabr — is duration: the holding of the effort through time, loss, and boredom, after the excitement has drained out of the cause. The pairing is the complete athlete: intensity and persistence, the sprint and the marathon. A believer can have one without the other and be half-tested — bursts of zeal that evaporate, or patient plodding that never ventures anything. The verse's exams are designed against exactly these half-profiles, because real events squeeze both axes at once: the battle tests courage in the morning and endurance by dusk; the vocation tests zeal in the first year and steadfastness in the tenth.

**Uḥud, the Prototype Exam**

The first audience had just lived the verse. Uḥud had taken the community's professions — we will fight for God's cause, we wished for the sūrah — and put them under load, and the load had shown what was inside everyone: archers leaving the pass, a rumour of the Prophet's death breaking the line, courage and confusion sharing the same hillside. The Qur'an's post-battle verses read like an examiner's report, including the exact question of this verse in the verse quoted above. The lesson the community was to draw was not that God lacked information; it was that the community did — about itself. The battle exposed the believers to themselves, and that is the exam's standing purpose: everyone carries a self-image, and the events God sends are the instrument by which the image is corrected against the person.

**Exposure as a Service to the Doer**

Then the verse adds its second clause, and reveal how you conduct yourselves (47:31) — and the revealing runs in both directions. The hypocrites' deeds are exposed to the community, as the sūrah has been doing verse by verse. But the believer's deeds are exposed to the believer: the courage he did not know he had, the generosity that only an actual need could summon, the prayer that only a hospital corridor could teach, and — the humbling half — the cowardice, the stinginess, the self-regard that comfort had kept off the books. There is a mercy in this that the sheltered life forfeits: a person cannot repent of what he has never seen in himself, and the exam, honestly read, is therefore the beginning of repair. The believer who asks God to test him is not asking for pain; he is asking for an accurate map of his own soul, which no other instrument supplies.

**The Hypocrite's Declaration, Audited**

In the sūrah's flow, this verse answers the previous one's cold war. The hypocrites' strategy — the whisper, the some-matters obedience, the malice — depended on the assumption that professions are never called in. God's reply is an exam schedule: you will be tested until what you actually are has been performed. Words are cheap in peacetime; the sūrah of fighting was, for that generation, the auditor general. Every wished-for command, every death-stare, every retreat from the line — filed. The principle generalises past battles: every community and every soul gets its own curriculum of load-bearing moments, spaced across a lifetime precisely far enough apart to test whether the last lesson held. The hypocrite's error was reading the intervals between exams as evidence there would be none.

**Living Examined, Without Terror**

The verse's last service is to its believing reader, and it must be read carefully to avoid the anxiety-misreading. The exam is certain; the Examiner is generous. The Qur'an's whole doctrine of testing is framed in mercy — tests purify, prove, and accompany, and the same Book promises that no soul is burdened beyond capacity. What the verse asks of the believer is neither dread nor exam-gaming; it is the posture the tradition called yaqīn with ṣabr — certainty of hand, endurance of foot. When the load arrives, meet it as an appointment, not an ambush; when it passes, read the transcript honestly and bring the findings to God in repentance or gratitude. That — the meeting, the enduring, the reading — is the entire art of living an examined life, and this verse is its charter.

**Why the Verse Stands Where It Stands**

Structurally, the verse is the hinge into the sūrah's final movement. The exposure it promises is about to be shown in its ultimate case: the next verses name the permanent opponents — those who disbelieve, hinder, and defy — and pronounce the verdict their trajectory ends in, followed by the command to obedience that keeps the believer's own file clean. The verse of testing is thus the last word before the verdicts: God has not finished examining, the community has not finished performing, and the reader — mid-exam, breath still in his body — is holding the one document in the sūrah that tells him what every question is for.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:32
> Indeed, those who disbelieve, hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and defy the Messenger after ˹true˺ guidance has become clear to them; they will not harm Allah in the least, but He will render their deeds void

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Three-Part Charge, Complete**

The verse reads like an indictment drawn with care, and each count deserves its own line. They disbelieve — the private verdict of the heart. They hinder others from the Way of Allah — the public obstruction, the same crime the sūrah opened with, road-blocking practiced as a career. And they defy the Messenger after true guidance has become clear — the personal rebellion, shiqāq in Arabic, from a root meaning to split: they knew the man, knew his truthfulness, saw the guidance arrive and clarify, and chose enmity against him personally. Creed, conduct, and grudge: unbelief in its full armour. The sūrah's opening verse had two counts; this, near the close, adds the third — the defiance of a specific, known, clear messenger — and the addition is what makes this the terminal form of the disease.

**Defiance After Clarity**

The qualifier is the crime's engine: after true guidance has become clear. This is not the ignorance of the never-taught, nor the honest confusion of the questioning — the Qur'an keeps a deep patience for both. This is the retreat of the informed: people who watched clarity arrive, could not refute it, and converted their inability into a personal war against its carrier. The sūrah has met them at every stage — the mockers who asked what he just said, the relapsers lured by deferred hopes, the whisperers with their some-matters treaty — and here the stages are gathered into one noun: schism, the splitting of oneself from a guidance one has seen. Defiance of a messenger, in the Qur'an's accounting, is never merely personal; the messenger is the station of God's address, and the grudge against him is filed against the Addresser.

**They Cannot Harm God in the Least**

Against this full armoured opposition, the verse states a fact that dissolves it: they will not harm Allah in the least (47:32). The Qur'an had said it before, at the community's lowest hour, in the verse revealed when rumour said the Prophet had been killed at Uḥud: "Muḥammad is no more than a messenger; other messengers have gone before him. If he were to die or to be killed, would you regress into disbelief? Those who do so will not harm Allah whatsoever." (3:144). The doctrine is the eternal proportion-corrector: wars against God are waged by creatures He sustains in existence moment by moment, using lungs He keeps filled, against a claim they cannot dent. The harm flows one way only — downhill, onto the opposer — and the entire drama of religious persecution, from the outside view the Qur'an supplies, is a man swinging at the ground he stands on.

**The Refrain, One Last Time**

Then comes the sentence the sūrah has stamped and re-stamped: but He will render their deeds void (47:32). Fifth and last occurrence — the statute (47:1), the sentence (47:8), the cause (47:9), the root (47:28), and now the verdict written over the fully-armoured opponent. But notice the tense, because it has changed. The earlier stamps were past — done, settled, the verdict phrased as an accomplished fact. Here the verb stands in the future: He will render. The shift is the verse's last mercy and last warning in one. As long as the verb is future, the file is open; the opposer breathing this verse is still, technically, an eligible penitent, and the sūrah's second verse — believe, do good, and the ledger reopens — has not expired. The refrain's final stamp is raised, and hovering; it falls at death, and not one breath sooner.

**Why Obstruction Is Named With Defiance**

Return to the middle count — hindering others — and ask why it cannot be left out of the indictment even at this late verse. Because obstruction is the only power the opposer actually has. He cannot harm God; he cannot stop the revelation; the one thing his unbelief can do is damage other people's access to it. And that, the verse teaches, is precisely what seals his file: the harm he cannot do to Heaven, he does daily to the souls on the road. The verse thus contains a quiet theology of influence — the only wealth an unbeliever truly controls is the access of others, and it is the currency he is judged for squandering. The believer reads the same clause as commission: the road he did not block, the hand he lent the passerby — the one area of the divine economy where human choice moves real goods.

**The Verse as the Sūrah's Mirror**

Stand back and the verse shows its architecture: it is the opening of the sūrah, returned at the close in its final form. Sūrah Muḥammad began with the two-count charge and the voiding; it ends with the three-count charge and the voiding, with the future tense holding the door. Between them, everything in between has been the evidence: the battles, the rivers, the mockers, the whisperers, the exposed secrets, the tested community. The ring structure is not decoration; it is the Qur'an's way of saying that everything said between the two verdicts was said inside a single case — one file, one defendant class, one Judge — and that the reader has spent the whole sūrah inside a courtroom, deciding, with every verse read, which side of the verdict he is standing toward.

**What the Hovering Stamp Asks**

For the believer, the verse asks for two simultaneous postures that are easy to hold separately and hard to hold together. Toward God: relief — the opposition, however loud, is weightless; the community's fortunes never depended on its enemies' success. Toward the opposers: seriousness — the stamp is hovering over them, and no believer is entitled to write anyone off whom God has not yet written off; the future tense belongs to God, and prayer for the defiant is the believer's share in it. And toward the self, the verse asks its smallest and largest question: is there any count of the three — the private disbelief, the road-blocking, the grudge at a guidance I have clearly seen — in which I am quietly enrolled? The stamp is still raised. That is the whole of the good news.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:33
> O believers! Obey Allah and obey the Messenger, and do not let your deeds be in vain

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Command That Comes Paired**

The verse's first half is one of the Qur'an's fixed formulas: obey Allah and obey the Messenger — the two verbs, the repeated obey, the pairing stated as a single architecture. The Qur'an legislates the pairing explicitly where it matters most: "O believers! Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. Should you disagree on anything, then refer it to Allah and His Messenger, if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day." (4:59). The logic of the pair is the doctrine of this whole sūrah: God's guidance arrived through a messenger; obedience to the guidance cannot be split from obedience to the one delivering it — the hypocrites of this very chapter spent their careers attempting exactly that split, with their some-matters treaty, and the attempt is the sūrah's definition of ruin. The formula stands as the wall the sūrah was built against.

**The Warning Addressed to Believers**

Then comes the verse's second half, and its addressee is the shock: do not let your deeds be in vain (47:33) — spoken not to the disbelievers, whose deeds this sūrah has voided five times, but to the believers, O believers, the same opening address. The implication is careful and grave: the voiding of deeds is not a misfortune reserved for card-carrying deniers; it is a possibility inside the religious life itself. A believer can build and lose. The commentators traced the ways: deeds undone by disobedience that contradicts them; charity spent then cancelled by reminder and injury; worship performed for an audience other than God; the slow substitution of habit for intention. The verse is the sūrah's one direct warning to its own constituency — the row of believers in the front — and its rarity gives it weight.

**The Intention Test, as the Mystics Read It**

Al-Tustarī, reading this verse, went to the root his school always went to: sincerity. Do not let your deeds be in vain, he taught, by seeing them as coming from yourselves and seeking their recompense from other than God — for the sincere action is the one that seeks no recompense at all. The reading cuts beneath behaviour to the deed's owner-record: the same prayer, the same coin, the same fast, is either a gift or a transaction, and the filing depends on an interior signature no committee can read. This is why the tradition could say that sincerity is the deed's life: a body of works without it is a warehouse of beautiful boxes, weightless at the scale. The believer's daily task, in al-Tustarī's line of teaching, is not more deeds but truer ones — actions freed, one by one, from the demand to be seen.

**Obedience as the Preservation Method**

Read the verse's two halves together and its logic appears: obedience is how deeds are kept. The connection is not arbitrary. Deeds exist in an economy — they are filed, weighed, preserved, or voided by the One who commanded them — and their validity depends on their standing inside the command that authorised them. A deed done in defiance may be magnificent by human measure — generous, brave, costly — but it stands outside the issuing authority, like superb paperwork on a form no office accepts. The believer's security for his lifetime of labour is therefore not the labour's volume but its jurisdiction: obey, and the deeds are filed under obedience; stray, and the filing lapses. The verse, small as it is, contains the sūrah's entire doctrine of works in two sentences.

**The Freedom the Command Assumes**

The formula the verse uses carries, in its very repetition, a doctrine of freedom. The Qur'an could have said believe, and the state could have enforced it; instead it says obey — a verb that presupposes a free hearer who might not — and the Qur'an itself states the limit elsewhere in the same breath: "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger! But if you turn away, then Our Messenger's duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly." (64:12). Delivery, not compulsion: the messenger's job description ends at clarity, and the choice that follows belongs to the hearer. A command addressed to the free, with the option of refusal built into its grammar, honours the human being in a way coercion never can — and it explains why the verse ends with a warning instead of a sanction: the waste it fears is chosen, and what is chosen can be re-chosen.

**For the Anxious Sincere**

There is a reader this verse has always found: the sincere believer who, hearing that deeds can go to waste, audits his lifetime of small prayers and quiet charities and feels the floor tilt. For him, the verse's placement is the answer. It stands in a sūrah that has spent thirty-two verses promising preservation — guidance increased, steps made firm, martyrs' deeds never voided, the fallen admitted and acquainted with their dwellings — and the warning is the shadow-side of those promises, not their contradiction. The God who asks that deeds not be wasted is the same God who seals them, multiplies them tenfold, and covers their defects. The believer's task is not the terror of bookkeeping but the direction of travel: an obedience kept roughly honest, an intention swept clean by daily asking, and a trust that the Preserver of deeds is better at preserving than the doer is at ruining.

**Where the Verse Stands in the Sūrah**

The verse's position explains its compression. The sūrah is entering its final panel: after this command, the remaining verses will speak of the unforgivable death-state, the faltering at the top of strength, the world's smallness, and the spending God asks of His people. Obedience — the sūrah's thesis in one word — is the platform all of it stands on: only the obedient have anything at stake in the warnings; only the obedient have anything to keep from going to waste. The verse thus functions as the door between the sūrah's courtroom and its last counsel — and the reader passes through it, as every reader must, on foot: obeying, one command at a time, the deeds accumulating behind him like a trail he prays God will count.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:34
> Surely those who disbelieve, hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and then die as disbelievers; Allah will never forgive them

**Expanded Commentary**

**When Dying Is the Defining Act**

Every clause in this verse has appeared before except the last two words: and then die as disbelievers. That is the hinge. The verse's terror is not in the charges — they are the sūrah's standing indictment — but in the file being closed with the charges still standing. Death, in the Qur'an's accounting, is the sealing of the ledger: whatever state a soul is in at the end is the state the audit reads. The verse is thus not a sentence on the living disbeliever, whatever his crimes; it is a sentence on a finished life. As long as breath remains, the file is open, the future tense of the previous verse still hovers, and this verse's grammar — those who die as — is precisely the description of what no living reader yet is.

**The One Category Left Outside**

The severity of the verdict aligns with the Qur'an's hardest doctrine, stated in the women's sūrah: "Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with Him ˹in worship˺, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills. And whoever associates others with Allah has indeed committed a grave sin." (4:48). The logic of the exception is relational, not arbitrary. Forgiveness presumes a relation — an offended party who can be turned to; the one who dies having refused the relation, blocking others from it to the end, has declined the address forgiveness travels by. Every other sin — and the Qur'an's catalogue is unsparing — is a wound inside a relationship that can still be healed. The dying-in-the-refusal is the refusal of the healer. That, and not the size of any particular crime, is why this verse and 4:48 stand where they do.

**Khālid ibn al-Walīd, the Standing Counter-Example**

The verse's category is not a caste, and history's proof was one of the most famous men of the first generation. Khālid ibn al-Walīd spent years on the opposite side — a commander in the Makkan cavalry that mauled the Muslims at Uḥud — a man who disbelieved, fought, and hindered with the best of them. Then he walked into Madinah and embraced the faith he had fought, and the amnesty the sūrah promised at its opening — believe, do good, and the former file is covered — was honoured in him to a degree that embarrasses every pessimistic reading of this verse. He became one of Islam's greatest commanders, revered by the tradition as the Sword of God, and died in his bed decades later with not one wound on his chest — remarking, in the words the memory preserved, that there was no spot on his body unscarred except where the swords had not found him. The verse of the dying-disbeliever never touched him, because he did not die one. The door's proof is biographical.

**An Opening Returned Sealed**

Read the verse beside the sūrah's first, and the ring closes with a darker metal. The sūrah opened: those who disbelieve and hinder — their deeds void. It now closes: those who disbelieve and hinder and die so — never forgiven. The same charges; the added clause is only death; and yet the difference is everything, for the opening verse's voiding is a loss, while this verse's verdict is a lock. Between the two verses the sūrah has laid out the entire interval — the battles, the mercy, the testing, the offers — and the structure teaches the one lesson the interval exists to teach: identical lives can end in opposite verdicts, and the only variable is what happens before the dying. The ring is not a literary flourish; it is the shape of the human situation, drawn in the sūrah's own frame.

**The Absoluteness of "Never"**

The verse's negative is the Arabic lan — the particle of absolute, unqualified negation: Allah will never forgive them. The Qur'an uses its strongest word here, and the tradition read the strength as part of the mercy, oddly enough. A warning with loopholes is not a warning; a judge who hints that the last category might be re-litigated after death has told the dying that procrastination is safe. The absoluteness of the never is what makes the while-you-breathe urgent — the verse is severe precisely because it wants no one to need it. And intercession, the loophole every culture hopes for, is closed by the same architecture: the Qur'an's throne verse had already asked, "Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?" (2:255). No advocate takes a case the Client declined while the Client was still offering settlement.

**Why God Publishes the Verdict**

The tradition kept returning to a question about verses like this: why does God publish the verdict at all, since the publication grieves the living and arrives too late to reform the dead? The answer they gave is visible in the verse's grammar: the terror of the verse is future-tense and conditional, and everyone reading it still holds the condition. Publication of the worst case is an act of care toward everyone not yet in it — the physician's chart of the untreated disease, hung where the patient can read it. The verse exists to make itself unnecessary: every reader who turns is a reader it never applied to. That is why the Qur'an follows its hardest verdicts with its widest invitations, and why this sūrah's remaining verses turn, at once, to counsel for the believers — the door still open behind them, and the next word about how to walk through it.

**What the Verse Asks the Believer to Feel**

Doctrine settles in the affections last, and this verse asks for a strange double feeling that only religion can hold: the believer mourns the verdict and trusts it completely. He does not gloat — the Prophet wept for the people who rejected him, and his example forbids the smirk of the saved. He does not edit — the verdict stands, and the modern instinct to soften every hard verse into metaphor dissolves the urgency the verse exists to create. What he does is hold both: grief for every soul that exits inside the refusal, and trust that the Judge who died on no one's behalf never errs in the audit. And then, because the verse's condition is still open for everyone breathing — including the hard-hearted person he prays for — he turns the verse's terror into petition: not never, O Lord, for anyone I love; not never, while there is breath to ask.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:35
> So do not falter or cry for peace, for you will have the upper hand and Allah is with you. And He will never let your deeds go to waste

**Expanded Commentary**

**No Faltering at the Top of the Hill**

The verse addresses a specific moment in war — the moment of advantage — and the temptation that arrives with it: faltering, the Arabic noun for weakness, the shoulders that drop when the finish line appears and the body decides, without consulting the will, that the race has been won. The command: do not falter or cry for peace, for you will have the upper hand (47:35). The scene is precise: a community that has endured years of the lower hand, suddenly holding the higher one, and discovering — as every long-oppressed party discovers — that victory tests characters that defeat never reached. The verse names the test before the community can fail it: strength is not for surrendering from.

**The Command in Its Century**

The reading of the peace clause occupied the jurists, and the discussion is itself instructive. The classical understanding held that the verse forbade suing for terms while in a position of strength — a command for its hour, when a young community kept being offered peace as a trap, its confidence the real target. But the Prophet's own later career complicates any absolute reading, and the jurists knew it: at Ḥudaybiyah, in the year 6/628, the Prophet concluded a treaty with the Quraysh from a position of clear strength — a pact the Muslims chafed at, and which the Qur'an itself, in the opening of the very next sūrah, called a clear triumph (48:1). Al-Qurṭubī and others resolved the tension with the principle this sūrah has already taught: verses are reconciled by circumstance before they are resolved by abrogation — a ruling for the situation of weakness-to-strength in battle, not a prohibition of treaties as such. The community's law emerges from the cases held together, not from either alone.

**Ḥudaybiyah: the "Defeat" That Was a Triumph**

The Ḥudaybiyah episode deserves its place beside this verse, because it shows the verse's wisdom in a form no legal discussion can. The Muslims came strong; the Quraysh stalled, blocked the pilgrimage, and negotiated terms that read, on paper, like capitulation — the believers to return home without completing the rites, grievances deferred, the treaty lopsided in words. The community's own veterans could not read it without grief, and the Qur'an answered by naming the thing its real name: a manifest victory. Within two years the apparent setback had converted the peninsula — peace bought at the price of pride, and paid back in the currency of access, witness, and time. The lesson completes this verse: God's promise to the strong does not mean treaties are treason; it means the terms of every treaty are weighed by a Hand the negotiators cannot see.

**The With-ness Clause**

Then the verse gives its reason, and the reason is not matériel: and Allah is with you (47:35). The Qur'an's maʿiyya — with-ness — is the presence that decided Badr against odds, steadied the line at Uḥud for those who held, and is promised in this sūrah to the community that stands up for Him. Note what the clause does to the faltering temptation: fear at the top of the hill is a question — what if we lose this? — and with-ness is the answer before the question forms. The verse does not say Allah will make you win; it says Allah is with you — the same word the Qur'an gives to the patient, the righteous, the doers of good — and with-ness is the believer's victory condition before any battle's outcome is known. The community that grasps this can neither falter at the top nor despair at the bottom.

**He Will Never Let Your Deeds Go to Waste**

The verse's closing clause answers the quieter fear that drives the louder one. Behind the temptation to settle from strength is an accountant's anxiety: what will all this fighting have been for, if we trade it away now? God answers the anxiety at its root: He will never let your deeds go to waste (47:35). The verb behind the promise is the sūrah's own verdict-verb — the one that voided the opponents' deeds time after time — here negated absolutely over the believers: the one account that cannot be closed. Every prayer said on the road, every hardship borne, every honest stroke fought — filed, preserved, un-shaved. The sūrah has said it before of the martyrs — He will never render their deeds void — and now says it to the living on the eve of terms: the ledger's safety does not depend on the settlement's wording. A community that believes this can negotiate from strength without terror, because its wealth was never in the terms.

**When the Enemy Actually Wants Peace**

Honesty about this verse requires its companion, and the Qur'an supplies it in the battle sūrah: "If the enemy is inclined towards peace, make peace with them. And put your trust in Allah. Indeed, He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing." (8:61). The two verses together form the ethic whole: this one forbids suing for peace from strength; that one commands accepting it when genuinely offered. What the pair outlaws is not peace but the coward's version of it — the settlement purchased by the strong out of fear, which teaches the enemy that pressure works and mortgages every weaker party's future. What the pair honours is peace as a real good, received with trust when it is real. The believer's martial ethic, drawn from the whole, is neither belligerence nor pacifism but a settled strength that neither begs for terms nor rejects them.

**The Verse's Word to Every Strong Moment**

The verse's register is military, but its address is every ascendancy. Every person and every community eventually holds the upper hand — in a marriage, a firm, a negotiation, an argument — and the verse's counsel transfers whole: do not falter, do not surrender from strength, remember the With-You and the un-shaved ledger. Strength is a test administered to find out what a soul does when it finally can do anything: forgive out of freedom, or fleece out of fear; hold the line for the right, or cash it in for the comfortable. The verse tells the believer what his strength is for — the mission he was strong enough to serve — and assures him that the record of serving it is beyond anyone's power to diminish, including his own. That assurance, held, is the difference between the strong who settle and the strong who stand.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:36
> This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But if you are faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, He will grant you your ˹full˺ reward, and will not ask you ˹to donate all˺ your wealth

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Diagnosis in Two Words**

The verse opens with the Qur'an's bluntest description of earthly life: no more than play and amusement — laʿib wa-lahw in the Arabic, two words drawn from the vocabulary of children. Play is activity with no consequence beyond itself; amusement is the laughter that accompanies it. The diagnosis is not that the world is evil — the Qur'an never says that, and forbids saying it — but that the world is unserious: it cannot bear the weight souls keep placing on it. A toy is not wicked; it is a category error as a life's purpose. The verse's genius is the qualifier no more than: not that play and amusement exist in the world, but that, as a total description, this is what the thing is — and a creature built for eternity, spending its whole capital on a game, has mistaken the fairground for the destination.

**The Full Portrait, Cast in Iron**

Elsewhere the Qur'an expands the two words into its most complete portrait of the world's texture: "Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children. This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff." (57:20). Every item in the inventory deserves notice: play, amusement, and then luxury — comfort as a programme; mutual boasting — the race run in other people's eyes; competition in wealth and children — the two currencies the audience actually traded in. And then the rain: the world's freshest season, the spring that makes every farm look eternal — and the same rain's aftermath, yellowing, chaff. The portrait's honesty is exact: the Qur'an is not denying the green; it is reporting its half-life.

**Play for One, Provision for Another**

How can the world be play and, in the same Book, a provision, a test, and a place of witness? Because the verse's verdict tracks the viewer, not the valley. For the soul that makes the world its end, the world obliges by behaving like a toy — glittering, endless, and finally weightless. For the soul that treats the world as a road — eating from it thankfully, spending from it justly, passing through it deliberately — the same world becomes provision, and the Qur'an describes the destination beside it in the same breath it names the play: "This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But the Hereafter is indeed the real life, if only they knew." (29:64), and again: "far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter for those mindful ˹of Allah˺. Will you not then understand?" (6:32). Real life — al-ḥayawān, the life that lives. The verse under commentary is not a curse on the world; it is an offer to upgrade one's address.

**The Conditional Alternative**

Mid-verse, the diagnosis turns conditional: if you believe and are mindful. The two verbs are the sūrah's whole prescription in miniature — faith, the allegiance of the heart; mindfulness, taqwā, the protective awareness that fences daily conduct — and both are aimed at the same enemy the verse has just named: absorption. Belief breaks the world's monopoly on attention; taqwā keeps it broken through the workweek. Then the promise: He will grant you your ˹full˺ reward — ujūrakum, wages in the plural, paid entire (47:36). The believer does not renounce reward; he relocates it. The world's payer pays in impressions that fade; the verse names a Payer whose wages do not, and its condition is the only currency He accepts.

**Without Asking You to Donate All Your Wealth**

Then the verse's last clause performs a quiet, astonishing courtesy: God will grant the full reward, and will not ask you ˹to donate all˺ your wealth (47:36). Read it twice. The Owner of everything — the One who lent every coin in every pocket in the first place — asks, for the entire arrangement of paradise-sized wages, only a fraction of what He Himself supplied. The mandatory alms, the zakāh, comes to a small share of certain holdings; beyond it, giving is voluntary. The verse's tact is the theology: God is not collecting revenue; He is offering a trade in which the buyer contributes tokens from the Seller's own gift shop. An economy like that makes stinginess not merely a vice but an absurdity — hoarding the shop's tokens while the shop is handing out its goods — and the sūrah's next verse will say exactly that, pressing the point with a hypothetical that exposes the hoarder to himself.

**A Word for the Affluent Believer**

The verse's first audience included merchants of real means, and its meaning for them was not asceticism. Nothing here commands the selling of houses; the command is about weight — what the heart assigns as ultimate. The believer may own the fairground without making it home; the tradition's shorthand for the needed posture is gratitude — the act that converts play into provision by naming the Giver at every use of the gift. The ungrateful owner of much is the verse's man of play, whatever his prayer calendar; the grateful user of little is already living the real life in preview. The test, for every comfortable reader since, is brutally simple and applies to this verse's own reader: if my wealth vanished tonight, would my religion notice?

**The Sūrah's Economics, Half-Disclosed**

Placed near the sūrah's close, the verse is the first panel of a final triptych on wealth: the world's weightlessness named, the divine tact displayed, and — in the verses remaining — the refusal to give answered with the revelation of what refusal costs. The sūrah has spent itself on voided deeds, preserved deeds, and rewarded deeds; now, at the end, it turns to the one subject that tests every doctrine above: money. What a soul does with its surplus, this final passage assumes, is the readout of everything it professed in the middle verses — and the reading is about to be taken.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:37
> If He were to do so and pressure you, you would withhold and He would bring out your resentment

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Hypothetical That Reveals the Tact**

The verse runs a thought experiment on its audience, and the experiment is the lesson. If God were to do so — to ask for the wealth outright — and pressure you, you would withhold. Every clause is deliberate. God asks nothing out of need; the pressure described is the hypothesis, not the reality. God does not press; the insistence imagined is what a demanding lord would do, and God is not one. The reader is made to watch a counterfactual in which the divine tact is removed — and to see, in that mirror, both his own frailty and the gentleness that spares him from meeting it. The verse is a confession written for us before we made it, and an acquittal of God before we thought to accuse Him of greed.

**The Clamp**

The condition the hypothetical would expose has a name in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary: shuḥḥ, stinginess — the word's root carries the sense of clamping, of holding shut. Stinginess in the Qur'an is not a personality quirk; it is a disease of the hand with a spiritual aetiology: the heart's secret belief that giving diminishes it, that security is a stockpile, that tomorrow is its own to guarantee. The verse's insight into human capacity is unsentimental: pressed hard enough, most givers would clamp — not because they never gave, but because their giving operated inside comfort's margin. Pressure, in this verse, is the X-ray that shows where the margin was. The believer is not shamed for having the limit; he is informed of it, gently, by the One who calibrated His asking around it.

**Why the Exposure Would Hurt**

The verse says He would bring out your resentment — and the bringing-out, not the loss, is the sting. To be shown, on one's own pulse, that beneath the giving sat a resentment of the Asker — that the professed love of God had a price ceiling and a temper — is a humiliation the soul could not survive intact. And here is the verse's deepest mercy, read in reverse: God's light asking is what keeps the exposure theoretical. He asks a fraction, seasonally, from the surplus — an asking calibrated so that the average believer can give without ever discovering, on his own pulse, how much pressure it would have taken to turn him away from the door. The gentle tithe is not God's need; it is God's shield, placed between the believer and the self-knowledge that would crush him.

**The Asking Is From What He Gave**

Hold the verse's premise in view: to do so means to ask for the wealth — all of it — and every coin of it arrived by His allocation. The Qur'an states the ownership plainly elsewhere, and prices the hoarding of what was lent: "And do not let those who ˹greedily˺ withhold Allah's bounties think it is good for them—in fact, it is bad for them! They will be leashed ˹by their necks˺ on the Day of Judgment with whatever ˹wealth˺ they used to withhold." (3:180). The leash image is the bookkeeping made physical: what a man clamps in this life accompanies him, worn, into the next. Against that ledger, the actual asking — a percentage of certain holdings, a share the Owner could have set at one hundred — stands revealed as almost embarrassing in its modesty. The servant is never asked for his own; he is offered receipts on the Master's.

**Training Against the Clamp**

Because the verse diagnoses the clamp, it also implies the physiotherapy. Generosity, in the Qur'an's pedagogy, is a muscle: it strengthens under regular load and atrophies in disuse, and the tradition's programme follows the anatomy. Give on schedule — the zakāh, fixed, non-negotiable, the baseline contraction. Give beyond schedule — the ṣadaqa, voluntary, varied, teaching the hand new ranges. Give in small denominations often rather than grandly once, for the habit is the target, not the amount. And give while the clamp is only mildly uncomfortable, because the verse's hypothetical teaches where untrained hands end: pressed beyond their training, they shut. The believer who has practiced at the margin finds, when the real pressure arrives — the neighbour's crisis, the community's famine — that his hand opens by reflex. That reflex is the whole curriculum, and it is buildable, which is the good news hiding in the verse's grim hypothetical.

**The Verse's Companion, One Step On**

The sūrah's final verse will complete this one: the call to spend goes out openly, the stingy are named, and the reflexive law is announced — whoever is stingy is stingy toward himself, since God is the Rich and the spender is the beneficiary of his own spending. That law is already latent here: the hypothetical's exposure would reveal the stinginess to the stingy man himself, first of all, and the man is the only party the revealing could cost. God loses nothing in the transaction, ever; the clamp closes only on the clamper's own hand. Read this way, the verse is less a threat than an anatomy lesson in advance of the surgery: here is what your hand would do under full pressure; here is the gentle regimen that will make sure you never have to find out on the table.

**What the Counterfactual Praises**

One last reading, the one the verse's first audience of generous souls deserved. There were believers — the tradition never lets the sūrah's darker verses eclipse them — who, when the pressing came, did not clamp: who gave until the Prophet had to tell them to keep something for their households; who, in the crisis expeditions, brought what they had and wept that they had no more. For them, this verse's hypothetical was not a warning but a hidden commendation: you, He implies, are the ones the experiment would not have exposed. The verse stands in the record with its dual honouring — mercy for the frail, whose limits God accommodates; applause, between the lines, for the few whose margins had been trained away. The reader closing this verse can choose which of its two audiences to spend his life training for.

## Sūrah Muḥammad 47:38
> Here you are, being invited to donate ˹a little˺ in the cause of Allah. Still some of you withhold. And whoever does so, it is only to their own loss. For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺. If you ˹still˺ turn away, He will replace you with another people. And they will not be like you

**Expanded Commentary**

**"Here You Are!" — the Summons That Stops the Crowd**

The sūrah's final verse opens with the Qur'an's most arresting vocative: Here you are! — hāʾantum, the grammar of a speaker who has caught the audience mid-motion, mid-excuse, mid-departure. Not O people, not O believers — but you, there, now: the invitation to donate in the cause of Allah is issued into a room where some of the invited are already looking at the door. The rhetorical urgency is the sūrah's signature; it opened its account with the hinderers and closes it by summoning the hesitating, and both speeches share the same assumption — that religion is a present-tense demand, arriving while the audience is doing something else. The verse thus begins by ending the pretence that the question of giving can be deferred: you are invited. The invitation is audible. The moment is this one.

**The Reflexive Law of Stinginess**

Then comes the verse's central law, stated with a precision that ends a thousand arguments: whoever does so, it is only to their own loss (47:38). The logic is reflexive — the act rebounds on the actor — and the Qur'an grounds the law elsewhere in the transaction's very design: "Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah. Whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged." (2:272). God needs no subsidy; the cause is not financed by the donation. What giving actually does is move wealth from the perishable column of the giver's ledger to the permanent one — from holdings that die with the estate into accounts that survive the grave. The withholder, in the verse's arithmetic, is a man refusing to deposit into his own name, while imagining he is refusing a beggar. No theft occurs; only a loss, entered against the only party who could have gained.

**The Rich and the Poor**

Why does the law hold? Because of the verse's next pair, the most radical inequality in all theology: Allah is the Self-Sufficient — al-Ghanī, rich beyond any need or deficit — whereas you stand in need — the fuqarāʾ, existentially poor, creatures whose next breath is drawn on credit (47:38). The Qur'an states the pairing as a universal address to mankind: "O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy." (35:15). The implication for the verse's summons is total: the cause of God is not God's pension fund; He is not soliciting. Every dinar of the religion's budget was minted by the Rich and is spent for the poor — visibly so. The early mystics read the pairing inward: al-Tustarī, commenting on this very language, taught that knowledge of one's poverty before God is the knowledge that wealth is in God — the believer's true fortune is the recognition itself, which no market can price and no withholder can keep.

**Replacement**

Then the verse makes the stakes historical: If you ˹still˺ turn away, He will replace you with another people. And they will not be like you (47:38). The Qur'an states the doctrine in the same register when the march is refused: "If you do not march forth, He will afflict you with a painful torment and replace you with other people. You are not harming Him in the least." (9:39). The teaching is one of the Book's most bracing: God's mission does not depend on any generation's cooperation. The community of the moment holds a commission, not a monopoly; refusal does not stop the work, it only reassigns the work — and the honour with it. The first audience watched the principle operate in real time: the call went out, and the answerers — the Companions who spent and marched — became the generation the world remembers, while whole tribes that hesitated read about themselves later, replaced. The verse's fear is not for God's project; it is for the replaced.

**"They Will Not Be Like You" — the Warning Wearing a Compliment**

The clause deserves a slower reading. They will not be like you: the threat and the flattery sit in the same five words. On the surface, a warning — your successors will surpass you; you will be the comparison's unfavourable side. But the clause can be read as a challenge in the imperative mood: be the people the verse cannot say this about. Every generation of believers since has stood under this sentence in exactly that double light — inheritors of a commission that could be reassigned at any season, and authors of a record the next people will be measured against. The tradition drew the pastoral point without sentimentality: vigour is renewable, replacement is real, and the community that treats its faith as an heirloom rather than a task should reread this verse with its genealogies in hand. Islam survived fourteen centuries not because any one century deserved it, but because in every century some people answered like the first ones.

**The Sūrah's Last Ledger**

Read as a whole, Sūrah Muḥammad has been one long audit of accounts: deeds voided and deeds preserved, wages granted in full, wealth asked in a fraction, and — in this last clause — the very membership of a people weighed. The economics are consistent from first verse to last: everything is lent; nothing owned outright; the only real transactions are the ones moved across death's border by faith, obedience, and spending in the cause. The sūrah opened with people who blocked the road to God and closed with people hesitating at the toll booth on it — and it addressed both in the same merciful grammar, warnings issued while the issuing still mattered. The verse that ends the chapter does not end the audit; it hands the ledger to the reader: Here you are. Called, addressed, resourced by the Rich, poor in oneself, replaceable — and, while breath remains, entirely free to answer.
