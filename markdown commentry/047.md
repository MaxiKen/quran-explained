# Sūrah Muḥammad (Chapter 47) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Threshold of Statehood and the Inevitability of Conflict**

The historical canvas of Sūrah Muḥammad is the tense, dangerous interlude between the arrival of the Muslim refugees in Madinah and the outbreak of the Battle of Badr. For over a decade in Makkah, the believers had been subjected to physical torture, confiscation of wealth, and relentless boycott without being permitted to retaliate. Upon reaching Madinah, the community was no longer an underground devotional circle; it was a sovereign sanctuary facing imminent, total military assault from the Quraysh, while surrounded by treacherous factions of hypocrites (*al-munāfiqūn*) within the oasis who sought every opportunity to sabotage the state. Sūrah Muḥammad descended to steel the resolve of the believers. It stripped away all pacifist illusions: when oppressors wage war to extinguish the freedom of faith, the believers must not falter or cry out for humiliating peace while they have the upper hand (*“So do not weaken and call for peace while you are superior; and Allah is with you,”* 47:35).

**The Structural Anatomy of the Discourse**

The architecture of Sūrah Muḥammad moves between military jurisprudence, psychological radiography, and Last-Day realities. The opening movement (1–3) establishes the grand of being divide: those who disbelieve and obstruct the path of God have their deeds rendered completely void, while those who believe in what was revealed to Muḥammad have their sins expiated and their inner state rectified (*aṣlaḥa bālahum*), because the deniers follow falsehood while the believers follow the truth from their Lord. The second movement (4–19) codifies the laws of active warfare: when meeting the combatant enemy in battle, strike with decisive firmness until the threat is broken, then secure the bonds of captives, after which they may be released either by gracious generosity (*mannan*) or by ransom (*fidā'an*), until the war lays down its burdens (47:4). It honors the fallen martyrs, promising that God will never let their deeds be lost, but will guide them, amend their state, and admit them into the Paradise He has already made known to them. It delivers the celebrated covenantal formula of victory in verse 7: *“O you who have believed, if you support Allah, He will support you and plant firmly your feet.”* It contrasts the rivers of Paradise—pure water that never stagnates, milk whose taste never changes, wine delicious to the drinkers, and purified honey—against the inmates of Hell forced to drink scalding water that tears their bowels to pieces. The third movement (20–34) conducts an unsparing psychological autopsy of the hypocrites: when an ordinary peaceful sūrah is revealed, they appear compliant, but the moment a decisive chapter mandating military defense descends, *“you see those in whose hearts is disease looking at you with a look of one overcome by death!”* (47:20). It exposes their secret conspiracies with the enemies of Islam, warning them that their treachery is known and that the angels of death will strike their faces and their backs at the moment of expiration. The closing movement (35–38) summons the community to financial mobilization, reminding them that worldly life is but play and diversion, and warning them that stinginess harms only the miser's own soul: *“And Allah is the Free of need, while you are the needy. And if you turn away, He will replace you with another people; then they will not be the likes of you!”* (47:38).

## Verse 47:1

> Those who disbelieve and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, He will render their deeds void

**Two Verbs of Damage**

The sūrah opens its account with a pair, and the pairing is the diagnosis. First the private act: they disbelieve. Then the public one: they hinder others from the Way of Allah. The Arabic verb rendered "hinder" — ṣadda ʿan — carries both turning away oneself and pushing others off the road, and the verse intends the double weight: these are people who left the path and then built their lives across it. Sin in the Qur'an is rarely a private hobby for long; unbelief, once adopted, becomes an interest with a maintenance schedule — pressure on the wavering, mockery of the committed, gates kept and guarded. The verse's first lesson is structural: what a person does with the road behind him — block it or leave it open — counts as much as which way he himself walked.

The Qur'an explains the mechanism elsewhere, and the explanation belongs beside this verse. Of those who tamper with sacred months it says: "Their evil deeds have been made appealing to them." (9:37).

**Ashes in the Wind**

The Qur'an gives this voiding a portrait, and the portrait is worth keeping beside the statute: "The parable of the deeds of those who disbelieve in their Lord is that of ashes fiercely blown away by wind on a stormy day. They will gain nothing from what they have earned. That is ˹truly˺ the farthest one can stray." (14:18). Note the materials. Ashes look like substance — they have mass, colour, a history of having been something. But they are what remains after combustion, and the first honest wind reveals them. The stormy day is the Day of Accounting, and the parable's promise is exact: they will gain nothing. Not a little; nothing. The striver without God has been labouring, in the parable's terms, at the manufacture of ash.

**The Exception the Same Sūrah Will State**

There is a second verdict in this chapter, placed like a pillar at the end of its battle verse, and the two belong in one view: "And those who are martyred in the cause of Allah, He will never render their deeds void." (47:4). The grammar of the sūrah's opening is thus bracketed: deeds voided for those who blocked the road; deeds sealed, never voided, for those who died opening it. The contrast teaches what the voiding is really about. Deeds lose their weight when they are aimed at a self that answers to nothing above it; deeds keep their weight when they are spent for the One who weighs. Direction, not volume, decides the reading on the scale.

## Verse 47:2

> As for those who believe, do good, and have faith in what has been revealed to Muḥammad—which is the truth from their Lord—He will absolve them of their sins and improve their condition

**The Mirror Image of the Opening Verse**

The sūrah's second verse reverses its first with surgical symmetry. There: disbelieve and hinder — deeds voided. Here: believe and do good — sins covered and condition repaired. The same divine accounting stands behind both sentences, and the two verses together form a completed diptych: one panel shows the loss, the other the gain. Nothing in either sentence is arbitrary; the outcomes track the inputs with the neatness of a ledger, and the reader is being taught, from the sūrah's first breath, that the Day of Judgment is not a lottery but an audit with two possible findings.

**What Islam Wipes Out**

The Prophet stated the doctrine of this verse as an amnesty, and the report is one of the tradition's most quoted. ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ — a man who had spent years opposing the Muslims before his own conversion — came to pledge allegiance and asked that his pardon be written in as a condition. The Prophet replied: "Are you not aware of the fact that Islam wipes out all the previous (misdeeds)?" adding that emigration and pilgrimage do the same (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 121). The amnesty's breadth is the point. A convert does not drag his old file into his new life; the file is destroyed at the border. Men like ʿAmr, and later commanders who had once drawn swords against the Prophet, built the first Muslim state on exactly this clause — proof that the verse was understood, from the beginning, as a real transaction and not a metaphor.

**Evil Replaced by Good**

The Qur'an elsewhere reveals a second stage past covering, and it belongs in any full reading of this verse: "As for those who repent, believe, and do good deeds, they are the ones whose evil deeds Allah will change into good deeds." (25:70). Not merely cancelled — converted.

**The Truth From Their Lord**

The verse's middle clause states the object of faith once more, in its own register: what was revealed to Muḥammad "which is the truth from their Lord" (47:2). The clause does double work. It protects the faith from becoming a personality cult — the object is a revelation and its truth, not a man's charisma. And it locates the truth's origin: from their Lord — their Lord, the possessive that the believers had already internalised before the verse was spoken. Belief, on this description, is not the wager of a gambler but the allegiance of a creature who has recognised whose he is; the absolution and the repair are simply what that Lord does for His own once they have come home.

## Verse 47:3

> This is because the disbelievers follow falsehood, while the believers follow the truth from their Lord. This is how Allah shows people their true state ˹of faith˺

**Truth "From Their Lord" — the Possessive That Decides**

The believers follow the truth "from their Lord" (47:3), and the possessive carries doctrine. The truth in question is not a discovery the believers own — an insight they generated, a system they can improve at will — but something sent to them by the One who owns them. The order of possession is reversed from the modern instinct: they do not have a Lord because they found a truth; they have a truth because they have a Lord, and He sent it. That is why the truth holds them up rather than the other way around. A manufactured truth collapses with its manufacturers — the fate of every ideology that promised permanence and delivered a decade — but a sent truth survives its followers' failures, because its structural support is not in them.

**The Because That Goes to the Root**

The verse opens with a "because", and the logic it supplies runs deeper than a reason for the previous two sentences; it reaches the root of both verdicts. Deeds are voided or sins covered not by an arbitrary selection but because of what each group follows. The Qur'an grades a life, before anything else, by its direction of travel — by the thing behind the marching. Men are followers by constitution; everyone walks behind something: an idea, a hunger, an ancestor, a fear, a book. The verse's quiet anthropological claim is that the following is not in question — only the object is. Choose the voided object and the deeds go with it; choose the standing one and the same feet arrive somewhere permanent.

**The Good Tree as This Verse's Exhibit**

The most famous of the likenings deserves a place here, because it is the portrait of the very people this verse calls the followers of ḥaqq: "Do you not see how Allah compares a good word to a good tree? Its root is firm and its branches reach the sky, yielding its fruit in every season by the Will of its Lord." (14:24). From it grows the doing of good, which is the fruit, and the fruit arrives "in every season", which is the Qur'an's way of saying that a rooted life produces across all weathers.

## Verse 47:4

> So when you meet the disbelievers ˹in battle˺, strike ˹their˺ necks until you have thoroughly subdued them, then bind them firmly. Later ˹free them either as˺ an act of grace or by ransom until the war comes to an end. So will it be. Had Allah willed, He ˹Himself˺ could have inflicted punishment on them. But He does ˹this only to˺ test some of you by means of others. And those who are martyred in the cause of Allah, He will never render their deeds void

**The Sequence Keeps Captives Off the Battlefield**

The order of operations carries a doctrine: strike until thoroughly subdued, then bind. Prisoners are taken only after resistance has collapsed, not during it, and the Qur'an states the reason elsewhere in a verse revealed after the battle of Badr: "It is not fit for a prophet that he should take captives until he has thoroughly subdued the land." (8:67). The concern is practical and moral at once: captives taken mid-battle become leverage, hostages, and the temptation to fight for prisoners rather than for the end of the fighting. The thorough-subduing clause turns the fighter's mind from capture to conclusion — war's purpose, in this sūrah's grammar, is to end war, and the sequence serves the purpose.

**Grace or Ransom: The Only Two Exits**

Once the fighting is done and the captives bound, the verse sets the menu: release as an act of grace, or ransom, until the war lays down its burdens. No third category of reprisal killing is commanded; and the Prophet's practice, recorded across the campaigns, was precisely this menu — free releases for the poor and for those who could teach, ransom payments, and exchanges. After Badr, the Qur'an itself instructed him to speak to the captives: "O Prophet! Tell the captives in your custody, 'If Allah finds goodness in your hearts, He will give you better than what has been taken from you, and forgive you. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.'" (8:70). The jurists of the later tradition, reading this verse, kept its flexibility: the commander decides between the exits case by case, because the verse legislates outcomes, not a single procedure. Rigidity was read into it by nobody who had read it closely.

**He Could Have Punished Them Without You**

Then the verse lifts the curtain on its own theology: had Allah willed, He could have inflicted the punishment Himself (47:4). The angels fought at Badr in the Qur'an's own account; armies could have been stopped by winds as ʿĀd's was; God needs no infantry. The fighting is therefore not God's necessity but the believers' test — and the Qur'an states the principle in the verse of the table-spread: "If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but ˹He willed otherwise˺, that He might test you with what He has given ˹each of˺ you. So compete with one another in doing good." (5:48). This is the hardest and cleanest theodicy of war in the Qur'an: conflict exists so that what is in people may come out where it can be seen and repaid. Cowardice, courage, mercy, greed — battle is the exam hall of the hidden.

## Verse 47:5

> He will guide them ˹to their reward˺, improve their condition

**A Verse Whose Only Actor Is God**

Read the verse slowly and count the performers. He will guide them; He will improve their condition. The fallen are the only humans in the sentence, and they do nothing — they are carried. In a passage that began with commands to fighters, this little verse changes key completely: the war verses are full of human verbs, and the reward verses have one subject only. The lesson is embedded in the grammar. Whatever the battle demanded of the living, what follows death is not another achievement but a reception; the martyr has finished acting, and the One who commanded the acting takes over every remaining step.

**Alive, With Their Lord**

The Qur'an does not leave the martyrs' condition to inference. Elsewhere it states it outright, forbidding the living to call the fallen in God's cause dead: they are alive with their Lord, provided for, even as their families stood at the graveside (2:154); and it adds that they rejoice in what God has given them, and are glad on behalf of those who have not yet joined them (3:169). This is the doctrine behind the little word "them" in this verse — the objects of God's guiding and repairing are conscious, receiving beings, not memories. The Islamic tradition built on these verses its whole understanding of the shahīd: not a man deleted, but a man relocated, with the accounting of his deeds still open and accruing.

**The Same Repair Promised to the Living**

The second verb repeats, word for word, the promise made to living believers earlier in this sūrah: God "will absolve them of their sins and improve their condition" (47:2). The repetition is a stitch joining the two halves of the sūrah's opening: the believers at home and the believers in the grave receive the same repairing God. Nothing about dying for the faith changes the fundamental transaction — sins covered, state set aright — it only accelerates and perfects it. For the recruit weighing his life against his death, the verse levels the terror: both states are in the same hands, under the same promise, and the hands have a record. The improvement the martyr receives is simply the believer's improvement completed, without the interference of a remaining lifetime of temptation.

**The Order of the Welcome**

Put the verses in a row and a sequence appears: He will guide them, improve their condition, and admit them into Paradise (47:6). Three steps, all divine, in order: the showing of the road, the setting aright of the traveller, and then the entry. The order is ceremonial, like the reception of a returning envoy — met on the road, honoured, escorted, and finally brought inside. Nothing in the sequence is hurried, and nothing is done by the guest. The Qur'an could have compressed all three into one word, rewarded; it chose a procession, and the choice tells the reader what kind of God is doing the receiving: One who lingers over the homecoming.

## Verse 47:6

> and admit them into Paradise, having made it known to them

**Admission as an Escort**

The verb is hospitality: He admits them — yudkhiluhum, causes them to enter. Not "grants them", not "allots them"; brings them in, the way a host brings a guest through his own door. The Qur'an's pictures of the last day are full of being driven, gathered, exposed; against that background, this word is almost domestic. The men who fell in battle — the sūrah's immediate subjects — are pictured not standing trial but entering a house, and the picture is the doctrine: the judgment they feared has already been settled by the sūrah's earlier verses; what remains is the welcome. The Arabic name for the destination carries its own picture: jannah, Paradise, comes from a root meaning to hide or to cover — a garden, in the oldest sense of the word, is growth so lush it conceals its own ground. Even the name, before any description begins, promises enclosure, shade, a world that wraps its residents rather than displaying them. And the verse's closing clause — having made it known to them — belongs to the same picture: a place whose residents arrive already familiar with it, greeted not as strangers at a gate but as heirs walking onto their own land.

**The Recognition the Prophet Described**

The Prophet gave this verse a narrative, and it is one of the tradition's most vivid. He said that when the believers are saved from the Fire, they will be stopped at a bridge between the Garden and the Fire, and there they will settle the injustices done among them in the world. When they are purified, permission to enter is given — and then: "By Him in Whose Hands the life of Muhammad is, everybody will recognise his dwelling in the Paradise better than he recognises his dwelling in this world" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2440).

## Verse 47:7

> O believers! If you stand up for Allah, He will help you and make your steps firm

**The Conditional That Governs the Community**

After the battle rules and the rewards, the sūrah states its engine: if you stand up for Allah, He will help you. The sentence structure places the whole communal destiny inside one conditional, and the placement is the teaching. The victory verses before and the doom verses after are not fate; they are consequences, switched on and off by a clause that begins with the believers' own act. A community that knows its history is conditional is protected from both of history's diseases: the arrogance that credits victories to itself, and the despair that reads defeats as abandonment. The condition named here is the cure for both — and the address, O believers, makes sure the sentence lands on the people who assume it is about someone else.

The phrase startles until it is read as the Qur'an reads it. Allah will certainly help those who stand up for Him." (22:40).

**Two Gifts: Rescue and Firmness**

The promise has two clauses, and they answer two different fears. He will help you — that is rescue, the intervention when the matter is beyond the community's strength. And He will make your steps firm — thabbit aqdāmakum in Arabic, literally make your feet stand fast — that is stability: the refusal of panic, the holding of the line, the mind that stays clear when the charge comes. Victory needs both. Armies have been saved by help and lost by wobble; the Qur'an promises the pair. At Badr, the Qur'an says, the help was even assigned staff — the angels were told before the battle: "I am with you. So make the believers stand firm." (8:12) — the firmness distributed to the fighters' hearts while the fighting was done through them.

**Make Our Steps Firm: The Prayer the Verse Teaches**

The phrase became the believers' own prayer, and the Qur'an records them praying it in the middle of their hardest day. Of the prophets and the faithful who fought beside them, it says: "And all they said was, 'Our Lord! Forgive our sins and excesses, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the disbelieving people.'" (3:147). The prayer's order is its theology: pardon before firmness, firmness before victory. Nobody asks an army to stand on unforgiven sin, and nobody asks God for an outcome while refusing the footing. The tradition took the phrase into every crisis since — the prayer whispered before every trial, the phrase a believer inherits as his own portable clause of this verse: the help is God's to give, and the asking is ours.

## Verse 47:8

> As for the disbelievers, may they be doomed and may He render their deeds void

**A Word Too Heavy for One English Word**

The first clause of the Arabic is taʿsan lahum — a construction of unusual force that translators have rendered "may they be doomed", "woe to them", "ruined are they". The root from which it is built carries, in the classical lexicons, a thesaurus of loss: ruin, misfortune, sorrow, defeat, revilement, ignominy, deprivation. The form is intensive; the verse compresses the whole dictionary into two words and drops it on one subject. This is the Qur'an's method with words of warning — it does not describe the loser's state from a menu; it picks the word whose sound and sense are the state. No single English phrase can carry it, which is why the translation keeps the invocation's rhythm: may they be doomed.

**Deeds Voided a Second Time**

The second clause repeats the sūrah's opening verdict word for word: He renders their deeds void. The repetition is not carelessness; legal documents repeat their operative clause, and this sūrah is a verdict document. The first statement (47:1) established the law; this one applies it; and the sūrah will repeat it twice more before closing (47:9, 47:28), like a stamp pressed at each page's foot. Repetition also serves the reader's memory: a doctrine this hard — that human effort can be real, costly, and finally weightless — must be stated more than once before proud minds will hold it. The verse is the stamp falling a second time on the page it will seal.

**The Symmetry of the Two Households**

Read 47:7 and 47:8 side by side and the theology of the passage stands exposed in its architecture. For the believers: help and firmness. For the disbelievers: doom and voided deeds. Four outcomes, two per household, and nothing arbitrary anywhere in the frame. The Qur'an loves this diptych form in its early Madinan sūrahs precisely because the young community needed the frame: persecution had inverted worldly appearances, making the believers look doomed and their opponents flourishing. The verses teach the members of a battered minority to read the ledger beneath the news — appearances are instalments, the audit is the total — and the lesson has lost none of its usefulness for every community since that has looked, from the outside, like the losing side.

**The Doom Already Rehearsed**

The verse's first audience could check the category against events. The Quraysh had driven the believers from their homes, planned assassinations, and finally met them at Badr — and the battle the Quraysh expected to win became, in the Qur'an's account, a day of help and firmness for the side they outnumbered. Uhud, the year after, ran the other way, and the sūrah's conditional explained even that: setbacks read by the light of 47:7 are not verdicts but tests, while the opponents' gains are the instalments of a different account. The verse trains this double vision: when the wicked prosper, the believer does not revise his theology; he files the prosperity where the verse files it — on the doomed account, accruing.

## Verse 47:9

> That is because they detest what Allah has revealed, so He has rendered their deeds void

**The Because of Detestation**

The verse supplies the root cause of the voiding, and the cause is not intellectual. They detest — kariha in Arabic, the verb of visceral aversion, the word for a thing the stomach turns from. The verse is not describing men who examined the revelation and found it unconvincing; it is describing men who hated it on arrival. That distinction organises the whole sūrah's psychology. Doubt asks questions; detestation mounts defences. The denier of this verse had reasons, arguments, charges — sorcery, forgery, ancient fables — but the verse strips the costume and shows the engine underneath: what was revealed was unwelcome, and what is unwelcome must be explained away at any cost. Unbelief, in this diagnosis, is not a conclusion; it is an allergy wearing a conclusion's clothes.

**The Third Stamp of the Same Verdict**

This is now the third time the sūrah has pronounced the voiding of deeds, and the repetition is structural, like the stamping of a document at every page. First the law was stated: those who disbelieve and hinder — deeds void (47:1). Then the verdict was read over the party: may they be doomed, deeds void (47:8). Now the cause is entered into the record: because they detested the revelation, deeds void (47:9). Each pass adds a layer — statute, sentence, motive — until the case is closed with the pathology documented. Repetition in the Qur'an is rarely redundancy; it is emphasis achieved through architecture, and the sūrah's architecture here teaches that the loss of deeds is not one punishment among many but the standing consequence, restated because the heart resists it.

**Deeds Wasted in Both Worlds**

The Qur'an is explicit that this voiding is not deferred to the Hereafter alone: "They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers." (3:22). The worldly half of the loss is visible in the pattern this sūrah keeps citing: schemes that collapse, alliances that betray, victories that rot on the branch. A project pursued against the grain of what God revealed carries its failure inside it from the start, like a building drawn on faulty geometry. The afterlife's half of the loss merely makes the ledger official. For the audience that watched the Muslim community grow while its own coalition corroded, the two halves of 3:22 were not a prophecy to await; they were the news.

## Verse 47:10

> Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those before them? Allah annihilated them, and a similar fate awaits the disbelievers

**The Command to Travel and See**

The Qur'an's recurring demand for evidence takes a surprising form here: travel. Not scripture, not argument — a field trip. The deniers are told to walk the land and inspect the endings of those who stood where they stand: confident, established, surrounded by power, and unimpressed by warnings. The demand assumes something remarkable about the world — that God has left His verdicts visible in it, that history's crime scenes are preserved as open files, and that any honest traveller can read them. Religion, in this verse, is not a retreat from evidence; it is an insistence on following the evidence farther than the comfortable want to go.

**Superior in Might, Richer in Monuments**

What the traveller will find, the Qur'an states elsewhere with the same vocabulary. Of the earlier peoples: "They were far superior in might; they cultivated the land and developed it more than these ˹Meccans˺ ever have. Their messengers came to them with clear proofs. Allah would have never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves." (30:9). And again, with the ruins itemised: "Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those ˹destroyed˺ before them? They were far superior in might and ˹richer in˺ monuments throughout the land. But Allah seized them for their sins, and they had no protector from Allah." (40:21). The repetition across sūrahs is deliberate. Every clause of the deniers' security — numbers, walls, works, legacy — is named in the record as having been tried already, by better resourced applicants, and the application was denied.

**Annihilation, Not Gradual Decay**

The verb of this verse is total: Allah annihilated them — dammara in Arabic, a word for destruction that leaves the thing ruined beyond repair, like a city flattened to its foundations. The verse does not describe the ordinary entropy by which all institutions fade; it names the ending that history knows as catastrophe — the empire that stopped mid-sentence, the civilisation whose ruins puzzled its heirs. The distinction matters to the argument. Gradual decline proves nothing; any materialist can read decay into economics. Catastrophic, patterned endings that follow the rejection of messengers — that is a signature, and the Qur'an invites its readers to check for it across every archive they can walk to: "Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers." (3:137).

## Verse 47:11

> This is because Allah is the Patron of the believers while the disbelievers have no patron

**The Emptiest Sentence in Arabic**

To an Arab ear, "they have no patron" was the emptiest sentence the language could build. Exile from every protection was the death that preceded dying. The verse lifts that social terror into the ledger of eternity and lets it stand as the disbelievers' permanent condition — no protector against Him, as the previous sūrah put the same doctrine. And the sentence carries a quiet irony the first audience could not miss: the disbelievers were not, socially speaking, patronless men. They had the finest patronage network in Arabia — clans, alliances, idols, wealth. The verse's "no patron" is not a census; it is a verdict on what all that patronage will be worth on the day it is invoked. Everything that cannot hear you is not protecting you.

**An Inventory of the Patron's Services**

Read the sūrah around this verse and it becomes an itemised contract of what the Patron provides. He guides, improves, and admits the fallen into the Garden (47:5-6). He helps and makes firm the steps of those who stand up for Him (47:7). He removes the sins and repairs the condition of those who answer (47:2). This is patronage in the old full sense — defence of the person's interests in every court, this world's and the next's — and the sūrah's earlier verbs are the services listed. No human patron, however generous, ever offered a portfolio like it: the best of them could avenge your death; this One intends to reverse it. The verse's claim is not that God out-ranks the patrons; it is that He provides what they were always imitating.

**The Confidence and Its Condition**

Two cautions keep the verse from becoming a tribal licence. First, the patronage is of the believers — the ones who believe, a word the sūrah has already defined with conduct attached; it is not a birthright of the religiously labelled. Second, the sūrah has just published the conditional that governs the services: if you stand up for Allah, He will help you (47:7). The Patron is not an insurer of the idle; the protection extends to clients who act like clients.

## Verse 47:12

> Surely Allah will admit those who believe and do good into Gardens under which rivers flow. As for the disbelievers, they enjoy themselves and feed like cattle. But the Fire will be their home

**Two Diets**

The verse sorts humanity by what it eats — not by menu but by relationship to provision. The believers, doing good, are admitted to Gardens watered by rivers: provision received from a host, at a table, in a home. The disbelievers enjoy themselves and feed like cattle: provision grazed, self-served, head-down. The image is agricultural and unsparing. Cattle are not wicked; they are complete — complete at the level of intake, wanting nothing, aspiring to nothing, accountable for nothing. The verse's accusation is that a human being, gifted with an address from his Maker, can live completely at the level of his own body and call that life. The two halves of the verse are not two punishments and rewards; they are two diets, and each is the natural destination of the appetite that chose it.

**The Cattle Comparison, Weighed Honestly**

The comparison is one the Qur'an makes more than once, and always with the same escalation. Of the heedless: "They have hearts they do not understand with, eyes they do not see with, and ears they do not hear with. They are like cattle. In fact, they are even less guided!" (7:179). And in al-Furqān, after asking whether the masses listen or understand: "They are only like cattle—no, more than that, they are astray from the ˹Right˺ Way!" (25:44). The escalation is the argument's honesty. Cattle cannot stray; there is no right road for a cow to leave. A human being grazing his way through a lifetime is worse positioned than the animal, because his detour was chosen with equipment the animal does not own. The verse is not sneering at the poor or the simple — hunger and simplicity have no entry here. Its target is the reduction of a covenanted creature to a digestive system with plans.

---

## Verse 47:13

> ˹Imagine, O Prophet,˺ how many societies We destroyed that were far superior in might than your society—which drove you out—and there was none to help them

**The Verse With a Wound in It**

Something is different here. The sūrah's history verses have spoken in the third person — those before them, the peoples of the past — and suddenly the address turns personal: your society, which drove you out. The verse names the Prophet's own injury, and in doing so reveals something about the Book's pastoral method. This is revelation pausing, mid-argument, to acknowledge that the man carrying it has a wound with an address. The expulsion from Makka was not an abstraction to him: the town of his birth, his people, his early life of standing among them — closed to him by force. God enters the detail into the argument itself. The God of the Qur'an does not counsel from above the grief; He argues from inside it.

**His Farewell to the City**

The memory the verse touches had a recorded moment. When the Prophet left Makka for the migration, he turned toward the city and said: "By Allah, you are the best land of Allah, and the most beloved of Allah's lands to Allah. Had I not been expelled from you, I would not have left" (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3925, graded ṣaḥīḥ by al-Albānī). Every word of that farewell belongs beside this verse. He loved the town that expelled him; he excuses his own leaving by naming its cause; he blames nothing of the valley itself. There is no curse in the sentence, no bitterness — and this from a man with a price on his head. The verse under commentary does what the farewell models: it names the injury without marinating in it, and puts the expellers on notice in the same breath.

---

## Verse 47:14

> Can those ˹believers˺ who stand on clear proof from their Lord be like those whose evil deeds are made appealing to them and ˹only˺ follow their desires

**The Road Out of the Question**

The tradition's commentators add a strand that deepens the verse's fairness. Following desire is not fixed at birth; the man whose evils are decorated is not beyond reach, because the decoration can crack. The verse itself implies the exit: those believers who stand on clear proof were once standing somewhere else, and what moved them was not the disappearance of desire but the arrival of ground under the feet. The Qur'an's most withering sentence about desire's destination — "And who could be more astray than those who follow their desires with no guidance from Allah?" (28:50) — contains its own cure: guidance from Allah, the thing this entire Book claims to be. The two conditions of the verse are therefore not castes; they are positions, and positions can be changed while the offer stands.

**A Question That Is Already a Verdict**

The verse's grammar does the arguing. In Arabic the question opens with the particle hal yastawī — can they be equal? — the Qur'an's standard formula for a comparison it wants laughed out of court. The device works because it makes the reader complete the syllogism himself: light and no-light, standing and stumbling — the equality is refused by the picture before the doctrine is stated. The Qur'an uses the formula across the Book for precisely its most important binaries — knowing and ignorant, seeing and blind, living and dead — and the formula's message is always the same: these two are not two opinions; they are two conditions. When a question mark carries a verdict, the reader is being asked to notice that he already knows the answer.

**Evil Made Beautiful**

The other party's condition is a workshop. Their evil deeds are made appealing to them — zuyyina lahum, the Qur'an's technical term for the decorating of sin. The verb is used of adornment, of a bride's preparation; the imagery is deliberate and uncomfortable. Sin, at the moment of choice, does not present itself as sin; it presents itself dressed, lit, and described in the vocabulary of the good. The Qur'an names the decorator elsewhere: "We have surely sent messengers to communities before you ˹O Prophet˺, but Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them. So he is their patron today." (16:63).

**Desire Enthroned**

The second half of the pair names the workshop's owner: they follow their desires. The Qur'an's sharpest single sentence on this condition is in al-Jāthiyah: "Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god?" (45:23).

---

## Verse 47:15

> The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey. There they will ˹also˺ have all kinds of fruit, and forgiveness from their Lord. ˹Can they be˺ like those who will stay in the Fire forever, left to drink boiling water that will tear apart their insides

**Four Rivers, Each One Repairing a Broken Copy**

Read the four rivers as a series and a pattern appears: each is this world's version of itself, with the defect removed. Water, everywhere prone to stagnation and taint, flows incorruptible. Milk, the most perishable of drinks, never changes its taste. Wine — forbidden in this life precisely for the harm and hostility it breeds — returns as the pleasure without the poison, for elsewhere the Qur'an sets the specification: a drink "that will cause them neither headache nor intoxication" (56:19). Honey arrives already pure, unstung by the filtering earth demands. The doctrine is quietly enormous: the next life is not a different kind of thing from this one; it is this one, matured — every legitimate pleasure of earth restored at full fidelity, minus the entropy.

**The Menu of the Night Journey**

Tradition preserves a scene that reads like this verse enacted. On the night of the Isrāʾ, the Prophet was presented at Jerusalem with two cups — one of wine, one of milk — and chose the milk. Gabriel told him: Praise be to Allah, Who guided you to the fiṭrah, the sound nature; had you taken the wine, your nation would have gone astray (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5576). The choice is the verse in miniature. Milk is the primordial drink — the first food of every human life — and the fiṭrah is the primordial constitution; the Prophet's instinct mapped the created order correctly. In the Garden, the tradition adds, the two drinks are reconciled: the milk that never sours and the wine that never harms, each river running past the same orchards — the repaired world, where nature and celebration are no longer rivals.

**The Other Table**

The verse's second portrait is drawn with equal economy: the Fire's residents, staying forever, drinking water that tears the insides apart. The Qur'an's images of that drink range wide — elsewhere, water "like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces" (18:29) — but the emphasis here is internal: the tearing apart of the insides. Intake, the body's most basic act of reception, becomes the organ of the punishment; what the damned drink destroys the drinker from within. There is an awful symmetry with the first half of the verse. The grazer of the earlier portrait lived by intake alone; his eternity is all intake, and nothing else arrives. The menu, in both houses, is the desire, served forever.

---

## Verse 47:16

> There are some of them who listen to you ˹O Prophet˺, but when they depart from you, they say ˹mockingly˺ to those ˹believers˺ gifted with knowledge, “What did he just say?” These are the ones whose hearts Allah has sealed and who ˹only˺ follow their desires

**A Scene Every Reader Can Stage**

The verse sketches a scene in three beats, and it is so exact that it can be staged in any century. A man attends the recitation — present, visible, counted in the assembly. He leaves. Away from the Prophet's presence, among the believers given knowledge, he asks his question with a curled lip: what did he just say? The scene's anatomy is the verse's lesson. Attendance is not attention; proximity is not reception; and the exit is where the truth about the session comes out. What a person does with the message after leaving its presence is the only evidence of what the message did to him.

**Hearts Sealed — the Anatomy of the Condition**

The verse then names the asker's condition with the Qur'an's most sobering image: hearts sealed. The sealing is not described as an arbitrary act that preceded the listening; the Qur'an's wider doctrine runs the other way — God's seal follows the self-sealing. Of another group of the same tribe the Book says: "There are some of them who ˹pretend to˺ listen to your recitation ˹of the Quran˺, but We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears." (6:25). Pretended listening, veiled heart, deafened ear: the order is instructive. The performance of hearing is exactly what the hearing cannot survive; a heart in the room for politics must keep politics out of the recitation, and keeping politics in the ears is, over time, how an ear dies.

---

## Verse 47:17

> As for those who are ˹rightly˺ guided, He increases them in guidance and blesses them with righteousness

**Guidance as a Growing Asset**

The verse's first surprise is its verb: increase. Guidance, in much religious speech, is a status — one has it or lacks it, like a membership. The Qur'an treats it as capital — something one holds in quantities that grow or shrink. Those who are rightly guided receive, of the same gift they already have, more of it. The Qur'an states the principle as an universal law: "And Allah increases in guidance those who are ˹rightly˺ guided. And the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in outcome." (19:76). The doctrine changes the believer's self-understanding at the root: he is not standing on a line he crossed once, but walking a road whose surface extends under his feet as he walks it. Standing still, on that road, is not neutral; it is how the surface retracts.

**Two Trajectories From One Recitation**

Placed immediately after the verse of the sealed hearts, this verse completes the diagram. The same recitation, the same session, the same words in the air — and two exits: the mockers walk out with less than they came with, and the guided walk out with more. Nothing in the message differed; everything in the reception did. The Qur'an has a whole theology of this split, and states it in the verse the Muslims used to recite over every new revelation: "Whenever a sûrah is revealed, some of them ask ˹mockingly˺, 'Which of you has this increased in faith?' As for the believers, it has increased them in faith and they rejoice." (9:124). Revelation, sūrah by sūrah, was the sorting instrument of Madinah — increasing those who received it as news from their Lord, and exposing those who had been attending for other reasons. The mechanism has not retired; every honest congregation is still being sorted by the same Book.

---

## Verse 47:18

> Are they only waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise? Yet ˹some of˺ its signs have already come. Once it actually befalls them, will it not be too late to be mindful

**Waiting as a Strategy**

The verse opens on a posture and exposes it: are they only waiting? The people in view are not arguing anymore; the sūrah has moved past their arguments. They are simply deferring — treating the Hour as a distant theoretical event that no present behaviour needs to reckon with. Deferral is the most common religious posture on earth, and the verse names its hidden logic: the waiter is not a skeptic but a gambler, betting that the reckoning is far enough away that today can be spent without accounting. The verse's question dismantles the bet in one stroke — what exactly are you waiting for, and what will waiting have purchased when it arrives?

**The Signs Already Delivered**

The wager's fatal flaw is the verse's middle clause: some of the Hour's signs have already come. The tradition reads the clause chiefly of the Prophet's own sending, and he himself supplied the scale of the nearness. Anas reported: "The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, 'I and the Hour have been sent like these two' — and he held up his index and middle fingers together" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6139; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2951). Two fingers, held together: the entire gap between the last Messenger and the end of history — no new prophet, no new dispensation, one joined span of time. Elsewhere the Qur'an voices the same nearness in a single line of prophecy: "The approaching ˹Hour˺ has drawn near." (53:57). For an audience that could date the signs' arrival to a living man in their own city, waiting was not caution; it was ignoring the arrival that had already happened.

**The Suddenness the Qur'an Keeps Insisting On**

The phrase "take them by surprise" belongs to a family of images the Qur'an uses for the Hour's arrival — sudden, unannounced, arriving in the middle of ordinary transactions. Elsewhere the Book asks whether they await the Hour coming upon them suddenly while they do not perceive (43:66), and pictures the moment with the markets still full and the cups still wet. The insistence is pastoral as much as forensic. Human beings plan around announced events and improvise around surprises; the Qur'an wants its readers to convert the surprise into an announced event — which is exactly what reciting its verses does. The believer who knows the appointment cannot be ambushed by it; the one who waits for it to ambush him has chosen the ambush.

---

## Verse 47:19

> So, know ˹well, O Prophet,˺ that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah. And seek forgiveness for your shortcomings and for ˹the sins of˺ the believing men and women. For Allah ˹fully˺ knows your movements and places of rest ˹O people˺

**The Command Whose Verb Is "Know"**

Between a sūrah of battles and betrayals, this verse plants the quietest and largest command in the Book: know. Not fight, not fear, not strive — know that there is no god worthy of worship except Allah. The verb is cognitive before it is anything else, and the choice is doctrine. Islam's foundation is not a mood of devotion, a heritage of belonging, or a chant repeated without content; it is a piece of knowledge, held with the same certainty one holds the existence of the sun. The scholars drew the consequence the verse implies: the first obligation of every responsible person is to know this truth — and everything else in the religion, prayer and charity included, is built on the knowing, and collapses with its collapse.

**The Formula That Carries Everything**

The sentence the verse commands to be known is the confession itself: lā ilāha illā llāh — no deity deserving worship except Allah. Its structure is a negation followed by an affirmation, and the order is its power: first the world's crowded counter is cleared, then the one true Object stands alone. The tradition has always measured the formula's weight by a saying the Prophet uttered about its minimum: whoever dies knowing that there is no god except Allah enters Paradise (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 26). Knowing — not merely reciting; the hadith's verb is the verse's verb. A sentence that can carry a soul across the boundary of eternity when its last breath carries it is not a slogan; it is the keystone, and this verse commands the masonry of the whole life to hang from it.

---

## Verse 47:20

> And the believers say, “If only a sûrah was revealed ˹allowing self-defence˺!” Yet when a precise sûrah is revealed, in which fighting is ˹explicitly˺ mentioned, you see those with sickness in their hearts staring at you like someone in the throes of death. It would have been better for them

**The Wish of the Oppressed**

The verse preserves a prayer the believers used to make in Makka: if only a sûrah allowing selfdefence would come down. Behind the wish lie the years the Qur'an itself remembers — the boycott that starved a clan, the tortures, the migration that emptied the town of its weakest. The believers were not warmongers; they were the beaten asking their Lord for permission to swing back. The Qur'an records the exact shape of their earlier complaint in the verse of al-Nisāʾ: "Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who had been told, 'Do not fight! Rather, establish prayer and pay alms-tax.'? Then once the order came to fight, a group of them feared those ˹hostile˺ people as Allah should be feared—or even more." (4:77). One group wished; a group within it, when the wish was granted, wilted. The verse under commentary is the story of that wish's granting.

**Granted, and Instantly a Test**

The wish comes true, and the verse's plot turns on the mechanism: a precise sūrah is revealed — muḥkama, firmly established, unambiguous — and fighting is named in it explicitly. No metaphor, no esoteric reading, no retreat into interpretation. The very precision the believers asked for becomes the instrument that separates them: a clear command is the only kind of command that can be clearly disobeyed. The episode teaches the Qur'an's recurring lesson about prayer itself: God's answers expose as much as they give. A person asks for strength until the weights arrive, for provision until the job demands the hours, for guidance until it points somewhere expensive. The sūrah descended; the room divided; the wish was audited.

---

## Verse 47:21

> to obey and speak rightly. Then when fighting was ordained, it surely would have been better for them if they were true to Allah

**The Sentence Completed**

The previous verse broke off at its verdict — it would have been better for them — and this verse supplies the missing term: to obey and to speak rightly. The completion is the diagnosis in miniature. Two remedies for the sickness of the heart, and neither is mysterious: obedience, the alignment of action with command; and upright speech, the alignment of words with truth. Everything the sūrah has shown of the hypocrites — the wished-for sūrah, the death-stare, the later verses' secret bargains — is the negative image of these two virtues. The sick heart disobeys and talks around its disobedience; the healed heart does what it was told and says what it means. The gap between the verses, where the reader held his breath, turns out to contain the whole of moral life.

**Why Speech Gets Equal Billing**

It startles, at first, that speech stands beside obedience in the remedy — as though talk were half of religion. But the sūrah's own material explains the priority. Hypocrisy, as this chapter draws it, is not mainly a failure to act; it is a success at talking — professions made, questions asked mockingly, secret assurances given to the enemy. Before hypocrisy can misbehave, it must speak. The Qur'an therefore aims its remedy at the crime scene: the tongue, the most mobile limb and the hardest to police. Elsewhere the Book gathers the command into one line, telling believers to be mindful of God and to speak with upright, direct words (33:70) — the same pair as here, obedience in the fear of God, and speech that goes straight. A community whose speech runs straight is largely inoculated; the sūrah is writing the vaccine into its own exposed cells.

---

## Verse 47:22

> Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship

**Corruption: the Qur'an's Word for Devastation**

The first projected crime is fasād — corruption, the word the Qur'an uses for the ruination of a whole ecosystem, moral and material at once. The Book's most sweeping statement of it pictures corruption appearing on land and sea because of what human hands have earned (30:41) — greed, exploitation, and injustice written into the physical world like poison into a watershed. Applied to the hypocrites, the projection is precise: these men, whose religion was convenience, would govern for convenience, and a state governed for the convenience of its governors is the definition of fasād — courts for sale, taxes as plunder, patronage for the loyal, ruin for the rest. The verse's warning is thus political science before it is theology: certain hearts, given power, produce certain countries. The corruption of the province begins in the corruption of the person.

**The Womb That Seized the Merciful**

The Qur'an gives kinship a founding covenant, and the Prophet narrated it in words the tradition never forgot. He said: Allah created the creation, and when He finished, the womb — the bond of kinship itself — arose and seized hold of the Most Merciful, saying: at this place I seek refuge with You from all who sever me. And Allah answered: Yes — will you not be pleased that I keep good relations with the one who keeps good relations with you, and sever relations with the one who severs you? The womb said: Yes, O my Lord. And Allah said: Then that is for you (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 4830). The report ends with the Prophet himself directing his Companions to this very passage of Sūrat Muḥammad as the verse to recite for it.

**The Horizontal Covenant of the Single Soul**

The Qur'an had already grounded kinship in theology, at the opening of the women's sūrah: "O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties." (4:1). The sequence is the doctrine: one origin, therefore one family; and God's Name invoked as the seal on the appeals humans make to one another through their kin. The hypocrites' program reverses the sequence — treating people as instruments and the Name as a tool — and the verse's warning stands as the eternal veto on every politics of instrumentality. The believer's map of society is drawn from a single soul; the corrupter's map is drawn from what he can use.

**The Brotherhood That Replaces What Was Cut**

For those the hypocrites would sever from, the Qur'an builds a new weave. And be mindful of Allah so you may be shown mercy." (49:10) — and the sūrah's community, soon to absorb the hypocrites' defection and the war's losses, would need that weave.

## Verse 47:23

> These are the ones who Allah has condemned, deafening them and blinding their eyes

**Condemned, and Then Senseless**

The verse carries two acts in sequence: God has condemned them — the Arabic laʿana, the solemn curse of exclusion — and then, as the outworking of the condemnation, deafening them and blinding their eyes (47:23). The order is doctrine. The curse is not a lightning strike of pain; it is a verdict of distance, removal from the circle of mercy's influence, and what follows it is deprivation. The punished senses of this verse are not seared; they are switched off. Hell's loudest punishments in the Qur'an are often of exactly this kind — not added agonies but withdrawn goods: light, hearing, the friendship of God. The worst thing that can happen to a soul is not suffering; it is being left to itself.

**Deaf and Blind With the Organs Intact**

The physical men of this verse could still hear a camel cough and spot a rider on the horizon. The deafness and blindness are moral, and the Qur'an removes all doubt about the organ involved: "Have they not travelled throughout the land so their hearts may reason, and their ears may listen? Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind." (22:46). A heart gone blind is the sūrah's own diagnosis of the hypocrites, and this verse names the consequence formally: what the heart refuses long enough, the whole person forfeits. The ears keep working at concerts and councils; they simply no longer carry the recitation anywhere. The tragedy of this deafness is that no one else can detect it — the man hears himself being warned and registers nothing but noise.

---

## Verse 47:24

> Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts

**The Verb That Names the Method**

The question the verse poses is not have they read it? But something more demanding: do they not reflect on the Quran? The Arabic verb, yatadabbarūn, is built from a root meaning to walk behind something until its end — to track a matter through, the way a tracker follows a trail. Tadabbur is reading as pursuit: following the verse through its connections, its consequences, its demands, until the reader arrives where the verse was going. The Qur'an has no patience with the scanning eye. It asks for readers who will walk behind the text as a Bedouin walks behind a trail — patiently, alert to every bent twig — and the verse implies that the community's problem is not the Quran's clarity but the reader's gait.

**A Book That Invites Verification**

The demand for reflection is not a demand for reverence in a vacuum; the Qur'an ties tadabbur to testing: "Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would have certainly found in it many inconsistencies." (4:82). The invitation is startling by the standards of scripture. This Book asks to be read with its seams checked — inviting readers to hunt for contradiction as proof-reading, and betting its divine origin on the result. A forger would suppress the auditing instinct; the Quran commands it.

**The Best of You**

The Prophet compressed the entire economy of this verse into one sentence he made famous: "The best of you are those who learn the Qur'an and teach it" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 5027). Three stations in five words — learn, teach, best — and the middle term is the first's proof. Learning that never teaches curdles; teaching that outruns learning lies. The hadith's best is not the fastest reciter or the largest memoriser, but the one in whom the Book passes through — entering by reflection, exiting by transmission. For a community that would eventually build universities on this sentence, the verse under commentary was the charter: reflection on the Book is not one pious option among many; it is the engine of whatever excellence the community will ever have.

---

## Verse 47:25

> Indeed, those who relapse ˹into disbelief˺ after ˹true˺ guidance has become clear to them, ˹it is˺ Satan ˹that˺ has tempted them, luring them with false hopes

**Who the Relapsers Were**

The commentators offered several identifications, each with its grounds. One reading points at the hypocrites who turned away when fighting was ordained — the sūrah's own immediate context; the battle-fatigued who discovered that faith with a price tag was not the product they had purchased. Another points at segments of the Madinan Jews who had accepted part of the revelation and refused the parts that cost — the Qur'an elsewhere asks them, in amazement, whether they believe in part of the Book and disbelieve in part. A third reads the verse of the Makkan deniers who knew the Prophet's trustworthiness for decades and rejected him once the message touched their idols. The first reading fits the sūrah's flow best; the others fit the psychology, which is the same in every case — the retreat happens exactly where the guidance begins to invoice.

**Satan's Currency Is Time**

The verse then credits the operation to Satan — but notice the method named, because it is not fire or possession: luring them with false hopes. Satan's instrument, in this verse, is the future tense. Not now; later; after the season of enjoyment; when the odds improve; when the family comes round — hope deferred as an anaesthetic for conscience. The Qur'an elsewhere gives the classic statement of the disease's terminal phase: "Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment." (3:178). The false hope is always the same hope: that delay is harmless. Satan does not need a person to renounce God; he only needs the person to reschedule — indefinitely, comfortably, one season at a time.

---

## Verse 47:26

> That is because they said ˹privately˺ to those who ˹also˺ detest what Allah has revealed, “We will obey you in some matters.” But Allah ˹fully˺ knows what they are hiding

**A Whisper, Quoted as Evidence**

The verse explains the relapse by quoting its social stage: a private message, delivered to the enemies of revelation — we will obey you in some matters (47:26). Consider what that sentence is. It is a declaration of allegiance with a limping qualifier; a treaty with a clause the other party is supposed not to read. The Qur'an preserves the actual words the way a prosecutor preserves a wiretap — because the wording convicts. The hypocrites' public record showed profession of Islam; their private record, here published, shows a competing subscription with a leakage clause: we will follow you — in some matters. The some is the whole person. Anyone whose obedience is sold in instalments has already priced the rest.

**Whose Ear the Whisper Sought**

To whom was the message sent? To those who also detest what Allah has revealed — the sūrah's earlier diagnosis of the deniers, now revealed as the hypocrites' true constituency. The Qur'an legislates about such private channels elsewhere in words that fit this scene exactly: "Have you not seen those who were forbidden from secret talks, yet they ˹always˺ return to what they were forbidden from, conspiring in sin, aggression, and disobedience to the Messenger?" (58:8). Secret counsel — najwā — is the habitat of this religion of some: it is where reservations become strategy, where the public self and the private self can be different products for different markets. The verse drags the channel into the open by the simple expedient of publishing its contents, and the publication is the punishment's first instalment.

---

## Verse 47:27

> Then how ˹horrible˺ will it be when the angels take their souls, beating their faces and backs

**Taking in Full**

The verse turns from the hypocrites' secrets to the hour those secrets end: the angels take their souls. The Arabic verb, tatawaffā, is built on a root meaning completeness — to take in full, to recover completely what was lent. The image behind the word is repossession: the soul was never the person's property, only a deposit, and the angels arrive as the authorised collectors, taking the deposit back with nothing withheld. For people whose entire strategy was the partial — some matters, some obedience, some visibility — the verb carries its own sentence: at the end, nothing partial remains. The some is collected like the all.

**The Twin Scene From the Battle Sūrah**

The Qur'an stages this death elsewhere in near-identical terms, and the twin belongs beside this verse: "If only you could see when the angels take the souls of the disbelievers, beating their faces and backs, ˹saying,˺ 'Taste the torment of burning!'" (8:50). The repetition across sūrahs does two things. It fixes the scene as doctrine rather than flourish — this is what the Qur'an teaches about the death of the insincere, stated twice, in war's sūrah and in hypocrisy's sūrah. And it attaches a spoken clause: taste. The sentence the angels deliver is not an argument, an explanation, or an invitation — the time for those is the years the person just spent. What remains at the exit is sensation, the account settling into the body that ran up the bill.

---

## Verse 47:28

> This is because they follow whatever displeases Allah and hate whatever pleases Him, so He has rendered their deeds void

**A Final Because, All About Love**

The sūrah has now asked four times why the deeds are void, and this last answer is the deepest of the four. Not because they hindered (47:1), not because they disbelieved (47:8), not even because they detested (47:9) — but because their affections are inverted: they follow what angers God and hate what pleases Him. The diagnosis has moved from conduct through creed through emotion to the root system itself. In the Qur'an's anthropology, loves are not the decoration of a life; they are its engine and its identity. Deeds are what the loves wore in public. A person is, at the last analysis, not what he did but what he delighted in — and the voiding of the deeds follows from the verdict on the loves the deeds were serving.

**The Fourth Stamp of the Verdict**

This is the fourth stamping of the verdict — He has rendered their deeds void (47:28) — and by now the refrain has taught its lesson the way refrains do. First stamp: the statute, with hindering named. Second: the sentence, at the sūrah's turn. Third: the cause, detestation of the revealed. Fourth: the root, the loves themselves. Each repetition added a deeper stratum, and the order of the strata is the sūrah's whole psychology of unbelief in miniature: act, allegiance, aversion, affection. The legal-document rhythm is not redundancy; it is drilling. A doctrine this costly to human pride — that impressive lives can be voided by what their owner loved — needs four statements before proud minds will hold it, and the sūrah spends them like a physician spending a long consultation on the one diagnosis the patient keeps refusing to hear.

## Verse 47:29

> Or do those with sickness in their hearts think that Allah will not ˹be able to˺ expose their malice

**The Arithmetic of Hiding From the Knower**

The verse states the hypocrites' gamble as a question, and the gamble, laid bare, is absurd: they believe God will not expose what is hidden inside them. Consider the components. They believe in God — hypocrites must, for hypocrisy is meaningless without a God to perform before. They believe He sent a Book, raises the dead, and audits the worlds. And they simultaneously believe — this is the working assumption their entire strategy rests on — that the interior of a human heart is beyond Him. It is the most incoherent position in the catalogue of unbelief: omniscience acknowledged in the creed and deleted from the plans. The verse's question format is mercy; it lets the hearer do the arithmetic himself and watch it fail.

**The Fear That Already Told on Them**

The sūrah's God had a way of confirming this verse before their eyes. The Qur'an records the hypocrites' own tell elsewhere, and it is one of the Book's most psychological passages: "The hypocrites fear that a sûrah should be revealed about them, exposing what is in their hearts. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Keep mocking! Allah will definitely bring to light what you fear.'" (9:64). They feared exposure — and fear of exposure is itself the confession. A man with nothing in the vault does not flinch when the auditors are announced. Their dread of each new revelation was the receipt for what the revelations kept finding, and the Prophet was told to answer the dread with the guarantee this verse supplies: what you fear is scheduled. Malice that arranges itself for concealment has already conceded the standard it will be judged by.

## Verse 47:30

> Had We willed, We could have truly shown them to you ˹O Prophet˺, and you would have certainly recognized them by their appearance. But you will surely recognize them by their tone of speech. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows your doings ˹O people˺

**The Miracle That Was Withheld**

The verse opens with a remarkable conditional: Had We willed, We could have truly shown them to you. The Prophet is told, in practice, that supernatural identification of the hypocrites was available — a divine labelling system, faces marked, files exposed — and God declined to install it. The withholding is the teaching. A community governed by revealed X-ray vision would obey for the wrong reasons and police the impossible: the interior of hearts. Instead, the verse points to the slower, humaner instruments — speech, conduct, time — and leaves certainty about hearts to the One who owns them. The refusal is a constitutional act: the Prophet's state was to judge what appears, not what hides, and every later Islamic jurisprudence of evidence descends from this restraint.

**Marks of a Different Kind, on a Different Day**

The marks exist — the verse says so — but their display is scheduled for the right court. The Qur'an describes that scheduling in the sūrah of the Most Merciful: "The wicked will be recognized by their appearance, then will be seized by ˹their˺ forelocks and feet." (55:41). What is concealed in this world — the malice of v. 29, the secrets of v. 26 — arrives at the resurrection already printed on its owners. The doctrine is two-edged. For the hidden hypocrite it is the exposure he assumed would never come; for the hidden believer — the sincere one whose goodness nobody recorded — it is the vindication he never sought.

**Ibn Ubayy's Sentence, Preserved as Evidence**

The Qur'an once caught the tone in an actual quotation, and it remains the masterclass of self-exposure. The leader of Madinah's hypocrites, ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, said of the Prophet and his Companions: "If we return to Medina, the honourable will definitely expel the inferior." (63:8). One sentence, and the tone has told on him ever after: the contempt, the self-appointment, the politics of expulsion in a mouth that publicly professed brotherhood. The Qur'an quotes the sentence verbatim — published in the Book, recited for eternity — which is the second exposure inside the first: not only the malice, but the very music of the malice, archived. The tradition drew the obvious lesson for readers: watch your own tone, because it is the part of your speech you least control and others hear first.

Then the verse ends by turning the X-ray around: And Allah ˹fully˺ knows your doings ˹O people˺ (47:30). The pronoun shifts — your, plural, everyone in the room, the reader included.

## Verse 47:31

> We will certainly test you ˹believers˺ until We prove those of you who ˹truly˺ struggle ˹in Allah’s cause˺ and remain steadfast, and reveal how you conduct yourselves

**Testing Until Proving — the Meaning of the Phrase**

The verse's boldest phrase is its most easily misread: We will certainly test you ˹believers˺ until We prove. God, in the Qur'an, is never the student learning the answer; His knowledge is complete before the exam is printed. The tradition resolved the phrase with precision: the proving is the making-manifest — God knows all things eternally, and the test brings that knowledge out of the hidden and into existence, where it can be witnessed, rewarded, and answered for. Creation is the courtroom in which what God already knows is performed. The Qur'an makes the same correction itself elsewhere, asking after the battle that staggered the community: "Do you think you will enter Paradise without Allah proving which of you ˹truly˺ struggled ˹for His cause˺ and patiently endured?" (3:142). Proving — the word of demonstration, not discovery. The exam is not for the Examiner; it is for the examined, and for an universe in which justice is done in the open.

**Exposure as a Service to the Doer**

Then the verse adds its second clause, and reveal how you conduct yourselves (47:31) — and the revealing runs in both directions. The hypocrites' deeds are exposed to the community, as the sūrah has been doing verse by verse. But the believer's deeds are exposed to the believer: the courage he did not know he had, the generosity that only an actual need could summon, the prayer that only a hospital corridor could teach, and — the humbling half — the cowardice, the stinginess, the self-regard that comfort had kept off the books. There is a mercy in this that the sheltered life forfeits: a person cannot repent of what he has never seen in himself, and the exam, honestly read, is therefore the beginning of repair. The believer who asks God to test him is not asking for pain; he is asking for an accurate map of his own soul, which no other instrument supplies.

## Verse 47:32

> Indeed, those who disbelieve, hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and defy the Messenger after ˹true˺ guidance has become clear to them; they will not harm Allah in the least, but He will render their deeds void

**The Three-Part Charge, Complete**

The verse reads like an indictment drawn with care, and each count deserves its own line. They disbelieve — the private verdict of the heart. They hinder others from the Way of Allah — the public obstruction, the same crime the sūrah opened with, road-blocking practiced as a career. And they defy the Messenger after true guidance has become clear — the personal rebellion, shiqāq in Arabic, from a root meaning to split: they knew the man, knew his truthfulness, saw the guidance arrive and clarify, and chose enmity against him personally. Creed, conduct, and grudge: unbelief in its full armour. The sūrah's opening verse had two counts; this, near the close, adds the third — the defiance of a specific, known, clear messenger — and the addition is what makes this the terminal form of the disease.

**They Cannot Harm God in the Least**

Against this full armoured opposition, the verse states a fact that dissolves it: they will not harm Allah in the least (47:32). The Qur'an had said it before, at the community's lowest hour, in the verse revealed when rumour said the Prophet had been killed at Uḥud: "Muḥammad is no more than a messenger; other messengers have gone before him. If he were to die or to be killed, would you regress into disbelief? Those who do so will not harm Allah whatsoever." (3:144). The doctrine is the eternal proportion-corrector: wars against God are waged by creatures He sustains in existence moment by moment, using lungs He keeps filled, against a claim they cannot dent. The harm flows one way only — downhill, onto the opposer — and the entire drama of religious persecution, from the outside view the Qur'an supplies, is a man swinging at the ground he stands on.

**The Refrain, One Last Time**

Then comes the sentence the sūrah has stamped and re-stamped: but He will render their deeds void (47:32). Fifth and last occurrence — the statute (47:1), the sentence (47:8), the cause (47:9), the root (47:28), and now the verdict written over the fully-armoured opponent. But notice the tense, because it has changed. The earlier stamps were past — done, settled, the verdict phrased as an accomplished fact. Here the verb stands in the future: He will render. The shift is the verse's last mercy and last warning in one. As long as the verb is future, the file is open; the opposer breathing this verse is still, technically, an eligible penitent, and the sūrah's second verse — believe, do good, and the ledger reopens — has not expired. The refrain's final stamp is raised, and hovering; it falls at death, and not one breath sooner.

## Verse 47:33

> O believers! Obey Allah and obey the Messenger, and do not let your deeds be in vain

**A Command That Comes Paired**

The verse's first half is one of the Qur'an's fixed formulas: obey Allah and obey the Messenger — the two verbs, the repeated obey, the pairing stated as a single architecture. The Qur'an legislates the pairing explicitly where it matters most: "O believers! Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. Should you disagree on anything, then refer it to Allah and His Messenger, if you ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day." (4:59). The logic of the pair is the doctrine of this whole sūrah: God's guidance arrived through a messenger; obedience to the guidance cannot be split from obedience to the one delivering it — the hypocrites of this very chapter spent their careers attempting exactly that split, with their some-matters treaty, and the try is the sūrah's definition of ruin. The formula stands as the wall the sūrah was built against.

**The Warning Addressed to Believers**

Then comes the verse's second half, and its addressee is the shock: do not let your deeds be in vain (47:33) — spoken not to the disbelievers, whose deeds this sūrah has voided five times, but to the believers, O believers, the same opening address. The implication is careful and grave: the voiding of deeds is not a misfortune reserved for card-carrying deniers; it is a possibility inside the religious life itself. A believer can build and lose. The commentators traced the ways: deeds undone by disobedience that contradicts them; charity spent then cancelled by reminder and injury; worship performed for an audience other than God; the slow substitution of habit for intention.

**The Freedom the Command Assumes**

The formula the verse uses carries, in its very repetition, a doctrine of freedom. The Qur'an could have said believe, and the state could have enforced it; instead it says obey — a verb that presupposes a free hearer who might not — and the Qur'an itself states the limit elsewhere in the same breath: "Obey Allah and obey the Messenger! But if you turn away, then Our Messenger's duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly." (64:12). Delivery, not compulsion: the messenger's job description ends at clarity, and the choice that follows belongs to the hearer. A command addressed to the free, with the option of refusal built into its grammar, honours the human being in a way coercion never can — and it explains why the verse ends with a warning instead of a sanction: the waste it fears is chosen, and what is chosen can be re-chosen.

## Verse 47:34

> Surely those who disbelieve, hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and then die as disbelievers; Allah will never forgive them

**When Dying Is the Defining Act**

Every clause in this verse has appeared before except the last two words: and then die as disbelievers. That is the hinge. The verse's terror is not in the charges — they are the sūrah's standing indictment — but in the file being closed with the charges still standing. Death, in the Qur'an's accounting, is the sealing of the ledger: whatever state a soul is in at the end is the state the audit reads. The verse is thus not a sentence on the living disbeliever, whatever his crimes; it is a sentence on a finished life. As long as breath remains, the file is open, the future tense of the previous verse still hovers, and this verse's grammar — those who die as — is precisely the description of what no living reader yet is.

**The One Category Left Outside**

The severity of the verdict aligns with the Qur'an's hardest doctrine, stated in the women's sūrah: "Indeed, Allah does not forgive associating others with Him ˹in worship˺, but forgives anything else of whoever He wills. And whoever associates others with Allah has indeed committed a grave sin." (4:48). The logic of the exception is relational, not arbitrary. Forgiveness presumes a relation — an offended party who can be turned to; the one who dies having refused the relation, blocking others from it to the end, has declined the address forgiveness travels by. Every other sin — and the Qur'an's catalogue is unsparing — is a wound inside a relationship that can still be healed. The dying-in-the-refusal is the refusal of the healer. That, and not the size of any particular crime, is why this verse and 4:48 stand where they do.

**The Absoluteness of "Never"**

The verse's negative is the Arabic lan — the particle of absolute, unqualified negation: Allah will never forgive them. A warning with loopholes is not a warning; a judge who hints that the last category might be re-litigated after death has told the dying that procrastination is safe. And intercession, the loophole every culture hopes for, is closed by the same architecture: the Qur'an's throne verse had already asked, "Who could possibly intercede with Him without His permission?" (2:255).

## Verse 47:35

> So do not falter or cry for peace, for you will have the upper hand and Allah is with you. And He will never let your deeds go to waste

**No Faltering at the Top of the Hill**

The verse addresses a specific moment in war — the moment of advantage — and the temptation that arrives with it: faltering, the Arabic noun for weakness, the shoulders that drop when the finish line appears and the body decides, without consulting the will, that the race has been won. The command: do not falter or cry for peace, for you will have the upper hand (47:35). The scene is precise: a community that has endured years of the lower hand, suddenly holding the higher one, and discovering — as every long-oppressed party discovers — that victory tests characters that defeat never reached. The verse names the test before the community can fail it: strength is not for surrendering from.

**The Command in Its Century**

The reading of the peace clause occupied the jurists, and the discussion is itself instructive. The classical understanding held that the verse forbade suing for terms while in a position of strength — a command for its hour, when a young community kept being offered peace as a trap, its confidence the real target. But the Prophet's own later career complicates any absolute reading, and the jurists knew it: at Ḥudaybiyah, in the year 6/628, the Prophet concluded a treaty with the Quraysh from a position of clear strength — a pact the Muslims chafed at, and which the Qur'an itself, in the opening of the very next sūrah, called a clear triumph (48:1). Al-Qurṭubī and others resolved the tension with the principle this sūrah has already taught: verses are reconciled by circumstance before they are resolved by abrogation — a ruling for the situation of weakness-to-strength in battle, not a prohibition of treaties as such. The community's law emerges from the cases held together, not from either alone.

**When the Enemy Actually Wants Peace**

Honesty about this verse requires its companion, and the Qur'an supplies it in the battle sūrah: "If the enemy is inclined toward peace, make peace with them. And put your trust in Allah. Indeed, He ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing." (8:61). The two verses together form the ethic whole: this one forbids suing for peace from strength; that one commands accepting it when genuinely offered. What the pair outlaws is not peace but the coward's version of it — the settlement purchased by the strong out of fear, which teaches the enemy that pressure works and mortgages every weaker party's future. What the pair honours is peace as a real good, received with trust when it is real. The believer's martial ethic, drawn from the whole, is neither belligerence nor pacifism but a settled strength that neither begs for terms nor rejects them.

## Verse 47:36

> This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But if you are faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, He will grant you your ˹full˺ reward, and will not ask you ˹to donate all˺ your wealth

**The Full Portrait, Cast in Iron**

Elsewhere the Qur'an expands the two words into its most complete portrait of the world's texture: "Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children. This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff." (57:20). Every item in the inventory deserves notice: play, amusement, and then luxury — comfort as a programme; mutual boasting — the race run in other people's eyes; competition in wealth and children — the two currencies the audience actually traded in. And then the rain: the world's freshest season, the spring that makes every farm look eternal — and the same rain's aftermath, yellowing, chaff. The portrait's honesty is exact: the Qur'an is not denying the green; it is reporting its half-life.

**Play for One, Provision for Another**

How can the world be play and, in the same Book, a provision, a test, and a place of witness? Because the verse's verdict tracks the viewer, not the valley. For the soul that makes the world its end, the world obliges by behaving like a toy — glittering, endless, and finally weightless. For the soul that treats the world as a road — eating from it thankfully, spending from it justly, passing through it deliberately — the same world becomes provision, and the Qur'an describes the destination beside it in the same breath it names the play: "This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But the Hereafter is indeed the real life, if only they knew." (29:64), and again: "far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter for those mindful ˹of Allah˺. Will you not then understand?" (6:32). Real life — al-ḥayawān, the life that lives. The verse under commentary is not a curse on the world; it is an offer to upgrade one's address.

**The Conditional Alternative**

Mid-verse, the diagnosis turns conditional: if you believe and are mindful. The two verbs are the sūrah's whole prescription in miniature — faith, the allegiance of the heart; mindfulness, taqwā, the protective awareness that fences daily conduct — and both are aimed at the same enemy the verse has just named: absorption. Belief breaks the world's monopoly on attention; taqwā keeps it broken through the workweek. Then the promise: He will grant you your ˹full˺ reward — ujūrakum, wages in the plural, paid entire (47:36). The believer does not renounce reward; he relocates it. The world's payer pays in impressions that fade; the verse names a Payer whose wages do not, and its condition is the only currency He accepts.

## Verse 47:37

> If He were to do so and pressure you, you would withhold and He would bring out your resentment

**The Clamp**

The condition the hypothetical would expose has a name in the Qur'an's moral vocabulary: shuḥḥ, stinginess — the word's root carries the sense of clamping, of holding shut. Stinginess in the Qur'an is not a personality quirk; it is a disease of the hand with a spiritual aetiology: the heart's secret belief that giving diminishes it, that security is a stockpile, that tomorrow is its own to guarantee. The verse's insight into human capacity is unsentimental: pressed hard enough, most givers would clamp — not because they never gave, but because their giving operated inside comfort's margin. Pressure, in this verse, is the X-ray that shows where the margin was. The believer is not shamed for having the limit; he is informed of it, gently, by the One who calibrated His asking around it.

**The Asking Is From What He Gave**

Hold the verse's premise in view: to do so means to ask for the wealth — all of it — and every coin of it arrived by His allocation. The Qur'an states the ownership plainly elsewhere, and prices the hoarding of what was lent: "And do not let those who ˹greedily˺ withhold Allah's bounties think it is good for them—in fact, it is bad for them! They will be leashed ˹by their necks˺ on the Day of Judgment with whatever ˹wealth˺ they used to withhold." (3:180). The leash image is the bookkeeping made physical: what a man clamps in this life accompanies him, worn, into the next. Against that ledger, the actual asking — a percentage of certain holdings, a share the Owner could have set at one hundred — stands revealed as almost embarrassing in its modesty. The servant is never asked for his own; he is offered receipts on the Master's.

## Verse 47:38

> Here you are, being invited to donate ˹a little˺ in the cause of Allah. Still some of you withhold. And whoever does so, it is only to their own loss. For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺. If you ˹still˺ turn away, He will replace you with another people. And they will not be like you

**"Here You Are!" — the Summons That Stops the Crowd**

The sūrah's final verse opens with the Qur'an's most arresting vocative: Here you are! — hāʾantum, the grammar of a speaker who has caught the audience mid-motion, mid-excuse, mid-departure. Not O people, not O believers — but you, there, now: the invitation to donate in the cause of Allah is issued into a room where some of the invited are already looking at the door. The rhetorical urgency is the sūrah's signature; it opened its account with the hinderers and closes it by summoning the hesitating, and both speeches share the same assumption — that religion is a present-tense demand, arriving while the audience is doing something else.

**The Reflexive Law of Stinginess**

Then comes the verse's central law, stated with a precision that ends a thousand arguments: whoever does so, it is only to their own loss (47:38). The logic is reflexive — the act rebounds on the actor — and the Qur'an grounds the law elsewhere in the transaction's very design: "Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah. Whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged." (2:272). God needs no subsidy; the cause is not financed by the donation. What giving actually does is move wealth from the perishable column of the giver's ledger to the permanent one — from holdings that die with the estate into accounts that survive the grave.

**The Rich and the Poor**

Why does the law hold? Because of the verse's next pair, the most radical inequality in all theology: Allah is the Self-Sufficient — al-Ghanī, rich beyond any need or deficit — whereas you stand in need — the fuqarāʾ, existentially poor, creatures whose next breath is drawn on credit (47:38). The Qur'an states the pairing as an universal address to mankind: "O humanity! It is you who stand in need of Allah, but Allah ˹alone˺ is the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy." (35:15). The implication for the verse's summons is total: the cause of God is not God's pension fund; He is not soliciting. Every dinar of the religion's budget was minted by the Rich and is spent for the poor — visibly so. The early mystics read the pairing inward: al-Tustarī, commenting on this very language, taught that knowledge of one's poverty before God is the knowledge that wealth is in God — the believer's true fortune is the recognition itself, which no market can price and no withholder can keep.

**Replacement**

Then the verse makes the stakes historical: If you ˹still˺ turn away, He will replace you with another people. And they will not be like you (47:38). The Qur'an states the doctrine in the same register when the march is refused: "If you do not march forth, He will afflict you with a painful torment and replace you with other people. You are not harming Him in the least." (9:39). The teaching is one of the Book's most bracing: God's mission does not depend on any generation's cooperation. The community of the moment holds a commission, not a monopoly; refusal does not stop the work, it only reassigns the work — and the honour with it. The first audience watched the principle operate in real time: the call went out, and the answerers — the Companions who spent and marched — became the generation the world remembers, while whole tribes that hesitated read about themselves later, replaced. The verse's fear is not for God's project; it is for the replaced.
