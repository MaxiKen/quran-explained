# Sūrah al-Mujādilah (Chapter 58) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Pleading Woman and the All-Hearing Ear of Heaven**

Al-Mujādilah, "The Pleading Woman"—also known in classical exegesis as *Al-Mujādalah* (The Dispute)—is the fifty-eighth chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 22 verses of Medinan revelation. It takes its name from its astonishing opening verse: *“Allah has certainly heard the speech of the woman who pleads with you, [O Muḥammad], about her husband and directs her complaint to Allah; and Allah hears your dialogue. Indeed, Allah is Hearing and Seeing”* (58:1). This commemorates the historic case of Khawlah bint Thaʿlabah, an ordinary Muslim woman who challenged an abusive, archaic pagan divorce practice before the Prophet ﷺ. Sūrah al-Mujādilah is universally unique in the entire Qur'anic corpus by a stunning mathematical and structural phenomenon: *every single verse of its twenty-two verses explicitly contains the majestic name "Allāh"*—a total of forty occurrences across twenty-two lines! Sūrah al-Mujādilah is the great charter of the abolition of domestic abuse, the regulation of public meetings, the condemnation of clandestine conspiratorial whisperings (*al-najwā*), and the ultimate victory of the Party of God (*Ḥizb Allāh*).

**The Structural Architecture and the Secret Assemblies**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Mujādilah moves from domestic justice to political counter-intelligence, and from social etiquette to cosmic allegiance:

1. The Protection of Women and the Abolition of Ẓihār (1–6): Exonerating Khawlah, criminalizing *Ẓihār*, codifying the tiered penalties, and warning those who defy Allah and His Messenger that their deeds are recorded in an incorruptible ledger by the God who was present at every moment.

2. There is in no private conversation three but that He is the fourth of them, nor are there five but that He is the sixth of them—and no less than that and no more except that He is with them wherever they are”* (58:7). It exposes the hypocrites and their Jewish conspirators who gathered in private cabals (*al-najwā*) to plot sin, aggression, and disobedience to the Messenger, and who greeted the Prophet with veiled insults (*“as-sāmu ʿalayka”*—death be upon you).

3. The Etiquette of Public Assemblies and Council Etiquette (11–13): Commanding believers to make room for others in public gatherings (*tafassaḥū fī al-majālis*), assuring that God will make room for them, and commanding them to rise when asked to rise, promising: *“Allah will raise those who have believed among you and those who were given knowledge, by degrees”* (58:11). It details the temporary test of giving charity before private consultations with the Prophet, gently easing the requirement when the community proved anxious.

4. The Two Parties: The Party of Satan vs. The Party of God (14–22): Exposing the hypocrites who took oaths of allegiance falsely, being terrified of exposure. Verse 19 defines their psychological state: *“Satan has overcome them and made them forget the remembrance of Allah. Those are the Party of Satan (*Ḥizb al-Shayṭān*). Unquestionably, the Party of Satan are the losers!”* Verse 21 proclaims the absolute divine decree: *“Allah has written: 'I will surely overcome, I and My messengers.' Indeed, Allah is Powerful and Exalted in Might.”* The sūrah concludes with the magnificent portrait of *Ḥizb Allāh* (The Party of God): souls whose allegiance to faith transcends all bloodlines, family ties. Ethnic bonds—in whose hearts God has inscribed faith and whom He has strengthened with a spirit from Him (*rūḥin minh*), entering the eternal gardens under the divine seal: *“Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. Those are the Party of Allah. Unquestionably, the Party of Allah are the successful!”* (58:22).

Khawlah did not surrender; she turned her face to the sky, crying repeatedly: *“O Allah, I complain to You of my loneliness, my poverty, and the ruin of my young children!”* ʿĀ'ishah, who was in the room, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī (4726) her sheer awe: *“Glory be to Him whose hearing encompasses all voices! I was in the corner of the room, and I missed parts of her speech, yet Allah heard her complaint from above the seven heavens!”* Verses 1–4 descended immediately: outlawing *Ẓihār* as a monstrous falsehood and an evil utterance (*munkaran min al-qawli wa-zūrā*), and legislating severe legal expiations (freeing a slave, fasting two consecutive months, or feeding sixty needy persons) before a husband could touch his wife again.

## Verse 58:1
> Indeed, Allah has heard the argument of the woman who pleaded with you ˹O Prophet˺ concerning her husband, and appealed to Allah. Allah has heard your exchange. Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing

**A Private Grievance Receives a Public Hearing**

A woman's complaint about her marriage stands at the opening of this revelation. She has a husband, a problem, and words with which to seek relief. None of these is dismissed as too ordinary for divine attention. The verse begins with the assurance that Allah has heard her. Before any account of a remedy, it establishes that her experience has entered the sphere of judgment. Domestic suffering is not outside the concerns of faith merely because it occurs within a home.

**The Exchange Is Heard Beyond Human Limits**

A report from ʿĀʾishah supplies a closely relevant contrast. She recalls Khawlah coming to complain about her husband and explains that some of the woman's speech escaped her hearing, although Allah heard it. This is ʿĀʾishah's account, not a saying placed on the Prophet's lips: Sunan al-Nasāʾī 3460, graded ṣaḥīḥ by Darussalam on the checked edition (Sunan al-Nasāʾī 3460). The report identifies the complainant while showing the limit of a nearby human listener. It does not require us to invent a louder voice, a public assembly, or a dramatic interruption. Its force lies in the opposite direction. What a person nearby could miss was fully known to Allah. Divine hearing is not measured by the reach of human ears, and a quiet grievance is not a lesser grievance because few people hear it.

**The Fuller Narration and Its Collectors**

Ibn Kathīr opens the sūrah with the longer form of ʿĀʾishah's report, which Aḥmad recorded and which al-Bukhārī placed, without a chain, in the Book of Tawḥīd of his Ṣaḥīḥ; al-Nasāʾī, Ibn Mājah, Ibn Abī Ḥātim and Ibn Jarīr also carried it. In that form she begins with praise: all praise is for Allah, whose hearing takes in every voice. In Ibn Abī Ḥātim's narration she names the woman and repeats what she managed to hear. Khawlah bint Thaʿlabah stood complaining to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ about her husband: he had used up her wealth and worn out her youth, her womb had borne him child after child, and now that she was old and could bear no more he had pronounced ẓihār on her — *"O Allah, I complain to You."* ʿĀʾishah adds that the husband was Aws ibn al-Ṣāmit. The words explain why the verse calls her speech an argument and an appeal at once. She was pleading a case before the Prophet and, in the same breath, laying it before Allah.

Her own account, which Aḥmad recorded and Abū Dāwūd placed in the Book of Divorce, fills in the exchange the verse says Allah heard. When she told the Prophet ﷺ what had happened, his first answer was restraint, not a ruling: *"O Khuwaylah, your cousin is an old man, so fear Allah regarding him."* No verse had yet come down, and she kept pressing her complaint. She says that before she left, the revelation came; the Prophet ﷺ showed the strain that receiving it always brought, then was eased, and told her that Allah had sent down Qur'an about her and her husband, reciting the passage to her from this verse to the end of the fourth. The rest of her account, with the three steps of the expiation, belongs to verse 4. Ibn Kathīr notes that the sources spell her name Khawlah bint Thaʿlabah, Khawlah bint Mālik ibn Thaʿlabah and Khuwaylah, that these are forms of one name, and that this report is what is correct about the occasion of the sūrah.

## Verse 58:2
> Those of you who ˹sinfully˺ divorce their wives by comparing them to their mothers ˹should know that˺ their wives are in no way their mothers. None can be their mothers except those who gave birth to them. What they say is certainly detestable and false. Yet Allah is truly Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving

**An Abusive Formula Cannot Create a Mother**

The verse breaks the authority of a marital declaration by exposing its false claim. A husband compares his wife to his mother to make their marital relationship forbidden. Allah answers that the comparison does not turn the wife into a mother. The point is more than a correction of vocabulary. The man has tried to make a personal utterance carry the force of a real relationship, with serious consequences for another person. Revelation refuses to let his claim define reality.

The practice is called ẓihār. Ibn Kathīr explains that this term comes from ẓahr, meaning the back, because the customary formula compared the wife to the speaker's mother's back. His commentary also identifies its use as a form of repudiation before Islam (Ibn Kathīr on 58:2 — *“Those of you who ˹sinfully˺ divorce their wives by comparing them to their mothers ˹should know that˺ their wives are in no way their mothers.”*). The linguistic detail explains why an apparently strange bodily comparison could have such serious force. It was not a casual observation about resemblance. It used a recognized expression to deny marital intimacy. The verse does not accept the convention merely because people understand it. A custom can give words social power while leaving the assertion they contain both false and unjust. Here divine judgment separates those two facts.

Khuwaylah's own account, which Aḥmad recorded, shows the formula in use. Aws ibn al-Ṣāmit had grown old and difficult. One day she argued with him over something and he said in anger, *"You are to me like my mother's back,"* then went out and sat with his people. When he came back he wanted her as a husband wants his wife. She refused — *"No, by the One in whose hand is Khuwaylah's soul, you will not have your way with me after what you said, until Allah and His Messenger judge between us"* — and pushed the old man off her. She borrowed a garment from a neighbour and went to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ. The story gives the verse its two speakers: a man who thought a sentence had changed his wife's standing, and a woman who would not let the sentence stand unexamined. Ibn Kathīr adds the legal point the sūrah settles here. Before Islam the words were one of the ways a man divorced; this community was given an expiation for them instead, and the saying was not made a divorce.

**The Two Words the Verse Uses for the Saying**

The verse does more than correct a comparison; it names what the comparison is. Those who make the declaration are said to utter *an evil word and a lie* — *munkaran min al-qawl wa-zūran* — and then the verse states the fact that settles it: they are not their mothers, and none is a man's mother but the woman who gave birth to him. The lying is not in the husband's intention to separate; it is in the claim the formula makes. He says *you are to me as my mother's back*, and the sentence is false the moment it is spoken, whoever meant what by it. The verse then closes with the two names that belong with the law that follows: *and indeed God is Pardoning, Forgiving*. The men who had done this had not been struck down for it, and the next verses give them the way back — which is the mercy the closing names are pointing at.

Ibn Kathīr reads the two names at the end against that background. The pardon covers what the men had done in the days of ignorance, when this was their way of divorcing, and it covers the word that slips out of a mouth without intent. What it does not do is turn the saying into truth, which is why the verse can call it detestable and false in one clause and name Allah Ever-Pardoning in the next.

## Verse 58:3
> Those who divorce their wives in this manner, then ˹wish to˺ retract what they said, must free a slave before they touch each other. This ˹penalty˺ is meant to deter you. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**Retraction Requires More Than a Change of Mood**

A husband who wants to undo his wrongful declaration cannot simply resume married life as though the words had never been spoken. The verse attaches an action to retraction. This prevents the husband's shifting wishes from becoming the only rule: first he repudiates the marital relation through a false comparison, then he expects its privileges to return whenever he desires them. The wife is not required to live under a sequence of declarations whose force is determined solely by his convenience.

**The Meaning of Return Has Legal Consequences**

The Arabic expression about return to what was said generated different legal readings. Ibn Kathīr records al-Shāfiʿī's view that retaining the wife after the declaration, with an opportunity to divorce her but without doing so, constitutes the relevant return. He also records Aḥmad's focus on resuming intercourse or intending it, and reports more than one formulation from Mālik (Ibn Kathīr on 58:3 — *“Those who divorce their wives in this manner, then ˹wish to˺ retract what they said, must free a slave before they touch each other.”*). These are positions about what activates the obligation, not disagreements over whether the abusive declaration was commendable.

Ibn Kathīr carries two more readings of the return. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr took it as the wish to resume the intimacy the words had shut off, and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī limited the forbidden act to intercourse itself, seeing no harm in less than that before the expiation. On the clause *before they touch each other*, ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that the touching meant is intercourse, and ʿAṭāʾ, al-Zuhrī, Qatādah and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān said the same; al-Zuhrī went further and said the husband is not to kiss or touch her until he has paid. The stricter reading has a prophetic case behind it. The compilers of the Sunan record from ʿIkrimah, from Ibn ʿAbbās, that a man came and said he had pronounced ẓihār on his wife and then slept with her before paying the expiation. The Prophet ﷺ asked, *"May Allah have mercy on you, what made you do that?"* and the man said he had seen the adornment she was wearing shining in the moonlight. The Prophet ﷺ told him not to go near her until he had done what Allah had commanded; al-Tirmidhī graded the report ḥasan gharīb ṣaḥīḥ, and Abū Dāwūd and al-Nasāʾī also recorded it. The expiation is not a fee owed after the fact. It is a condition that stands between the man and his wife until it is met.

The slave to be freed is described without a qualifier. Ibn Kathīr points out that the verse says a slave, not a believing slave, unlike the expiation for a killing by mistake, where the Book specifies belief (4:92 — *“And whoever kills a believer unintentionally must free a believing slave”*). He reads the clause *meant to deter you* as the warning attached to the rule, and the closing name as the assurance that the One who set it knows what serves the two people it is set between. The wording matters because the expiation is not a punishment scaled to a crime. It is the cost of a word, and the Book sets the cost where it can be paid.

**What the Verse Does Before It Legislates**

The verse first states the wrong that has been committed: those who make the declaration and then take back what they said. The phrase *thumma yaʿūdūna li-mā qālū* describes the return, and the return is met with a condition rather than a welcome: *they must free a slave before they touch one another*. The order of the two clauses matters. The expiation comes before the resumption of the marriage's intimacy, not after, so the words cannot be washed away by a night and forgotten by the morning. And the sentence that ends the verse gives the purpose: *that is what you are admonished with, and God is aware of what you do*. The last clause is the one the man needs, since the reconciliation takes place behind a closed door where no court can see whether the slave was freed or the weeks were fasted.

**What the Declaration Does Not Do**

The sūrah has already said the most important thing about the husband's words. They are false: *they are not their mothers* — the wives are not the women who bore them, and the saying creates no real kinship (58:2 — *“What they say is certainly detestable and false.”*). So the wife keeps her status; she is not repudiated without divorce, and she is not his mother. What she has acquired is a claim on him for a repair the law makes him carry out in his own body or from his own wealth, and a record of conduct kept where nothing is lost.

## Verse 58:4
> But if the husband cannot afford this, let him then fast two consecutive months before the couple touch each other. But if he is unable ˹to fast˺, then let him feed sixty poor people. This is to re-affirm your faith in Allah and His Messenger. These are the limits set by Allah. And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment

**Inability Changes the Duty Rather Than Erasing It**

The verse makes room for two distinct limits: lack of means and inability to fast. A husband who cannot afford the required emancipation is directed to fasting. If he cannot undertake that fast, he must feed the poor. These are ordered alternatives, not three interchangeable ways of selecting whatever seems easiest. Each transition is tied to a stated inability. The form of the obligation changes because the person's capacity matters; the responsibility to respond does not disappear merely because its first form is unavailable.

**The Founding Case Shows That Help Can Make Repair Possible**

The report of the founding case gives the rule a human setting. Khuwaylah describes her husband's lack of means and inability to fast, after which the Prophet directs that sixty poor people be fed. She offers to contribute a second basket of dates to help fulfill the obligation. The account is in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 2214. Al-Albānī grades it ḥasan with an exception about the basket-measure clause; the reservation concerns only the clause describing the measure (Sunan Abī Dāwūd 2214).

**The Order and the End of the Verse**

The verse closes with what the penalties are for: *that is so that you may believe in God and His Messenger; and these are the limits of God, and for the disbelievers is a painful punishment*. The expiation is not a fine paid to a court but a repair of the marriage, and the verse states it before the couple resume. Then it calls the rule a limit set by God, and it names the consequence of ignoring limits as belonging to disbelief — the phrase that gives the passage its weight, since the matter has been taken out of the zone of private arrangement.

Ibn Kathīr explains the fixed order of the three steps by the reports that give them in the same sequence, above all the ḥadīth in the Two Ṣaḥīḥs about the man who had intercourse with his wife in the daytime of Ramadan. He too was told to free a slave, and when he could not, to fast two months in succession, and when he could not do that, to feed sixty poor people. The same ladder in two different cases shows that it is a ladder: each rung is opened only by inability on the one above it. He also reads the clause *This is to re-affirm your faith in Allah and His Messenger* as the purpose of the law — the rule is legislated so that the believers acquire that quality — and takes the limits of Allah to mean the things He has forbidden, which are not to be crossed. The last clause, on his reading, is a warning to those who do not believe and will not keep to the rulings: they should not imagine that they will escape the punishment, in this life or the next.

**What the Founding Case Shows**

The case behind the passage gives the rule a face. Khuwaylah came to the Prophet complaining about her husband Aws ibn al-Ṣāmit, who had declared her unlawful to himself in the manner of the days before Islam; she had no money for the slave, no strength in her body for two months of fasting, and the Prophet directed that sixty poor people be fed. She offered part of that from the dates she had, and the account is preserved in Sunan Abī Dāwūd 2214. The report is what shows the law bending to a person's actual capacity: the woman was not told to find a way, she was given one.

## Verse 58:5
> Indeed, those who defy Allah and His Messenger will be debased, just like those before them. We have certainly sent down clear revelations. And the disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment

**Resistance to Guidance Is Not a Rival Source of Authority**

The verse addresses those who place themselves in defiance of Allah and His Messenger. Their stance is active opposition, not simply the presence of a difficulty they have not yet understood. The warning belongs to the relation between revealed authority and a will that refuses it. A person may have wealth, influence, or an accepted custom on his side, but none of these turns resistance into a competing right to define what Allah has commanded.

**Debasement Reverses the Claim to Stand Above the Command**

Ibn Kathīr explains the verse's final humiliation as a fitting response to proud refusal to follow and submit. That checked explanation adds a moral relation between the stance and its consequence: the abasement answers arrogance, rather than functioning as an unexplained display of force (Ibn Kathīr on 58:5 — *“Indeed, those who defy Allah and His Messenger will be debased, just like those before them.”*). This is the commentator's interpretation of the verse, not a separate Prophetic report. It helps distinguish divine judgment from the human wish to humiliate an opponent simply because he is disliked. The passage identifies a refusal of revealed authority and declares its end. It does not command listeners to invent degrading treatment of people they accuse. The right response is to abandon the conceit that resistance establishes independence. Allah's command is not weakened because a person regards obedience as beneath him, and a person's social standing does not alter that relation.

The verb the verse uses, *kubitū*, is read by Ibn Kathīr as three things at once: humiliated, cursed and disgraced, as happened to those like them before them. The comparison with earlier peoples is the sūrah's first hint of the decree it states plainly later, that Allah and His messengers will prevail (58:21 — *“I and My messengers will certainly prevail.”*), and the same word family returns when the defiant are counted among the most debased (58:20 — *“they will definitely be among the most debased”*). On the middle clause, the clear revelations, he says that no one contradicts or opposes them except a disbeliever, a rebel and a sinner. The clarity of the signs is what makes the defiance a choice rather than a mistake.

## Verse 58:6
> On the Day Allah resurrects them all together, He will then inform them of what they have done. Allah has kept account of it all, while they have forgotten it. For Allah is a Witness over all things

**Resurrection Brings the Whole Group Before Its Judge**

The people described are raised together. No one can remain outside the reckoning by outliving a witness, moving elsewhere, or belonging to a generation that came later. The gathering closes the distance that earthly life places between actors and the consequences of their actions. Death has not dissolved the identity of those who acted, and the passage of time has not dissolved their responsibility. Allah raises the people whose deeds will be disclosed.

**Disclosure Makes Their Own Actions the Subject of Judgment**

The verse's order is important: resurrection is followed by being told. The people concerned are present for the unveiling of their account. This is not only information stored somewhere beyond their awareness. They must confront it. At the same time, the sentence does not make Allah's knowledge depend on their agreement with the account. He is the One who informs, not a judge awaiting facts that only the defendants can supply. Their knowledge changes; His does not. Ibn Kathīr explains the disclosure as including the details of deeds, whether good or evil (Ibn Kathīr on 58:6 — *“Allah has kept account of it all, while they have forgotten it.”*). That explanation fits the comprehensive language without narrowing the account to only the actions a person considers memorable. The moment of disclosure is therefore also a reversal of control over the story one has told about one's life.

Ibn Kathīr reads the gathering as the assembling of the first generations and the last in one plain, so that no one is missing when the account is read, and he takes the clause about forgetting as the contrast the verse turns on: Allah recorded and kept every action, and they lost hold of it. The sūrah will use the same opening again for the hypocrites, who are raised on that Day and swear to Him as they swore to people (58:18 — *“On the Day Allah resurrects them all, they will ˹falsely˺ swear to Him as they swear to you”*). Read together, the two verses give one scene from two sides. Here the deeds are announced to those who forgot them; there the men who forgot try one more oath.

**Brief Speech Belongs to the Same Serious Account**

A checked parallel in 50:18 — *“not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺”* describes even an uttered word as attended by an observer ready to record it (Qurʾān 50:18 — *“not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺”*). This adds a concrete case to the general statement here without turning the commentary into an account of another passage. Words can vanish from memory almost as soon as they are spoken. Their short duration does not mean they fall outside the deeds Allah preserves. A remark that took seconds to utter can outlast the speaker's recollection in its effect on another person.

## Verse 58:7
> Do you not see that Allah knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth? If three converse privately, He is their fourth. If five, He is their sixth. Whether fewer or more, He is with them wherever they may be. Then, on the Day of Judgment, He will inform them of what they have done. Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**A Closed Conversation Remains Within an Open Knowledge**

The opening question asks for recognition of something already implied by belief in Allah: His knowledge encompasses heaven and earth. A private meeting cannot be an exception to that scope. Its participants occupy a place within creation, and their words occur within the world He knows. Closing a door changes who among human beings can listen; it does not change the conversation's relation to its Creator. The argument moves from the largest field of knowledge to a small and carefully enclosed human setting.

**Being With Them Is Not Becoming One of Their Bodies**

The opening and closing concern knowledge. That frame governs the statement that Allah is with the speakers. Ibn Kathīr explains the accompanying presence here through Allah's complete knowledge, hearing, and sight. He also preserves Aḥmad's observation that the verse begins and ends by naming divine knowledge (Ibn Kathīr on 58:7 — *“Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”*). That observation gives the reading a reason rather than an appeal to a scholar's name. The context itself identifies what the statement is asserting.

Ibn Kathīr records that more than one scholar reported a consensus that the *with* of this verse refers to Allah's knowledge, and he adds that the meaning is beyond doubt once His hearing and sight, which take in all things, are joined to it. He supports it from the verse in which the plotters are asked whether they think their secrets go unheard, and are answered that the recording angels are at their side (43:80 — *“Or do they think that We do not hear their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks? Yes ˹We do˺! And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording ˹it all˺”*). The angels write what is said even though He knows it better than they do, so the secret has two witnesses: the One who needs no record, and the record.

**The Counting That Sets No Limit**

The verse moves through the numbers of a meeting — three, four, five, six — and then stops counting: *and no fewer and no more, but He is with them wherever they are*. The escalation is not arithmetic but argument: it takes the smallest private gathering a person can imagine, two people plus their talk, and shows the presence that is inside it. The sentence then answers the question the secret-keeper is really asking, which is not whether God is in the room but whether the room can keep a secret. The answer comes at the end: then He will inform them on the Day of Resurrection of what they did.

**The Knowledge That Frames the Verse**

Ibn Kathīr marks that the verse opens and closes on the same attribute — it begins with God's knowledge of what is in the heavens and the earth and ends with *God knows all things* — and that the "with" in the middle sits inside that frame. The nearness is not a mixing or a body in a chair; it is the presence of one who hears and sees. For the men at Madinah who were meeting in the dark to arrange matters against the Prophet, the verse removed the only advantage secrecy ever gave them. The verses before this one describe the men who held their secret meetings and then went to the Prophet with what they had agreed, and the anger of God is named on them (58:8 reports it — *“And when they come to you ˹O Prophet˺, they greet you not as Allah greets you, and say to one another, “Why does Allah not punish us for what we say?”*). The verse in front of the reader answers them with the knowledge that surrounds every gathering, and the sūrah then tells the believers what to do in private: to discuss good and the fear of God rather than wrongdoing and hostility (58:9 gives both — *“And fear Allah, to Whom you will ˹all˺ be gathered”*). The Book had already said of the men who plot at night that God keeps what they plan (9:78 states it — *“Do they not know that Allah ˹fully˺ knows their ˹evil˺ thoughts and secret talks, and that Allah is the Knower of all unseen”*).

## Verse 58:8
> Have you not seen those who were forbidden from secret talks, yet they ˹always˺ return to what they were forbidden from, conspiring in sin, aggression, and disobedience to the Messenger? And when they come to you ˹O Prophet˺, they greet you not as Allah greets you, and say to one another, “Why does Allah not punish us for what we say?” Hell is enough for them—they will burn in it. And what an evil destination

**Returning After a Prohibition Makes the Conduct Deliberate**

The people described have already received a prohibition and have resumed the same conduct. Their fault cannot be explained as simple ignorance of the boundary. The movement back to what was forbidden reveals a decision to preserve the practice despite correction. Private speech becomes a means of sustaining defiance rather than an innocent exercise in confidentiality.

Three objects of their consultation are named: sin, aggression, and disobedience to the Messenger. These overlap without being empty repetition. Sin marks the conduct as wrong before Allah. Aggression identifies its movement against others. Disobedience names the rejection of the authority that has already told them to stop. Ibn Kathīr makes this distinction between misconduct about the speakers themselves and wrongdoing that reaches other people (Ibn Kathīr on 58:8 — *“and disobedience to the Messenger?”*). Their meeting is so judged by its purpose and persistence, not merely by its low voices. The verse does not make every confidential conversation suspect. It identifies a specific kind of counsel whose participants know the rule, coordinate harmful purposes, and return to the practice. Secrecy provides them a setting for mutual encouragement in something they have already been required to abandon.

Ibn Kathīr names the people. Ibn Abī Najīḥ reports from Mujāhid that those forbidden from secret talk were the Jews of Madinah, and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān gives the scene. The Prophet ﷺ had a treaty with them. When one of his Companions passed a group of them they would put their heads together and whisper, and the believer would think they were arranging to kill or harm him; he would fear for himself and change his route. The Prophet ﷺ told them to stop this, and they went back to it, and the verse came down about them. The detail explains the verb of the verse: they returned to what they had been forbidden. It also explains the harm. Nothing had to be done for the whispering to work; a Muslim who walked the long way home had already been injured.

**An Apparent Greeting Can Carry an Intended Injury**

A report from ʿĀʾishah records a group using al-sām, death, rather than the greeting of peace. She understood the hostile wording and replied sharply. The Prophet restrained her response and explained that he had already answered them by returning the words upon their speakers. This is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6256. The report establishes the incident and the distinction between the two expressions without requiring an invented speech. It concerns the group that performed the act, not every person sharing their religious identity. The moral fault lies in the intentional misuse of a greeting. A social sign normally offering safety has been turned into a vehicle for wishing harm while retaining enough resemblance to ordinary courtesy to evade straightforward acknowledgment.

**The Greeting the Verse Names**

The sentence in the middle of the verse says that these men, when they come to the Prophet, greet him with a greeting that God has not greeted him with. The commentators tie it to the practice of the hypocrites and of the Jewish community in Madinah, who came in and used a greeting that sounded close to the Muslim one and meant something else. ʿĀʾishah's report in Bukhārī records the exchange and the Prophet's answer that the community was to reply with the same words to the other party, and the Book records the whole matter in the clause of this verse.

Ibn Kathīr gives the exchange in its fuller forms. In the narration Ibn Abī Ḥātim recorded from ʿĀʾishah, some Jews came to the Prophet ﷺ and said, *"al-sām ʿalayka, Abū al-Qāsim,"* and she answered, *"and upon you be al-sām."* The Prophet ﷺ said, *"O ʿĀʾishah, Allah does not love rudeness and foul speech."* She asked whether he had not heard what they said, and he replied that she had not heard him answer *"and upon you."* In the wording of the Ṣaḥīḥ he added that the supplication of the Muslims against them is answered, and theirs against the Muslims is not. Ibn Jarīr records the same lesson from Anas ibn Mālik. A Jew passed the Prophet ﷺ and his Companions and greeted them, and they returned the greeting; the Prophet ﷺ asked whether they knew what the man had said, and told them it was *sām ʿalaykum*, not *salām*. He had the man brought back and asked him, and the man admitted it. Then he told the Companions that when one of the People of the Book greets them they should answer, *"upon you."* Ibn Kathīr notes that the basis of Anas's report is in the Ṣaḥīḥ, as is the report of ʿĀʾishah. The rule is measured to the wrong: no curse is added, and nothing is given back but what was sent.

**What They Said Among Themselves**

The verse gives their private talk as well: they ask why God does not punish the community for what they say. The Book answers them with one line, naming Hell as sufficient, and it says that they will be roasted in it, so that the question is turned around and left with them. The Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān records the same posture: a group whose real question was about the promise of a delay (3:154 — *“They ask, “Do we have a say in the matter?”*). The chapter in front of us is dealing with a council that met to arrange the community's business and used the time to speak against it.

The inner speech of the verse has a report of its own. Aḥmad recorded from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿAmr that the Jews used to say *sām ʿalayka* to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ and then say among themselves, *"Why does Allah not punish us for what we say?"* — and the verse came down about it. Ibn Kathīr grades its chain ḥasan and notes that al-Bukhārī and Muslim did not collect it. He also spells out the reasoning behind their question. They twisted the greeting into abuse and then told themselves that if this man were a prophet Allah would have punished them for it at once, since Allah knows what they concealed. The verse lets the argument stand and moves the date: the punishment they were waiting for is not withheld, it is Hell, and it is enough.

**The Rule That Replaces Their Council**

The verse after it states the positive command: when the believers hold private counsel, it should not be for sin and enmity and disobedience to the Messenger, but for righteousness and the fear of God, and they should be mindful of the One to whom they will be gathered (58:9 — *“And fear Allah, to Whom you will ˹all˺ be gathered”*). The Book does not forbid consultation. It regulates the purpose of it. In a passage that has dealt with secret talk used as a weapon, the sūrah hands the community back the same instrument with its proper use spelled out, and the last words of that verse name the gathering that all of it will end at.

## Verse 58:9
> O believers! When you converse privately, let it not be for sin, aggression, or disobedience to the Messenger, but let it be for goodness and righteousness. And fear Allah, to Whom you will ˹all˺ be gathered

**Belief Places Private Deliberation Under a Moral Rule**

The address calls upon believers precisely where other people may not be able to inspect their conduct. Religious identity does not make every private conversation good. Those who believe must govern the purposes of their meetings by the guidance they profess. The verse therefore treats confidentiality as an area of responsibility, not an exemption granted to insiders who trust one another.

Its opening assumes that private conversation will occur. The command is not to abolish it but to direct it. People need settings in which they can ask advice, consider delicate matters, and organize help without making every fact public. The moral question concerns what the privacy serves. A closed meeting may protect someone who needs assistance, or it may protect speakers from accountability for harming him. The same outward arrangement can serve contrary ends. The verse resolves that ambiguity by specifying prohibited and approved purposes. Believers cannot simply ask whether their meeting is hidden; they must ask why it is hidden and what they are helping one another to accomplish. Faith becomes a rule for the use of confidentiality rather than a label that automatically validates whatever a believing circle decides to say.

Ibn Kathīr reads the command as a deliberate contrast with what has just been described. The believers are being taught to keep clear of the way of the People of the Book who whispered in the road and of the hypocrites who copied them, and to make their own private talk carry the two things the verse names, goodness and mindfulness of Allah. He ties the closing clause to the verses before it: the One to whom they will be gathered will tell them their deeds and their words, having counted and kept them (58:6 — *“Allah has kept account of it all, while they have forgotten it.”*). A private conversation is therefore never quite private. It is spoken in the presence of the fourth, and it is kept for the Day the fourth will read it back.

**A Parallel Passage Names Charity and Reconciliation**

Qur'an 4:114 — *“There is no good in most of their secret talks—except those encouraging charity, kindness, or reconciliation between people. And whoever does this seeking Allah’s pleasure, We will grant them a great reward”* identifies private counsel directed toward charity, kindness, and reconciliation as exceptions to much unprofitable secret speech. It also connects reward to seeking Allah's pleasure (Qurʾān 4:114 — *“And whoever does this seeking Allah’s pleasure, We will grant them a great reward”*). This addition supplies concrete examples of the goodness required here. Confidentiality may be valuable precisely because a person in need should not have to exchange public exposure for help, and disputants may need space to retreat from hostile positions without losing face.

## Verse 58:10
> Secret talks are only inspired by Satan to grieve the believers. Yet he cannot harm them whatsoever except by Allah’s Will. So in Allah let the believers put their trust

**The Prophetic Rule Protects the Person Left Out**

The instruction the Prophet gave makes the concern concrete. Ibn Masʿūd reports that when three people are together, two should not hold a private exchange that excludes the third until they are among others, because such exclusion would grieve him. This is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6290. The report adds a practical rule beyond the verse's general description of satanic counsel. It addresses behavior that can cause hurt even without a proven malicious plot.

Ibn Kathīr gives the rule in a second wording. ʿAbd al-Razzāq narrated from ʿAbdullāh ibn ʿUmar that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said, *"If you are three, two should not whisper apart from the third without his permission, for that grieves him,"* and Muslim collected it. The permission clause is the addition. What wounds the third man is not the private word itself but being shut out of it, and his leave removes the injury. Ibn Kathīr draws the practical conclusion for the one who is the subject of such talk: he should seek refuge with Allah and rely on Him, for none of it will harm him unless Allah wills. The Sunnah thus forbids the small whisper for the same reason the verse exposes the large one. Both work on the heart of the person left outside.

**Trust Refuses to Let Hidden Speech Govern the Heart**

The conclusion directs believers to put their trust in Allah. Trust is not an instruction to admire the secrecy of their opponents or to surrender practical judgment. It is the response appropriate to knowing that the enemy lacks independent power. The believer may seek clarification, avoid an unsafe situation, or ask for help without treating those steps as evidence that reliance on Allah has failed.

**What the Verse Leaves the Believer With**

The sentence that closes the verse is the practical one: *and upon God let the believers put their trust*. The whispering is real, the plotting is real, and none of it is denied; what is denied is the power to decide the outcome. The believer is told to take the measures of a sane person — ask, avoid, seek help — and then to lay the result where the verse lays it. Trust is not a refusal to look at the danger. It is looking at the danger without giving it the rank of a god.

**The Scene Behind the Rule**

The verse names the whisper as *from Satan*, which is the Qur'an's way of describing counsel that works on fear rather than on fact. The hypocrites held these meetings to frighten the Muslims before the treaty and after it, and the verse tells the community what to do with the result: nothing, except trust. The Prophet's instruction about three men and two of them whispering — reported by Ibn Masʿūd in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6290 — is the same teaching in miniature: fear is cheap to cause and easy to refuse, and a community that will not let a whispered word govern its heart has taken the verse seriously.

## Verse 58:11
> O believers! When you are told to make room in gatherings, then do so. Allah will make room for you ˹in His grace˺. And if you are told to rise, then do so. Allah will elevate those of you who are faithful, and ˹raise˺ those gifted with knowledge in rank. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**The Prophetic Rule Distinguishes Expansion From Displacement**

A report from Ibn ʿUmar states that the Prophet forbade making one person leave his seat merely so another could occupy it, while directing people to spread out and provide room. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī places this report under the present verse: Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6270. This additional evidence is important because making room could otherwise be misused as a slogan for displacing someone less influential.

**Allah's Expansion Answers a Willingness to Ease Another's Situation**

The relation is not a commercial exchange in which a measured bodily movement purchases a fixed quantity of reward. Allah's favor exceeds the action that occasions it. What the act reveals is willingness to ease another person's situation under His command. A report from Abū Hurayrah connects relieving difficulty and helping another believer with Allah's help: Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2699a. That parallel supports a broad moral relation while leaving the promise's particular forms to Allah. It also prevents apparent smallness from becoming an excuse for neglect. The person who cannot solve another's whole difficulty may still remove one avoidable obstacle. In the gathering, that obstacle is literal lack of room. The promised divine response dignifies the action without allowing its performer to boast that another person's access now belongs to him as a favor owed.

**Knowledge Raises Rank Without Authorizing Arrogance**

Allah will elevate believers and those given knowledge. The phrase given knowledge presents learning as a gift and trust rather than self-created superiority. The elevation comes from Allah; it is not secured by forcing oneself into the most visible seat. Within a verse about making room and responding to direction, the promise unsettles the assumption that dignity depends on refusing to move.

Ibn Kathīr closes the verse with a report Aḥmad recorded from Abū al-Ṭufayl ʿĀmir ibn Wāthilah, and Muslim collected. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, who had put Nāfiʿ ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥārith in charge of Makkah, met him at ʿUsfān and asked whom he had left over the people of the valley. The answer was Ibn Abzā, one of their freed slaves. ʿUmar asked whether he had really set a freed slave over them, and was told that the man had memorised the Book of Allah, knew the law of inheritance and was a sound judge. ʿUmar said, *"Your Prophet ﷺ said: Allah raises some people by this Book and lowers others by it."* The report is the verse in one appointment. Rank in the community followed knowledge of the Book, and a freed slave sat over the people of Makkah because of it.

**What the Verse Does in Three Movements**

The verse gives three instructions in one sentence: make room in the gathering when you are asked, and God will make room for you; rise up when you are told to rise, and God will raise those who believe among you and those who have been given knowledge by degrees. The passage is dealing with the manners of the community's gatherings, and the report from Ibn ʿUmar that the Prophet forbade a man to make his brother get up so that he could take the seat is read with it, along with the Prophet's own practice of making room for the latecomers until he once told the men to spread out.

**What the Verse Says About Rank**

The second half names the two things that carry rank in the community: belief and knowledge, and it puts them in one sentence with the promise of degrees. The Book gives knowledge an elevated place in the Sūrah of the Crowds, where those who know are not equal to those who do not (39:9 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Are those who know equal to those who do not know?”*), and Sūrah Ṭā Hā commands the Prophet to ask for an increase of knowledge (20:114 — *“Increase me in knowledge.”*). The verse in front of us joins the honour to the charity of making room, which is why the two halves belong together in the scholars' treatment of it.

Ibn Kathīr draws the two halves of the verse together. No one should think that making room for his brother, or rising when he is told to, takes anything from his standing. It raises him with Allah, who does not let a good deed go to waste and rewards it in this life and the next; whoever lowers himself before the command of his Lord is lifted by Him and made known for his good conduct. The closing name, All-Aware, is read as the guarantee behind the promise: Allah knows who deserves the degrees and who does not, so the raising is never mistaken.

**The Occasion and the Community's Use**

The passage was given in Madinah, where the Prophet's gatherings had become crowded and the men who came early were losing their places to those who came later. The Book's answer is not a rule about seating alone: it is a rule about the disposition that gives space to another person and takes the lower before the higher. The passage that follows warns the community against secret talk (58:9-10 — *“Secret talks are only inspired by Satan to grieve the believers. Yet he cannot harm them whatsoever except by Allah’s Will. So in Allah let the believers put their trust”*), and the two passages together form the sūrah's teaching on the conduct of a public gathering.

Ibn Kathīr preserves Qatādah's account of the occasion: the verse came down about the gatherings of remembrance. When men came to join the Messenger of Allah ﷺ, those already seated were slow to make room, not wanting to lose their places, and Allah commanded them to spread out and make room for one another. He also records two readings of the command to rise. Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and others took the assemblies to be the councils of war and the rising to mean getting up to fight; Qatādah took it more broadly, as answering whenever one is called to any good. The two are not rivals. A community that will not give up a seat will not readily give up its ease for a harder duty, and the verse trains the one habit through the other. Ibn Kathīr adds the reports Aḥmad carried from Abū Hurayrah in which the Prophet ﷺ forbids making a man get up from his place in order to sit in it and says instead, *"Spread out and make room, and Allah will make room for you"* — the promise of the verse put into the mouth of the rule.

## Verse 58:12
> O believers! When you consult the Messenger privately, give something in charity before your consultation. That is better and purer for you. But if you lack the means, then Allah is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**The Gift Comes First So That Preparation Becomes an Act**

The instruction to give before the consultation makes preparation concrete. Good intentions alone are not the whole response. The believer is asked to perform an act that changes something outside his own desire to speak. By the time the private exchange begins, a benefit has already reached another person. The conversation therefore enters a setting shaped by generosity rather than mere competition for attention.

This order also encourages seriousness about the proposed consultation. A person must pause between wanting access and using it. That pause can help distinguish a genuine question from the wish to appear especially close to the Messenger. The verse does not accuse every seeker of an audience of vanity, but the structure of the rule checks a form of vanity that private access could invite. Ibn Kathīr explains the charity as cleansing the person in preparation for the consultation (Ibn Kathīr on 58:12 — *“O believers! When you consult the Messenger privately, give something in charity before your consultation.”*). This explanation gives the sequence a devotional meaning: one approaches with an act of obedience already offered, not simply with a claim that one's concerns must take priority. The good deed does not replace the need for a truthful question. It helps form the person who will ask it.

**Respect for the Messenger Includes Respect for His Burden**

Ibn Kathīr preserves an explanation attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās that repeated private questioning had become burdensome for the Prophet. This is an interpretive report transmitted in the commentary, not a newly authenticated Prophetic saying. It supplies a practical setting for the command: a valued opportunity can be overused when every individual sees only his own wish for access. The combined burden is greater than any one request appears in isolation.

**The One Man Who Acted on It**

Ibn Kathīr limits the command to those who could meet it. The clause *if you lack the means* is read as poverty, and the charity was asked only of the able. He then records who actually gave it. ʿAbd al-Razzāq recorded from Mujāhid that ʿAlī said, *"No one acted on this verse except me, until it was abrogated,"* and ʿAlī is also reported to have said that it stayed in force no more than an hour. The report is a Companion's own statement about himself, and Ibn Kathīr introduces it with a cautious *it was said*. What it shows is the pace of the episode: a rule given, obeyed by one man, and lifted, which is the sequence the next verse describes.

## Verse 58:13
> Are you afraid of spending in charity before your private consultations ˹with him˺? Since you are unable to do so, and Allah has turned to you in mercy, then ˹continue to˺ establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and obey Allah and His Messenger. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**The Question Exposes Anxiety About the Cost of Access**

The opening asks whether the believers have become afraid of giving charity before private consultation. Its concern is not simply the amount of money but the reluctance revealed by attaching generosity to a desired opportunity. People may value a private conversation highly until something must be surrendered before it. The question brings that difference into view without requiring a detailed account of every individual's means or motives.

Ibn Kathīr explains the apprehension as fear that the requirement would remain in force continually (Ibn Kathīr on 58:13 — *“Are you afraid of spending in charity before your private consultations ˹with him˺?”*). The observation adds a useful dimension. A repeated obligation can be felt differently from a single gift, especially when a person does not know how often he will need consultation. The verse addresses that anxiety while directing attention away from self-justifying calculations. It does not invite the audience to argue that charity has no value because the condition was difficult. Nor does it erase the distinction between genuine inability and reluctance. Its question disciplines the heart, and its following statement grants relief. The two actions belong together: mercy can recognize human difficulty while allowing the experience of that difficulty to teach something about attachment to resources.

**What the Verse Does After the Question**

The question is followed by a concession, and the concession is followed by an instruction. The verse tells the believers that if they did not do it — if they did not bring the charity before the private consultation — and God turned to them in mercy, then the way forward is named in plain terms: establish the prayer, give the zakāh, and obey God and His Messenger. The charity before consultation was a temporary discipline laid on a particular community at a particular moment, and the verse itself shows the door being opened again. What remains, and what the sentence leaves the reader with, is the standing shape of the religious life: worship, the giving of wealth, and obedience. The messenger was never in the market for a fee, and the consultation he offered was never for sale; the requirement was a lesson about what the believers were prepared to part with for access to him, and the lesson had been learned or it had not.

Ibn Kathīr records the reports that give this verse its place. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥah reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that the Muslims had kept putting questions to the Messenger of Allah ﷺ until it became a burden on him; Allah wished to lighten his load, and when the charity was required many held back, afraid to pay, and stopped asking. Then this verse came down and eased the matter. Saʿīd ibn Abī ʿArūbah carries the same account from Qatādah and Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, adding that a man with a real need to speak to the Prophet ﷺ could not do so until he had given, that this weighed on people, and that Allah then sent relief. ʿIkrimah and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī say plainly that the earlier command was abrogated by this verse, and Maʿmar reports from Qatādah that it stood for no more than an hour of a day before it was lifted. Ibn Kathīr's conclusion is that the obligation was abrogated, and that the words *and Allah has turned to you in mercy* mark the lifting.

**The Names at the End**

The verse closes with *and God is aware of what you do* — the same attribute that opened the passage's teaching about the secret counsels. What the charity was for is not written on the coin, and what a man withheld is not recorded in the ledger of the mosque. The verse leaves both in the hands of the One whose knowledge is named at the beginning and the end of this whole section of the sūrah, which is the sūrah's way of saying that a discipline imposed for the sake of appearances would have been pointless from the start.

## Verse 58:14
> Have you not seen those ˹hypocrites˺ who ally themselves with a people with whom Allah is displeased? They are neither with you nor with them. And they swear to lies knowingly

**The Alliance Is Judged Together With Its Deception**

The verse identifies people whose declared place among believers does not match their concealed commitments. Their alliance with a people under Allah's displeasure is not an isolated social fact. It belongs to a larger pattern of divided allegiance and deliberate lying. The three clauses interpret one another. A reading that detaches the first clause from the false oaths would miss the particular moral disorder being exposed.

Ibn Kathīr explains the case as the hypocrites' covert aid to their allies while publicly presenting themselves as believers (Ibn Kathīr on 58:14 — *“Have you not seen those ˹hypocrites˺ who ally themselves with a people with whom Allah is displeased?”*). This explanation makes the relation more precise than an indiscriminate prohibition of ordinary kindness toward people of another religion. The conduct concerns support joined to deceit about one's own position. The verse does not condemn neighbors for dealing fairly across religious differences. It exposes people who seek the benefits of a declared allegiance while acting contrary to it and lying when challenged. Their problem is not honest recognition of multiple social relationships. It is the use of those relationships without truth. Allah's displeasure marks the seriousness of the alignment, and the knowingly false oath reveals the means by which its speakers try to conceal what they are doing.

Ibn Kathīr names the people the verse has in view. The people under Allah's anger are the Jews, with whom the hypocrites were allied in secret, and the middle clause is read literally: the hypocrites were neither with the believers nor with the allies they had chosen. The oath at the end has a name in the law. The hypocrites swore that they believed while knowing that they did not, and an oath sworn on a known lie is the *yamīn al-ghamūs*, the oath that plunges its swearer into sin; Ibn Kathīr pauses to seek refuge with Allah from their way. He notes the strange edge of the case. What they swore, that Muḥammad ﷺ was the Messenger of Allah, was true in itself; the lie was in swearing that they believed it.

**A Parallel Verse Describes Wavering Rather Than Genuine Balance**

Ibn Kathīr connects this description with 4:143 — *“Torn between belief and disbelief—belonging neither to these ˹believers˺ nor those ˹disbelievers˺. And whoever Allah leaves to stray, you will never find for them a way”*, where the hypocrites are portrayed as wavering between sides without belonging to either. That checked cross-reference adds an useful distinction. Balance is a principled ability to recognize different legitimate claims; wavering here is a refusal of a truthful allegiance while seeking advantage from incompatible appearances. The two should not be confused merely because both can place a person between groups.

## Verse 58:15
> Allah has prepared for them a severe punishment. Evil indeed is what they do

**A Punishment Already Prepared**

The verse says that God has prepared a severe punishment for these people. They may hide what they do from other people, but hiding does not leave the matter undecided with God. The preparation belongs to His knowledge and judgment, not to some future inquiry whose result is still open. The sentence announces that there will be a consequence, without giving a date the offenders could turn into one more thing to calculate.

**Why the Punishment Is Severe**

The punishment is severe because the conduct being judged is not a harmless gap between private feeling and public manners. Ibn Kathīr explains the verse in relation to these people's deceit, their betrayal of the believers and their support for those who fought them. These are actions that damage the trust communal life depends on. People are led to rely on a show of loyalty while the show is being used against them.

**A Warning Beyond Reputation**

A parallel elsewhere in the Qur'an places the hypocrites in the lowest depth of the Fire (4:145 states it — *“Surely the hypocrites will be in the lowest depths of the Fire—and you will never find for them any helper”*). That comparison makes clear that this warning is not only about losing standing in a community. The seriousness belongs to a person's relationship with God and to the final judgment. Saying the words of religion outwardly does not protect someone who knowingly uses those words as a cover.

## Verse 58:16
> They have made their ˹false˺ oaths as a shield, hindering ˹others˺ from the cause of Allah. So they will suffer a humiliating punishment

**The Immediate Protection Serves a Wider Obstruction**

The consequence is hindering others from Allah's cause. The false oath does not end with preserving the speaker's reputation. It allows him to retain a position from which he can influence people. Trust obtained through deception becomes a means of diverting others from the guidance the speaker outwardly claims to respect.

Ibn Kathīr explains the sequence through concealed disbelief and the people deceived by its outward covering (Ibn Kathīr on 58:16 — *“They have made their ˹false˺ oaths as a shield, hindering ˹others˺ from the cause of Allah. So they will suffer a humiliating punishment”*). That reading shows why the two clauses are joined. A person openly known to oppose a path may have less power to misrepresent it than someone accepted as belonging to it. The deceptive insider can make obstruction appear to be advice, caution, or concern. The verse does not list every technique by which the hindrance occurs, so a commentary should not invent a catalogue of incidents. Its structural claim is enough: false assurance creates cover, and the cover helps diversion. The harm extends beyond the oath-taker's own inward state. Others may form judgments about religion, trust, and obedience under an influence whose real allegiance has been concealed from them.

Ibn Kathīr reads the humiliation at the end of the verse as the fitting return for the offence at its beginning. The oath was sworn by the Name of Allah, and they made light of that Name, using it as a cover for lies and hidden betrayal. A man who swears falsely to escape shame in front of people has bought a small dignity with a great one, and the punishment takes back the dignity he was protecting.

**A False Oath Can Win an Earthly Advantage Without Making It Right**

A verified Prophetic report gives a concrete parallel. In Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6676–6677, Ibn Masʿūd transmits a warning about using a false oath to get another Muslim's property. The same report includes al-Ashʿath ibn Qays's account of a disputed well and the requirement of evidence or an opposing oath (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6676–6677). This addition concerns property rather than the present obstruction of faith, but the misuse of solemn assurance is directly relevant.

## Verse 58:17
> Neither their wealth nor children will be of any help to them against Allah whatsoever. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever

**What Accompanies a Person Is Not Identical With What He Owns**

A report from Anas ibn Mālik offers a concrete parallel. The Prophet describes family, property, and deeds accompanying a dead person, after which family and property return while the deeds remain. This is Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2960. The report adds a vivid distinction beyond the verse's wording: what can gather around a person at departure is not necessarily what continues with him in his account.

The distinction does not make the proper use of wealth irrelevant. Giving, providing, and acting honestly with possessions are deeds rather than a tried purchase of immunity. Likewise, the way one treats children belongs to conduct, not merely to the fact of having descendants. The warning therefore redirects attention from ownership to responsibility. A person may count what he has while neglecting what he is doing with it. The report's contrast exposes the weakness of that accounting. Material things have a history of use, and that history matters in a way mere possession cannot. The verse concerns those whose wealth and children are imagined as protection against Allah. Their mistake is to count the resources around them as though those resources could replace a truthful relation to Him. The account follows action, not the prestige attached to having much.

**Forever Denies an Escape Based on Another Waiting Period**

The closing statement declares that they remain there forever. The sentence removes the possibility of treating the destination as a temporary loss that wealth or kinship might eventually help reverse. Earthly influence often operates through delay: one waits for an ally, a payment, or a changed circumstance. The final clause denies that familiar pattern as an escape from this judgment.

## Verse 58:18
> On the Day Allah resurrects them all, they will ˹falsely˺ swear to Him as they swear to you, thinking they have something to stand on. Indeed, it is they who are the ˹total˺ liars

**Resurrection Removes Distance Without Removing Their Habit of Lying**

Allah raises all the people concerned, and they try the same kind of false assurance they used among human beings. The change of setting is absolute, yet their response repeats an old practice. The verse exposes the depth of a habit through that contrast. They confront divine judgment but still reach for speech designed to manage appearances.

Ibn Kathīr explains the repetition with a rule he states in plain words: whoever lives on a certain path will most likely die on it, and will be raised on it. The men who spent their lives swearing to the believers that they were believers are raised still swearing, now to Allah, and still thinking the oath will do for Him what it did for people. The verse opens with the same words the sūrah used earlier for the Day the deeds are read back (58:6 — *“On the Day Allah resurrects them all together, He will then inform them of what they have done.”*), and the second scene explains the first. Those who forgot what they did are told it; those who lied about what they were try the lie once more.

**An Outward Profession Had Been Mistaken for a Final Acquittal**

Ibn Kathīr explains their expectation through the outward treatment they received in earthly life: people dealt with them according to their apparent profession, while Allah knew what lay beneath it (Ibn Kathīr on 58:18 — *“On the Day Allah resurrects them all, they will ˹falsely˺ swear to Him as they swear to you, thinking they have something to stand on.”*). This addition supplies an important distinction beyond the bare fact of their lying. Human recognition of an outward status is not the same as Allah's final approval of a person's inward reality.

A social or legal community cannot operate by claiming unrestricted knowledge of hearts. Its treatment of a person may therefore rest on public evidence and profession. Exploiting that necessary limitation does not make the hidden falsehood true. The hypocrites mistake a rule governing human treatment for a verdict governing divine judgment. That mistake is what their expectation of having something to stand on expresses. They imagine that the form which secured standing among people must carry the same force before Allah. The verse refuses the inference without requiring human beings to abandon fair outward procedures. The defect lies in the deceiver's abuse of those procedures and his false confidence about what they accomplish. Being treated as a believer by people does not authorize someone knowingly to conceal rejection and then claim that the treatment has purified his actual stance.

## Verse 58:19
> Satan has taken hold of them, causing them to forget the remembrance of Allah. They are the party of Satan. Surely Satan’s party is bound to lose

**Remembrance Joins Speech to the Direction of Life**

Remembering Allah includes words of praise, but the verse's contrast concerns a whole moral orientation. Pronouncing His name while deliberately excluding His command from action would not undo the condition being exposed. The remembrance neglected here should keep the person conscious of truth, responsibility, and the authority of the One remembered. Its absence allows a false account of security to become habitual.

Abū Mūsā reports the Prophet's comparison between the person who remembers his Lord and the person who does not as the difference between the living and the dead. The report is verified in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6407. The comparison concerns spiritual vitality, not a denial that the neglectful person remains biologically alive. It explains why remembrance cannot be treated as an ornamental addition to an otherwise complete life. The heart needs a living relation to its Lord if knowledge of right is to become responsive conduct. The report also avoids reducing the remedy to speculation about Satan. Attention is turned toward the act that gives the heart life. The believer counters domination not by endlessly describing the enemy but by restoring the remembrance and obedience that the enemy seeks to remove.

**The Verb of the Verse in a Prophetic Warning**

The verb the verse uses for Satan's hold, *istaḥwadha*, appears in a warning the Prophet ﷺ gave about prayer. Abū Dāwūd recorded from Abū al-Dardāʾ that he heard the Messenger of Allah ﷺ say, *"There are no three in a village or in the desert among whom the prayer is not established but Satan has taken hold of them; so keep to the congregation, for the wolf takes the sheep that strays."* Zāʾidah added that al-Sāʾib explained the congregation as praying together. Ibn Kathīr places the report here because it shows what the verse's overpowering looks like in an ordinary place: not possession, but a group that has let the prayer lapse, and so has let go of the thing that held Satan off.

He then reads the two halves of the verse in that light. Satan took over their hearts to the point that he made them forget Allah, which is what the devil does with everyone he controls; and those who have been mastered in this way, and have forgotten the remembrance as a result, are the party he leads. The name is a description, not a decree. Whoever restores the prayer and the remembrance has walked out of the party, which is why the sūrah can set against it, three verses later, the party of Allah and its success (58:22 — *“They are the party of Allah. Indeed, Allah’s party is bound to succeed”*).

## Verse 58:20
> ˹As for˺ those who defy Allah and His Messenger, they will definitely be among the most debased

**Defiance Is a Position Taken Against Revealed Authority**

The verse identifies its subjects by their action: they defy Allah and His Messenger. The description concerns a stance of opposition, not merely the presence of a question, an incomplete understanding, or a difference in social identity. The gravity of the warning follows from the authority resisted. The Messenger conveys Allah's guidance; defiance of that mission cannot be reduced to an ordinary disagreement between private individuals.

Ibn Kathīr explains the opposition as a separation between the position of stubborn refusal and the position of guidance (Ibn Kathīr on 58:20 — *“˹As for˺ those who defy Allah and His Messenger, they will definitely be among the most debased”*). This explanation sharpens the sense of the verb without turning it into a slogan against every critic. The question is not whether a person has disagreed with a powerful believer. It is whether he has taken the stance toward Allah and His Messenger that the verse names. That distinction matters because human authority can err. A teacher or ruler cannot simply appropriate the Messenger's standing and make opposition to himself identical with opposition to Allah. The verse's severity must remain attached to its actual object. It judges rejection of divine guidance, not the wounded pride of anyone claiming to speak in its name.

Ibn Kathīr's gloss on *the most debased* is a list: the wretched, the driven out, the ones barred from good, humiliated in this life and the next. He reads the verse as the second statement of what the sūrah said at its fifth verse, where the same defiance ended in the same debasement and was compared with those before them (58:5 — *“Indeed, those who defy Allah and His Messenger will be debased, just like those before them.”*). The repetition frames the middle of the sūrah. Between the two statements lie the whisperers, the false swearers and the party of Satan, and the frame tells the reader what all of them have in common.

**Firmness Against Defiance Does Not Cancel Kindness and Justice**

The Qur'anic distinction between hostile opposition and peaceful relations must remain intact. In 60:8 — *“Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly and fairly with those who have neither fought nor driven you out of your homes. Surely Allah loves those who are fair”*, Allah permits kindness and fairness toward those who have not fought believers on account of religion or driven them from their homes (Qurʾān 60:8 — *“Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly and fairly with those who have neither fought nor driven you out of your homes. Surely Allah loves those who are fair”*). This parallel prevents the present warning from becoming a general prohibition on decent relations with people of another faith. The command concerns the stance being judged, not an obligation to be unjust toward everyone outside the believing community.

## Verse 58:21
> Allah has decreed, “I and My messengers will certainly prevail.” Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty

**The Promise Begins With a Decree, Not a Calculation**

Allah's declaration is presented as decreed. Its certainty does not depend on a favorable balance of visible resources or on observers agreeing that success seems likely. Human forecasts begin with limited evidence and remain open to correction. This statement begins with the authority of the One who determines the outcome. The order of the sentence makes the source of confidence unmistakable.

The verb translated decreed can carry the sense of writing or prescribing. Ibn Kathīr explains it here as a settled divine determination recorded in the decree (Ibn Kathīr on 58:21 — *“Allah has decreed, “I and My messengers will certainly prevail.” Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty”*). The point is not that the reader must imagine a physical process of inscription before trusting the promise. It is that the matter is established beyond the changes that unsettle human intention. A ruler may issue a declaration and later lack the power to carry it out. Allah's decree is not exposed to that division between intention and capacity. The final divine names will make that explicit. At the opening, the believer is already directed away from treating current appearances as the highest authority over what can happen. The promise has a ground stronger than the calculations by which an anxious or confident audience might assess it.

Ibn Kathīr expands the verb with three phrases: Allah has decreed it, written it in the First Book, and settled it in the decree that cannot be turned back or altered — that the final outcome belongs to Him, His Book, His messengers and the believers, in this world and the next. He reads it beside the promise given to the Prophet at the end of the story of Noah (11:49 — *“Surely the ultimate outcome belongs ˹only˺ to the righteous”*) and the pledge of help to the messengers in this life and on the Day the witnesses stand, which is treated below. The word *first* in *the First Book* is the point. The victory was written before the contest, so the contest cannot rewrite it.

**Prevailing Is Larger Than a Single Visible Encounter**

Ibn Kathīr connects the verse with 40:51–52 — *“We certainly help Our messengers and the believers, ˹both˺ in this worldly life and on the Day the witnesses will stand forth ˹for testimony˺— the Day the wrongdoers’ excuses will be of no benefit to them. They will be condemned, and will have the worst outcome”*, which speaks of support for the messengers and believers in earthly life and at the standing of the witnesses. This parallel adds the final judgment explicitly to the horizon of the promise. It prevents a reader from measuring all vindication by one campaign, one lifetime, or one period of political power. The comparison does not make worldly events irrelevant; it places them within a larger account. An immediate setback cannot by itself refute a promise whose scope exceeds that moment. Equally, a temporary victory cannot justify whatever a victorious party happens to do. The truth of the mission remains the measure. Prevailing under Allah's decree is not a blank approval of every human act performed while claiming to defend religion. The promise concerns His messengers and His truth, not ambition borrowing their names.

## Verse 58:22
> You will never find a people who ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day loyal to those who defy Allah and His Messenger, even if they were their parents, children, siblings, or extended family. For those ˹believers˺, Allah has instilled faith in their hearts and strengthened them with a spirit from Him. He will admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him. They are the party of Allah. Indeed, Allah’s party is bound to succeed

**Faith Sets a Limit to Loyalty Even Within the Family**

The verse confronts the strongest ordinary bonds: parents, children, siblings, and the wider family. These ties can carry love, gratitude, duty, and a sense of shared identity. Their force is acknowledged by naming them. Yet none can make active defiance of Allah and His Messenger into something a believer must support. Faith establishes a limit that kinship cannot erase.

The wording concerns loyalty to those who defy, not an order to extinguish every natural affection toward a relative. A believer can love a parent while refusing the parent's demand to abandon faith. The distinction becomes explicit in 31:15 — *“But if they pressure you to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, do not obey them. Still keep their company in this world courteously, and follow the way of those who turn to Me ˹in devotion˺. Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and then I will inform you of what you used to do”*, which forbids obeying parents who press for association of partners with Allah while still requiring courteous companionship in worldly life (Qurʾān 31:15 — *“Still keep their company in this world courteously, and follow the way of those who turn to Me ˹in devotion˺.”*). This parallel adds a crucial practical boundary. Refusal of religious betrayal and continued good treatment can coexist. The present verse is not a license for private violence, cruelty, or indiscriminate severing of family obligations. It denies that family allegiance is ultimate. Where relatives seek support against divine guidance, the believer cannot make love an excuse for joining the defiance. The pressure is real, but it does not become a source of religious authority.

Ibn Kathīr sets two earlier verses beside this one. The first is the general rule that believers do not take disbelievers as guardians in place of the believers, with its one exception for real fear (3:28 — *“Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah—unless it is a precaution against their tyranny.”*). The second is the verse that lists the same relatives this one lists, adds wealth, trade and homes, and asks whether all of them together are dearer than Allah and His Messenger (9:24 — *“If your parents and children and siblings and spouses and extended family and the wealth you have acquired and the trade you fear will decline and the homes you cherish—˹if all these˺ are more beloved to you than Allah and His Messenger and struggling in His Way, then wait until Allah brings about His Will.”*). The present verse is the mirror of that question. There the believers are asked what they love more; here they are described as the people who have already answered.

**Faith Is Established in Hearts Rather Than Merely Displayed in Speech**

Ibn Kathīr explains the inscription of faith as Allah's establishing it and making it dear within their hearts (Ibn Kathīr on 58:22 — *“For those ˹believers˺, Allah has instilled faith in their hearts and strengthened them with a spirit from Him.”*). That explanation does not remove their action from the verse. They are the people whose conduct has been described, and their firmness is then identified as a blessing from Allah. Grace and responsibility are not offered as rival accounts. The believer acts faithfully while recognizing that the faith enabling fidelity is not a possession he created independently. This prevents the costly refusal of a wrong allegiance from becoming a new source of arrogance. One may have resisted pressure successfully without becoming self-sufficient on that account. The heart's firmness is a gift to preserve through obedience and gratitude, not a title that authorizes contempt for people still struggling with difficult attachments.

**What God Writes Into the Heart**

The verse is not only a prohibition against siding with the enemies of the faith. After naming the limit, it states what is given to the people who hold to it: God has written faith into their hearts and supported them with a spirit from Him. The commentators read *kataba* as a decree and a guarantee, and the spirit as the aid God gives to a believer, with some reading it as the Qur'an and others as a strength that comes with guidance. The verse then names where these people end up: gardens with rivers running under them, dwelling in them forever, well pleased with God and pleasing to Him.

Ibn Kathīr records al-Suddī's gloss that *written faith in their hearts* means He placed it there, and Ibn ʿAbbās's that the spirit from Him means He gave them strength. On the clause about pleasure he finds what he calls a beautiful secret. These people were angered against their own fathers and brothers for Allah's sake, and He repaid them in the same coin, being pleased with them and making them pleased with Him through what He gave: lasting delight, victory and an encompassing favour. The exchange is exact. They gave up the approval of their kin, and received the approval of their Lord.

**The Party of God**

The verse closes with a name that the sūrah uses twice more: these are the party of God, and the party of God are the successful (58:22; compared with 58:19 — *“Satan has taken hold of them, causing them to forget the remembrance of Allah. They are the party of Satan. Surely Satan’s party is bound to lose”* and 5:56 — *“Whoever allies themselves with Allah, His Messenger, and fellow believers, then it is certainly Allah’s party that will prevail”*). The Book puts the phrase at the end of a verse about family because the choice being described is not small. A man who has to disagree with his father, his brother or his tribe over the faith has been placed in a different party, and the verse reassures him that the party is not a crowd of strangers. The Sūrah of the Table states the same arrangement for the believers who take God and His messenger and the believers as allies (5:55-56 — *“Whoever allies themselves with Allah, His Messenger, and fellow believers, then it is certainly Allah’s party that will prevail”*).

**The Reports Behind the Verse**

The commentators connect the verse with men in Madinah whose closest relatives were still in the ranks of the disbelievers, and with those who were being pressed by their families to go back. Abū ʿUbaydah ibn al-Jarrāḥ is named in several of the reports, since his father was in the enemy ranks at Badr. The Book does not record a single case as the occasion, and the wording is deliberately general, because the situation repeated itself for every convert whose household had not believed. What the verse settles is where the line is: courtesy towards a relative who disagrees is required — the Sūrah of Luqmān commands kind treatment of a parent who pushes towards idolatry (31:15 — *“But if they pressure you to associate with Me what you have no knowledge of, do not obey them. Still keep their company in this world courteously, and follow the way of those who turn to Me ˹in devotion˺. Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and then I will inform you of what you used to do”*) — and being moved from the faith to keep the peace is not.

Ibn Kathīr gives the names the reports attach to each clause. Saʿīd ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz and others said the verse came down about Abū ʿUbaydah ʿĀmir ibn al-Jarrāḥ, who killed his disbelieving father at Badr — the reason, Ibn Kathīr adds, that ʿUmar said when he left the caliphate to a council of six, *"If Abū ʿUbaydah were alive I would have appointed him."* Another account divides the clauses: the fathers for Abū ʿUbaydah; the sons for Abū Bakr, who set out to kill his son ʿAbd al-Raḥmān at Badr; the brothers for Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr, who killed his brother ʿUbayd there; the kindred for ʿUmar, who killed a relative that day, and for Ḥamzah, ʿAlī and ʿUbaydah ibn al-Ḥārith, who killed ʿUtbah, Shaybah and al-Walīd ibn ʿUtbah. Ibn Kathīr closes the list with *Allah knows best*, which is the right note. The verse is general, and Badr simply supplied the first men it described.

He adds a scene from the same battle that shows the disposition the verse praises. When the Prophet ﷺ consulted his Companions about the captives of Badr, Abū Bakr advised taking ransom, since the prisoners were cousins and kin who might yet embrace Islam, and the money would strengthen the Muslims. ʿUmar answered, *"I do not see it so, Messenger of Allah. Let me kill so-and-so, my relative, and let ʿAlī kill ʿAqīl, and let each man kill his kinsman, so that Allah knows there is no softness in our hearts for the idolaters."* Ibn Kathīr does not give ʿUmar's counsel as the ruling but as a witness to the verse. The bond the believers were being asked to put second was real, and they knew it.
