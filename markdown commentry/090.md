# Sūrah al-Balad (Chapter 90) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:1

> I do swear by this city ˹of Mecca˺—

**Expanded Commentary**

**An Oath Lifts a Place Above Itself**

An Arabic oath is an instrument of weight. The speaker swears by whatever is heaviest in the mind of the listener, and the weight of the thing sworn by carries across into the sentence sworn for. When revelation swears by a city, it declares that the city stands in God's reckoning and not merely in the reckoning of caravans. Mecca is identified here without being named: *bi-hādhā al-balad*, "by this city", with no proper noun attached. For the first audience the reference was never ambiguous, since only one place in their world answered to the definite article, yet the choice of the common noun keeps the object double. It is a settled place in the general sense, and it is the exact ground under the hearers' feet. The same gesture recurs twice more. Mecca closes the oath list of 95:1–3 as "this secure city ˹of Mecca˺" (95:3), and the command that grounds the Prophet's mission in geography reads: "I have only been commanded to worship the Lord of this city ˹of Mecca˺, Who has made it sacred" (27:91). Sanctity is a property of the place, and the oath is one way of saying so out loud.

**The Negation That Strengthens the Oath**

The Arabic opens *lā uqsimu*, literally "I do not swear", and the negative particle has to be accounted for. One line of grammatical reading treats *lā* here as a particle of emphasis rather than negation, so the sentence means "indeed I swear by this city", and the oath stands at full force. A second line keeps the negation and reads it rhetorically: the point is so plain that swearing is unnecessary, "no need for an oath — this city is what it is". A third treats it as a rebuttal of a prior denial by the hearers, "no, I do swear". The emphatic reading carries the argument, because an oath that is announced and then abandoned leaves the following oath-list hanging. The text proceeds to swear by a second object and then delivers a sworn statement, which is the grammar of an oath actually taken. Rhetorical negation also survives inside the emphatic reading: to say "I need not swear" and then swear is a common Arabic intensification, and both effects land together. The listener hears a claim placed under the strongest available guarantee, and the guarantee is a place.

**Sacred Because of What Stands Inside It**

A city has no sanctity of its own. Mecca is honoured for the House it holds, and the revelation says this directly: "Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people" (3:96). The sanctity is derivative, and it is old. Abraham and Ishmael were given the task of purifying that House "for those who circle it, who meditate in it, and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺" (2:125), and Abraham had earlier settled part of his family "in a barren valley, near Your Sacred House" (14:37). None of this describes fertile land or a strategic harbour. The valley was dry, and the city's claim on reverence rests entirely on worship. That matters for the oath in front of us. Swearing by Mecca is not swearing by prosperity or by tribal prestige; it is swearing by the fact that God placed a House in a waterless valley and made the place holy on that account. Every later claim Mecca's residents make about their own status has to be measured against that origin.

**Places Sworn By Are Arguments, Not Decoration**

The Qur'an's oath lists are compact summaries of its evidences, and this one is typical. The fig and the olive, Mount Sinai and the secure city are named together in 95:1–3 before the sūrah turns to the creation of humanity in the best form. Three of those four items are places tied to revelation: a land associated with prophetic nourishment, a mountain where a law was given, a city where a House was built. Geography is doing theological work. It says that God's dealings with human beings have happened at identifiable coordinates, in valleys and on mountains that people can walk to, and that the record of those dealings is not a private philosophy. Swearing by Mecca continues that method. The city is not an ornament in the sentence; it is a piece of evidence. Something was done there, by a specific family, at the command of God, and the oath asks the listener to admit that the fact is public enough to be sworn by. A claim that could be sworn by a city had to be a claim a city could witness.

**The Oath Binds the Speaker Too**

Every oath has two halves: the thing sworn by and the thing sworn for. The first half magnifies, and the second half commits. When God swears, the commitment is not for His own benefit, since nothing constrains Him, but for the listener's: the sentence that follows is placed beyond retraction. This is why the oaths of the Qur'an cluster where the content is hardest to accept — the reality of resurrection, the certainty of judgment, the condition of the human soul. The form itself is a concession to human doubt. A hearer who will not accept a plain assertion may accept an assertion that has been guaranteed by the most venerable object in his world, and once he accepts it the guarantee can be dropped. Structurally, then, verse 1 exists to make what comes after it undeniable. The oath's subject is a city; the matter sworn to, as the sūrah unfolds, concerns human beings rather than stones, and the mismatch is the point.

**A Sanctuary That Obligates Its Residents**

Abraham's own prayer for this place asked for two things: "make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure and provide fruits to its people—those among them who believe in Allah and the Last Day" (2:126). Security and provision were requested as conditions for worship, and the answer included a warning for the ungrateful within the same verse. Mecca's status is repeatedly described in these terms elsewhere: God "fed them against hunger and made them secure against fear" (106:4), and the safe haven is set against the surrounding lands where "people ˹all˺ around them are snatched away" (29:67). The residents therefore live inside a favour, not inside an exemption. The oath of this verse invokes the honoured status of the city in a sūrah that goes on to interrogate the people who benefit from it, and that juxtaposition is the first cut the sūrah makes. To be sworn by is to be held. A place that God dignifies with an oath has residents whose conduct is now under the same oath, and the privilege of living in the sanctuary carries a debt that residence alone does not discharge.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:2

> even though you ˹O Prophet˺ are subject to abuse in this city—

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Parenthesis Inside a Solemn Sentence**

The oath is interrupted before it finishes. Between the swearing by the city and the swearing by parent and child, a clause is inserted about the Prophet's own situation in that city. Arabic allows this kind of parenthetical, and the interruption is the point: the city has just been honoured with an oath, and the very next breath records how the city treats its most honoured resident. The grammar of the clause is compressed. The key word is *ḥill*, a verbal noun that can mean either "one who is permitted, for whom something has become lawful" or "one who is residing, alighting, present in a place". Both senses are live in the classical language, and the choice between them decides whether the verse is a promise of military permission or a statement of honourable residence. Exegetes have taken both, and the tradition leans heavily toward the first.

**The Dominant Reading: Permission Granted to One Man**

Most commentators read *wa-anta ḥillun bi-hādhā al-balad* as "and you are free to act as you judge best in this city", with the sense that the restraint which normally forbids bloodshed in the sanctuary is lifted for this man alone. On that reading the clause is a prediction. If the sūrah is Makkan — and the early dating is the majority position — the verse foretells an event that would not occur for years: the entry into Mecca in which a small number of the Quraysh were killed. The alternative reading treats the clause as descriptive rather than predictive: the city has been honoured by having God's messenger living in it, and the honour is the point. The predictive reading is the stronger for a simple reason. The sūrah's whole movement is toward the exposure of people who imagine themselves untouchable, and a clause that announces a future reckoning in the sanctuary fits that movement, whereas a clause of pure honour would sit oddly beside it. The reading of residence is not wrong so much as incomplete; it states half of what the verse does.

**The Verified Rule About Fighting in the Sanctuary**

The claim that the Prophet alone was permitted what others were not is not an inference of the exegetes; it is his own statement, delivered on the day of the conquest. Ibn ʿAbbās reported that the Messenger of Allah ﷺ said: "Allah has made Mecca a sanctuary since the day He created the heavens and the earth, and it will remain a sanctuary by virtue of the sanctity Allah has bestowed upon it till the Day of Resurrection. Fighting in it was not made lawful for anyone before me, nor will it be made lawful for anyone after me, and it was not made lawful for me except for a short period of time" (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 3189). The same report is transmitted in the Book of Sales with the added detail that its thorny bushes may not be uprooted and its game may not be chased (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2090). Two things follow. The exception is personal, not general: it belongs to one man and to one hour, and it establishes no precedent for anyone after him. And the exception is short, which means the sanctuary's rule survives intact even in the moment it is set aside.

**Why an Exception Proves the Rule**

A licence limited to an hour is a strange gift, and its strangeness is instructive. Had fighting in Mecca been made lawful as a matter of policy, the conquest would have been an ordinary military event. Instead the permission is framed as a suspension with a stated expiry, delivered in the same speech that restates the prohibition for all time. The legal effect is to protect the sanctuary against exactly the argument a conqueror would make: that victory creates new rules. It does not. The victor in this case announced that the rules were unchanged and that his own action was a singular dispensation. Read against verse 2, the point sharpens. The clause about the Prophet being *ḥill* is not a grant of licence in the ordinary sense; it is the naming of the one man through whom God would settle the city's account, and the narrowness of the grant is a measure of how seriously the sanctuary's status is taken.

**The Irony the Clause Records**

Whatever reading is preferred, the clause carries a rebuke aimed at the city's custodians. The Quraysh held the keys of the Kaʿbah and drew their prestige from the House. Their claim was religious as much as political: they were the servants of the sanctuary, the ones who gave water to pilgrims and maintained the rites. The verse places inside the oath a fact that dismantles the claim from within — the sanctuary's own keepers were abusing the man whom God had sent to them, in the city whose holiness they administered. Sanctity of place did not produce reverence in its inhabitants. The pattern is not unique to Mecca, and the Qur'an states it as a general principle where it asks how a safe haven can coexist with ingratitude: "Have they not seen how We have made ˹Mecca˺ a safe haven, whereas people ˹all˺ around them are snatched away? How can they then believe in falsehood and deny Allah's favours?" (29:67). A holy place is not a guarantee about the people in it.

**A Prophecy and Its Restraint**

The fulfilment of the clause is worth noting for what did not happen. When the armies entered Mecca, the permission for bloodshed was exercised within the narrowest limits, and the general amnesty that followed was the event remembered. A man who believed he had been given a licence would have taken the widest reading of it. The record shows the opposite: the entry was conducted as a mercy, and the sanctuary's status was proclaimed in the same breath as the exception. That restraint is the meaning of *ḥill* in practice. The Prophet was free in the city not because the city's rules had collapsed but because God had appointed him as the one who would settle its account, and he settled it in a way that left the sanctuary more honoured than he found it. The clause inserted into the oath is therefore a promise with a moral shape: the reckoning would come, and it would come without the vengeance the city's own conduct had earned.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:3

> and by every parent and ˹their˺ child!

**Expanded Commentary**

**From a City to the People in It**

The second object of the oath moves from stone to flesh. After the city, the sūrah swears by every begetter and what he begets, and the shift is not incidental. A place is honoured by its inhabitants, and the inhabitants of this place trace themselves through lines of descent. The Arabic is deliberately broad: *wālid wa-mā walad*, a parent and that which he begot, with no name attached to either half. Two readings follow from that openness. The wide reading takes the pair as Adam and his whole progeny, which makes the oath cover the entire human race from its first father to its last child. The narrow reading takes it as Abraham and Ishmael, or Ishmael and the Arabs descended from him, which ties the second oath back to the first through the city itself, since Abraham's association with Mecca is what made the valley holy. Both readings are grammatically available, and the choice between them changes the scope of what follows. The wide reading is the more economical, because the sūrah's later material concerns humanity as a created kind rather than one genealogy, and a general oath prepares a general statement.

**Why the Second Term Uses "What" Rather Than "Who"**

The pairing is asymmetric in Arabic, and the asymmetry is meaningful. The first half is a definite noun, *al-wālid*, the begetter, a person with an identity. The second half is introduced by *mā*, the relative that ordinarily covers non-personal things, "that which he begot". Grammarians and exegetes have read that particle in two ways. It can be an indefinite covering a plurality, "and his offspring", which keeps the second term personal while refusing to give it a single shape; or it can mark the begetting itself, the act and its product considered together. The indefinite form does real work. Had the verse said "and his child", the oath would have named one relation and left the rest outside. By saying "that which he begot", it sweeps in every generation, every descendant, and every link in a chain that no one can count. There is also a rhetorical economy in the choice. The pair is stated at maximum generality so that no listener can stand outside it. Every hearer is somebody's child, and almost every hearer is somebody's parent. The oath closes over the audience completely, which is what an oath at the head of a sūrah is meant to do before it says something unwelcome.

**Generation as the Deepest Human Bond**

Swearing by parent and child puts the strongest bond human beings know at the centre of the argument. That bond is not chosen. It is given, it precedes consent, and it carries obligations that no contract creates. The Qur'an builds large parts of its ethics on it: honour to parents sits next to worship of God in the covenant taken from the children of Israel, "be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy" (2:83), and the decree of the later revelation is starker still, "never say to them ˹even˺ ‘ugh,’ nor yell at them" (17:23). Lineage also organises human societies, and nowhere more visibly than in the city this sūrah swears by, where a man's standing followed from whose son he was. To make that bond the object of a divine oath is to take it seriously on its own terms and then to place it under God's authority. The bond is real, and it is not ultimate. It exists inside a creation that owes its existence to One who neither begets nor is begotten, and the oath uses the strongest human tie to point beyond it.

**The Orphan Prophet Inside an Oath About Fathers**

There is a biographical edge to this verse that the first audience could feel. The man to whom the sūrah was revealed had no father. ʿAbdullāh, his father, died before he was born; his mother Āmina died when he was about six; and his grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, who took him in, died when he was about eight. In a society where protection came from a male line, his line had thinned to an uncle. An oath that swears by every parent and child, revealed to a man whose own parentage was a public fact, would not have been heard as an abstraction. It frames his case inside the general human condition rather than outside it. Some exegetes draw a further link and read the begetter here as a reference to the Prophet's own descent from Abraham through Ishmael, which would make the genealogy of the city and the genealogy of its messenger the same genealogy. Whether or not that narrow reading is adopted, the wider point holds: the verse speaks of the bond the Prophet lacked, and it does so with reverence rather than pity.

**An Oath With No Escape Clause**

Oaths by created things work because the thing sworn by is fixed. A city cannot decide to be elsewhere; a father cannot decide not to have begotten. The immobility of the object is what gives the oath its force, and it also determines what the oath is for. Something permanent is being invoked to guarantee something the hearer is inclined to doubt. The two objects sworn by in these verses — a sanctuary and a lineage — are exactly the two things the Meccan audience trusted most. Their security came from the first and their status from the second. By making both of them the framework of His oath, the text takes the audience's own certainties and turns them into the setting for a verdict on themselves. Nothing here is being borrowed from outside their world. The guarantee is built from their own most valued possessions, and the sentence guaranteed will not flatter the possessors.

**What an Oath By Generation Rules Out**

Placing parent and child under an oath removes the possibility of treating descent as a shield. A man cannot say that his father's standing answers for him, because the very relation he would invoke has been made the frame of a divine statement, not the content of one. The Qur'an makes the same point elsewhere with brutal clarity: "O humanity! Indeed, We created you from a male and a female, and made you into peoples and tribes so that you may ˹get to˺ know one another. Surely the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous among you" (49:13). Genealogy is real and it is useful for knowing one another; it is worthless for ranking. The oath in this verse performs that teaching in the form of a solemn declaration. It honours the bond by swearing on it, and it humbles the bond by making it a witness rather than a defence. Every hearer is inside the oath, and every hearer is answerable within it.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:4

> Indeed, We have created humankind in ˹constant˺ struggle.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Statement the Oath Was Taken For**

After two oaths the sūrah delivers what was sworn to, and it is a single sentence about the human condition. Humanity was created *fī kabad*, in struggle. The word is the pivot of the whole chapter. *Kabad* carries the sense of toil, strain and difficulty, and it is set here as a created feature rather than an accident. Human beings are not placed in struggle as a punishment added later; they are constituted in it. The verse answers a question nobody asked but everybody feels, which is why existence costs so much. It is not a flaw in the arrangement. It is the arrangement. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī's gloss, preserved in the early commentaries, states the two halves of the cost: the hardships of this world in life and the severities of the Hereafter after it. The first is experienced; the second is promised. Between them the human being has no interval of untroubled ease, and the sūrah treats that as the starting datum for ethics rather than a complaint to be resolved.

**Three Readings of Kabad, and Which Holds**

The exegetical tradition offers three distinct accounts of the word. The first and most widely held takes *kabad* as toil: life is labour from birth to death. The second narrows it to the physical sequence of human existence, connecting the verse to "His mother carried him ˹in hardship˺ and delivered him ˹in hardship˺" (46:15), and reading the struggle as the stages that follow — weaning, teething, earning a living, ageing, dying. The third is physical rather than moral: *kabad* also means erect or upright, and on that reading the verse notes that the human being alone among creatures walks upright on two legs. Each reading is philologically possible. The toil reading is the stronger, for two reasons. It is the primary sense of the root, and the sūrah's own argument depends on it. What follows is an accusation against a man who imagines himself beyond reach because of his wealth, and a definition of the good as a steep path that must be charged. Neither makes sense if the verse is only a note on posture. Uprightness would be a fact about anatomy; toil is a fact about moral pressure, and only moral pressure can support the argument that comes next.

**Struggle as the Setting for Choice**

A condition of constant difficulty is not a defect in the design; it is the condition under which choice becomes visible. Ease hides preference. When nothing costs anything, a person's decisions reveal nothing about him. Difficulty forces the question every time: what will you give up, and for whom? The Qur'an states the same logic where it asks, "Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you?" (2:214), and it describes the testing as so severe that even a messenger cries out for help. Hardship is not the interruption of a moral life; it is the medium of one. This verse supplies the premise, and the rest of the sūrah supplies the two responses to it. One response is to conclude that the struggle can be escaped by accumulation, which is the error the following verses expose. The other is to accept the struggle and direct it, which is what the definition of the steep path later in the chapter describes. The verse itself chooses neither. It states the condition and leaves the reader inside it.

**Why Toil Is Not the Whole Truth About the Human Being**

The same sūrah that calls humanity created in struggle also honours it in the verses that follow, and the two claims have to be held together. Toil is the condition; dignity is the endowment. Elsewhere the revelation states the honour side of the account: "Indeed, We have dignified the children of Adam, carried them on land and sea, granted them good and lawful provisions, and privileged them far above many of Our creatures" (17:70). A creature made in the best form is also a creature made to labour, and neither statement cancels the other. The pairing is what makes the human case distinctive. Animals labour and do not know why; angels know and do not labour. The human being does both, and the strain is the price of the knowing. Read against that background, *kabad* is not a slur on humanity. It is a description of the only creature whose effort can be morally significant, because only a creature that resists something can be said to have chosen it.

**A Diagnosis Before an Accusation**

The rhetorical placement of the verse matters as much as its content. It arrives before any rebuke. The sūrah does not begin by blaming the boastful spender; it begins by locating him, and everyone else, inside a shared predicament. That order prevents the reader from using the accusation as a way of separating himself from the accused. The man who says he has wasted enormous wealth is not an alien type; he is a human being responding badly to a condition that applies to him too. The strategy recurs throughout the revelation, which typically establishes the common ground before it draws the line. Once the ground is fixed, the line has force. A reader who has just been told that he was made in struggle cannot hear the exposure of another man's arrogance as gossip. He hears it as a description of what he himself is tempted to do with his own wealth, and the sūrah has made that hearing unavoidable before the exposure begins.

**The Verse Against the Consolation of Comfort**

Modern readers tend to treat hardship as a malfunction to be engineered away, and to treat its absence as the goal of a life. This verse refuses both moves. It does not promise that struggle will end, and it does not treat the reduction of struggle as a moral achievement. What it does is remove the two consolations people reach for. The first is that the difficulty is a private misfortune, which it is not, since the condition is created and universal. The second is that the difficulty is meaningless, which it is not, since a created condition is created by One who knows what He intends by it. Between those two refusals the reader is left with the only useful posture: to stop arguing with the terms of existence and start asking what the terms are for. The sūrah asks that question immediately, and it answers it in terms of other people — a slave to be freed, a hungry relative, a poor man in the dust — rather than in terms of the sufferer's own relief.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:5

> Do they think that no one has power over them,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Exposes an Assumption**

The sūrah turns from statement to interrogation, and the interrogation is aimed at a belief rather than at an act. *A-yaḥsabu an lan yaqdira ʿalayhi aḥad* — does he reckon that no one will ever have power over him? The question is not asked for information. It is asked to drag a private assumption into the open where it can be examined. The assumption is the real offence; the spending and the boasting that follow are only its symptoms. The form of the question does precise work. A yes-or-no question about a belief forces the hearer to inspect a belief he has probably never stated, and probably would not state if asked directly. Nobody says out loud, "no one can overpower me". People simply act as though it were true, and the acting is what the verse targets. The shift from "humankind" in the previous verse to "he" here is deliberate too: a general condition is now being lived out in a particular temperament, and the reader is invited to recognise the temperament in himself before he recognises it in anyone else.

**The Grammar of Denial in "No One"**

Arabic negates existence in two different ways, and the difference is the point of this verse. *Lā* in a bare statement denies a fact; *lan* with a following verb denies it into the future. The construction here combines *lan* with *yaqdira* and then closes with *aḥad*, the indefinite that in negative contexts means "anyone at all". The result is a claim of permanent immunity: not "no one is powerful now", but "no one will ever be able to act on me". The temporal reach of the denial is what makes it a theological error rather than a passing mood. A man who believes he cannot be touched today may still expect to be touched tomorrow; this man expects never to be. That expectation is the quiet form of unbelief, and it does not require a stated creed. It requires only a way of handling money, and the next verse supplies it. The verse therefore identifies a category of error that is very hard to preach against, because the person who holds it does not know that he holds it, and would deny it if he were asked.

**Wealth as the Evidence for Immunity**

The belief the question exposes has a material basis. In a mercantile city, a man's security was purchased: allies bought, debts owed to him, dependants who needed his protection, and a store of wealth that answered every demand before it arrived. Money converts into influence, influence converts into immunity, and immunity feels like permanence. The Qur'an treats this chain as the standard mechanism of delusion, and it names the mechanism wherever it appears. Wealth and children are described repeatedly as a test rather than as a possession: "And know that your wealth and your children are only a test and that with Allah is a great reward" (8:28), and again "Your wealth and children are only a test" (64:15). The verse about competition is blunter still: "Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺" (102:1). What the boaster mistakes for a fortress is, on the revelation's account, an examination paper. The error is not that he values wealth; it is that he reads wealth as a verdict about his own standing, and then infers from the verdict that he cannot be reached.

**The Historical Target and the Limits of Naming Him**

The exegetical reports attach this verse to a named Meccan. The identification most often given is al-Ḥārith ibn ʿĀmir, who is said to have complained that his money had gone to atonements and payments since he entered Islam, treating the required expenditure as waste. Other reports name different men, and the identifications do not agree with one another. That disagreement is worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over. The reports agree on the type — a wealthy man who resents religious obligation on financial grounds — and differ on the individual. Since the verse is phrased generally and the accusation is a matter of temperament rather than of biography, nothing in the argument turns on the name. What the reports contribute is the setting: this is not a philosophical position held in the abstract but a commercial grievance voiced by men who counted what piety cost them. The verse's force comes from that concreteness. It is addressed to a person who has just done a calculation and drawn the wrong conclusion from it, and such people exist in every mercantile age.

**Power Over Him, Not Merely Around Him**

The verb in the question is *yaqdira ʿalayhi*, to have power over him, and the preposition matters. It is not a statement about whether others are strong in general, but about whether anyone can act upon this man. The immunity claimed is personal. He does not deny that powerful people exist; he denies that their power reaches him. The same structure appears in the divine self-description of the Qur'an, where God's knowledge and ability are asserted as reaching into exactly those places a person imagines himself hidden. The reversal of the claim is what the following verse supplies, when the boaster is asked whether he supposes no one sees him. Power and sight are the two faculties a man most wants to deny in an observer, because together they make accountability possible. To deny both is to construct a private world in which no action has consequences. The verse names that world in a single clause, and leaves the reader to notice how familiar its architecture is.

**Delusion Versus Confidence: Where the Line Falls**

Nothing in the verse condemns a man for being strong, for owning property, or for relying on resources he has lawfully acquired. The condemned belief is narrower and more specific: the conviction that he is beyond reach. Confidence in one's means is ordinary and often necessary; the assumption of untouchability is a claim about the structure of reality, and it is false. The distinction can be drawn from the behaviour each produces. A man who trusts his wealth but expects to be called to account spends with an eye on how it was gained and how it is used. A man who believes himself immune spends for display and resents what is demanded of him. The sūrah exposes the second type by quoting his own complaint in the next verse, which is why the accusation lands: the reader can hear the difference between a man who manages a fortune and a man who is managed by the idea of his own security. Only the second is deluded, and only the second is addressed here.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:6

> boasting, “I have wasted enormous wealth!”?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Complaint Quoted as a Confession**

The verse lets the boaster speak for himself, and the speech indicts him without any comment attached. *Ahlaqtu mālan lubadā* — I have destroyed, or consumed in heaps, wealth piled up. The verb *ahlaka* means to ruin or wear out; the noun *lubad* describes something massed and heaped, from the same root as the felt or matted layer that lies thick upon a surface. The boast has two parts, and both are damaging. He claims to have spent enormously, which is a claim to generosity, and he describes the spending as loss, which reveals what he thinks generosity is worth. The Qur'an quotes him rather than describing him, and the quotation does the argumentative work. A narrator could have said "he resents what he gives"; the man's own sentence says it better, because it shows the resentment operating inside the very act that is supposed to display virtue. He gives, and while giving he is calculating the damage.

**Two Ways to Read the Boast**

The reports differ on the setting, and the two settings produce two readings. In the first, the speaker is a man pressed to spend on religious obligations — atonements, compensations, support — who complains that his fortune has been eaten up since he accepted the faith. On this reading the boast is a grievance dressed as generosity, and the man's real position is that obedience is expensive. In the second, the speaker is a man who has spent lavishly on his own reputation and now announces the total, expecting the audience to be impressed; the loss is stated as a trophy. Both readings are supported by the text, because the word *ahlaktu* is genuinely ambiguous in tone. What settles the sense is the following verse, which asks whether he thinks no one sees him. A man announcing a trophy would not need to be reminded of a witness; a man hiding a grievance would. The grievance reading is therefore the stronger, and it makes the boast an admission: he has spent, and he regrets the spending, and he wants the world to know how much the spending cost him.

**Why Regret Nullifies the Gift**

The Qur'an has a precise doctrine on this point, and it is stated in commercial language. Charity followed by reminders and hurtful words is compared to "a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone", and the verdict is that "such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity" (2:264). The image is agricultural and exact: the soil that looks fertile is only a film, and the first heavy rain washes it off to show the rock beneath. A gift given with a running commentary on its cost is that rock. The contrast verse describes the other case: "The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains" (2:261). Multiplication belongs to the gift that is released without a ledger attached. The boaster of this verse has inverted the arithmetic. He has paid the price of giving and forfeited its return, and the verse he is quoted in exists to tell him so in his own words.

**Wealth as a Stewardship, Not a Possession**

Underneath the complaint is a false premise about ownership. The man speaks of his wealth as something that has been taken from him, which assumes that it was his in an absolute sense. The revelation does not grant that assumption. Wealth is repeatedly described as provision from God rather than as an acquisition: "O believers! Donate from the best of what you have earned and of what We have produced for you from the earth" (2:267), where both halves of the property — earnings and produce — are attributed to divine provision. The test language makes the same point from the other direction: "Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward" (64:15). Something held on trust cannot be said to be wasted when it is spent on the terms of the trust. The boaster's error is therefore not stinginess alone. It is a misdescription of his own position. He is a trustee accounting to himself as though he were an owner who has been robbed, and the misdescription is what makes his generosity feel like plunder to him.

**The Boast as a Social Performance**

The speech is public, and its audience matters. A man who announces how much he has destroyed is performing for other men: look at what my standing costs, look at what I can afford to lose. In a city where rank was measured by expenditure, the announcement buys status even as it protests the expense. The Qur'an names this mechanism in a single phrase when it calls worldly life "play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children" (57:20), and it warns that the competition diverts before the reckoning arrives: "Then, on that Day, you will definitely be questioned about ˹your worldly˺ pleasures" (102:8). The performance has a cost the performer does not see. He is buying the applause of people who cannot pay him, at the price of a reward that could have been settled in full. The verse captures the whole transaction in five words, which is why it is quoted rather than paraphrased. Nothing needs to be added to a sentence that convicts itself.

**What the Verse Is Not Saying**

The condemnation here is not a condemnation of spending. The sūrah goes on to define the good in terms of expenditure — freeing a person, feeding the hungry in a famine — and the Qur'an elsewhere praises those "who spend their wealth in charity day and night, secretly and openly" (2:274). Large gifts are not the problem; the sūrah asks for them. The problem is the inner accounting that accompanies this man's gift. He measures the giving in loss, and a gift measured in loss is not really a gift but a purchase, with the seller being other people's opinion. The distinction protects the reader from a wrong conclusion. There is no virtue in holding wealth back, and no verse in this chapter recommends frugality for its own sake. The verse asks only that a man not stand in the middle of a generous act and complain about it, because the complaint converts the act into a transaction and leaves him with neither the reward nor the peace he was buying.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:7

> Do they think that no one sees them?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sight Added to Power**

Two questions stand together in this passage, and the pairing is deliberate. The first asked whether he reckons that no one will ever have power over him; this one asks whether he reckons that no one sees him. Power and sight are the two faculties whose absence would make wrongdoing safe. If no one can act on you, you cannot be stopped; if no one sees you, you cannot be charged. A man who believes both has constructed a world in which his conduct has no consequences at all, and the two questions dismantle that world one plank at a time. The order is also telling. Power is denied first because it is the more obvious claim — everyone knows that stronger people exist. Sight is denied second because it is the subtler claim, and the one that actually governs behaviour. Most people do not act as though they were beyond force; they act as though they were beyond observation.

**The Recurring Formula of Divine Seeing**

The Qur'an returns to this question in a form so close that it reads as a refrain. Of the man who drags another to prevent him from praying, the text asks: "Does he not know that Allah sees ˹all˺?" (96:14). The accountability of the senses is stated outright elsewhere: "Do not follow what you have no ˹sure˺ knowledge of. Indeed, all will be called to account for ˹their˺ hearing, sight, and intellect" (17:36). In the legislation on charity the same assurance appears with commercial precision: "Whatever charities you give or vows you make are surely known to Allah" (2:270), and "And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do" (2:265). None of these statements is offered as consolation. They are offered as the closing term of an argument about conduct. Once a person accepts that his acts are seen, the calculation he has been running collapses, because the calculation assumed an audience of other people only.

**What the Wealth Will Be Asked About**

The tradition specifies the interrogation this verse implies. A report transmitted from ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd in Sunan al-Tirmidhī — al-Tirmidhī himself called the tradition *gharīb*, unusual in its chain — states that on the Day of Resurrection the feet of the son of Adam will not move until he has been questioned about five matters: on what he spent his life, in what he let his youth pass, whence he acquired his property, on what he spent it, and what he did with what he knew. Two of the five are financial, and both concern the same sum of money from two directions: where it came from and where it went. That is exactly the boaster's exposure. He announced the amount he had destroyed; the report says he will be asked how he obtained it. The verse in front of us is the short form of the same warning, and it is why the question about being seen follows the boast about spending rather than some other act.

**Seeing the Motive Behind the Gift**

Observation reaches further than the act. A person can perform a generous deed and still fail, because what is seen includes the intention underneath. The revelation states the rule in agricultural terms: charity followed by reminders and hurtful words is "a hard barren rock covered with a thin layer of soil hit by a strong rain— leaving it just a bare stone", and "such people are unable to preserve the reward of their charity" (2:264). The boaster of the previous verse is precisely this case. He gave, and he resented giving, and he advertised the cost of it. Nothing about the transfer of money was hidden, and nothing about the resentment was visible to the recipients. The verse about being seen addresses the second fact. The witnesses who applauded the gift cannot see the complaint running underneath it, and the One who is asked about is not limited to what they see. This is the sharpest edge of the question, and it is why the verse is phrased as a question rather than as a statement.

**The Private Moment Is the Test**

Human beings behave differently under observation, and everyone knows it. A market with witnesses produces honesty that the same market would not produce in the dark. The verse targets that instinct and identifies it as the false ground of the boaster's confidence. He is not a philosopher denying omniscience; he is a man who has never had to answer to anyone who could see him clearly, and who therefore assumes the arrangement is permanent. The exposure of this assumption is a recurring method of the Qur'an, which places its most searching questions at the point where a person is alone with his own accounting. The question has no visible addressee and needs none. It is designed to be overheard by the reader in a private moment, when the calculation about who is watching is at its most active, and to interrupt it.

**From Fear of Exposure to Conscience**

There is a difference between fearing that someone might find out and knowing that someone already knows, and the verse is aimed at producing the second state. Fear of exposure produces concealment; knowledge of observation produces restraint that operates even in secret. The first is prudence, and it fails as soon as the risk disappears. The second is conscience, and it survives the disappearance of the risk because it does not depend on the risk. The sūrah needs the second state in order to make its later demand intelligible. It will ask for gifts to the orphaned relative and the poor man in the dust, gifts that no audience may ever applaud. A man who acts only when watched cannot perform them, and the question about being seen is the preparation for that demand. It moves the reader from the economics of reputation to the economics of accountability, and only the second economy can support what the chapter asks for next.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:8

> Have We not given them two eyes,

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Records a Debt**

*A-lam najʿal lahu ʿaynayn* — have We not made for him two eyes? The form is a negative rhetorical question, and in Arabic that form does not ask whether something happened. It states that it happened and challenges the hearer to deny it. The construction carries an accusation inside a reminder. Nobody disputes that he has eyes; the dispute is about what the eyes oblige him to do. The verse therefore functions as the first item in an account. Something has been given, the giving is undeniable, and the hearer is being asked to admit that a gift creates a claim on the receiver. The list that follows — eyes, tongue, lips, then the two ways — builds the account item by item, and each item makes the previous one harder to treat as a mere fact of biology. By the end of the list the reader is not contemplating an anatomy lesson but answering a charge.

**Why Sight Is Named First**

Of all the faculties, sight is the one on which most human knowledge depends, and the revelation consistently names it first when it enumerates what God has given. "And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing, and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect so perhaps you would be thankful" (16:78). The order in that verse is instructive: the newborn knows nothing, and the first endowments are the instruments of knowing. The same triad appears in the command to speak about the gift: "He is the One Who brought you into being and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks" (67:23). Sight comes at the head of this sūrah's list because it is the sense through which the world becomes evidence. A man who has never seen a mountain, a starving child, or another man's face has not merely lost data; he has lost the raw material from which moral judgements are made. To give eyes is to make a person capable of being addressed by what he sees.

**Two of Them, Not One**

The verse specifies the number, and the specification is not filler. Two eyes give depth, range and redundancy: the world is perceived in three dimensions rather than as a flat image, and the loss of one does not end seeing. The doubling also makes the gift harder to dismiss as accidental. A single aperture might be explained as a simple opening; a paired organ, wired to a single visual field and coordinated with movement, is a construction. The pattern continues in the next verse with a tongue and two lips — one organ of production and two of its gates. The sūrah is describing an apparatus, and an apparatus implies a designer with an intention. The rhetorical aim is to move the hearer from "I happen to have eyes" to "eyes were made for me", and the move is made through the number rather than through argument. Precision in the description is doing the work that a sermon would otherwise have to do.

**The Fineness of the Instrument Proves the Knowledge of the Maker**

The exegetes draw a direct inference from these verses, and it is a sound one. Whoever could build instruments this subtle, and coordinate them so that a person sees, speaks and chooses on the basis of what he has seen, is not a being who could be ignorant of what that person does. The argument runs from the artifact to the knowledge of its maker, and it is aimed at the man described in the previous verses. He has assumed that no one has power over him and that no one sees him. Both assumptions are answered by his own body. The eyes he uses to assess his wealth were made by the One he imagines to be inattentive; the tongue with which he boasts was fashioned by the One he imagines to be absent. The inference does not require any new evidence, which is why the verse is phrased as a reminder. The proof is already installed in the reader's face.

**The Sense Most Easily Abused**

Sight is also the faculty most exposed to misuse, and the sūrah knows it. The eye can be directed or averted. A man walking past a starving relative can see him, or can manage not to. The Qur'an's moral vocabulary for this is exact: the ones who "repulse the orphan" (107:2) are not blind to him; they have seen him and refused. The connection to this verse is the reason the faculties are listed at all. God has not given eyes in order to make the receiver comfortable; He has given them so that the world can present its claims. Every faculty listed here has a corresponding obligation, and the obligation of the eye is attention. A person who has been given the capacity to notice and chooses not to has converted a gift into an offence, and the verse's question form — have We not made for him two eyes? — is the first step in charging him with it.

**An Instrument Given, Thanks Withheld**

Both verses that list the triad of hearing, sight and intellect end on the same note, and it is not gratitude. In 16:78 the purpose clause is "so perhaps you would be thankful"; in 67:23 the outcome is stated flatly, "˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks". The gap between gift and response is the human problem in miniature. This sūrah compresses the same gap into a shorter space: the faculties are named, and the very next claim is that the recipient has not charged the steep path. The gift is acknowledged and the obligation is declined. That sequence is what makes the rhetorical question so effective. It cannot be answered with a denial, because the eyes are there; and it cannot be answered with a promise, because the behaviour is already known. All it leaves is the admission that a debt exists, and the rest of the chapter is about how the debt is to be paid.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:9

> a tongue, and two lips;

**Expanded Commentary**

**Speech Completes the Equipment**

The list of endowments moves from receiving to producing. Eyes take the world in; a tongue puts something out into it. The sūrah adds speech to sight without comment, and the addition is the largest of the four items, because speech is what makes a human being an agent among other agents rather than a spectator. The revelation treats speech as a direct gift rather than as a biological by-product: among the mercies enumerated in the sūrah of the Merciful is that God created humanity and "taught them speech" (55:4). The pairing with creation in that verse is significant. Speech is not described as something human beings developed but as something they were taught, which places it in the same category as the eyes and lips named here. Every item in this list is presented as received, and the receiver has no claim to have earned any of it. Completing the equipment is what the verse is doing, and the completion matters because the earlier items were passive. A reader who has been given eyes and a heart has been fitted out; the addition of speech makes him an agent, and the agency is what the chapter's commands assume.

**One Small Organ With Unlimited Reach**

The tongue is the smallest of the instruments named and by far the most consequential. An eye takes in what is in front of it; a tongue can reach a person who is absent, a reputation that took a lifetime to build, and a community that will never learn who started the harm. The Qur'an states the accountability of speech in a single line that leaves no room for exceptions: "not a word does a person utter without having a ˹vigilant˺ observer ready ˹to write it down˺" (50:18). The construction is absolute. There is no category of harmless speech that escapes the record, and no audience small enough to make a word unaccountable. The verse in this sūrah prepares that teaching by naming the organ. Once a reader has been reminded that he possesses a tongue, the statement about the observer ceases to be a general theological claim and becomes a fact about his own day, since he has already spoken several times before reading it.

**Two Lips as the Gate**

The sūrah does not stop at the tongue. It adds two lips, and the addition is not anatomical padding. Lips are the gate of speech: they shape sound into language, and they can also close. A tongue without lips produces noise; lips without a tongue produce silence. The pairing names both capacities in one breath, which is to say that the gift includes the power of restraint. Every faculty in this list has been given with its own limit built in — eyes that can be lowered, a tongue that can be held — and the limit is part of the gift rather than a restriction on it. The Qur'an legislates the same principle where it commands believing men "to lower their gaze and guard their chastity", adding "That is purer for them" (24:30). Control of the instrument is presented as the condition of its proper use, and the two lips are the physical sign of that condition in the organ of speech.

**The Instrument Most Used for Harm**

The tongue's power is set out at length in the sūrah that opens with a curse on its misuse: "Woe to every backbiter, slanderer, who amasses wealth ˹greedily˺ and counts it ˹repeatedly˺, thinking that their wealth will make them immortal!" (104:1–3). The conjunction is not accidental. The same passage joins the abusive tongue to the hoarding of wealth, which is the pairing this sūrah also makes when it moves from the boast about wasted wealth to the question about being seen. Two harms travel together: the man who counts his money and the man who spends his speech on other people's faults. Both treat a capacity given to them as a possession to be used for advantage. Naming the tongue in a list of divine gifts is the sūrah's way of removing that assumption before the reader can make it. The tongue is not his property. It is a loan with terms. The instrument most used for harm is the one the verse names last, and the placement is not neutral. A reader who counts the damage done by speech in his own week will find that it exceeds what his hands have done, and the verse orders the list accordingly.

**The Boast Came Through This Organ**

Everything said by the man exposed earlier in the chapter reached the world through the instrument named here. "I have wasted enormous wealth" is a sentence, and sentences require a tongue and lips. The sūrah quotes him, and the quotation is a demonstration of the point: a gift of God was used to advertise a grievance against God's command. Nothing needed to be added to his own words to convict him, and nothing needs to be added to the description of his equipment. The reader who has just been told that he was given a tongue is being told, in effect, that he has already used his. The question is not whether he will speak but what he has said, and the sūrah has provided a specimen. That specimen is what makes the general reminder about the organ into a specific accusation. The boast came through this organ, and the connection is what makes the reminder pointed. A reader who has heard a man claim to have consumed wealth in quantity has heard the specific abuse the verse has in mind, and the abuse is verbal rather than financial.

**The Tongue's Proper Work**

An instrument's value is measured by what it is for. Eyes are for seeing the world's claims; a tongue is for answering them, and the highest answer is acknowledgment. The Qur'an's own vocabulary for gratitude is verbal — to speak well of a favour is part of receiving it properly. The organ that produced the boast is the same organ capable of praise, of testimony, of asking forgiveness, and of telling the truth when the truth costs something. The sūrah lists it among the gifts for that reason. It is not a neutral capacity that happens to be useful; it is the instrument by which a human being declares his position, and the position is never undeclared. A man who says nothing has said something, and a man who boasts has said something louder. The two lips close the account of the body, and the following item in the list moves from the body to the direction in which it is to be used.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:10

> and shown them the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Fourth Item Is Not an Organ**

Three instruments are listed, and then a fourth gift of a different kind. Eyes, a tongue and two lips are parts of a body; the two ways are not. After endowing the human being with the capacity to perceive and to speak, the sūrah records that he was shown where to go. The shift is the hinge of the chapter's opening argument. Equipment without direction produces a creature who can act and cannot choose, and the choice is what the rest of the sūrah is about. The verse therefore completes an account that began with the body: here is what you were given to see with, to speak with, and here is what you were shown. Nothing in that account was earned, and everything in it was sufficient. The rhetorical question that frames the list — have We not made, have We not shown — closes on the item that makes the previous three morally meaningful.

**Najdayn: The Two High Roads**

The Arabic word is *najdayn*, the dual of *najd*, which denotes an elevated tract and, by extension, a road that runs across high ground and is visible from a distance. The dual form is precise: not many roads, but two. Arabic usage supports the sense of a clear, well-marked route rather than a vague direction, and the Qur'an's own parallel confirms it. Speaking of the same human being, the revelation says: "We already showed them the Way, whether they ˹choose to˺ be grateful or ungrateful" (76:3). The showing is stated as a completed act, and the two outcomes are named as options rather than as destinations already assigned. A second parallel describes the same endowment from the inside, as an act of inspiration: God fashioned the soul and then "with ˹the knowledge of˺ right and wrong inspired it!" (91:8). Between the external showing of the way and the internal recognition of good and evil, the human being is equipped twice over — once by what is placed before him and once by what is placed within him.

**Showing Is Not Compelling**

The verb in this verse is *hadaynāhu*, we guided or showed him, and it is used in a sense that stops short of forcing the outcome. The revelation distinguishes these uses carefully. God shows the way to every human being; He guides to the way's end those who take it. The distinction is why the two ways remain two. A person shown a road has not been placed on it. The Qur'an makes the point in the plainest terms where it tells the Prophet that guidance of hearts is not his office: "You surely cannot guide whoever you like ˹O Prophet˺, but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills" (28:56). The same limit is why the question in this verse is a question. It asks the hearer to admit the showing, and the admission leaves the choice entirely with him. The sūrah does not claim that the man exposed earlier was unable to find the road. It claims the opposite, which is the whole force of the accusation.

**The Two Ways Are Named, Not Balanced**

Calling them two ways does not make them equivalent. The sūrah that inspires the knowledge of right and wrong continues immediately with the two outcomes: success for the one who purifies his soul, ruin for the one who corrupts it. The Qur'an's moral geography consistently describes a single straight path and its alternatives, and it does not present the alternatives as parallel options of equal standing. The duality in this verse is a description of the human situation, not an endorsement of symmetry. Two roads exist because a creature with a will can go either way, and the existence of the second road is the condition of the first road's value. A way that could not be refused would not be a way at all. What the verse asserts is that the distinction is clear enough to be seen, which is precisely what the boastful man's behaviour denies. He acts as though the two ways were indistinguishable, and the verse records that they were shown to him.

**The Universality of the Showing**

The pronoun is general, and the gift is not restricted to a community, a time or a place. The revelation describes the same universal endowment elsewhere in the language of an original disposition: "the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in ˹all˺ people. Let there be no change in this creation of Allah" (30:30). Read alongside the two ways of this verse, the picture is consistent. Human beings are not born morally blank and then instructed; they are born oriented, and the instruction they receive confirms an orientation already present. That matters for the argument the sūrah is making. Ignorance is not available as a defence to the man who boasts about his wealth. The way was shown to him, and the showing was legible to the faculties listed immediately before. His error is not a failure of information but a refusal to use the information he has.

**The Account Is Now Complete**

Four gifts, one list, one conclusion. The eyes give him the world; the tongue gives him a voice in it; the lips give him control of that voice; the two ways give him a direction. Nothing further is required in order to hold him responsible, and the sūrah has taken care to establish that nothing further was withheld. The rhetorical form of the list — have We not? — cannot be answered with a denial, and it cannot be answered with a complaint about difficulty. The equipment is adequate and the road is visible. What follows in the chapter is the charge, and the charge is now intelligible: a being furnished this completely, who then declines to act, has no explanation available except preference. The verse closes the case for the prosecution before the prosecution has said a word.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:11

> If only they had attempted the challenging path ˹of goodness instead˺!

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Sentence That Is Both Wish and Reproach**

*Fa-lā iqtahama al-ʿaqabah* is one of the most compressed lines in the Qur'an. The particle *lā* after *fa* can carry an exhortation — "would that he charged the pass!" — or a plain negative — "but he did not charge the pass". Both readings are grammatically sound and they converge on the same accusation, because a wish that something had happened is a statement that it did not. The exhortative reading has the stronger rhetorical effect. It presents the ascent as something still possible, something the hearer can undertake while the sentence is being read, and it registers the loss as a loss rather than as a verdict already sealed. Translations split accordingly: some render the line as a statement of failure, others as a reproachful wish. The difference in tone matters more than the difference in grammar. The verse is not closing a file; it is standing at the foot of a slope asking why nobody has started climbing.

**Iqtiḥām Is Not Walking**

The verb is *iqtahama*, and its sense is specific. It does not mean to climb steadily or to make progress; it means to throw oneself into a hard thing, to plunge into a matter whose difficulty is known in advance. Classical usage attaches the verb to entering battle and to undertaking a task whose cost is visible from the start. The choice of this verb rules out a reading of the good life as gradual self-improvement. What the sūrah describes is a deliberate assault on something that will resist. That resistance is named in the verses that follow, and it is money: the pass consists of freeing a person, feeding the hungry, and standing with the believing community in patience and mercy. Each of those costs something a man would rather keep. A verb of assault is the only verb that fits an act whose price is felt at the moment of performance. Walking is passive and applying oneself is not, and the distinction is the whole of the word's force. A reader who thinks of the ascent as a route he happens to be on has missed the term; it describes effort undertaken, and the undertaking is what the verse demands of him.

**A Pass Drawn From the Landscape**

The image is not invented. *ʿAqabah* is the ordinary Arabic word for the steep section of a mountain road, the narrow ascent that a traveller must charge because there is no way around it. The first audience lived in that landscape. The roads into and out of Mecca cross exactly such terrain, and every merchant in the audience had negotiated a pass on foot or with a laden animal. The word therefore carries a body's memory: the short breath, the slipped footing, the knowledge that turning back is more dangerous than going on. By choosing this image for the moral life, the sūrah borrows every feature of that experience. The ascent is short but severe; it cannot be delegated; the load you carry determines how hard it is; and nobody arrives at the top by standing at the bottom admiring it. The metaphor also contains its own irony for a trading city: the pass these men crossed for profit is the same pass they refuse to cross for God.

**Why the Ascent Costs What It Costs**

The verses that define the pass list expenditures, and the list explains the verb. Freeing a slave in a slave economy is the surrender of capital. Feeding people in a famine is the surrender of a store that is worth more in that season than in any other. Standing with a community that urges patience and mercy is the surrender of the option to walk away when things become uncomfortable. None of these can be done from surplus that no one would notice. Each is felt, and the feeling is the reason the verb is *iqtahama* rather than something gentler. The man exposed earlier in the chapter is the counter-example. He spent and complained; the pass asks a man to spend and be glad. The difference is not the amount but the direction of the will, and the sūrah has arranged its material so that the two cases sit next to each other. The ascent costs what it costs because every item on it requires surrender. A reader who examines the list will find that each act involves giving up something he holds: property, advantage or comfort. The cost is the reason the ascent is steep, and the steepness is stated rather than lamented.

**The Alternative Reading: A Pass in the Hereafter**

Some commentators take the *ʿaqabah* to be an obstacle of the next world rather than a task of this one — a barrier the deniers will meet after death, or one of the difficulties of the crossing. The reading has the support of the Qur'an's frequent use of terrain as eschatological imagery. Against it stands the immediate context. The verses that follow define the pass in terms of acts performed in this life, in this economy, with this money, and a definition in present-tense terms sits badly with an obstacle encountered after death. The eschatological reading also weakens the exhortative force of the particle, since a barrier in the next world cannot be charged now. The worldly reading is therefore stronger, and it has the further advantage of making the verse actionable. A pass that must be charged today is a verse that can be obeyed today, which is what the form of the sentence demands. The alternative reading places the pass in the hereafter, and the reader should know that it exists. On that reading the ascent is not an act performed now but a terrain crossed later, and the difference changes what the verse asks of him. Both readings are transmitted and both have been defended.

**A Rebuke That Presumes Capacity**

The reproach in this verse only makes sense against the list that precedes it. Eyes, a tongue, two lips, the two ways shown — the human being was equipped for the ascent, and the ascent was visible. The verse does not accuse him of failing something he could not do; it accuses him of not starting something he was built for. That is a different charge, and a heavier one. It removes the two excuses people ordinarily reach for: inability and ignorance. Neither is available here. What remains is preference, and preference is what a person is. The sūrah has spent ten verses building to that word, and it does not need to say it. The image of a pass at the foot of which a fully equipped traveller stands still is enough. The reader who has followed the list cannot look at the picture without finding himself in it. Presuming capacity is what makes the rebuke a rebuke rather than a description. A reader who is told that he did not climb is being told that he could have, and the presumption is what turns the statement into an accusation. The verse does not soften it.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:12

> And what will make you realize what ˹attempting˺ the challenging path is?

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Formula Reserved for Great Things**

*Wa-mā adrāka* is a fixed formula, and the Qur'an deploys it with discipline. It appears where the subject exceeds the hearer's frame of reference and the text intends to enlarge it. Of the Fire described as the Crusher, the verse asks: "And what will make you realize what the Crusher is?" (104:5). Of the abyss that will house those whose scale is light, it asks: "And what will make you realize what that is?" (101:10), and answers, "˹It is˺ a scorching Fire" (101:11). The construction does three things at once: it declares the subject weighty, it admits that the hearer does not yet grasp it, and it prepares him for a definition he could not have produced himself. Its use here tells the reader that what follows is not a footnote to the previous verse but the centre of the sūrah. Everything said so far — the oath, the human condition, the faculties, the two ways — has been leading to a definition the text treats as beyond ordinary expectation.

**The Expectation Set Up, and the Answer Given**

The formula trains the reader to expect scale. After the Crusher and the abyss, a question of this shape announces something large: a mountain, a terror, a scene of judgment. The answer that arrives is a list of small mercies. Free a person. Give food in a famine. Feed the orphaned relative and the poor man in distress. Stand with believers who urge patience and compassion. The mismatch between the grandeur of the question and the plainness of the answer is the sūrah's sharpest rhetorical device. It is not a disappointment but a correction. The reader has been taught, by the formula itself, that whatever comes next will be of the first importance, and he is then handed ordinary acts of social kindness and told that these are the thing of first importance. The ascent he imagined as a cosmic event turns out to be a set of transactions with the people nearest to him. The expectation is set up so that the answer lands with weight. A reader who has been asked what the ascent is expects a description of terrain, and the answer given is a list of ordinary acts. The mismatch is deliberate and it is the point of the exchange.

**Why the Ordinary Answer Is the Real One**

The correction works because it removes an evasion. A moral demand framed as a cosmic ordeal can be admired from a distance; nobody is expected to storm a mountain of judgment, so nobody feels reproached by it. A demand framed as feeding a relative cannot be admired from a distance, because the relative exists and the famine is happening. The sūrah's strategy is to make the good unromantic. It strips the ascent of spectacle and leaves the cost, and the cost is denominated in money, food and time. The same method appears in the answer given to the people of the Fire when they are asked what landed them there: "We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor" (74:43–44). Two failures are named, and the second is a failure to feed. The definition of the pass in this chapter belongs to that same economy of concrete acts. The ordinary answer is the real one because the ascent is performed in ordinary life. A reader looking for a heroic act has been given feeding and freeing, and the substitution tells him where the difficulty actually lies. The verse relocates the climb from a mountain to a week.

**The Question Also Signals Difficulty**

The formula does more than announce importance; it concedes that the subject is hard to grasp. The pass is difficult, and its difficulty is not physical. A man can understand in the abstract that giving is good and still not give, which means the obstacle is not information but the will. The question acknowledges that gap. It says, in effect, that the reader does not yet know what this undertaking requires of him, and that the requirement will be stated rather than assumed. What follows is a specification precise enough to be checked: a neck to be freed, food in a season of scarcity, a relative who is an orphan, a poor person in extremity, and a community held together by mutual patience and mercy. Precision is the answer to the difficulty of grasping the whole. The reader who cannot imagine charging a pass can imagine feeding one named person, and the sūrah gives him exactly that unit of measure.

**The Question Is About the Act, Not the Terrain**

Many commentators read the verse as asking what charging the pass consists in, rather than what the pass is. The distinction is small in wording and large in effect. If the question were about the terrain, the answer would describe a place; instead it describes deeds. The grammatical support for this reading is the way the following verses attach to it: the definition is given in verbal nouns — freeing, feeding — which name acts rather than locations. The reading also preserves the metaphor. A pass is defined by what a traveller does on it, and the sūrah is interested in the climbing, not in the geology. On this reading the verse is a question about practice, and its answer is a curriculum. That is why the list that follows has no mystical or ceremonial content at all. Every item is an act performed on another person's behalf, in this world, with resources the actor already holds. The question concerns the act and not the terrain, and the reader who notices this notices the chapter's method. What matters is not how steep the pass is but what a person does on it, and the verse answers accordingly. The terrain is scenery; the acts are the content.

**A Definition Placed Where It Cannot Be Argued**

The placement of the definition inside a rhetorical question has a legal effect. A command can be debated; a question that answers itself cannot. The reader is not instructed to free a slave and then asked to weigh the instruction. He is told that the matter is greater than he supposes, and then shown what it is. By the time the list ends, the instruction has already been accepted in the form of knowledge. This is a characteristic method of the Qur'an's shorter Meccan chapters, which rarely legislate directly and instead build recognition until the required act appears self-evident. The sūrah has spent eleven verses establishing the human condition, the endowment of the faculties and the refusal to climb. The twelfth verse asks what climbing would have meant, and the answer converts the whole preceding argument into a demand on the reader's own resources. Placing the definition where it cannot be argued is a rhetorical choice with a purpose. A reader who has just been rebuked for not climbing is given the definition immediately, so that he cannot claim ignorance. The verse closes the excuse before it can be offered.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:13

> It is to free a slave,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Ascent Begins With Somebody Else's Chains**

The definition of the pass opens with an act of liberation, and the opening is a statement of priorities. The sūrah could have begun its list with prayer, with pilgrimage, or with any of the observances that marked religious seriousness in Mecca. It begins with the release of a human being from ownership. Nothing in the verse explains the choice, and none is needed. A slave economy was the frame of the city's life: households held people, wealth was measured in them, and a man's standing rose with the number he controlled. To place their release at the head of the good is to invert the city's scale of value in a single phrase. Honour, on this account, is not accumulated by owning people but by giving them back to themselves. The verse does not argue for that inversion. It performs it, by making liberation the first thing a reader encounters when he asks what climbing the pass requires. Beginning with somebody else's chains tells the reader what kind of ascent this is. The first act is not self-improvement but the release of another person, and the ordering sets the standard for everything that follows. The verse is not describing a private discipline.

**A Neck Rather Than a Person**

The Arabic is *fakk raqabah*, literally the unloosing of a neck. The choice of the part for the whole is not casual. A neck is where bondage is physically located: the yoke, the collar, the rope. To speak of the neck is to speak of the exact point at which one human being is fastened to another's will. The word also carries the sense of a life — the neck is the seat of the self in Arabic idiom, and *raqabah* can mean the person as a whole. Both senses are present, and both do work. The concrete sense keeps the act physical: there is a bond, and it is undone. The wider sense makes the act total: what is released is not a limb but a life, returned to its own direction. The verse asks for neither a partial manumission nor a kindness within slavery. It asks for the bond to be cut. A neck rather than a person is the phrasing the verse chooses, and the choice is worth holding. A reader who notices the part named for the whole understands that the emphasis is on the bondage rather than the individual, and the emphasis explains why freeing is placed first in the list.

**The Verified Promise Attached to the Act**

The reward promised for manumission is stated in the same physical terms as the act. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, in the Book of Manumission, transmits from Abū Hurayrah that the Prophet ﷺ said: whoever frees a Muslim slave, Allah will save every limb of his body from the Fire, limb for limb, as he freed the limbs of the slave. The symmetry is exact — a body given back is answered by a body saved — and the tradition preserves the reasoning of the early community, since the same chapter records ʿAlī ibn al-Ḥusayn freeing a slave upon hearing the report. The verse in this sūrah supplies the moral frame for that promise: manumission is not one charitable option among many but the first item in the definition of the difficult road. The jurists who debated whether manumission or ordinary charity is greater had this ordering in front of them, and the placement of the verse before the feeding verses was cited as evidence for the priority of freeing.

**What the Act Cost in That Economy**

Freeing a slave was not a symbolic gesture. It was the surrender of capital, and in a city whose wealth was partly denominated in people, it reduced the owner's standing as well as his assets. The Qur'an acknowledges the cost and legislates around it: masters are told to make contracts of manumission with those slaves they know to be worthy, and to give them from the wealth God has provided (24:33). The same act appears inside the definition of righteousness itself, where the righteous are those "who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars, and for freeing captives" (2:177). The phrase "cherished wealth" is the operative one. The good that counts is the good that hurts, and no expenditure in that list hurts more than the release of a person whose labour the owner was entitled to command. This verse is therefore the sharpest test the sūrah sets. What the act cost in that economy can be measured, and the measurement is what makes the act significant. A reader who knows what a person was worth as property understands that freeing one was a substantial transfer, and the transfer is the reason the verse treats it as an ascent rather than a courtesy.

**Charity or Manumission: The Exegetical Argument**

The tradition records a genuine dispute about which act is greater, ordinary charity or the freeing of a slave. One side argues from this verse's order: what is named first in a definition of the difficult road is the greater. The other side argues from scope: charity reaches the hungry, the traveller and the captive at once, and a single act of manumission benefits one person. Both arguments are reasonable, and the second is the stronger on the Qur'an's own evidence, since 2:177 places charity to relatives and the poor before the freeing of captives and treats all of them as constituents of a single righteousness. What the dispute reveals is more useful than its resolution. Both acts are ranked by cost, and both are measured against the giver's attachment. The ordering in this sūrah is best read as dramatic rather than legal: the road is steep, so the steepest act is named first. The exegetical argument about charity and manumission is worth setting out because a reader will meet it. Whether freeing counts as charity or as a distinct category affects how the list is read, and knowing the disagreement allows him to hold the verse without needing to resolve it.

**Freedom as the First Act of the Ascent**

There is a logic to putting liberation at the head of the list, and it is not merely economic. A person held in bondage cannot climb anything. He cannot fast by choice, give by choice, or urge others to patience as a free member of a community. Every other act the sūrah requires presupposes a will that is one's own. Freeing a slave therefore creates the conditions for the rest of the ascent, in the person freed as well as in the person who frees. The one who gives away ownership is released from the illusion that ownership is security, and the one released is given the capacity for a moral life. That double effect is why the verse stands alone as the first definition of the pass. The road up is not a private discipline; it begins with a decision about another human being's status, taken at cost, and it is measured by whether that person ends the day freer than he began it.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:14

> or to give food in times of famine

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Season Is Part of the Definition**

The verse does not say "give food". It says give food *fī yawmin dhī masghabah*, on a day of hunger, in a season of famine. The specification is the point. Food given in abundance costs little and proves little; food given when supplies are short is given out of the giver's own security. The Qur'an states the principle in the language of attachment: the righteous give "out of their cherished wealth" (2:177), and the measure of a gift is not its size but what its absence will cost the one who gives it. By naming the famine, the verse converts charity from a general disposition into a dated act. There is a day, the day is hard, and the gift is to be made then. A reader cannot satisfy this verse by remembering that he has been generous in comfortable years. The verse asks for the specific occasion on which generosity is most expensive.

**Why Scarcity Multiplies the Gift**

Two things change in a famine, and both increase the weight of what is given. The first is the value of the thing itself. Grain in a year of plenty is a commodity; grain in a year of shortage is survival, and the same measure carries a different meaning. The second is the state of the giver. A man who feeds others while his own store is adequate performs a kindness; a man who does it while his own household is short performs a sacrifice. The revelation names the second case explicitly in its description of the righteous: they "give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive" (76:8), and the motive is recorded in the next verse as being for God's sake alone, seeking neither reward nor thanks (76:9). The phrase "despite their desire for it" is the definition of a famine gift. The giver wants the food, and gives it anyway.

**Food, Not Money**

The verb is *iṭʿām*, feeding, and it names a completed act rather than a transfer of means. The distinction is deliberate. Money can be given at a distance, quickly, and without any contact with the person who receives it; food has to be obtained, prepared and placed in front of somebody. Feeding requires presence. It also removes the possibility of the gift being misused or lost, and it establishes a relation between the giver and the receiver that a coin does not. The sūrah's other items work the same way: freeing a neck requires an owner and a person; feeding an orphaned relative requires a household. None of these can be delegated to an intermediary and forgotten. The verse is written in the grammar of contact, which is why it fits a chapter that begins by rebuking a man who has reduced religion to an accounting of his own expenditures. Food rather than money is what the verse specifies, and the specification is practical. A reader who gives money has transferred a means; a person who gives food has met a need directly. The verse names the thing eaten because the thing eaten is what the day requires.

**Famine as the Test of a Whole Community**

A shortage does not test individuals only; it reveals what a community is. In plenty, most societies look generous, because generosity is cheap. In scarcity the ranking of obligations becomes visible: who eats first, who is fed from whose store, and who is left to the dust. The Qur'an's legislation anticipates that pressure and legislates against it, placing relatives, orphans, the poor, travellers and beggars inside the single category of righteous expenditure (2:177). This verse performs the same work in a single clause. It does not describe an ideal economy; it describes the behaviour required inside a bad one. The reader is being told that famine is not a suspension of moral obligation but the occasion on which that obligation is measured, and that the man who waits for comfortable conditions has misunderstood the nature of the demand. Famine tests a whole community rather than an individual, and the verse chooses that setting. A reader who has seen scarcity knows that generosity becomes harder as supplies fall, and the difficulty is what makes the act steep. The verse is not describing giving in comfortable circumstances.

**The Difficulty That Makes the Act Steep**

Everything in this list is defined by difficulty, and this verse states the difficulty most plainly. The pass is steep because the acts on it are expensive, and no act is more expensive than giving away what you need. The verb of the previous verse was *iqtahama*, to charge into a hard thing, and the famine is the hard thing. A man who feeds others in a famine is not merely transferring resources; he is accepting risk on their behalf. That acceptance is what the sūrah means by climbing. It also explains why the verse pairs with manumission rather than following it as an afterthought. Both acts surrender something the giver can least afford to lose — a person's labour and a season's food — and both are performed at the moment when holding on to them would be most excusable. The difficulty is what makes the act steep, and the reader should note where the difficulty lies. It is not in the amount but in the timing: giving when the giver is also short. The verse identifies the ascent with the moment of greatest reluctance rather than with the size of the gift.

**The Famine That Never Ends**

Read literally, the verse addresses a season. Read as a rule, it addresses a condition. There has never been a time in which some people lacked food while others had surplus, and the verse's requirement is therefore always in force somewhere. The specification of the famine serves to prevent a reader from discharging the obligation cheaply. It says: when the giving is easy, you have not yet done what is asked. The same logic runs through the sūrah's whole ethical list — the orphan who is a relative, the poor man in the dust — and it converges on a single test. The good is defined by its cost to the doer, not by its convenience. A chapter that began by quoting a man complaining about how much his wealth had cost him ends by demanding expenditures that cost more, and demands them gladly. The famine that never ends is the condition the verse's audience still lives in, and the identification makes the command current. A reader who supposes that the famine belongs to a past century has misread the setting; scarcity is continuous, and the act remains available to him today.

**The Famine Gift Is Best Given Where Nobody Watches**

Scarcity changes the audience as well as the cost. In a famine everyone knows who has stores, and a public distribution buys reputation at the exact moment the giver can least afford the food. The verse does not ask for a display, and the Qur'an is explicit about the ranking: "To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do" (2:271). Private giving in a season of shortage is the hardest form of the act, because the giver loses both the food and the credit. That is precisely the combination the boastful man at the head of the chapter could not imagine. He needed the audience, and his need for it is what turned his expenditure into a complaint. Feeding in a famine is set against that posture. The verse asks for the transfer of food, not for the announcement of it, and the announcement is worth nothing in the accounting the sūrah has already described.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:15

> to an orphaned relative

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Claims Held by One Person**

The recipient named here carries a double title, and the doubling is the whole force of the verse. He is an orphan, which makes him a claim on the community at large; and he is a relative, which makes him a claim on you personally. Either title alone would generate an obligation. Together they generate an obligation that cannot be transferred. The community's duty to orphans can be discharged through institutions, collections and other people's generosity; a relative's duty to a kinsman cannot. The verse names the person for whom both excuses fail at once, and it names him first among the recipients of food. That ordering is not arbitrary. The sūrah is describing an ascent, and the steepest section of the road is the one that runs past the person you cannot pretend not to know. Two claims held by one person is what makes the obligation binding. A reader who is both a believer and a relative cannot discharge one claim by pointing to the other, and the verse joins them so that neither can be used as an excuse. The pairing is deliberate.

**Yatīm: A Word With Legal Precision**

*Yatīm* in Arabic denotes a child who has lost his father, and the majority of the exegetes and jurists define it that way, while some extend it to the loss of both parents. The word's legal sense is tied to the loss of the provider rather than to grief as such, and it lapses at maturity: in Islamic law the orphan's special status ends when he reaches adulthood and can manage his own affairs. That precision matters for reading the verse. The person to be fed is not any bereaved person but a child who has lost the one who fed him. The sūrah's other items are equally concrete — a neck to be freed, a poor man in the dust — and this one is no exception. The demand is addressed to a specific situation that a reader can check against his own family: is there a child in your kinship who no longer has a father, and what has your wealth done for him?

**Maqrabah: The Claim You Cannot Avoid**

The second half of the phrase is *dhī maqrabah*, one of near kinship. The word comes from the root of nearness, and in the exegetical tradition it denotes the relative whose relationship to you is close enough to be known. That nearness is what makes the case hard. A stranger's need can be met at a distance and then set down; a relative's need arrives with a history, a name, and a set of people who will notice what you do. There is also the sharper difficulty that nearness breeds resentment: families are where obligations are most keenly felt and most often resented. By specifying the relative, the verse targets precisely the giving that carries social friction. It asks a man to support a person whose dependence he may find embarrassing, and it places that support at the head of the list of feeding. The claim of kinship cannot be avoided, and the verse says so by naming it first. A reader who has been giving to strangers while neglecting his own family has been addressed directly, and the ordering tells him where the priority lies. The relative comes before the general case.

**A Concern Repeated Across the Revelation**

The orphan is among the most frequently named recipients of moral attention in the Qur'an, and the repetition is a rebuke rather than a reminder. The sūrah of the Dawn states the failure flatly: "Absolutely not! In fact, you are not ˹even˺ gracious to the orphan" (89:17). The sūrah of Small Kindnesses identifies the denier of the Judgment by his treatment of the child without a father: "That is the one who repulses the orphan" (107:2). The covenant taken from the children of Israel places kindness to orphans next to worship of God and kindness to parents (2:83). Read together, these verses describe a society's standing as measurable by a single variable: how it treats children who have lost their provider. This sūrah makes the same measurement, with the added weight of kinship. The orphan who is your relative is the case in which your society's failure and your own failure are the same act. The concern is repeated across the revelation, and the repetition is what establishes it as an obligation rather than a preference. A reader who finds the same instruction in several chapters understands that he is not reading an isolated remark. The consistency is the evidence of its weight.

**The Orphan to Whom the Sūrah Was Revealed**

The verse has a biographical resonance for its first hearer. The Prophet ﷺ was an orphan: his father ʿAbdullāh died before he was born, his mother Āmina died when he was about six, and his grandfather ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib died when he was about eight, leaving him in the household of an uncle. The revelation returns to this repeatedly, and the sūrah of the Morning Light puts it as a question from God: "Did He not find you as an orphan then sheltered you?" (93:6). A chapter that demands food for the orphaned relative was revealed to a man who had been exactly that, and to a city that had watched him grow up without a father. The demand is therefore not abstract for its first audience. It is addressed to a community that contained the most prominent orphan in Arabia, and it asks what its wealth has done for children in its own families who have lost theirs.

**Why the Relative Comes Before the Stranger**

The order of the recipients in the following verses is significant. The near kinsman is named first, and the poor person in distress next, with no conjunction that subordinates one to the other. The arrangement sets the obligations side by side and refuses to let either cancel the other. A man who gives generously to distant causes while neglecting a bereaved nephew has not climbed; a man who looks after his own family and walks past the starving stranger has not climbed either. The sūrah wants both, and it wants them in that order because the first is the one people fail. Charity that reaches strangers is easier to perform and easier to display; charity that reaches kin is often invisible, unthanked and resented. Placing it first is the sūrah's way of saying that the road up begins where the credit is smallest. Placing the relative before the stranger is an ordering with consequences. A reader who reverses it may still be generous, but he is not doing what the verse describes. The order is not a suggestion about efficiency; it is a statement about whose claim is nearest.

**The Orphan's Property Is Protected by the Same Concern**

Feeding an orphaned relative is one half of the duty; the other half is not consuming what he already has. The revelation legislates the second with unusual sharpness: "Do not come near the wealth of the orphan—unless intending to enhance it—until they attain maturity" (17:34). The permission granted inside the prohibition is narrow and specific — a guardian may manage the child's property in order to increase it — and the deadline is fixed. Where the community asks how to deal with orphans, the answer is the same: "Improving their condition is best" (2:220). The sūrah of the Dawn indicts the opposite behaviour in a single line: "And you devour ˹others’˺ inheritance greedily" (89:19). Read against this verse, the picture is complete. The orphaned relative is exposed twice: he needs food because he has no provider, and he holds property that his relatives are positioned to absorb. The chapter asks for the first need to be met, and the wider revelation forbids the second temptation from being acted on.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:16

> or to a poor person in distress,

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Stranger at the End of the List**

After the relative comes the person with no claim of blood. The verse names a *miskīn* described as *dhā matrabah*, and the second recipient completes the range of the demand. The first was near enough that you could not deny knowing him; this one has no such hold on you. He is a stranger in need, and the only reason to feed him is that he is hungry. The pairing of the two recipients is a deliberate construction of the sūrah's ethics. Obligation in this chapter is not limited to kinship, and it is not limited to strangers who inspire sympathy. It covers both, and the ascent requires both. A reader who satisfies one half of the list has not climbed the pass; he has chosen the easier half. Placing the stranger at the end of the list extends the obligation outward. A reader who has been given the relative first is now given the person with no claim on him at all, and the extension is what completes the circle. The verse leaves nobody outside it.

**The Dust as a Description of Homelessness**

*Dhā matrabah* is literally "possessor of dust", and the exegetes gloss it as the man who has no home other than the ground. The image is exact and unsentimental. A person whose bed is dust has lost not only wealth but shelter, privacy and standing; he is visible in public in a state that most people arrange never to be seen in. The Qur'an's use of concrete physical description here does the same work it does elsewhere in the chapter — the neck for bondage, the famine for scarcity, the dust for destitution. Each term fixes the reader's attention on a body rather than on a category. The poor person of this verse is not a statistic in a discussion of poverty. He is a man lying on the ground, and the verse asks whether he was fed. The dust describes homelessness rather than filth, and the reader should hold that sense. A person lying in the dust has no floor, no roof and no place to be, and the verse names the condition by its visible mark. The image is chosen because it cannot be mistaken for anything else.

**Miskīn and Faqīr: A Distinction the Jurists Debated**

Arabic has two words for the needy, and the jurists have argued about their precise relationship. One position holds that the *faqīr* is the one who has nothing at all, while the *miskīn* has something but not enough to live on; the other reverses the order. The debate is recorded in the legal literature because both words appear in the verse on zakāh recipients, and the categories determine who qualifies. For the reading of this verse the technical distinction matters less than the qualifier attached to it. The *miskīn* here is not simply needy but *dhā matrabah*, in extremity. The sūrah is not sorting the poor into classes; it is pointing at the worst case, the person whose need has reached the point where he is on the ground. Whatever the lexical ranking of the two words, the verse addresses the reader about the person he is most likely to avoid looking at.

**Distress as the Qualifier That Removes Excuses**

The addition of a qualifier to the noun does real argumentative work. A general command to help the poor can be satisfied by a modest, routine gift that changes nothing. A command that names a specific state — the man in the dust, on a day of famine — cannot. The reader is required to assess the degree of need before he decides the size of the gift, and that assessment brings him into contact with the person. This is the same method the sūrah used with the famine: it dates the obligation and specifies its severity so that the obligation cannot be discharged at a distance. The qualifier also answers an objection. A man might say that he cannot feed every poor person he meets. The verse does not ask him to. It asks him to recognise the one in extremity and to act, which is a demand that can actually be met.

**Two Circles, One Climb**

The recipients named in these verses describe two circles of obligation, and the sūrah holds them together without ranking the second below the first. The inner circle is kinship, with its history and its resentments; the outer circle is common humanity, with its anonymity. Most moral failure happens at the boundary between them: people over-give to the outer circle because it is admired, and under-give to the inner circle because it is expected. The sūrah's order reverses the temptation, naming the relative first and the stranger second, and it makes the ascent depend on both. The man who climbs is the one who has fed the child in his own family and the man on the ground outside it, and who has done so in a season when doing either was costly. Two circles make one climb, and the joining is the chapter's practical instruction. A reader who separates his obligations to kin from his obligations to strangers has divided what the verse holds together, and the division is what allows him to perform one and neglect the other.

**The Cost Is the Same in Both Cases**

Nothing in this verse softens the demand made by the previous one. The famine still applies. The food given to the poor man in the dust is food taken from a store that is short, and the giver feels the loss. The sūrah's ethics are built on this: the value of an act is measured by what it costs the actor, not by what it produces for the recipient. A large gift from surplus ranks below a small gift from need, which is why the season is specified and the extremity is named. The verse therefore completes a definition that began with liberation and continues through feeding. Every item costs the giver something he would rather keep, and every item is directed at a person rather than at a cause. That combination is what makes the road steep, and it is why the sūrah asks not whether a man has given but whether he has given where it hurt.

**The Man in the Dust Has No One to Speak for Him**

Destitution removes a person's ability to make a claim. A relative can ask; a neighbour can complain; a debtor can be pressed by others on his behalf. The man lying in the dust has none of these, and his need is known only to those who look. The verse addresses that asymmetry directly by making his case a religious obligation rather than a matter of his own advocacy. The Qur'an's concern for the voiceless is consistent across its legislation, and it appears wherever power is uneven: the same chapter that commands kindness to the orphaned relative forbids approaching the orphan's property except to improve it (17:34), because the child cannot defend it. The poor person in distress is in the same position with respect to food. He cannot compel anyone, and he is unlikely even to ask, since asking in that state costs what little dignity remains. The verse therefore places the initiative on the one who has the food.

**Two Recipients, and No Room for a Choice Between Them**

The construction of the two verses is deliberately parallel. An orphaned relative, or a poor person in distress — the particle joining them presents alternatives, not a hierarchy. Neither recipient discharges the obligation owed to the other, and the parallelism prevents the reader from substituting the easier case for the harder one. Giving to strangers is often more attractive than giving to kin, because strangers are grateful and kin are entitled; giving to kin is often more comfortable than giving to strangers, because kin are known and strangers are inconvenient. The sūrah names both and asks for both. A reader who has fed only one of them has completed half the definition, and the definition was introduced as the answer to a question about what the steep road consists in. Half an ascent is not an ascent. Two recipients with no choice between them is what the verse establishes. A reader who has been allocating his giving by preference has been told that both claims stand, and the standing of both is what makes the ascent an ascent rather than a matter of inclination.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:17

> and—above all—to be one of those who have faith and urge each other to perseverance and urge each other to compassion.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Faith Stated as the Ground, Not as a Fourth Item**

Three acts of expenditure have been listed, and then a fourth clause is added that is not an act at all. The conjunction joining it — "and" — can be read either as adding another requirement or as attaching a condition to the ones already given. The conditional reading is the one the exegetes prefer, and it is the one that makes sense of the sentence. Faith is not a fourth deed standing beside freeing and feeding; it is the state in which those deeds count. The verse says "to be one of those who have faith", using the language of belonging rather than of performing. A person is inside a group, and the group is defined first by belief and then by two forms of mutual encouragement. Everything he does follows from that membership, and without it the deeds have no footing. Stating faith as the ground rather than as a fourth item changes how the list is read. A reader who counts three acts and a belief has misread the structure; the belief is the condition under which the acts count. The verse places it accordingly and the placement is the argument.

**Why the Condition Is Necessary**

The Qur'an states the point directly where it explains the rejection of a people's spending: "And what prevented their donations from being accepted is that they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger, they never come to prayer except half-heartedly, and they never donate except resentfully" (9:54). The verse names the two symptoms — reluctant prayer and resentful giving — and traces both to a single cause. This is precisely the man quoted at the head of the chapter. He gave, and he resented giving, and his resentment was not a mood but a symptom of what he actually believed. The condition of faith in the present verse is therefore not a theological technicality appended to an ethical list. It is the difference between a gift and a loss, and the whole sūrah has been arranged to make that difference visible. The condition is necessary because acts performed without it are not the acts described. A reader who frees a person for reputation has done something else, and the verse excludes the substitution by making the ground explicit. The necessity is not an addition to the list but its foundation.

**Urging One Another to Perseverance**

The first of the two reciprocal acts is exhortation to patience, and the Qur'an's shortest chapter makes it one of the four marks of those who escape ruin: "except those who have faith, do good, and urge each other to the truth, and urge each other to perseverance" (103:3). Patience here is not passive endurance but sustained practice under opposition. It is what a person needs in order to keep praying when prayer is mocked, to keep giving when giving is resented, and to keep telling the truth when the truth is expensive. The exhortation is mutual, which changes its character entirely. A man can resolve to be patient on his own and fail within a week; a community that reminds him does not depend on his resolve. The verse asks for that reminding, and it asks each member of the community to do it for the others.

**Urging One Another to Compassion**

The second reciprocal act is exhortation to mercy, and it is distinct from the first. Patience concerns a person's relation to difficulty; mercy concerns his relation to other people. The pairing covers both axes of the moral life, and the Qur'an grounds the second in the purpose of the revelation itself: "We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a mercy for the whole world" (21:107). A community whose members urge one another to patience alone becomes hard; a community that urges mercy as well keeps its endurance humane. The verse's insistence on the second exhortation is what prevents the ascent described in this chapter from turning into a private discipline of grit. The pass is climbed with other people in view, and the mercy demanded is not a feeling but a practice that members of the community keep recommending to one another. Urging one another is a collective act, and its inclusion in a list of personal duties is significant. A reader who performs the other acts privately has not yet met this one; it requires other people and it requires speech. The verse makes the community part of the ascent.

**Reciprocal Verbs Make the Climb a Shared Act**

The verb in both clauses is reciprocal: they urge one another, they exhort each other. Nothing in the verse describes a solitary ascent. This is a significant feature of a chapter whose central image is a steep road, because a road suggests an individual effort. The sūrah corrects that impression at the last moment. The deeds that define the pass — freeing, feeding, supporting — are performed by individuals, but the capacity to keep performing them is produced by a community. A man who gives in a famine needs to be told by others that the giving is worth it; a man who frees a slave needs to hear that he has not been foolish. The verse supplies that need in the form of an obligation. Encouragement is not a courtesy here; it is one of the two things the community is required to exchange.

**The Condition That Makes the List Coherent**

Taken with the verses before it, this clause completes an argument that began with an oath. Human beings were made in struggle; they were given faculties and shown two ways; the road up is defined by costly acts toward specific people; and those acts count only when they are done in faith and sustained by a community that encourages patience and mercy. Every part of that chain is necessary. Remove the faculties and the demand is unjust; remove the definition and the demand is vague; remove the condition of faith and the demand becomes a transaction; remove the mutual exhortation and the demand becomes unsustainable. The verse is the clause that holds the others together, and it does so by locating the moral life inside belief and inside a group. Neither location is optional, and neither is a substitute for the other. The condition makes the list coherent by supplying what the individual items lack. A reader who asks why these three acts and not others has his answer in the ground beneath them; the acts are expressions of one disposition rather than three separate requirements.

**Encouragement Is an Obligation, Not a Temperament**

The verse does not describe a community that happens to be supportive; it requires one. The verbs are in the form of mutual action, and the objects are two specific virtues, which means the exhortation has content. A community is not merely asked to be kind to its discouraged members; it is asked to recommend patience and mercy to them by name. That turns encouragement from a personality trait into a duty with a checkable form. The duty also has a direction. It runs between members rather than downward from leaders, which distributes it across the group and makes every member both a giver and a receiver of it. The practical effect is that no one in such a community is left to sustain himself on his own resolve, and no one is permitted to sit outside the exchange. The sūrah's ascent is demanding enough that solitary effort would fail, and this clause supplies the structure that keeps the effort going.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:18

> These are the people of the right.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verdict Delivered in Five Words**

After a definition that runs over several verses — freeing, feeding, believing, urging patience and mercy — the sūrah closes the case with a single short sentence. The people just described are the people of the right. There is no elaboration, no promise of gardens or rivers, no list of rewards. The identification is the reward, and its brevity is calculated. A long description of the outcome would shift the reader's attention from the acts to the prize; a short one keeps the weight on what was done. The verse also functions as a seal on the definition. Everything named in the previous verses — the neck, the famine, the orphaned relative, the man in the dust, the community of mutual exhortation — is now gathered into a single title, and the title is eschatological. The deeds have been given their final classification. A verdict delivered in five words is what the verse is, and the brevity is the force. A reader who expects an argument finds a classification instead, and the classification is final. The verse does not explain itself because the preceding verses have already done the explaining.

**The Right Hand and What It Holds**

The imagery of right and left in the Qur'an is tied to the record of deeds and to the hand in which it is received. "As for those who are given their record in their right hand" (84:7) introduces a scene of relief; the same scene appears in the sūrah of the Inevitable, where the one given his record in his right hand cries out happily, "Here ˹everyone˺! Read my record!" (69:19). The Day of Summoning is described in the same terms: "whoever will be given their record in their right hand will read it ˹happily˺ and will not be wronged ˹even by the width of˺ the thread of a date stone" (17:71). The right hand is therefore not an abstract symbol of favour but a specific moment: the receipt of one's own account, in public, without shame. The people of this verse are the people who can look at what they did. That is the meaning the sūrah has been building toward, since the acts it lists were all performed where other people could see them or not.

**Defined by Deeds, Not by Descent**

The title given to this group carries no tribal, ethnic or genealogical content. It is not the people of a clan, a city or a lineage, and it excludes nobody in advance. The qualification is entirely behavioural, and the behaviour was specified in the previous verses with unusual concreteness. In a city where standing was inherited, that is a direct challenge. The oath at the head of the chapter swore by the city and by parent and child, honouring both, and this verse then defines the honoured group without reference to either. Membership is open to anyone who performs the acts, and closed to anyone who does not, whatever his family. The sūrah has moved from the sanctity of a place to the conduct of persons, and the move is the argument. Defining people by deeds rather than by descent is what the classification does, and the reader should note what it excludes. Lineage, wealth and standing are all absent from the description, and their absence is the point. The verse identifies a group by what its members did.

**A Company, Not a List of Individuals**

The word used is *aṣḥāb*, companions or people of, and it denotes a group held together by a shared attribute. The plural is not incidental. The acts described in the previous verses were individual — a man frees a slave, a household feeds a relative — but the final exhortation was reciprocal, and the title reflects that reciprocity. The people of the right are a company. They are described elsewhere in the same collective terms: "the people of the right, how ˹blessed˺ will they be" (56:8), and "And the people of the right—how ˹blessed˺ will they be!" (56:27). The exclamation in those verses belongs to a scene of arrival, and it is addressed to the group rather than to any member of it. This sūrah uses the same title without the exclamation, which leaves the emphasis on the qualification rather than on the celebration. A company rather than a list of individuals is what the term denotes, and the difference matters. A reader who reads the verse as naming particular people has narrowed it; the description fits anyone who meets the conditions, and the openness is what allows him to place himself in it.

**Why the Reward Is Not Described Here**

The absence of a description of Paradise in this verse is a feature of the chapter's method. The sūrah's whole energy has been spent on the cost of the good — the famine, the extremity, the surrender of ownership — and it declines to balance that cost with a catalogue of compensation. The reader who wants the compensation must go to the longer chapters, where the people of the right are given gardens, companions and peace. Here the verse offers only the identification, and the identification is enough for the argument the chapter is making. What a person does with his wealth determines which group he belongs to; the terms of the belonging are stated elsewhere. The sūrah is concerned with the classification, not with the itinerary. Not describing the reward here leaves it to the rest of the revelation, and the leaving is deliberate. A reader who wants details has them elsewhere; the verse is concerned with identification rather than description, and the concern explains the brevity.

**A Title the Reader Can Still Earn**

The verse is written in the present of judgment but read in the present of life, and that double timing is what makes it an invitation rather than a verdict on the past. The people of the right are described by acts that remain available: a person can be freed, a relative can be fed, a stranger in the dust can be given food, a community can urge its members to patience and mercy. None of these is closed. The sūrah ends its description of this group without naming a single individual, which means the title is unoccupied in the reader's own case until he acts on it. The verse therefore does two things at once. It closes the definition of the good, and it leaves the door into the definition open. The next verse turns to the other group, and the contrast is what gives this title its edge.

**The Record Can Be Read in Public Without Shame**

The title of this group points to a moment of exposure. The record of deeds will be laid open, and the scene is described without mercy for the wicked: "They will cry, ‘Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?’ They will find whatever they did present ˹before them˺" (18:49). Nothing is omitted, and the omission of nothing is exactly what makes the moment bearable for one group and unbearable for the other. The people of the right are the ones who can be handed their own account in front of witnesses and read it, because the entries in it were made deliberately: a person freed, a relative fed, a stranger in the dust given food, patience and mercy urged on others. The verse's short verdict is therefore a statement about visibility. What these people did, they did where it could be seen, and the seeing does not undo it.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:19

> As for those who deny Our signs, they are the people of the left.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Mirror That Completes the Chapter**

The sūrah's structure closes with a symmetry. Two oaths opened it, a statement about the human condition followed, an accusation was made, a road was defined, and now two groups are named. The symmetry is exact in form and unequal in substance. One side was defined by what it did; the other is defined by what it rejected. That asymmetry is the chapter's final judgement, and it is stated without emphasis. No argument is added, no evidence is repeated, no warning is elaborated. The verse simply places the deniers opposite the doers and lets the reader see the two descriptions side by side. The rhetorical effect depends on everything that came before. A reader who has followed the definition of the pass knows what the denial costs. The mirror completes the chapter by giving the second outcome. A reader who has been shown one company is now shown the other, and the pairing is what makes the classification useful. Without the mirror, the first description would be a compliment rather than a choice.

**Denial of the Signs Is the Specific Charge**

The charge is *kafarū bi-āyātinā* — they disbelieved in Our signs — and the signs have been on display since the first verse. The city sworn by is a sign. The faculties listed, the eyes and the tongue and the lips, are signs. The two ways shown to humanity are signs. The verse therefore does not accuse this group of ignorance but of refusal. They were shown, and they denied what was shown. That distinction matters, because it removes the defence of incomprehension. The signs in this chapter are not obscure propositions; they are the reader's own eyes and the road in front of him. Denial of them is not an intellectual position but a decision, and the verse treats it as one. Denial of the signs is the specific charge, and the specificity matters. A reader who assumes the charge is general wrongdoing has widened it; the verse names a particular refusal, and the naming allows him to check whether the refusal is present in himself.

**The Boaster Belongs to This Group**

The man quoted at the head of the chapter is the case in point. He asked whether anyone would ever have power over him and whether anyone saw him, and then announced how much wealth he had destroyed. Each of those positions is a denial of a sign. The first denies that he is a creature under a Creator's power; the second denies that his acts are observed; the third treats obedience as waste, which denies that the expenditure has any value beyond the visible. The verse does not name him, and the generalisation is deliberate. The deniers are not a specific clan but a disposition, and the disposition has already been illustrated in detail. A reader who recognised himself in the boast has just been told which group that recognition places him in, unless he changes.

**The People of the Left Described Elsewhere**

The title given here is expanded in the sūrah of the Event, where the two groups are announced and then described: "the people of the left, how ˹miserable˺ will they be" (56:9), and again "And the people of the left—how ˹miserable˺ will they be!" (56:41). The description that follows is physical and unremitting — scorching heat, boiling water, the shade of black smoke, "neither cool nor refreshing" (56:42–44). The contrast with the people of the right is drawn in the same chapter and in the same terms. This sūrah borrows the title without borrowing the description, which keeps the emphasis where the chapter has placed it throughout: on the acts and their rejection rather than on the scenery of the outcome. The reader is expected to know the rest, and the reference is enough.

**What the Denial Rejected Was Available**

The most severe feature of the charge is that nothing was withheld from the deniers. The chapter opened by swearing on things they valued, listed faculties they possessed, and described a road they could have charged. The two ways were shown to them, and the showing is stated as a completed act. The verse therefore describes a refusal made with full equipment. This is why the group is defined negatively. One group has a positive definition because it did something; the other has none, because denial leaves nothing to describe. A reader comparing the two verses finds that one is a list and the other is an absence, and the difference between the lists is the difference between the two groups. What the denial rejected was available, and the availability is what makes the denial culpable. A reader who supposes that the signs were hidden has been told otherwise; they were present and were refused. The verse establishes the availability before establishing the charge.

**A Category, Not a Roster**

No individual is named in this verse, and no group is excluded from leaving it. The charge is stated in the plural and in general terms, which is how the Qur'an typically describes a condition rather than a census. The sūrah that began with an oath on a city and a lineage ends by classifying people according to their response to what they were shown, and the classification remains open to change for as long as a person can act. The verse is not a sentence passed on named persons; it is the description of a position, and positions can be left. The final verse of the chapter states what the position ends in, and its severity is measured against the ease with which it could have been avoided. A category rather than a roster is what the verse supplies, and the distinction protects it from being used as an accusation against named people. A reader who meets the description in himself has met the verse's purpose; the category is open in both directions.

**The Signs Were Local Before They Were Universal**

The denial charged in this verse begins at home. The first sign this chapter swears by is the city itself, and the city's status is a fact the deniers lived inside. Their own objection to following the Prophet was economic, and the answer to it is a reminder of the sign they already enjoyed: "Have We not established for them a safe haven ˹in Mecca˺ to which fruits of all kinds are brought as a provision from Us? But most of them do not know ˹this favour˺" (28:57). The final clause states the denial precisely. It is not that the favour was hidden but that it was not recognised. A safe haven in a region of raiding, fruit carried into a waterless valley, custodianship of a House that drew pilgrims from every direction — each of these is a sign, and each was refused by the people who benefited most from it. The verse classifies that refusal, and it does so in the plainest available terms.

**Denial in This Chapter Has a Social Shape**

The group is named in the plural, and the plurality is not a grammatical convenience. In Mecca, disbelief was a public position with social consequences: it was held by custodians of the sanctuary, defended in assemblies, and enforced against those who abandoned it. The Qur'an consistently describes such denial as collective, and it addresses the collective as such. This verse continues that method. It does not isolate an individual thinker who reached a wrong conclusion; it identifies a party defined by what it rejected. The practical consequence is that leaving the party is possible only by an act, since the party is held together by shared refusal rather than by shared argument. Those named in the previous verse were also a group, held together by shared practice rather than shared refusal. The chapter's last two verses describe two communities, and the difference between them is what their members do.

## Sūrah al-Balad 90:20

> The Fire will be sealed over them.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Word Carries the Whole Verse**

The chapter ends with a single image, and the image is closure. *Muʾṣadah* describes something shut fast, its doors closed and secured from the outside. The Qur'an uses the same word in the sūrah of the Slanderer, where the Fire is described as sealed over its inhabitants and "˹tightly secured˺ with long braces" (104:8–9). The addition of the braces in that parallel tells the reader what this verse leaves implicit: the sealing is not a temporary measure that might be reversed but a fastening. The brevity of the verse is part of its effect. After twenty verses of oath, argument and definition, the last line is four words long and contains no qualification, no explanation and no offer. The chapter ends where its argument ends, and it ends shut. One word carries the whole verse, and the reader who understands the word understands the chapter's ending. The term describes a closure that is final rather than temporary, and the finality is what gives the last verse its weight. The verse is brief because the word is sufficient.

**What Sealing Excludes**

The exegetes read the word in its plain sense and draw out its consequences. The doors of the Fire are locked upon its inhabitants so that they cannot leave, no relief reaches them, and no air enters. The Qur'an states the same permanence elsewhere: of the punishment of the Fire it says, "It will never be lightened for them, and there they will be overwhelmed with despair" (43:75). Two things are excluded by the sealing, and both are the reverse of what the chapter demanded. The first is exit. The pass was a road that could be charged and crossed; the Fire is a place from which there is no crossing. The second is change. The ascent described in the middle of the chapter was available at every moment until death; the sealed place admits no further act. The verse is therefore not merely a description of punishment but the final statement of the chapter's central contrast.

**The Alternative Sense: Covered Over**

Some commentators take *muʾṣadah* in the sense of covered rather than locked, so that the Fire is closed over its inhabitants as a lid is closed over a vessel. The two readings differ in image but not in meaning. A covered thing is as inaccessible from inside as a locked one, and the sense of finality is identical. The choice between them changes only the picture in the reader's mind — doors and braces, or a lid pressed down — and both pictures carry the same conclusion. The exegetical preference for the locked reading rests on the parallel in 104:8–9, where the braces make the fastening explicit. Either way, the verse describes a state that cannot be altered from within, which is the point the chapter needs in order to complete its argument. The alternative sense of being covered over is worth holding alongside the dominant one. A reader who takes it receives a description of confinement rather than of pursuit, and the confinement is the more severe of the two. Both senses are transmitted and both have been used.

**A Climb Refused and a Closure Accepted**

The chapter's two halves are movements in opposite directions. The middle of the sūrah describes an ascent: a steep pass, charged deliberately, at cost, in the company of people who urge one another to patience and mercy. The end describes a descent into a place that closes. The two movements are not presented as equal alternatives. The ascent was defined in detail, with named recipients and specified seasons, and it was made available to everyone with eyes, a tongue and a shown way. The closure is described in a single clause. The disproportion is the argument. The road up required effort, money and company; the ending required only that a person keep refusing it. That is why the verse is short. Nothing more needed to be said about a state entered by default. A climb refused and a closure accepted is the contrast the chapter ends on. A reader who has followed the argument sees the two outcomes stated in the last two verses, and the stating leaves nothing further to be decided. The verse closes the chapter by closing the options.

**The Architecture of the Whole Chapter**

Twenty verses, and every part has a function. An oath on a sanctuary and on parent and child establishes the ground. A statement of the human condition — created in struggle — supplies the premise. An accusation exposes the man who imagines himself beyond power and beyond observation. A list of faculties removes the excuse of incapacity. A question about the two ways removes the excuse of ignorance. A rhetorical question announces that the road is greater than the reader supposes, and the answer defines it in acts of liberation and feeding, grounded in faith and sustained by mutual exhortation. Two titles classify the doers and the deniers. The last verse states the end of denial. Nothing in that sequence is decorative, and nothing can be removed without breaking the case. The architecture of the whole chapter is visible in the ending. A reader who returns to the opening oaths will find that everything between them and this verse has been moving toward the classification, and the movement is what makes the structure intelligible rather than merely sequential.

**The Final Word Is Not the Only Word**

The chapter closes on a sealed Fire, and a reader might conclude that the last word has been spoken. It has not. Everything in the sūrah before the final verse is addressed to the living, and the pass it describes is chargeable until the moment it is not. The severity of the ending is a measure of what was refused, not a statement that the refusal is already complete. The sūrah's method throughout has been to place the reader inside the argument rather than outside it: the oath is his city, the faculties are his own, the road is in front of him. The sealed place is described in the third person because it belongs to those who have finished refusing. For everyone still reading, the definition of the ascent stands, and its first item is a person to be freed. The final word is not the only word, and the reader should not treat it as the chapter's summary. What precedes it is the instruction, and the ending is the consequence. A person who reads only the last verse has read the verdict without the case.

**The Sanctuary's Doors and the Fire's Doors**

The chapter opened by swearing on a city whose defining feature was access. The Sacred House was made "a centre and a sanctuary for the people", a place for those who circle it, meditate in it, bow and prostrate (2:125), and its residents were fed and kept safe so that worship could continue (106:4). Doors in that city were open by design: pilgrims came, traders came, and the House existed to be approached. The last verse of the chapter describes the opposite kind of door. The Fire is sealed over its inhabitants, fastened from outside, with no approach and no departure. The contrast between the two doors is the chapter's final image, and it is not accidental. A city built for approach ends by producing, for some of its residents, a place that cannot be approached or left. What decides which door a person meets is the conduct described in the middle of the chapter.

**Withholding Food and Meeting Fire**

The punishment described in the closing verses answers the offences described in the middle ones. The man who complained about the wealth he had spent refused to feed; the sūrah of the Clothed One records the confession of those in the Fire, "We were not of those who prayed, nor did we feed the poor" (74:43–44). The correspondence is exact, and it is the same in this chapter. Food was withheld in a famine, the orphaned relative was passed by, the man in the dust was left where he lay, and a neck stayed bound. The Fire is then described in terms of what those people refused to give: no relief, no air, no exit, and no lightening of the punishment (43:75). The verse is short because the correspondence does the explaining. Every refusal recorded earlier in the chapter has a shape, and the shape of the ending is the same shape turned around.
