# Sūrah al-Ḥadīd (Chapter 57) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary
## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:1

> Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah, for He is the Almighty, All-Wise.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Opens With a Fact, Not a Command**

Six imperatives will arrive before this sūrah is done — believe, spend, fear Allah, race — but the opening clause gives no order at all. It states what is already happening: the heavens and the earth are mid-sentence in praise. This is a deliberate placement. Before the reader is asked to do anything for God, the sūrah reports that creation has been doing it since the first moment of its existence. Human obedience is therefore not the debut of worship in the universe; it is the voluntary joining of a chorus already under way. The reassurance hidden in that structure is easy to miss: the believer who finds prayer difficult enters a stream that does not depend on his effort to flow, and the cosmos does not require his participation to keep glorifying — though it loses something if he declines.

**"Whatever," Not "Whoever": an Inclusive Grammar**

Arabic distinguishes two relative pronouns: man for rational persons, mā for everything else — and the verse uses the broader, non-personal form. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth: stones and stars, angels and planets, rain and the silence between them. Some commentators note that if the verse had said "whoever," the reader could have supposed the praise belonged only to minds, to the angels and the praying servants; "whatever" closes that exit. Even the inanimate is a worshipper, which means that materialism is refuted at the level of grammar before a single argument is offered. Matter is not dead weight in this picture; it is a tongue — an unarticulated one, but a tongue of the kind God understands.

**Tasbīḥ: Swimming Past Every Defect**

The verb is sappaḥa, from the root s-b-ḥ, whose basic sense is to swim or to move swiftly past something. To glorify God is thus, literally, to speed past everything unfitting about Him: defect, partner, need, death. The morphology of the word carries a lesson too. In the Qur'an this verb appears in the past tense, the ongoing present, the command, and the verbal noun — every slot the Arabic verb offers. Praise, in other words, is conjugated through all time: it has been said, it is being said, it is commanded, and it is the very substance of worship, not merely its acts. The verse's present-tense construction keeps the glorification perpetual; it never says "glorified Him once."

**Two Tongues: Spoken Praise and the Praise of Being**

Exegetes distinguish what creation says with utterance from what it says with existence — a mountain glorifies by being what it was made to be. The distinction matters because the Qur'an itself insists the second kind is real, not poetic: "The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him. There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification. He is indeed Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving." (17:44) The closing sentence is not decoration; the verse ends by naming patience and forgiveness precisely because creation's praise continues despite being incomprehensible to us, and despite our failure to match it. Whatever praises God in its own language, we are not qualified to audit it. What remains for us is forbearance toward the chorus and gratitude for our part in it.

**The Glorifiers and a Verse Better Than a Thousand**

This sūrah heads a family — 57, 59, 61, 62, and 64, all opening on the same verb of glorification — known in the sciences as al-Musabbiḥāt, "the Glorifiers." Al-ʿIrbāḍ ibn Sāriya reported that the Prophet ﷺ would not go to sleep before reciting them, and said of them, "In them is a verse better than a thousand verses" (Sunan Abū Dāwūd 5057; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2921, graded ḥasan by al-Tirmidhī). Exegetes have generally pointed that report at 57:3, whose four names gather theology into one line. The report does not name the verse, so the identification stays a judgment rather than a text — but it explains why the earliest scholars gave these openings disproportionate weight, and why the sūrah's first verses are about God alone, before the world and its duties are mentioned once.

**Almighty, All-Wise: the Ground of Praise**

The clause "for He is the Almighty, All-Wise" answers why the cosmos glorifies: not because it is coerced by raw power alone, but because power and wisdom coincide in Him. al-ʿAzīz is the irresistible, al-Ḥakīm the perfectly placing of every thing — might without caprice, order without weakness. This pairing recurs at the hinge of the Qur'an's arguments about creation, and here it defines the tone of everything that follows in this sūrah: the spending commanded in its later verses is an act of wisdom, not a tribute extracted by force. The next verse grounds the same praise in ownership: to Him belongs the kingdom, and none of this belongs to us.

**Praise Directed To Him, Not Merely Said About Him**

The verb of glorification can attach to God directly or through the preposition li — to glorify Him, or to glorify for Him — and the construction here opens that second meaning. Classical grammarians allowed that the preposition may be doing no extra work, a common redundancy in Arabic verbs of speech; but the richer reading, favored in devotional commentary, is that creation's praise is offered "for God" — oriented to Him alone, asked for by no one, useful to nobody. That nuance is not incidental in this sūrah. The chapter that opens with the cosmos praising disinterestedly will spend its middle verses testing whether the community can give the same way, and its sharpest rebuke, at verses 23–24, lands on the man who gives only to be seen and the man who withholds to keep. The heavens pass the test the wallet will be given. Praise with no angle on the praiser is the sūrah's first and last word: the universe worships the Almighty, the All-Wise, and asks nothing back except that the praise be His.

**A State, Not an Event**

The tense deserves half a sentence of attention: everything in the heavens and the earth "glorifies" — an ongoing present, not a completed past — so the verse describes a standing condition of creation rather than one act on a timeline. Creation is not pictured as having praised once at the beginning, the way a factory might be said to have "saluted" its maker on opening day; it is mid-hymn at every moment, and the praise of things is as continuous as their existence. This is why the next clause can slip from liturgy to ownership without a seam: the same God who is being glorified without ceasing is the God whose kingdom never changed hands. Worship and property arrive as one doctrine. The audience that will be asked to let go of wealth in verse 7 has been shown, in verse 1, a universe in which no one was ever holding anything on his own authority.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:2

> To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death. And He is Most Capable of everything.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Sovereignty First, Commands Later**

The sūrah's second statement establishes ownership before it introduces obligation. The kingdom — mulk — is total: the heavens, the earth, and everything between, with no clause of exception. That sequence is the argumentative spine of the whole chapter, because the verses that follow will ask for money, presence, and loyalty; the opening line makes sure the reader already knows whose money it is. A king who commands tribute from his own property has not dispossessed anyone. Read this way, verse 7's call to "donate from what He has entrusted you with" was prepared by verse 2's claim to the kingdom. The economics of the sūrah are not a tax but a reallocation of the owner's goods among trustees, and the moral pressure it exerts loses its sting once ownership is settled.

**Giving Life and Causing Death: a Claim Tested in Court**

The pairing yuḥyī wa-yumīt does more than list two biological events; it assigns both ends of existence to one agent. The Qur'an elsewhere stages the claim as a courtroom challenge: "˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.”" (2:258) The disputant there could not follow through, and the argument moved to a stronger example, the sun's rising. In this sūrah there is no dialogue, only assertion — the claim is stated as settled fact in a verse of praise, not offered for debate. The denier of resurrection elsewhere in the Book asks who can revive bones; the reply is that the One who produced them first "has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being." (36:79) The sūrah's phrasing assumes that same answer is already accepted by its audience.

**Life and Death as One Envelope, Not Two Realms**

Because both acts are His, death loses its status as an interruption. The universe is not a zone of life occasionally invaded by death; it is one continuous act of divine giving and taking, and the praise of verse 1 therefore never breaks. This is why the classical lists of God's names count the Giver of Life (al-Muḥyī) and the Causer of Death (al-Mumīt) as names of dignity, not of terror: neither name describes a change of mood in God. Neither name occurs in the Qur'an in its nominalized form, but both are built directly from verbs like these, which recur often enough to function as attributes, and they are fixed in the received lists of the divine names.

**"Most Capable of Everything": the Attribute That Ends the Verse**

The closing phrase — ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr — is one of the Book's signature conclusions, and it is precise: capability over everything, not over many things. No exception is carved out for the second death, the rebuilt body, the turned heart, or the redistributed wealth of the community. The same formula stands at the end of a nearly identical claim elsewhere: "To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and everything within. And He is Most Capable of everything." (5:120) Its placement here matters for what follows: a community being told to spend for an unseen return is first told that repayment is not difficult for the One who promises it.

**Why the Opening Block Accumulates Claims**

Read as a unit, the first two verses say: He is praised, He is mighty and wise, He owns, He gives and takes life, He is capable of all. Each clause is load-bearing for the sūrah. Glorification grounds worship, sovereignty grounds the call to spend, capability grounds the promise of resurrection, might and wisdom ground the warning to the hypocrites that their interior is visible. The opening is a small creed, and the verses that follow are its consequences drawn in cash, light, and hearts. Verse 3 tightens that creed into four words.

**Two Fears, One Verse**

Read against the verses this one precedes, verse 2 is addressed to the two engines of human refusal. Fear of loss is answered by "to Him belongs the kingdom": what you are being asked to give was lodged with you, and the Owner has not gone bankrupt by honoring the loan. Fear of people is answered by "He is Most Capable of everything": the community that spent and fought before Mecca had every reason to look frightened, and this clause is the answer to dread that outlasts a single battle. The pairing compresses the sūrah's entire psychology of obedience — money and safety — into an ownership statement and a capability statement. Elsewhere the Book says the same thing with even blunter economics: "And surely to Us ˹alone˺ belong this life and the next." (92:13) If both homes belong to Him, the transaction the sūrah will propose involves no real transfer; the only genuine movement is the heart's, from holding to trust.

**Why Sovereignty Is Proven by Life and Death**

The middle clause — He gives life and causes death — is the verse's evidence, not a second doctrine bolted beside the first. Kingdom is claimed, and the claim is tested at the only two doors every living thing must pass: the door it came through and the door it will leave by. Whoever holds both holds everything, because there is no third entrance to existence. The sūrah elsewhere in the Book stages this proof as a courtroom scene: Abraham's royal opponent claimed the power to give life and cause death, and was redirected to the rising sun, where his claim collapsed (2:258). Here there is no argument, only the assertion, because the audience is being prepared rather than refuted. And note what kind of kingship this is: a monarch whose chief acts are animating and reanimating, so that governance in this Book is nearer to resuscitation than to taxation — which is precisely the sense in which its commands are meant to be obeyed.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:3

> He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near, and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Four Names That Cover Every Coordinate**

Time has two ends and space has two directions; this verse claims all four. First and Last run the line of before and after; the Most High and Most Near run the line of above and within. Nothing in the created order falls outside both axes, so the four names state, in the smallest possible compass, that God is not absent from any region of existence. This is the line to which commentators pointed when they explained the report that "in them is a verse better than a thousand verses" — the hadith about the Glorifiers recited before sleep — because a single verse that summarizes the whole of doctrine about God deserves weight no narrative verse can match. The report itself does not specify the verse, so the identification remains a judgment, but the fit is hard to resist.

**First and Last: What the Pair Excludes**

To say God is the First is to say there is no "before" in which He was not; to say He is the Last is to say the series of things has an end that He outlasts. The Qur'an's own evidence for each half is distributed: perishability belongs to everything else — "Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺." (55:26–27) — while the priority belongs to Him alone, the point of the old confession that there was nothing besides Him. The two names therefore do double duty: they describe Him, and they demote every rival. A dynasty, a mountain, an idol: each has a first moment, so each fails the test of the First; each has a last, so each fails the test of the Last.

**The Outward and the Inward: Manifest and Hidden**

The Arabic is al-Ẓāhir and al-Bāṭin, and their sense is not spatial in a crude way but evidentiary. He is the Outward because everything that appears is His sign; the faces of things are windows, not walls. He is the Inward because nothing reaches inside a creature the way its maker does: "No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision. For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware." (6:103) And nearness is claimed in the first person plural: "Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein." (50:16) The pairing forbids two errors at once: searching for God only in the distance, and domesticating Him as an inner feeling.

**Two Pairs, Not a List of Four**

The grammar permits — and the great lexicographer of Q 57's tafsīr tradition urged — reading the names as two coordinated pairs rather than a flat series: the First and the Last are one truth about time, the Outward and the Inward one truth about presence, each pair answering a different anxiety. Al-Zamakhsharī explained the pairing this way: at every moment He is manifest to insight and hidden from sight, early in history and late in it alike. The advantage of this reading is that it blocks a misreading of the verse as a roster; a roster of attributes invites collection, a pair of polarities invites resolution. The four names are one statement with four faces.

**The Prophet's Bedtime Summary of This Verse**

The most direct commentary on these words is a practice. ʿĀʾisha reported that when the Prophet ﷺ lay down at night he supplicated: "O Allah, You are the First, there is nothing before You; the Last, there is nothing after You; the Manifest, there is nothing above You; the Hidden, there is nothing below You. Relieve my debt and enrich me beyond poverty." (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2713) Two things stand out. The names are not analyzed but addressed — the verse is doctrinal, the duʿāʾ is devotional, and the doctrine exists to become prayer. And the petition the names support is the humblest of needs: debt. The most comprehensive verse about God in the Book ends in the sūrah by asking, in the Prophet's practice, for enough.

**Knowledge Seals the Verse**

The final clause — "and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things" — grounds the four names in epistemology. Owner of time and interior would be a distant abstraction if He did not actually know each particular; knowledge makes transcendence operational. That is also why the sūrah can move without transition in verse 6 to "He knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart": the seed is here, in the verse that the hypocrites' exposure will later grow from.

**Abstract Names for a Concrete Community**

It is a strange and deliberate order: a sūrah that will command loans, measure ranks, and name metal begins by emptying the reader's hands of everything but God. Nothing in verses 1–3 is practical, and that is its practicality — the community being trained to part with wealth needed its idea of God widened first, because stinginess and small theology turn out to be the same posture measured at different distances. The four names do that widening: no moment is unowned (First and Last), no interior is unsearched (Outward and Inward). When verse 25 eventually speaks of iron "with its great might," the metal will already have been framed by the God who is nearer than the jugular vein — the brute material of power placed inside a universe that is, in its grain, prayer and knowledge. The abstraction of this verse is the sūrah's way of arming the concrete verses that follow it.

**The Cadence of Attributes**

One more structural note before the opening block's pattern breaks. Each of the first four verses closes with a divine attribute or pair: the Almighty, the All-Wise (1); Most Capable of everything (2); knowledge of all things (3); All-Seeing of what you do (4). The cadence makes each verse a claim about God sealed by a name, and it gives this verse its own last words their force: "He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things" is not an afterthought but the seal on the four names, resolving transcendence into intimacy. Power alone could in principle be shared — no; by definition it could not, but knowledge is the attribute the Qur'an never lets anyone approximate. Placing it at the hinge between "wherever you turn" and "wherever you are" (verse 4's next clause) tells the reader that the First and the Last is also the one who is, at every instant, informed. That is the theology of the entire sūrah in a single cadence.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:4

> He is the One Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne. He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the sky and whatever ascends into it. And He is with you wherever you are. For Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Creation and Throne: the Smallest Creed in the Longest Verse of the Opening**

Four movements are compressed into one line: making, seating, knowing, seeing. The first two are the stock structure of the Qur'an's creation accounts — "Indeed your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne." (7:54) — and the sūrah reproduces that structure exactly, which already tells us something: its purpose is not cosmology but authority. Six Days of making, then the throne: the God who built is the God who rules. The sūrah that will judge a community by how it handles wealth uses the verse with a single word for the whole of production and the whole of government in it.

**What the Days Mean, and What They Do Not**

The ambiguity of the word yawm — day, epoch, period — has occupied commentators since the earliest layers of exegesis, and it is real: the same Book relativizes the length of divine days elsewhere, so a literal twenty-four-hour reading has no foothold. Most classical exegetes accepted a number of long periods without assigning them to physics. That reserve is methodologically correct: the verse's claim is about who made and when nothing else made Him, not about geology. An interpretation that treats the "Days" as a scientific disclosure both over-reads the grammar and under-reads the purpose — the number six appears here to place creation inside an act, a sequence, and an end, not to date it.

**A Knowledge with Four Doors**

The middle of the verse inventories movement: entry into the earth and exit from it, descent from the sky and ascent into it — rain and seed and buried treasure and the dead going down; spring and harvest and mine and root coming up; decree and revelation descending; prayer and deeds rising. The list's rhetorical force is in its boring completeness: nothing crosses any border of the world without being in view, and the same wording reappears verbatim in Saba (34:2), a repetition that marks the formula as fixed liturgical material, not an ad hoc image. What the verse denies to creatures it reserves to God: the passage from one realm to another is where human surveillance fails, so omniscience is claimed precisely at the hinges of the map.

**"He Is With You Wherever You Are"**

The turning point of the verse is also the most debated sentence in classical theology. The majority read the with-ness as God's knowledge and power reaching everywhere, not His essence located alongside bodies — a reading that protects transcendence and fits the immediate context, which closes with "Allah is All-Seeing of what you do." Others, especially within devotional and Sufi commentary, allowed a stronger nearness, an accompanying of the servant Himself, anchored in verses like the jugular-vein claim (50:16). The majority position is stronger here for a textual reason: the verse moves from knowledge of all movement to knowledge of all conduct; the with-ness is introduced as surveillance-with-care, not spatial co-location. Both sides agree on the pastoral effect the clause exists to produce: the reader is not unattended.

**Seeing as the Closing Argument**

The verse ends not with might but with sight: "For Allah is All-Seeing of what you do." This is the ethics gate of the opening block. The creation and the Throne could be admired from a distance; the seeing of deeds cannot. The sūrah's audience is one being asked for money, and the verse's final words make wealth a witnessed act, which is exactly what verses 7–11 will exploit. The first six verses, in order, are praise, ownership, four names, this verse, return, and the night-and-day exchange into the knowledge of hearts — and every one of them has now touched human conduct from one side or the other.

**The Throne and the Question of the "How"**

"Then established Himself on the Throne" is one of the most carefully handled sentences in the science of theology, precisely because it says so little. The mainstream classical position was to affirm the establishment as befitting God while refusing to ask after its mode — the Throne is real, the seating is not like sitting, and "how" is a question bodies ask about furniture. A second school, strong among rationalist exegetes, read istiwa as a figure for mastery and conquest, "He mounted the Throne" the way a king "mounts" his authority over a city. The dispute is genuine and both sides have grammar on their side; what decides it, for most readers, is that the verse pairs the Throne with total knowledge of every entry and exit — the governance imagery favors a sovereignty you can distrust your secrets to, whichever grammar you choose. What neither school permits is the projection of a body onto God or the dilution of the claim into mere metaphor for human awe. The Throne says: the world has a seat of judgment, and it is occupied.

**An Inventory Written for a Caravan Economy**

The four gates this verse lists — into the earth, out of it, down from the sky, up into it — would have sounded like the ledgers of this sūrah's first audience, whose wealth literally moved through just such borders: goods entering a valley, caravans departing it, rain falling as the season's one reliable deposit, tribute rising. God's knowledge is described with the vocabulary of their customs house, and the theological point is that the real customs record precedes theirs: nothing was ever "passed through" uncounted, so no acquisition was ever unowned. This is verse 2's kingdom read at the level of traffic. It also prepares the exact sin the sūrah will indict: hoarding (verse 24) is the attempt to build a wall inside a world whose every border is already watched, and boasting about wealth is celebrating a line item in somebody else's book. The omniscience clause is therefore not reassurance only; it is property law.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:5

> To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. And to Allah all matters are returned.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Short Verse Assembled From Two Long Ones**

The first half of this verse repeats verse 2 word for word, while its second half is new. That is not laziness of composition; repetition was a structural instrument in the Qur'an's hands, and here it brackets the two central verses of the opening block. Read together: kingdom at the start, return at the end — the two horizons between which everything the sūrah says in the next twenty-three verses will be spoken. A reader who finds an odd verse in this chapter — a wall with a gate, an invented monastery, metal sent down — is reading inside brackets that were set here. The repetition also repairs a possible gap: verse 2's middle clause about giving life and death could be heard as a past act, while verse 5 re-opens the same kingdom as a present possession and a future receipt.

**The Return of Affairs: What "Matters" Means**

The word for "matters" is umūr — affairs, concerns, the running business of existence — not "days" or "lives." The claim is therefore administrative: the conduct of everything now underway is heading toward a decision point that belongs to God. The same phrase closes one of the Book's most concentrated passages on trust: "And to Him all matters are returned. So worship Him and put your trust in Him." (11:123) The logic there is instructive for here: because the affair ends in God's court, it is rational to worship and entrust now. The sūrah of Iron uses the clause identically, as the engine of obedience, not as metaphysics for its own sake. Every deferred injustice has a deadline; every paid bill of the world is on a conveyor.

**Why Ownership and Return Are Said Together**

The two halves are not a list of unrelated doctrines. A thing is owned twice over: by the one who holds it now, and by the one it returns to — and in the sūrah's account only one of those holdings is permanent. The inheritance of the heavens and the earth, which verse 10 will invoke against stinginess, is the practical face of verse 5: heirs do not own what they inherit from God in the first place. Property in this sūrah has a single owner with successive custodians, so "return" is not confiscation but repossession. That is why the warning against boast and hoard (verses 23–24) is attached to this cosmology rather than delivered on its own: greed is treated as a calendar error, a failure to register the date every affair already carries.

**The Quiet Function of an Echo Verse**

Little in this verse is new information, and that is its contribution. The opening block is dense — praise of all things, four names, six Days and a Throne — and the echo gives the sequence a second hinge and a pause, letting the fixed phrases sink before the imperatives begin. This is the same technique as the refrain of the Beneficent Sūrah, where one question is asked thirty-one times, but turned around: there the repetition drives the same question into new material; here it parks a new claim on top of an old one. Verse 1 also closes on the pair al-ʿAzīz, al-Ḥakīm; verse 5 keeps might and adds only return. Between the two halves of the block, therefore, the sūrah's audience is given time to lose the habit of ownership.

**"All Matters," Down to the Smallest Affair**

The word translated "matters" is plural and ordinary — umūr, affairs — which is its scope. Not "the great events" but every transaction the word covers: a loan made in secret, a grudge nursed for a decade, a child's burial, a treaty broken, the price of iron in the market at Taifa. The verse does not promise that these affairs end dramatically, only that they end at a particular place: "to Allah," the same preposition that opens the verse on ownership ("to Him belongs the kingdom"). Direction and destination match. This is why the parallel verse in Hud pairs the return with trust rather than fear: "And to Him all matters are returned. So worship Him and put your trust in Him." (11:123) Return to an owner is not annihilation of the affair but its delivery to judgment — and a judgment this sūrah will describe, at verses 12–15, in the calm language of receipts, lights, and walls.

**The Verse That Breaks the Cadence**

The opening block had been closing each verse with a divine attribute — the Almighty, the All-Wise; Most Capable; knowledge of all things; All-Seeing — and verse 5 stops the rhythm. There is no name appended. Instead, the verse's last word is the noun "Allah" standing at the head of the clause: "to Allah all matters are returned." The block's pattern of claims-with-seals ends on an unsealed claim, which reads like the sūrah setting down its pen and waiting. It is also the hinge verse: half of it is verse 2's praise, half is the first mention of the Day's mechanics. The next two verses resume description of the cosmos (night and day, hearts), but the register has changed — from who God is to what is coming. Read as architecture, verse 5 is the doorway between creed and accounting, and the community's wallet is the floor it stands on.

**One Root for Hope and for Homecoming**

Arabic ties this verse's last word to the oldest emotion in religion. Return is rujūʿ, from the root r-j-ʿ, and hope — rajāʾ — is the same root: to hope is to expect a return to you of what you entrusted elsewhere. A community being told that all affairs go back to Allah is therefore not offered a bleak accounting; the verse is quietly the Book's license to hope. What was spent (verses 7–11 will say) was placed on the road to that destination, and what was withheld arrives there anyway, minus the journey's credit. The sūrah of Iron keeps this root alive in its endings — matters returned, the Fire said to be the destination (masīr) of the refuse — so that every arrival in the chapter is a homecoming whose quality is fixed by what was carried toward it. Hope, in this vocabulary, is the other name of return when the Owner is kind.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:6

> He merges the night into day and the day into night. And He knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Verb for the Overlapping of Hours**

The single verb behind "merges" is wlj, to enter — the night enters the day and the day enters the night, each slipping into the other and displacing it inch by inch. Translators render this as "causes to overlap" or "makes to merge," and the image is not the swing of a switch but the slow subtraction and addition of light: the lengthening of one share is the shrinking of the other. The Qur'an uses the same verb at the end of the Pilgrimage sūrah as evidence of sustained divine control: "That is because Allah causes the night to merge into the day, and the day into the night. Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing." (22:61) That pairing of hearing and seeing with the astronomical claim shows the genre of both verses: natural constancy is presented as an attribute of a knowing agent, not of a machine.

**Two Readings of the Exchange**

Exegetes divide, gently, over what exactly is exchanged. One party takes the verse as the daily turnover — twenty-four hours in which night washes out day and day washes out night. Another, including several of the later commentators, presses the verse toward the seasons: the "merging" is best seen in the yearly drift of daylight, which grows and shrinks by minutes. The seasonal reading has more to work with grammatically, since a bare daily alternation would not obviously require the careful image of one entering the other; but the daily reading keeps the verse in earshot of every listener who has watched a dawn. Neither party needs to win: the two cases differ only in clock speed, and both describe the same submitted cosmos. What the verse wants is not astronomy but obedience — if the hours are handled by God, verse 2's claim of kingdom has visible receipts.

**"He Knows Best What Is in the Breasts"**

The second half changes scale from the horizon to the sternum. The Arabic says ṣudūr — chests or breasts — while the English translation's "heart" names the resident rather than the room. The physical image should be kept: the old Arabic mind located the seat of intention and secret thought behind the breastbone, and the phrase means that the contents of every chest are as present to God as the passing of dawn. The comparative in "knows best" is the idiom of God's superlative knowledge rather than a race among knowers; the same expression ends a verse elsewhere that ties the claim to exposure on the Last Day: "He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal. For Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart." (64:4) The two verses are near twins: chest-knowledge here, concealment-and-revelation there, and in both cases the point of the knowledge is disclosure.

**Why This Clause Comes Just Before the Hypocrites**

Structurally the placement is exact. The first six verses close; from verse 7, the audience is commanded to believe and spend, and by verses 13–15 the sūrah's most specific drama is the exposure of men and women whose chests did not match their rows. A community that hears only verse 4 might suppose it could stand before God on its visible record. Verse 6 removes that hope: the merger of night and day is the public proof that this God handles coverings, and the knowledge of chests is the private application. The hypocrite in the sūrah's sense is precisely one who trusts the difference between the room and the resident — hidden in the breast, safe in the assembly. The verse makes that difference disappear from God's side of it.

**God's Verbs, the Sky's Obedience**

Count the verbs in the first half: merges, one subject, no partners. The night does not wrest the day, the day does not retire gracefully; neither is said to do anything at all. This matters because the ancient sky-religions — and the Arabs' own star-cults — gave the lights of heaven at least a share of agency, and the Qur'an's grammar is a standing demotion of everything worshipped besides God. The same verb-family is used elsewhere of subjection: "He merges the night into the day and the day into the night, and has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term." (35:13) The alternation of light and dark is thus a proof by submission — a cosmos that obeys without choice is the courtroom in which a creature with choice will be judged. That is also why the clause lands the way it does: "He merges…" is physics stated as worship; "He knows…" is worship witnessed by omniscience. The two halves are one verse because the two topics are, in the Qur'an, the same topic.

**The Double Edge of Chest-Knowledge**

He knows best what is hidden in the chest, and the sentence is a sword with two hands on the hilt. To the hypocrite named in the block's next movements, it is the end of camouflage — the one room with no door is the one he was counting on. To the sincere man whose faith is invisible, whose restraint nobody sees and whose patience earns no citation, it is the only court whose verdict he needs. This sūrah exploits both edges: its exposure scenes (verses 13–15) could not run without the claim, and its consolation of hidden donors could not either — the one who gave in secret in verse 11's economy has a knowledge-of-chests for a witness. The verse refuses to let the community grade itself: interiors are the one terrain on which human rankings go blind and divine rankings go into effect. That is the last ground, before the commands begin, for why the whole opening block matters to the wallet.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:7

> Believe in Allah and His Messenger, and donate from what He has entrusted you with. So those of you who believe and donate will have a mighty reward. Why Do You Not Believe?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Two Imperatives, One Transaction**

Believe and spend: the sūrah compresses the whole of its agenda into a pair of commands, and the pairing itself is doctrine. Faith alone is stated as insufficient for the sentence that follows — the great reward is promised to "those of you who believe and donate," both participles in play — while donation without faith is not even addressed, because in this sūrah's logic every act is already referred back to God's kingdom (verse 2) and return (verse 5). The two commands are not a checklist of separate duties but the inner and outer halves of one transaction: what the heart confirms, the hand executes. A believer who is surprised that wealth appears so high on the agenda has not been listening to the opening verses, which made the money God's before this verse asked anyone to part with it.

**"From What He Has Entrusted You With"**

The single Arabic word behind "entrusted" carries the sūrah's anthropology: mustakhlaf fīhi, made to succeed others in holding a thing. The root is the one used for humanity's appointment in the first place — "˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.”" (2:30) — and the same idea returns with its test spelled out: "He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others, so He may test you with what He has given you." (6:165) Read against that background, the verse does not command generosity; it commands the honest handling of trust property. Unequal wealth is itself part of the trial, not a sign of favor, which is why this sūrah will refuse to read success as approval when it reaches the boast of the rich (verse 20). The trustee's freedom is in how he manages what was never his.

**A Mighty Reward, and Why the Adjective Matters**

The promise attached to the pair is "a mighty reward" — ajr ʿaẓīm, the Qur'an's familiar superlative for what cannot be carried by any human scale. Later in this same sūrah the reward for the goodly loan is called "generous" (karīm, verses 11 and 18), and the shift is worth noticing: ʿaẓīm measures size, karīm measures quality and grace. The great reward is what the transaction is worth; the generous reward is how it is paid — with a return that honors the giver, not merely tallies up. Nothing in the Arabic of the two verses makes them alternatives; they are the ledger and the courtesy of the same payment. The sūrah can afford both words because it has already declared that the Owner of the kingdom is not running a deficit.

**The Rebuke at the End of the Line**

This translation prints the interrogative "Why Do You Not Believe?" at the close of verse 7, while most classical arrangements begin the next verse with the same question expanded — the Messenger calling, the covenant invoked. Whichever division one follows, the rhetoric is identical: a command that has just landed is immediately restated as an incredulous question, the tone of astonishment rather than legislation. The question form assumes the answer is obvious — there is no reason not to believe, only excuses — and it moves the burden of proof to the reader in a single sentence. The double question (why not believe, and, at verse 10, why not give) frames the whole passage on wealth: both halves of the transaction are defended by showing that no ground exists for refusing either.

**Belief First Because Spending Fails Without It**

The order of the imperatives is not arbitrary. A command to donate issued to hearts not yet committed would collect taxes, not offerings — and the sūrah, which will later condemn the proud boaster and the miser who enjoins miserliness (verses 23–24), knows the difference. Faith is what converts disposal of property into worship; without it the identical cash transfer is just economics. That is also why the verse speaks to "you who will ever believe" in the conditional that follows in this position: the address acknowledges that the command's force depends on the very act it asks for, and that the covenant underneath the act (next verse's ground) is already older than the request.

**"Believe in Allah and His Messenger": the Sequence Is the Creed**

The first imperative names two objects in a fixed order, and the order carries the sūrah's epistemology. Faith in God is not, in this Book, free-floating reverence; it runs through the messenger because the covenant (verse 8) and the sending down (verse 9) are the channels through which the summons reached this audience. The pair recurs throughout the Qur'an as a single formula and becomes this sūrah's refrain — verses 19, 28 and 21 all restate "believe in Allah and His messenger(s)" — so that belief itself is conjugated through the person who delivered it. That is also why rejecting the Messenger here is not an insult to a man but a refusal of the covenant's latest installment. The imperative plural, meanwhile, marks the addressee as a community: āminū, "you all," not "you, individually." What is commanded is a collective condition, and the spending in the second half is its public proof.

**The Vocabulary of Spending, and of Hypocrisy**

The Arabic word for "donate" is from the root n-f-q, to let a thing exit — spending is literally your wealth leaving your hand, and the same root gives the market (where goods leave), the tunnel of a desert rodent (nafīq), and, famously, the hypocrite (munāfiq), who hides in a burrow of plausible belonging. The sūrah uses that whole family against its audience with remarkable economy: it commands infāq here, and by verse 13 its men and women in the hypocrites' burrows will be knocking at the wall asking to borrow light. The linguistic circle closes exactly where the theology points: hoarding and hypocrisy are kin — both are versions of holding things inside, wealth locked in a hand, disbelief locked in a chest, each comfortable in the dark. The verse's two commands are therefore one: come out, with your light and with your money, into the open where the Owner can bless what you were clutching.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:8

> Why do you not believe in Allah while the Messenger is inviting you to have faith in your Lord, although He has already taken your covenant, if you will ever believe.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Question That Lists Its Own Evidence**

The interrogative "why do you not believe" would normally demand reasons for the unbelief; here the verse immediately supplies everything that makes unbelief impossible, and does it in the order a court would expect: the invitation of a trustworthy messenger, and a prior covenant. The grammar piles two circumstantial clauses ("while… although…") onto a question, so that the refusal is pictured as a man standing in a room with two doors open, asking why he cannot leave. This is the Qur'an's characteristic argument from insufficiency — not "believe because it is true" but "explain, if you can, why you have not believed." Unbelief is not defeated here by a new proof; it is embarrassed by old ones.

**The Messenger's Invitation as a Standing Fact**

The present tense of the invitation matters: the Messenger is calling, continuously, in the audience's own hearing. The sūrah's listeners are not inheriting a closed prophecy but living inside an active summons, which removes the excuse of lateness that verse 10 will confront among those who came after the conquest, and that verse 29 will answer in full — no one is too late for grace, and no lineage holds the franchise on it. Invitation, not compulsion, is the verb of prophecy in this passage; the covenant described next was sealed before any messenger existed, so the appeal arrives as reminder rather than novelty. The weight the clause carries depends on the audience's own estimate of the man calling them, which in Medina was, by the sūrah's context, not in doubt.

**The Covenant Taken in the Backbone of Adam**

"He has already taken your covenant" refers, in the dominant reading, to the scene the Qur'an narrates in al-Aʿrāf, when the descendants of Adam were brought forth and asked: "˹Allah asked,˺ “Am I not your Lord?” They replied, “Yes, You are! We testify.”" (7:172) That pre-cosmic testimony is why unbelief is incoherent in this verse's logic: faith is not the addition of something foreign to the self but the honoring of a confession the self made before it had a history. A minority of exegetes instead take the covenant as the one given to the People of the Book through their prophets — plausible in a sūrah that ends by addressing them — but the context prefers the universal reading. The verse rebukes everyone, not one archive of scripture, and the verb "your Lord," not "your Book," fixes the covenant's content to lordship, which is precisely what 7:172 tested.

**Two Readings of the Same Clause**

The sentence "He has already taken your covenant" can be read actively — God took it — or passively, "the covenant has been taken," which removes God's act from the front of the clause and leaves only its fact. The active voice is the majority's choice and the more difficult reading, and here difficulty is a merit: a God who personally seals the promise is personally present to enforce it, which is exactly the emotional register of a verse asking why nobody is moving. The passive reading harmonizes with verses elsewhere where covenant language attaches to scripture; neither reading changes the conclusion, and the verse seems built to be legible at both. What both secure is that the summons in this life rests on a prior yes.

**"If You Will Ever Believe"**

The closing conditional is not a condition on God but an exposure of the audience: if belief is ever to happen to you, the ground for it is now, complete, and twice certified. Classical commentators read the clause as rebuke rather than doubt — the form "if you are believers" (or "if you will ever believe") appears at the end of demands the text considers overdue. The effect is to convert the whole question into a single deadline: the Messenger is calling today, the covenant was sealed before the first sunrise, and the only variable left is the reader. No fourth evidence is coming. The next verse will name the medium the invitation travels in — clear signs sent down — so that the ground shifts from the covenant beneath to the revelation among.

**The Idiom of Astonishment**

Behind "Why do you not believe" is the construction mā lakum — literally, "what is the matter with you," the Book's standing formula for incredulity (it reappears at verse 10's "what is the matter with you that you do not spend"). The device is rhetorical but not merely ornamental: it treats unbelief as a symptom requiring explanation rather than a position requiring refutation. In the sūrah's logic there is no good reason on offer — evidence, invitation, and covenant are all in place — so the reader is handed a question with no furniture to answer it with. Notice too the three grounds stacked around the question: belief in Allah, the Messenger's call, the taken covenant — the outer world, its authorized voice, and the inner memory. Any one of them would convict, in this telling; all three together make refusal a labor of forgetfulness, requiring a believer to succeed, verse by verse, at not knowing what he has always known.

**Whom Is the Rebuke For?**

Commentators differ on the audience, and the difference is measurable in the chapter's shape. The majority take the address as general — the whole assembly, newly Muslim or not — because the covenant cited is the pre-eternal one of 7:172, which admits no subgroup. Some of the older exegetes tied these verses instead to the hypocrites of Medina, since the passage's later verses (13–15) give them so much space; the hypocrite, on this reading, is the man who hears "if you will ever believe" as sarcasm because he already performs belief. A narrower party attaches verse 8 to the People of the Book, anticipating verse 29's address, since "your Lord" and the covenant vocabulary sit comfortably in the sūrah's closing argument. The general reading remains strongest: the sūrah keeps returning "O you who believe" to its own community, and rebuke addressed to outsiders would waste the only tense — astonishment at insiders — in which these imperatives can sting.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:9

> He is the One Who sends down clear revelations to His servant to bring you out of darkness and into light. For indeed Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to you. Why Do You Not Donate?

**Expanded Commentary**

**Revelation Described From the Sending End**

The verse continues the ground of belief by describing the mechanism of the invitation: God is "the One Who sends down" — the divine name-form munazzil, a heavy intensive participle, not a casual verb of delivery. Revelation in this sūrah is not an idea that occurred to a man; it is a descent, the same vocabulary the Qur'an reserves for the Book as for the rain, and the pairing is no accident: just as verse 20 will make the rain the figure of worldly growth, verse 9 makes descent the mode of what grows the interior. "Clear revelations," āyāt mubiynāt — the signs are characterized by clarity, not ambiguity, so that failure to believe can never be pinned on the evidence's obscurity, only on the will's refusal.

**"To His Servant": the Prophet's Chosen Title**

The recipient is named in the third person and with the humblest title in the language: ʿabd, servant. Not the messenger, not the beloved, not the intercessor — the servant, the same word the opening verses applied to everything in the heavens and the earth that glorifies. In a sūrah whose first line enrolled all creation in praise, the prophet stands as the chief example of the grammar, a creature doing creaturely things by commission. The theological payoff is anti-royal: authority in this community is delegated, never inherent, and the one who brings the light is himself under it. That single word quietly prepares the sūrah's later refusal of hierarchy by lineage or tenure — the early donor's rank over the late one (verse 10) is favor, not possession.

**From the Plural of Night to the Singular of Dawn**

In the Qur'an's idiom, the phrase "out of darkness into light" almost always keeps the darkness plural and the light singular, and this translation follows the Arabic exactly. The shape of that number is the whole sermon: error is many-hued — every delusion its own shade — while guidance is one. The same figure appears in Abraham's sūrah, where the Book's purpose is stated as leading "people out of darkness and into light, by the Will of their Lord." (14:1) The clause "by the Will of their Lord" is the companion here of "He is the One Who sends down": the exit is neither automatic nor self-caused. Light is given, darkness merely left, and the leaving must be renewed each day, which is why a community that has exited still needs a Messenger calling.

**Five Thresholds the Classical Commentators Numbered**

Later exegetes expanded the verse's single preposition into a ladder of passages: out of the darkness of sin into the light of repentance; of heedlessness into wakefulness; of selfish desire into purity; of the merely sensual into the spiritual; of ignorance into knowledge of God. Ibn ʿAjībah, whose enumeration this paraphrases, treats the verse as a summary of the entire path rather than a one-time conversion — the plural "darknesses" naming each stage the heart outgrows. That reading fits the sūrah's audience precisely: it addresses believers commanded to believe again (verse 28 will say the same), which means exit is iterative. The light of verse 12, running ahead of the believers on the Day, is then the final installment of the lights this verse promises in advance.

**Gracious, Merciful — and the Rebuke Repeated**

The verse closes the ground of belief with two names, raʾūf and raḥīm, gentleness and mercy, and prints at its end the question "Why Do You Not Donate?" — the second rebuke of the pair, its twin attached to verse 7 in this translation's division. The rhetorical movement is exact: from the covenant beneath you to the revelation among you to the mercy over you; three grounds for one summons, and still the pocket stays shut. Donation in this passage is not fundraising; it is the visible tense of the belief that all three grounds already compel. The next verse will make the wallet's theology explicit by naming God the inheritor of everything.

**"Clear Revelations": What āya Names**

The word translated "clear revelations" is āyāt — signs — in the plural and with the adjective mubināt, "making-distinct," from the root that means the sharp line between things. An āya in the Qur'an is never just a sentence; it is a marker that divides true from false the way a boundary stone divides fields. The same phrase, in the Book's telling, describes Moses's wonders — the signs "given" as proof to a court (17:101) — and scripture itself; the overlap is the point. Revelation here is not private inspiration or a literary event; it is the public endowment of a community with distinctions it can check. The sūrah's audience is thus given the least ambiguous possible basis for the faith it is being teased about: the signs are clear, the Messenger is present, and any obscurity left is in the recipient. That is why the same root returns at verse 17: "We have certainly made the signs clear for you so perhaps you will understand" — the passage on hearts will reopen the same claim about evidence.

**Pronoun Shifts: One Servant, Many Addressed**

Watch the sentence's persons. "He sends down… to His servant… in order that He may bring you out…" — third, third, second. Revelation descends on one human being and radiates to a community; nobody inherits the light, everyone must be brought out into it, personally and by name. The grammar also keeps the initiative where the sūrah has placed it from the first verse: God sends down, God brings out, God is "Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to you." The believer's part is to stop resisting a grace already moving. That is the meaning of the double question that brackets this verse on either side — why not believe, why not donate — because on this theology refusal is not an argument but a stillness, a refusal to walk toward what is already walking toward you. The darknesses, plural, stay behind only because they have no engine; the light does.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:10

> And why should you not spend in the cause of Allah, while Allah is the ˹sole˺ inheritor of the heavens and the earth? Those of you who donated and fought before the victory ˹over Mecca˺ are unparalleled. They are far greater in rank than those who donated and fought afterwards. Yet Allah has promised each a fine reward. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Inheritor Argument**

The second "why not" of the passage now targets the hand instead of the heart, and its evidence is an attribute of God: He is the inheritor of the heavens and the earth. Inheritance presupposes death of the owner — the title assumes that every current holder dies, and that what is "held" therefore lies, already legally, in an estate whose sole heir is named. The Qur'an elsewhere seals the same claim with the plainest possible verb sequence: "Surely it is We Who give life and cause death. And We are the ˹Eternal˺ Successor." (15:23) Spend what is His, the verse reasons, or decline and watch it pass to the same destination anyway; the only variable is whether the transfer earned anything. This is the most economical theology of wealth in the sūrah, and it converts stinginess from prudence into a legal misunderstanding.

**Before and After the Opening**

The sūrah draws a line through its own community at the conquest of Mecca in 8/630. "Those who spent and fought before the victory" — the Meccan years, the Hijra, Ḥudaybiyya, when adherence cost everything and returned nothing — hold a rank the latecomers cannot reach. The measure is not piety's total but cost's ratio: contribution made when the cause looked doomed carries weight no gold can equal later. The Prophet is reported to have set the proportion bluntly: a mountain of gold spent after would not match one donor's handful spent before, not even half of it. The report circulates in the commentaries without a single fixed number, and its force here is directional, not accounting — the sūrah's own text is the authority, and it says simply: not equal.

**Hierarchy Without Despair**

Immediately after the distinction, the verse closes the gap it opened: "Yet Allah has promised each a fine reward." The gradation is real — ranks differ — and the promise is universal for both parties. This double sentence is the sūrah's model for every later comparison of the first and the second generation: measure acts, do not measure people's end. The same pairing of favor and fairness governs the closest parallel in the Book, where those who stayed home are promised "that which is most beautiful" even as the strivers receive a great reward above them (4:95). Exegetes who worried the verse discourages latecomers misread the order of the clauses: the "yet" comes after the distinction for exactly this reason — hierarchy is stated last only after hope is secured.

**"The Fine Reward" — Ḥusnā, the Undefeatable Word**

The noun translated "a fine reward" is al-ḥusnā, the feminine superlative used across the Qur'an for what is best and unsurpassable, and for the ultimate itself — "for those who do good is the best (al-ḥusnā) and more besides." That choice is tact itself. The late donors are not consoled with a smaller version of the early reward; both are promised the same undifferentiated best. Within the sūrah, ḥusnā also quietly answers verse 7's "mighty reward" and verse 11's "honourable" one: the vocabulary of recompense multiplies while the thing promised stays one, which is itself a lesson about what reward language can and cannot carry.

**Knowledge as the Final Witness**

"And Allah is All-Aware of what you do" ends the verse, and its placement is the remedy for the specific sickness this distinction could breed. Where rank is published, the late risk envy and the early risk pride; the verse sends both feelings to a court no one else can see into, the court of ʿalīm, All-Aware, who weighs intentions the record cannot show. The sūrah's opening block has by now completed its circuit: cosmos glorifying (1), ownership (2), four names (3), throne and surveillance (4), return (5), chests (6), commands (7–9), and here — the first community's ledger, published with its two ranks and then immediately sealed with a line that says the true audit is private. The next verse leaves history for arithmetic: who will lend.

**"In the Cause of Allah": Spending as Travel**

The prepositional phrase attached to the verb of spending — fī sabīlillāh — turns the ledger into a road. Sabīl is the way, the path a traveler follows; giving "in Allah's cause" is therefore not tribute paid to a distant king but provision carried along a route whose terminus is named in verse 5: all matters return to Him. The idiom gathered, in the earliest Muslim usage, everything the community needed funded — expeditions, yes, but also the weak, the stranger, the cause itself — and the sūrah leaves it open on purpose. What matters is direction: the same coin, given to vanity, exits the road entirely. This one phrase, then, performs the sūrah's whole argument about money better than any statistic could: wealth is motion; what is not moving along the cause is stuck in a chest that God already sees through (verse 6). The inheritor clause supplies the map's end; the sabīl clause supplies the lane.

**The Permanent rebuke of the Late Arrival**

What does the verse want from its second generation? Not the impossible: it does not ask the post-conquest donors to be the pre-conquest ones — the sūrah itself says rank differs and promise does not. What it does, quietly, is install a permanent ceiling on religious pride. Any later age that measures itself only by the volume of its giving, institutions, and martyrdom-adjacent comforts is measured here by cost's ratio, not cash's. The first Muslims gave when the community looked like a target; the second gave when it looked like a future. Every age since has been the second, and the verse keeps the question alive: what does your giving cost you, and is there any risk in it at all? The closing "Allah is All-Aware of what you do" is thus the softest possible blade — the audit of motive, not method, is what ranks the ranks.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:11

> Who is it that will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply ˹many times over˺ for them, and they will have an honourable reward?

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Metaphor That Should Be Impossible**

The Qur'an asks for a loan from the One who owns everything — and the ask is the most startling commercial image in the sūrah. A loan requires that the lender possess what he lends and the borrower be able to repay; on this sūrah's own theology neither condition holds, since the wealth is already God's (verse 2) and the repayment comes from Him (verse 5's return). Every element of the figure therefore breaks under scrutiny except its purpose, and the purpose is dignity: God did not say "give," because giving positions the giver below; borrowing positions him as the one doing the favor. The same phrase appears as an open invitation in the Coward's sūrah elsewhere — "Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺." (2:245) — and there the borrowing verb is bracketed by the assertion that He controls both directions of wealth's flow, so the risk of the loan is nil.

**What Makes a Loan "Good"**

Commentators did not leave the adjective qaraḍ ḥasan, a "goodly loan," undefined, and the conditions they compiled read like a manual. Al-Rāzī's ten-point list is the fullest: the money must be lawfully earned; it must be the best of what one owns, not the offcuts, which is the plain sense of "O believers! Donate from the best of what you have earned." (2:267); one must give while loving what is given, since "You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish." (3:92); it must reach the most deserving; secrecy is preferred; no reminders of favor may follow the gift; the giver must not seek to be seen; the gift must be counted small however large it feels; and the giver must not think himself better than the recipient. Strip the list to its nerve and it says: the "goodness" of the loan is entirely about what the money does to the lender, not the borrower.

**Multiplication at Divine Rates**

The return on the loan is not interest — the word for it is ፍaʿḍ, doubling, and the sūrah explains the rate by parable elsewhere: "The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills." (2:261) Seven hundred, "and Allah multiplies even more" — the arithmetic is offered precisely in order to be abandoned mid-sentence; the promise is growth without a ceiling, which no loan agreement has ever contained. The verb "multiply" here also governs the sūrah's next appearance of the same figure (verse 18), where the doubling is promised to men and women equally, the one detail the commercial metaphor needed most to survive intact.

**"An Honourable Reward"**

The verse's final phrase, ajr karīm, a generous or noble reward, is worth weighing against the "mighty" reward of verse 7. Karīm in Arabic describes what arrives on the receiver's terms, unembarrassed and abundant; a karīm lord pays without reminding. For a loan, that is the perfect closing clause: the borrower's honor is protected twice — He borrowed from you, and He repays as a noble party repays, with a return that does not mention the debt. The reward is "honourable" in both senses: it is generous, and it dignifies. No other verse in this passage on spending says so much about the recipient's self-respect in ten words.

**A Question Form for a Voluntary Act**

"Why is it that no one will lend" is, grammatically, a question — the sūrah never converts it into an imperative — and the form is the last piece of the metaphor's ethics. A loan obtained by command is confiscation; the interrogative leaves the desk open and the decision unforced, the same courtesy that turned giving into borrowing. Within the sūrah's architecture the question also functions as a hinge: belief and spending have now been demanded (7), grounded in covenant and revelation (8–9), priced by inheritance (10), and offered as a divine credit operation (11); everything after this verse is about what the ledger looks like from the far side — the light running ahead of the donors on the Day, and the wall that shows who had none.

**Borrowing and Buying: Two Metaphors That Complete Each Other**

Elsewhere the same transaction is figured as a purchase: "Allah has indeed purchased from the believers their lives and wealth in exchange for Paradise." (9:111) A sale and a loan are opposite risk structures — in a sale, the buyer takes title and the seller's claim ends; in a loan, title stays with the lender and the borrower owes. The Book lets God hold both positions at once: He buys what is given, and He owes for what is lent. Theology through commercial metaphor never means to be literal, but the pairing of figures is itself a statement: in God's economy, surrender is rewarded as a sale and trust as a debt, and neither metaphor lets the giver stand empty-handed. The sūrah chooses the loan here because it is addressing people about to keep their money — a loan asks for returnable custody, not final title, which is a smaller ask and therefore a truer one for hearts still doing arithmetic.

**"Who Is It That Will…" — the Grammar of the Challenge**

The interrogative opening — man dhā alladhī, "who is it that will lend" — is one of the Qur'an's rare formulas, used of this loan in three other places (2:245; 64:17; 73:20), and always attached to a demand for expenditure. Rhetorically it functions as scarcity made audible: the question pretends to count hands, and every believer who does give knows the question was about him. There is also, in classical preaching, a note of urgency — God does not say "many will lend"; He asks, as if the desk were open and the line short. Read next to verse 10's account of first-generation donors, the formula sharpens: those who spent before the conquest are exactly the answer to this question in its first years, and everyone after is being asked the same question with the same short line, minus the danger. Honour, in the verse's final clause, is the reward the risk cannot buy later.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:12

> On that Day you will see believing men and women with their light shining ahead of them and on their right. ˹They will be told,˺ “Today you have good news of Gardens, under which rivers flow, ˹for you˺ to stay in forever. This is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph.”

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Day Seen From Inside**

The verse's construction is disorienting on purpose: "On that Day you will see" — the seeing happens on the Day it describes, so the reader is pulled into the crowd of spectators at the very moment being foretold. Two genders are named explicitly, believing men and believing women, which the sūrah will repeat verbatim in verse 18 for charity; this pairing marks one of the Qur'an's consistent refusals to let the reward for giving run through the male line alone. What is seen is not first a garden but a light, and the order matters. The landscape of the afterlife in this sūrah begins with illumination and only then moves to residence; the trees come after the torch.

**What the Light Is**

Exegetes did not settle on a single referent, and the disagreement is instructive. Some said it is faith itself, internal in this world and externalized on the Day — the chest's contents made visible, exactly the reversal verse 6 promised. Others said it is the light of good deeds, generated at the moment of their doing and stored. A third group took it literally: a radiance on faces and in hands. Al-Rāzī pressed the question to its end — that real light is God, and the light of knowledge the truest of all — reading the scene as proximity to the source rather than a luminous object. The majority practice was not to choose among these but to notice they share one feature: the light is earned, personal, and portable. Whoever held the trust of verse 7 in this life carries his own illumination into the next; no one can borrow it there.

**Ahead of Them and on Their Right**

The direction is not decorative. The record of deeds arrives in the right hand, and the right side is where the recipients of that scroll are gathered, so the light's geometry is the geometry of acquittal: in front, what the future holds; on the right, what the account shows. The same coordinates appear in the Prophet's taught supplication for the believers' exit from it all — "Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right." (66:8) — a duʿāʾ that asks not for the absence of darkness but for light within it, and adds the one petition that belongs in the scene: "Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us." (66:8) The completion requested there is the detail this verse omits: light can be given, and it can be perfected. Its size, tradition says, is proportional to the deeds that earned it.

**"Good News" Addressed to the Already Saved**

The speech act inside the verse — "Today you have good news" — answers a question the scene would otherwise leave open: who greets them? The glad tidings are delivered to men and women whose light is already blazing, which means the announcement is confirmation, not information. The Qur'an elsewhere reserves the phrase "the ultimate triumph" for exactly this register — forgiveness and Gardens in exchange for lives and wealth: "So rejoice in the exchange you have made with Him. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph." (9:111) The trading vocabulary is why this sūrah's economics mattered: a triumph is the closing of a contract, and the good news reads like a receipt.

**Eternity in Two Words**

"To stay in forever" — khulūd — is the verse's last line of defense against every version of afterlife as interim state. Gardens under which rivers flow is the oldest and most repeated of the Book's images, so much so that its plainness becomes its point: permanence, water, and shade in a scripture preached in a dry land promise precisely what the hearer's life lacked, and their ordinariness is the proof of their seriousness. The verse then names the whole transaction "triumph," falāḥ, the word the Qur'an keeps choosing for success in its widest sense — survival, victory, and salvation at once. On the far side of the wall of verse 13, the same word will be used in irony by those who asked to borrow what cannot be lent anymore.

**Men and Women, Said Twice**

The sūrah names believing men and believing women here and again at verse 18, where the subject is charity. That double specification is not style. In verses on abstract faith the Qur'an commonly uses the masculine default; on the verses about spending, this chapter deliberately spells both genders out, and it does so in the two places where reward is issued for money. The consequence for its first audience was concrete — women's trusts, dowries, and inheritance were live disputes in the Hijaz — and the consequence in the sūrah's argument is architectural: light at the end of a loan is not indexed to the household's male head. Within this passage the formula also echoes verse 7's plural "you," keeping the whole community inside the transaction; nobody in the assembly can hear the command as issued to somebody else's wallet. Whatever the market does with women's property, the ledger of lights, at the end of this sūrah, has their names written in the same ink.

**Two Botanics of the Beyond**

Note what this verse has not yet done: it has not described the Garden's architecture at all, only its hydrology — "under which rivers flow" — and its duration, "to stay in forever." Compare verse 20's portrait of the world: rain up above, plants down below, delight, then yellowing, then chaff. Paradise here is the same image inverted: the water moves from beneath, and nothing wilts. The Qur'an's Gardens are irrigated from below precisely because the world's gardens are watered from above; the source of life in the next home cannot be withheld by sky, season, or man. The triumph the verse calls "ultimate" is thus quiet and botanical — a river you do not look up at. And the verb of giving, "today you have good news," places even this on the Day itself: the gladness arrives from outside the self, spoken to them, the way the sūrah's whole opening block said that what matters most is always addressed before it is earned.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:13

> On that Day hypocrite men and women will beg the believers, “Wait for us so that we may have some of your light.” It will be said ˹mockingly˺, “Go back ˹to the world˺ and seek a light ˹there˺!” Then a ˹separating˺ wall with a gate will be erected between them. On the near side will be grace and on the far side will be torment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Request to Borrow What Was Never Lent**

The hypocrites' petition — wait for us, let us borrow from your light — is the verse's whole theology in one verb. The word for borrow, iqtibās, is the one used for lighting one lamp at another, and it assumes the believers own surplus. That assumption fails twice over. The light is not property but record: it was manufactured by the deeds the hypocrites declined to do, and it belongs to the chests God read at verse 6, not to hands that could open or close it. Nothing is being withheld from them; there is simply nothing they can carry over. The scene is merciless because it is accurate — the request arrives one moment too late, at the only border where the earned and the unearned can finally be told apart.

**"Turn Back and Look for Light"**

The reply — turn around and look for a light — is delivered with the Book's customary savage politeness. Some exegetes take it as direction to the place where the believers first got their light: return to the source of revelation, the teaching, the covenant, and see if you can earn what you asked to borrow. Others hear it as dismissal to the world they abandoned — go back to the life where lights are made, and note that the road home from the Day is closed. Both readings converge on the deadline logic of verse 14: the time for seeking was the span before the Command came. The mockery is not divine cruelty added to the scene; the scene is what the choice looked like from the other side, and the sentence merely names the closed door they themselves built.

**The Wall, Its Gate, and the Early Commentary on 7:46**

A wall with a gate is erected between the parties — ṣakhra in one reading, the barrier in the other. Mujāhid, among the oldest exegetes, identified this barrier with the veil mentioned in al-Aʿrāf: "There will be a barrier between Paradise and Hell." (7:46) The identification is worth keeping: on the heights of that barrier stand people who recognize everyone by their marks — the same intermediate vision the hypocrites of this verse get, able to see the mercy they cannot enter. The gate in an otherwise seamless wall is the detail that turns geography into irony: an opening exists, and it faces away from them. Separation is not enforced by thickness but by direction; they stand on the far side of a door that was built, in this sūrah's account, out of their own un-borrowable minutes.

**Inside Mercy, Outside Labor**

The translation says grace lies on the near side and torment on the far one; the Arabic pairs raḥma and ʿadhāb — the two words the opening block used of God's names in verses 2 and 9. The sūrah has now distributed them geographically. Read back into the body of the chapter, the wall is the final version of verse 6's claim about chests: what was hidden is now placed. The hypocrite lived religion as an exterior, and the Day exteriorizes his interior into architecture — mercy with an inside, torment with an outside, and the wall is simply the truth of a chest, installed at grade level. This is why the scene is about placement rather than punishment; the torment does not need to pursue anyone, and the mercy does not need to be unlocked. Each side is already where its inhabitants chose to live.

**What May Still Pass Through the Gate**

That a barrier of judgment has a door at all has not escaped the commentators, and some read the gate as the sūrah's withheld mercy — evidence that separation is not yet annihilation, and that the affair (amr) of verse 5 has not fully returned while the door stands. Long eschatological traditions describe a final man turned away from the Fire toward the Garden and admitted by a God who keeps converting His own deadline into rescue; this sūrah does not narrate that hope, and its gate stays a gate, not a promise — but the grammar will not let the reader close it either. [UNVERIFIED: the "last man out of the Fire" intercession tradition — wording and numbering vary across the Ṣaḥīḥ collections and were not re-checked this session, so it is referenced generally, not cited.] The dialogue continues on the far side of the wall.

**The Divine Passive and the Absent Executioner**

It will be said to them: turn back. The sūrah does not say by whom the reply is delivered — the believers, the angels, God Himself — and the passive voice is a deliberate mercy even in a sentence of rejection. In the Qur'an's eschatological dialogues the damned are habitually answered by voices that are present, not by the throne directly: the companions of the Fire are addressed by the keepers of Gehenna, by their former allies, by their own limbs in the chapter that catalogues excuses (40:47–48), and here by whoever stands at the gate. The effect is that the sentence reads as the natural consequence of the position taken, not as an act of spite; nobody has to be angry at the hypocrites for them to be outside. The sūrah's God does not chase anyone across the wall. The wall is what refusing the light builds, and the reply is what building it sounds like when it is finally heard from the inside.

**Light Is the Only Currency That Cannot Be Lent**

Every other asset in this sūrah can be transferred — wealth moves from hand to hand at God's command, rank accrues to the early at the expense of nobody's claim, and even the credit of a goodly loan passes, doubled, back to the lender. Light refuses the mechanism. The verse stages the refusal precisely in the hypocrites' chosen idiom — they ask to borrow, the commercial verb the whole chapter has been celebrating, and for once the answer is no. The economy of the Day has converted back into an economy of nature: light is to faith what ripeness is to fruit, transferable in description only. That is the sūrah's final word to the wait-and-see strategy, which had always assumed the community was an asset whose yield could be picked up at the end without holding it through the season. The wall with a gate is what happens when the last negotiation fails.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:14

> The tormented will cry out to those graced, “Were we not with you?” They will reply, “Yes ˹you were˺. But you chose to be tempted ˹by hypocrisy˺, ˹eagerly˺ awaited ˹our demise˺, doubted ˹the truth˺, and were deluded by false hopes until Allah’s decree ˹of your death˺ came to pass. And ˹so˺ the Chief Deceiver deceived you about Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Credential of Proximity**

"Were we not with you?" is the argument of the co-attendant: we stood in your rows, marched in your lines, bled at your sides — so share the payout. The verse does not deny the premise. The reply is "Yes" — you were — and the sūrah refuses the comfortable heresy that association with the righteous earns their wages. Presence was real, and it was everything the hypocrite had; that is precisely the indictment. The Book makes this claim elsewhere in the same terms, quoting the men who waited to see which way the wind blew and then claimed a seat in both victories: "So if Allah grants you victory, they say ˹to you˺, “Were we not on your side?”" (4:141) Attendance is cheap in this economy; the wall between the two camps exists because the sūrah's God has priced companionship by chest, not by coordinates.

**Three Verbs That Autopsy a Hypocrisy**

The reply then names the disease in a triad, and the sequence is a biography. First, "you chose to be tempted" — fatana, to test, reflexive here: you tested yourselves, handled the fire and got burned by design, or brought upon yourselves the trial you are now suffering for failing. Second, "you waited" — tarabbuṣ, the posture of the lookout: sitting on a wall, watching to see whether the community would fall so the transaction could be renegotiated. Third, "you doubted" —irtab, plain unbelief that never committed to its own case. Temptation, wait-and-see, doubt: the three tenses of a soul that would not decide — it tried the truth, delayed at the door, and finally refused to enter. Nothing in the list is a sin the audience has not flirted with, which is why the verse continues to function after the hypocrites named in its first audience had all died.

**The Deluder, or the Delusion**

The last phrase of the reply carries a famous ambiguity. Read with one vowel as "the Deceiver" (gharūr), it is Satan — the chief liar whose trade is to make God's forbearance look like approval. Read with another as "delusion" (gharūr), it is the substance of wishful thinking itself: the man who sins and counts on amnesty, the community that hears "Most Merciful" and files the warning. Both readings are in the apparatus, and the sūrah rewards whoever keeps both, because the sentence works exactly like the mechanism it describes: the external tempter and the internal alibi are never found apart. The Qur'an uses the same formula as a warning to all humanity: "So do not let the life of this world deceive you, nor let the Chief Deceiver deceive you about Allah." (35:5) — deception "about Allah" is the one sin against God directly, a false report filed about His character.

**"Until the Command Came"**

The deadline idiom of the sūrah arrives in a single clause: until the command of your Lord came. Amr here means the decree — in practice, death, in the larger reading, the Hour — and the choice of the word is a last grace, because "affairs" (umūr) were said at verse 5 to be returning to God already. The deadline is not an alarm switched on late; it is the point at which the universal return becomes a personal one. This is why the sūrah's hypocrites cannot fix their paperwork on the far side of the wall: the command came while they were still mid-argument with it. The phrase is also the standard for the entire scene's horror — not that they were damned, but that time ran out in the middle of their postponement, the only event the wait-and-see man cannot survive by strategy.

**How the Believers Answer, and Why It Matters**

Notice the manners of the reply: they do not mock, they fact-check. "Yes, you were — but…" They confirm what is true about their former companions and decline only the inference, and this is the sūrah teaching its whole audience, in the past tense, the tone it demanded in the present: the truthful need not exaggerate against anyone, not even the lost. The grammar sharpens it. "You were" — kuntu — is the past of coexistence; "you chose, you waited, you doubted" — all past too, all active, all second person. The verbs belong to the hypocrites. God is not said to have deceived them; the Deceiver did, and the tenses distribute responsibility cleanly, so that the verdict reads less like a sentence than a receipt. The final verse of this unit takes the receipt to its end.

**"But" — the Small Word That Ends the Argument**

The reply pivots on a single particle. Yes, you were with us — but. Classical Arabic rhetoric calls the adversative lākin a tool of exemption: it concedes the premise and severs the inference at the same instant, which is exactly what the scene requires. The hypocrites' case had one fact and no argument; the answer grants the fact entirely and lets the argument die of exposure. After the "but," the sūrah fills the sentence with the men's own verbs — you tested, you waited, you doubted, you were deluded — and the rhythm does the theology: four active pasts against one nominal concession ("you were with us"). Presence is a state; states are shared. Action is the only thing that compounds into a wage, and it was all theirs. The verse is thus also a lesson in how the truthful answer: never deny the opponent's facts, only the conclusion he hoped nobody would look for in them. The same distinction between co-presence and co-accounting will harden into architecture in the next verse's wall.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:15

> So Today no ransom will be accepted from you ˹hypocrites˺, nor from the disbelievers. Your home is the Fire—it is the ˹only˺ fitting place for you. What an evil destination!"

**Expanded Commentary**

**When Ransom Stops Being a Price**

The first clause closes the market. Fidyah — ransom, compensation, the substitution that in this world still functions: repentance trades one thing for another, restitution repairs what was broken, and even God's own economy, in the sūrah's earlier verse, allowed a loan to be repaid with interest. "No ransom will be accepted" is therefore not a refusal of money; it is the end of the era in which anything could be exchanged at all. The commentaries on the ransom-verses elsewhere put the same point against the grave: no one whose day is over can pay. The exchange the whole sūrah exists to arrange — believe, donate, lend — is offered precisely because today the price still moves. Verse 15 is the picture of the counter closed, and it is worth reading as the reason, backwards, for every command in the chapter.

**"Nor from the Disbelievers" — the Collapse of a Social World**

The parenthesis makes the sūrah's most uncomfortable equation: the hypocrite and the open denier are now answered in the same breath, on the same terms. Nothing could be further from the chapter's earlier behavior, which kept the categories of Medina painfully distinct — ranks of donors, ranks of fighters, the wall of the preceding verses raised between two camps that attended the same funerals and markets. Here the distinction is retained and then voided: the hypocrites are addressed directly, "from you," and only then joined, "nor from them." The social world they tried to live in twice — inside and outside at once — is ended by a verdict that treats the outside as the only address that survived them. Their punishment, the sūrah implies, includes the loss of their own cleverness.

**"Your Patron Is the Fire" — the Mawlā Wordplay**

The Arabic behind "the fitting place" is awlā — the word that means most deserving, nearest, and most fitting at once, from the same root as mawlā, patron and client, master and next of kin. The sentence therefore says more than the translation lets out: the Fire is their mawlā. What they made their protection in this life — concealment, the party of the disbelievers, the safety of the outward show — is now the one patron that has no exit. Elsewhere the same grammar of fittingness describes the human being's relation to what he fed and trusted; here the reciprocity is total. The verse's courtesy is terrible: the Fire is described as "suitable," as the job that fits the applicant. Nothing arbitrary is left in the image; damnation in this sūrah reads like hiring.

**"What an Evil Destination" — the Idiom of the Verdict**

The closing exclamation — biʾsa 'l-maṣīr, evil the destination — is the Qur'an's standard formula of sentence, the same construction used for every failed alliance and false god in the Book, and the pair to its opposite: niʿma, "excellent is the." Rhetorically the idiom is doing something specific. A judgment stated as proverb — as what everyone already knew about such outcomes — has no drama left, and the sūrah has finished the drama in verse 14; what is left is the settling of an account against a maxim. The word maṣīr, destination, comes from the root of return used at verse 5: "to Allah all matters are returned." Every affair arrives; the quality of arrival was decided before the arrival. The hypocrites' destination is not imposed on their return — it is their return.

**The Structure of the Four-Day Sequence**

Verses 12 through 15 are built as a single descent, and the verse you have just read is its floor. Verse 12 set the scene and handed the triumph to the owners of light; 13 introduced the borrowing that failed and the wall; 14 let the two sides speak and defined the three tenses of hypocrisy; 15 removes every remaining lever — ransom, patronage, exit — and answers with an idiom that has no conditions attached. The sequence's verbs slow from seeing to speaking to being told, and then stop. There is no verse 16 of dialogue at the wall; what follows, abruptly, is a command addressed to the believers' hearts. That break is the point: the far side is now unaddressable, and the sūrah turns, mid-chapter, to the side where words can still land.

**The Ransom the Qur'an Always Refused**

"No ransom will be accepted" is not this sūrah's invention but the refrain of an entire Book-wide settlement about the Day's economy, and hearing the echo here sharpens the verse. The Cow that would be ransomed from the Fire by a lifetime on earth (2:96), the wrongdoer who would offer all the earth's gold at the drowning moment (10:54), the intercession and compensation both barred for the guilty (2:123, 2:254) — the Qur'an runs one consistent line across the legal language of blood-money and war-capture and applies it eschatologically: the Day has no fidyā because it has no second-hand goods, and no creditor who can be satisfied by anything the bankrupt party still holds. Verse 15 closes exactly that door, mid-sentence: "no ransom will be accepted from you, nor from those who disbelieved." What is left, in the market that failed, is only what verse 11 bought while currency was valid — a goodly loan, repaid with honor by the only lender who could guarantee a return after the exchange desk shut.

**The Two "Todays"**

The passage keeps saying "Today," and it is worth hearing how many verdicts the word is asked to carry. Verse 12 put it in the mouth of the good news: today, the garden, forever. Verse 15 puts it on the ransom: today, nothing accepted from you, forever. The Day's tense is a single instant stretched over both announcements, and the sūrah is not being repetitive — this Book's most careful eschatology is its vocabulary for time. "Today" here has no tomorrow beside it, which is exactly why the ransom fails and the gladness holds: both are payments settled in a currency that no longer circulates. The reader of 57:15 is meant to feel the adjective the saved got and the denied did not: their "today" came with news addressed to them personally. The hypocrites' "today" comes as a closed desk. Verse 14's deadline had made this inevitable; verse 15 makes it permanent — and the chapter then turns, mid-breath, from their today to the believers' hour.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:16

> Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah and what has been revealed of the truth, and not be like those given the Scripture before—˹those˺ who were spoiled for so long that their hearts became hardened. And many of them are ˹still˺ rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Most Frightening Verse in a Community's File**

"Has the time not yet come" is astonishment again, but turned against the inside of the congregation, and the sūrah preserves for it a place in the earliest accounts of the Muslim conscience. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said that only four years had passed between this community's entry into Islam and the arrival of this verse — that is, the rebuke came before the first generation could settle. It is a datum the chapter's own logic supplies: the wall of verse 13 and the dialogue of verse 14 are not propaganda about hypocrites of old; verse 16 announces that the same failure mode is live in believing hearts, only years from its first symptom. A sūrah that has just closed the ransom market for one party now reopens the file on the other, and does it with a question.

**Khāshiʿīn: The Physics of a Soft Heart**

The verb is takhshūʿ, to be humbled, and its root describes things that submit visibly — land that slumps, eyes that drop, the body going still in prayer. The verse wants the heart's humility to be as observable as that: the remembrance of Allah and "the truth that has come down" must produce a bodily fact, not a mood. Islamic ethics made khushūʿ the master virtue for this reason; a devotion without it is a body at prayer and a heart at market. The negative proof stands across the chapter's whole argument about money: he whose heart humbles at the remembrance of Allah can open his hand at the same motion, because both are the same capacity — being genuinely addressed by something outside yourself. The hardened man cannot be addressed at all; he can only be managed, and the sūrah will spend its remaining verses on the one Person who can.

**The Case of the Long-Spoiled**

The comparison is drawn from the same sūrah's own catalog of failure — "those given the Scripture before." Their sequence is exact: long duration, then hardness. It is a diagnosis with a mechanism. A community that lives generations past its founding encounter reads the memory as description rather than demand; the commands age into custom, the custom into identity, and identity makes the remembrance of Allah a phrase, "the truth sent down" an inheritance. The verse names no party and needs none: every scripture that outlives its first disciples is a candidate, and the sūrah's realism about its own community is proven by the clause appended at the end — "many of them are rebellious" — the same verdict this chapter will deliver, word for word, against the followers of Noah, Abraham and Jesus in its closing pages.

**Hardness in the Qur'an's Anatomy**

The Book's psychology of the heart is a clinic, and hardening is only one of its ward names. The Qur'an's images for the diseased organ travel across several figures at once: a covering over the eye rather than in it (22:46), a sickness that fluctuates (2:10), a pigment laid down sin by sin until the surface loses reflectivity (83:14), a seal whose key was thrown with the act, padlocks on the doors of entry (47:24). What the figures share is that each leaves the heart active — stained things still stain, locked rooms still have tenants. Hardness is the sūrah's chosen word because it names the one state in which the organ can no longer be soft at all, and the sūrah's cure matches: not information, but remembrance, the repeated wet touch of the Name upon the surface. Isaiah's prayer against this very fate — that God would un-harden a heart that has strayed, turn back and repair the inheritance — is quoted in the Hebrew Bible almost as the Qur'an phrases it here (Isaiah 63:17), the same complaint from the inside rather than the verdict from outside.

**The Verse That Redemocratizes the Warning**

Who is the rebuke for, exactly? Some exegetes, reading the context of exposure, assign it to the hypocrites — hearts that performed belief while inwardly hard. The majority, and the plain opening words, take it for the believers: "for those who believe, that their hearts should humble." The reason to prefer the majority is internal to this sūrah. Its whole design has been to place the line between chests and rows inside every reader: verse 6 said God knows what chests contain, verse 13 built a wall out of that knowledge, verses 7–11 tested the wallet, not the attendance list. A chapter organized by interiority would lose its point if its final summons to softness were addressed to somebody else. "O you who believe" is the vocative this chapter will still use in its last verses; here it is used first.

**Four Years, or Thirteen: When the Rebuke Landed**

The reports about this verse's timing disagree, and the disagreement is about the community, not the text. One tradition makes ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd date the rebuke at four years from the group's conversion to Islam — the chapter on hearts arriving while the first generation was still green. Another, traced to Ibn ʿAbbās, places it later: the believers' hearts had cooled, the report says, and the reproach came at the beginning of the thirteenth year of the mission. The four-year version is the more widely cited and the more uncomfortable, and it is also the one the sūrah's structure prefers: a community that needed thirteen years to start slipping would have had time to hear the wall of verses 12–15 and build its own; at four years the rebuke is preventive, delivered before the hardening the comparison to older scriptures describes has set in. Either dating teaches the same lesson about revelation's timing in this Book — the summons arrives while the soil can still be worked, which is why being reproached is still mercy. What both reports assume, and no dating can disturb, is the verse's own claim: for the believing heart, the time for humility is always short by one day.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:17

> Know that Allah revives the earth after its death. We have certainly made the signs clear for you so perhaps you will understand.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Verse, Two Arguments, One Soil**

The imperative "know" introduces the sūrah's most compressed piece of evidence. "Allah revives the earth after its death" is at once a proof of resurrection — the dead land is brought back to green each year, and the God of that cycle is the God of the grave — and a parable for the heart just discussed: verse 16 asked whether hearts can humble, and verse 17 answers with the only soil the audience farmed, saying yes, by the same act that wakes the seed. The two arguments share a mechanism: neither the earth nor the heart reforms itself; each needs something to come down onto it, rain from the sky, remembrance and revelation (verse 16's two ingredients), and each returns to life as an act of another. The sūrah moves from rebuke to this line without a seam because it was already making the seasonal reading of its own rebuke.

**The Book's Standing Proof**

The dead-earth argument is fixed Qur'anic property, and its wording here is only one instance of a formula: "There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from them to eat." (36:33) The verse pairs its own signs with the same claim: "We have certainly made the signs clear for you so perhaps you will understand." What the formula shows, by repetition, is that resurrection in the Qur'an is never argued from metaphysics alone — it is argued from agriculture, from the water cycle, from the annual resurrection everyone had watched and nobody had interpreted. That is also the sūrah's rebuke to hard hearts: the signs are not scarce, they are seasonal, and a community that sees the green shoot each spring and draws nothing from it has not missed the evidence, only the analogy. Understanding is offered, not imposed — the purpose clause says "so perhaps," the Qur'an's modest grammar of hope.

**The Living and the Dead in the Commentators' Ear**

Early teachers heard verse 17's pairing of life and death in two registers at once, and one of the Prophet's most-quoted similes confirms the ear was right: "The likeness of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is like the living and the dead." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6407) Remembrance is what the heart drinks; the dead land and the dry heart share one diagnosis. Read beside verse 16, the hadith supplies the missing clause: the verse asks "has the time not come for hearts to humble," and the simile answers that the difference between a soft and a hard heart is the difference between life and death — no third state, no intermediate condition of "functional but fallow." The sūrah itself, remember, had called God the Giver of life and the Causer of death in verse 2. Verse 17 lets that name do its work on the reader's chest.

**Why This Small Verse Sits at the Chapter's Hinge**

Structurally, verse 17 is the pivot of the second half. Before it: the light, the wall, the verdict, the rebuke — everything addressed to what is coming and what failed. After it: the doubled payment for charity (18), the truthful and the martyrs (19), the five words for the world's amusement (20), the race to a Garden as wide as the heavens (21). The hinge turns on the one image the sūrah has now twice used — revival — to change the reader's relation to both halves: the scenes of verses 12–15 belong to the dead earth of verse 20's parable (rain, delight, yellowing, chaff), while the Garden of verse 21 is the revived one, irrigated from below (verse 12's rivers). "Know that Allah revives the earth after its death" is thus the sūrah's quiet center of gravity: everything it will say about money, boast, and balance in the next eight verses hangs on the claim that revival is a divine present tense, not a future hope.

**From the Giver of Life to the Revived Earth: a Chapter in a Circle**

Put verse 17 where the sūrah's own architecture puts it: at the chapter's center, and it closes the circle verse 2 opened. "He gives life and causes death" (2) was cosmology — the claim about the one act that makes two verbs God's alone. "Allah revives the earth after its death" (17) is the same claim delivered as soil, with the seasons doing the arguing, and the two verses' pairing frames everything between them: the commands to give (7–11), the light and the wall (12–15), the rebuke to the believing heart (16). Between opening creed and centering parable, the dead-land image quietly re-describes the sūrah's two failures — the hypocrites as land that was never seeded, the hardened believers as land that has gone compact — and its one cure — revival, which the grammar reserves to God in both halves of the circle. No one in the chapter can do what verse 17 says. That is the argument the wallet could not make for itself.

**"Perhaps You Will Understand": the Qur'an's Modest Grammar of Thought**

The purpose clause at the end of the verse — so perhaps you will understand, laʿalla taʿqilūn — uses the Book's standard verb of reasoning, from the root of holding back and tethering: ʿaql, to restrain a camel, to hold a thing steady enough to use. Qur'anic "understanding" is therefore less about comprehension than about navigation — not grasping the argument but being stopped by it — and the modest particle "perhaps" keeps the sūrah honest about what signs can do: they make the road visible, they do not walk it. Compare the sūrah's own verbs in the same block: He makes the signs clear, He explains, He sends down — divine verbs of disclosure in the indicative; human verbs of response in the subjunctive of hope. The asymmetry is the whole theology of evidence this chapter carries into its later scenes: the wall in verse 13 divides people who saw the same signs, and the only difference the record admits is which side the "perhaps" became.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:18

> Indeed, those men and women who give in charity and lend to Allah a good loan will have it multiplied for them, and they will have an honourable reward.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Charity and Truth in the Same Word**

The participle translated "those who give in charity" is muṣaddiqīn, and Arabic will not let the sūrah's readers keep it in one sense: the same form means both "givers of ṣadaqa" and "verifiers of the truth." Charity is called by the name of sincerity because, in this Book's ledger, it is the proof of sincerity — the one act of worship that costs what the chest loves. The sūrah exploits the pun structurally: this verse pays the givers (muṣaddiqūn), the next verse names the truthful (ṣiddīqūn) as a class beside the witnesses, and the chain of reward runs through the root without ever separating money from confession. A donation that is not truth-verification is, on this vocabulary, not charity; a truth-verification that keeps its wallet shut is, on the same chapter's repeated demand, not believed. The two readings of the word are not variants; they are one statement about a community in which speech and wealth must match.

**The Loan Formula, One Verse Shorter This Time**

"Lend to Allah a good loan" returns verbatim from verse 11 — qaraḍan ḥasanan — and its reappearance now completed is the verse's contribution: back in 11 the question was who would come forward; here the payment is settled, and to both sexes named in the same breath. The multiplier is God's, not the market's: "The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills." (2:261) The parable's arithmetic (seven hundred, "and more") matches this verse's verb yuḍāʿif — doubling in installments — and both keep the rate open-ended on purpose: the sūrah wants the reader to stop calculating at the second sentence. What stops being calculated starts being trusted.

**"An Honourable Reward," or the Courtesy of Payment**

The adjective karīm on the reward — "generous," "noble," "honourable" — repeats the same closing epithet as verse 11, and its placement at the end of a verse about charity is one of the Qur'an's small masterpieces of etiquette. A karīm repayment does not shame: it arrives as favor on the giver's terms, not as charity back to the supplicant; the root also describes a soil that yields without being manured, a horse that runs without the spur. Read against this sūrah's earlier anatomy of the miser (verse 24 — the man who withholds and enjoins withholding), the phrase dismantles the fear on which hoarding feeds: the one who receives your loan pays it honorably, which means without reproach, without reminder, and with increase — the exact manner the sūrah demands of human lenders in return. The Book that borrows teaches the terms of credit by using them first.

**A Verse Built as the Mirror of the Whole Chapter**

This verse is small and central, and the sūrah's shape uses it as a mirror. Its first half — men and women giving and lending — replays verses 7–11: the two commands, the goodly loan, the mighty reward. Its second half — multiplication and the karīm wage — anticipates 21's race to forgiveness and verse 28's double share of mercy, both of which are pure grace language with nothing calculated left in them. Between the two halves sits the only theology of wealth the chapter will finally defend: give out of what you love, let God fix the rate, accept honor instead of the ledger. The women and men named in this verse have, by the sūrah's own sequence, already heard the wall built (13), the ransom closed (15), and the hearts' rebuke (16); verse 18 is what remains movable. It is addressed in the indicative mood — "their reward and their light" (19) keeps going in the same tense — because by now the chapter has finished threatening. What it pays, it pays as a promise.

**A Reward That Is, Not a Reward That Happens**

The Arabic of the closing clause says "for them is a reward noble" — no verb, a bare prepositional sentence — and the grammar deserves the small attention the sūrah's ear always repays. Nominal sentences in Arabic carry the sense of permanence; "it is being multiplied" is eventful, while "theirs is an honourable reward" presents the wage as a standing fact, true whether or not anyone is watching it arrive. The two halves of the verse therefore hold their tense like the two accounts in the sūrah's own bookkeeping: the deposit grows (an act, in God's hand), the balance is (a state, in theirs). It is also a tact: the givers are not told when, how much, or in what order — a karīm payer does not need to specify, and the verse that opened with the most common verb in the Book closes with the quietest clause in it. The chapter has now said everything it needs about paying. What remains, in the next verses, is who else is on the ledger: the truthful, the martyrs, and the residents of a fire that pays no one.

**Why Both Genders, Again — the Legal Ear**

Commentators noted that the sūrah, which elsewhere addresses the assembly in the masculine plural's default, spells out "men who give charity and women who give charity" twice (12, 18) precisely at the two verses that pay. The specification answers the economics the chapter is restructuring: in the Prophet's community the richest donors were women — Khadija's estate funded the first decade of the mission — and no theology of the goodly loan could tolerate a ceiling on who may lend. The doubled formula also completes the sūrah's arithmetic of rank from verse 10: precedence there belonged to whoever spent, regardless of the order of arrival; precedence nowhere in these verses turns on the sex of the hand. The reward, the light, the multiplication, and the honour — the chapter's four payments — are addressed to "for them," and the "them" is deliberately, in its own idiom, everyone named.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:19

> ˹As for˺ those who believe in Allah and His messengers, it is they who are ˹truly˺ the people of truth. And the martyrs, with their Lord, will have their reward and their light. But ˹as for˺ those who disbelieve and reject Our signs, it is they who will be the residents of the Hellfire.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Plural That Widens the Door**

Through verses 7, 8, and 28 the sūrah has said "Allah and His Messenger," singular — the address of this community to its own prophet. Here, and again at verse 21, the formula switches to "His messengers," plural, and the switch is the verse's first piece of argument. Whoever believes in all of them is counted among the truthful; whoever believes in God while rejecting one of His messengers has, on the Qur'an's own logic, mis-believed in the message as delivered (2:285). The plural also widens the audience at the exact moment the sūrah is paying out reward: this class is not the earliest donors of verse 10, not the martyrs alone, not the miracle-workers — it is everyone who affirmed the whole chain, which in the Hijaz included Jews and Christians for whom belief in the messengers was already the shape of their faith. The verse recruits them by its grammar before verse 28 recruits them by name.

**"It Is They Who Are the Truthful" — Restriction in Two Words**

The Arabic ulāʾika humu ṣ-ṣiddīqūn — "those, they are the truthful" — is a clause of exclusivity, and its effect is to transfer a title the community reserved for a few. Ṣiddīq is the intensive of ṣādiq, truthful in word, deed, and heart at once; in the sūrah's own idiom the giver of ṣadaqa (charity, verse 18) and the verifier of truth share one root, and now the two senses fuse into a single class label. An early tradition from Mujāhid takes the verse at full width — every believer belongs among the truthful and the witnesses — and the context supports it: the sūrah has just said (18) that the charitable are the truthful, and says here that the believing are. The minority reading reserves the class for believers of extraordinary consistency is weaker here: the verse conditions nothing on the exclusivity except belief in Allah and His messengers, and a sūrah closing in on "the people of the Book" would not shrink the title at the peak of its generosity.

**Witnesses — and What Their Testimony Costs**

The word shuhadāʾ stands at the center of the verse's old syntax argument. Read it into the first clause and the witnesses are the same truthful believers, attaining the rank of testimony by consistency; read it as the subject of what follows and the sentence means: the witnesses — the martyrs who sealed their witness with blood — have, with their Lord, their reward and their light. Both parsings are classical. The second has the harder grammar against it and the fuller honor attached to it: shahīd in this usage means one whose death is his testimony, and the same root gives the covenant-word in 7:172, where creation testified before being created. The first parsing keeps the verse democratic — martyrdom is not required for the class — and the sūrah's whole behavior at verse 10 (rank among donors, promise to all) says the chapter can grant rank and withhold despair at the same time. Nothing forces a choice; the ambiguity itself is the policy.

**"With Their Lord" and the Payments Already Made**

The martyrs' portion is described with a preposition of presence — ʿinda rabbihim, "at their Lord" — before any reward is named, which reverses the order of interest: the wage is secondary to the address. Their pay, then, is borrowed from the sūrah's own earlier ledger: "their reward and their light" — the karīm reward of verses 11 and 18, the light of verse 12. That reuse is the structural point of this verse. Everything paid on the Day in this chapter has already been earned in it by belief and spending, and verse 19 now collapses the timeline, giving the class its due in a present-tense nominal clause: theirs is, and is, and is. The sūrah that opened by saying the whole cosmos glorifies without waiting for payment has, at its midpoint, the same tense for the servant: the reward is not a future event; it is a standing account, already credited to those who kept faith in the messengers.

**The Mirror Clause: Denying Signs After the Signs Were Cleared**

The verse's closing sentence is its verdict, and its charge is specific: those who disbelieve and "reject Our signs." The first-person plural Majesty appears for the third verse running (revived earth, "We have made the signs clear," "Our signs"), and the sequence is a trial transcript: God displays, explains, then indicts the refusal. To reject the āyāt is not, in this sūrah's court, failure to find evidence — verse 17's parable of the rain and the revived soil is the evidence — it is reading the display and declining the inference. The "companions of the Blaze" that results is named by its quality, jahīm, blazing: the root describes fire that reaches its prey, and the sūrah's own earlier words for the hypocrites' quarter ("its outside is torment") belong to the same vocabulary. Presence in the row of the saved, denial among the signs' clearers — the two ends of the chapter's social science, and the heart's rebuke (16) is the only bridge between them.

**Four Ranks, Two of Them Borrowed**

The verse assembles a ladder — prophets, the truthful, witnesses, and the righteous — that appears complete in only one other place, at the end of the Women's chapter, where whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger is promised company with that company (4:69). This sūrah takes the two middle rungs for its own sentence and drops the top and bottom, and the omission tells you what the verse is about. It is not promoting supererogatory saints; it is showing that ordinary belief, stated plainly at verses 7–9 as the chapter's demand, already qualifies a person for the class of ṣiddīqūn. The four-rank list elsewhere is the reward of obedience; here the two middle ranks are the condition and the class in one. Read against that borrowing, the martyrs' clause that follows — "with their Lord, their reward and their light" — is the sūrah's way of adding, to its generous minimum, a rank that cannot be earned by planning. Belief is the door, witness is the cost, and both are addressed to the same assembly, which is precisely how a community being reorganized around money is kept from organizing itself instead around holier-than-thou arithmetic.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:20

> Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children. This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff. And in the Hereafter there will be either severe punishment or forgiveness and pleasure of Allah, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Five Words, Ascending, for One Disease**

The verse dissects the life we share with five terms, and the order is the argument. Laʿb, play: activity done for its own exercise, abandoned without residue. Lahw, amusement: the root means turning aside — attention diverted from what was waiting for it. Zīna, adornment: beauty that exists to be seen, value relocated to the surface. Takābur, mutual boasting: from kibr, and now plural and reciprocal — pride outsourced to the crowd. Tanāfus fī al-amwāl wa-l-awlād, rivalry in increase: the market's own verb, competing over the two ledgers that never balance, capital and heirs. Play is solitary; amusement needs an audience; boasting and rivalry need rivals. The five words walk the reader from an idle hour to a civilization organized around accumulation, in the space of a breath, and then the sūrah hands the whole sequence to a farmer's almanac.

**The Parable of the Crop, and What the Water Was For**

Rain falls, vegetation delights the sowers, then yellowing, then the dry rattle of chaff — the same hydrology that proved revival in verse 17 now proves transience, and the precision is in what changed: nothing about the water. The identical blessing is, in one telling, God reviving dead earth and, in this one, the world deceiving the living. The difference was never in the rain but in what grew from it and who was watching it die. The sūrah elsewhere sets the image in a full narrative — "Then they ˹soon˺ turn into chaff scattered by the wind" (18:45) — and in the Garden parables it is the dryness that carries the warning (the withered orchard of the Cave, the scorched garden of the Pen). Verse 20 strips the parable to its skeleton on purpose: play, rain, delight, dust. A hearer who has watched one wheat season in the Hijaz has watched the whole verse.

**"To the Delight of the Planters" — and the Word the Arabic Hides**

The noun here for farmers is ḥammād, and it is almost the same word as kuffār, disbelievers — the two collide beautifully at the verse's hinge, and bold early readers leaned into it: the sowers marvel at the world's green flush exactly as the unbeliever does, delighting in a harvest they believe they manage. The plain sense remains the literal one — farmers love crops — and it carries its own rebuke: even the professional, the man whose trade is growing things, cannot grow permanence. The pun adds the theological layer the chapter keeps insisting on: delight is not the issue, ownership is; the planter who knows the rain is God's is already halfway out of the parable, while the one who signs the sky's gift as his own is chaff in advance. The sūrah will say the same thing about wealth (24) and about children (the rivalry's other ledger) in the next four verses.

**Two Exits, No Third**

"And in the Hereafter there will be either severe punishment or forgiveness from Allah and His pleasure (riḍwān)." The either/or refuses a neutral landing — there is no section of the saved reserved for sensible agnostics about the world's purpose — and the positive side is described with the sūrah's rarest word: riḍwān, God's being pleased. That noun is the chapter's whole moral horizon returned: at verse 27 the inventors of monasticism will be said to have sought "the pleasure of Allah," and here the reward on offer is precisely the pleasure they and everyone else was taught to seek by softer means. Forgiveness removes what was owed; riḍwān adds what was never owed. The verse that began by dissolving the world's pleasures ends by naming a pleasure the dissolution cannot reach, because its object is a Person, not a possession.

**"The Enjoyment of Delusion" — the Vocabulary Closes on the Deceiver**

The verse's last words — "the life of this world is no more than the enjoyment of delusion (gharūr)" — return to the sūrah's most dangerous noun. At verse 14 the hypocrites were deluded by the gharūr, the Deceiver or delusion itself, into believing that forbearance meant approval; verse 20 defines the world as that same instrument. The linkage is deliberate doctrine: a world whose enjoyment is delusion is a world where patience is the only realistic posture, and everything the chapter has commanded — spending, humility, racing to forgiveness, reading the pre-written record — is training in refusing the Deceiver's arithmetic. The parallel at 6:32 ("This worldly life is no more than play and amusement") names two of the five symptoms; this sūrah adds the social three and the closing diagnosis, because its audience has money, not just leisure, to repent from.

**The Two Ledgers at the End of the List**

The fifth disease is rivalry "in wealth and in children," and the pairing is not incidental to the sūrah's program; it is the whole portfolio of a worldly estate, its present and its future. The Book elsewhere names precisely these two as the trial that diverts from remembrance (63:9) and as the fitna whose owners forget the home (64:15), and the pairing's logic here is temporal as much as material: wealth is the money you have not spent yet, children are the name you believe will outlive you. To compete in both is to gamble on the two things verse 22 will declare pre-written. That is also why the sūrah's therapy targets the heirs of the whole chapter — the men and women of the loan (18) — since the family whose children are its capital and whose vaults are its epitaph is exactly the household that cannot lend what it cannot bear to lose. The parable of the crop then adds the quiet clause every parent already knows: the plants delight the sowers, and neither crop nor child was ever an asset at all, only a season.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:21

> ˹So˺ compete with one another for forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those who believe in Allah and His messengers. This is the favour of Allah. He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Same Racing, Re-Aimed**

The imperative sābiqū — race, outstrip one another — borrows the verb of competition from verse 20's anatomy, where vying for increase was the fifth and lowest symptom of the world's disease, and commandeers the adrenaline without the object. The sūrah does not recommend walking; it never in this chapter tells the believer to stop, only to redirect — the wallet that was hoarding now lends, the rivalry that chased wealth now forgives. That move is the answer to the verse's own implicit objection: a religion that calls competition delusion has no business commanding it. The distinction it draws is between rivalry that accumulates (tanāfus, zero-sum, the crop that ends in chaff) and rivalry that reaches (the race to what cannot be owned in excess, because the prize is distributed to every racer complete). The market's verb survives the critique of the market.

**"Forgiveness from Your Lord," First in the Lane**

The runners are told to race to two things, and the order is the chapter's whole spiritual economy in miniature: first forgiveness, then the Garden. Forgiveness precedes Paradise because, in this sūrah's bookkeeping, the ledger closes before the estate opens — the ransom that fails on the far side of the wall (verse 15) is the same debt the race is meant to outrun while prices still move. "From your Lord," not "from Allah": the relational name appears mid-command so the runner races toward the one who fed and owned them, the Lord whose kingdom opened the chapter. The Garden then gets described in the only figure the audience could verify from its own horizon — the width of the heavens and the earth — and the verse pauses on that image long enough to be worth weighing carefully, because its geometry is deliberately wrong.

**Width Without Length: The Geometry of Excess**

"A Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth" — the verse states the breadth alone, and the commentators noticed what a poet would not miss. Measure a garden by its width when its length is left unspoken, and the field goes past speech; the image is built to break the reader's arithmetic exactly at the point the sūrah has spent a dozen verses insisting divine payment outruns calculation (seven hundred-fold, and more — 2:261). The near-identical summons elsewhere makes the choice visible: "And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺." (3:133) Two commands, one image, different footnotes — there, prepared for the pious; here, "prepared for those who believe in Allah and His messengers," the same plural door verse 19 opened. The shift matters to a sūrah that ends by telling the People of the Book that the estate is not inherited by lineage: faith in the messengers, not prior tenancy, is the deed.

**"This Is the Favour of Allah" — Grace at the Finish Line**

Immediately after commanding a race, the verse disowns the race as a cause: what they win is faḍl, favor, a gift from the One who grants "to whoever He wills." Read in sequence, the two sentences are the chapter's settled position on the oldest quarrel in moral religion — earning versus grace. The sūrah has spent ten verses on loans, multiplication, rank, and wages; now, at its peak, the vocabulary of commerce dissolves into gift language, and the dissolving is not a contradiction but the destination the commerce was always heading to: the goodly loan (11) was dignified precisely because repayment is a courtesy, and here the courtesy is finally named as what it was. "He grants it to whoever He wills" repeats verbatim from verse 29's polemic, and its placement mid-race prevents the two uses from becoming either fatalism (do not run) or Pelagianism (the run owns the prize).

**"Lord of Infinite Bounty" — the Sūrah's Preferred Ending**

The closing name — dhū l-faḍl al-ʿaẓīm, the Lord of the great favor — is the formula the sūrah reserves for its high points, and here it does double structural work. It rhymes the opening block's habit of sealing every verse with an attribute (mighty, wise; capable; knowing; seeing), and it hands the chapter its final ladder of property words: kingdom (2), inheritance (10), loan (11), reward (11–18), and finally favor — a sequence in which human holdings are progressively renamed as divine dispositions. A listener who followed the sūrah from the cosmos glorifying to this sentence now knows the whole legal theory of the chapter without ever having heard a threat: everything was always on loan, the rate is the Owner's courtesy, and the last word about property is that it is His bounty. The next verse steadies the runner's heart by other means.

**Hasten, Race — the Two Imperatives, and Why Both**

The near-twin summons at the closing of the Family of Imran commands hastening to forgiveness and the vast Garden (3:133), using the verb of speed (sāriʿū); this verse uses the verb of the race (sābiqū), and the chapter has already spent one verse proving that racing in the world's sense is its fifth sickness. So the sūrah keeps both verbs because it needs both corrections: speed against procrastination — the wait-and-see man of verses 13–14 died mid-negotiation, and the runner, not the sitter, arrives — and competition against envy, since a race with a prize big enough for all racers (verse 21's width without length) has no loser to gloat over. The two imperatives also differ in what they exclude. Hastening is against your own delay; racing presumes other runners, and the sūrah, which has just ranked its first and second generations (10) and then abolished the ranking at the promise (10, again 19), permits the competition only where the envy would be absurd: nobody envies a neighbor arriving first at a gate that opens for everyone simultaneously.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:22

> No calamity ˹or blessing˺ occurs on earth or in yourselves without being ˹written˺ in a Record before We bring it into being. This is certainly easy for Allah.

**Expanded Commentary**

**One Noun for Both Accounts of the World**

The verse's key noun, muṣība, comes from aṣāba, to strike a mark: what "hits," whether the shot lands as ruin or as relief, is the verse's subject, and this translation's bracketed "or blessing" preserves what the Arabic insists on. That choice is the doctrine. Had the verse said "no disaster," the reader could file it under grief-counseling; saying "no stroke of fate at all," it writes out of the world, in one sentence, the category of accident. Everything that befalls — harvest and blight, birth and burial, the loan repaid and the loan defaulted — has the same prior status in the same book. The sūrah's audience, traders who lived on the edge between caravan-profit and caravan-loss, receives the two extremes of their year described in one noun. Even the market's god, fortune, is demoted to a clerical entry: there is no such thing as luck, only reading and its arrival.

**"In a Record Before We Bring It Into Being"**

The preposition matters more than the noun: the writing is before the creating — not a report filed after the event, not divine surveillance keeping pace with the world, but a text the world then performs. The same kitāb is the word for this sūrah's own medium, the revealed Book sent down with the messengers (verse 25), and the chapter lets the two usages touch without explaining them: what God writes, happens; what He reveals, is meant to be read. The Qur'an elsewhere names the prior register with the sūrah's own adjectives — a "preserved Tablet" (85:22), a "well-guarded Record" that swallows nothing (50:4) — and the shared vocabulary signals that the Book is not a second, lesser kind of knowledge but the accessible copy of the first. The sūrah of the loan now tells the lender that the ledger he is being invited to trust is not an abstraction: it is already complete, and his line items are in it before he chooses.

**"Easy for Allah" — the Refrain That Closes an Argument**

The verse ends on the clause that, in the Qur'an, marks a claim too large for human arithmetic but trivial for its addressee: "that is easy for Allah." The exact formula seals the same doctrine of pre-written life elsewhere: "And no one's life is made long or cut short but is ˹written˺ in a Record. That is certainly easy for Allah." (35:11) The word the two verses share for life's length and its shortening — yuʿammar, yunqas — shows the claim's reach: lifespans themselves are counted before they run. "Easy" here is not a softening but a leveling. What would be impossible for a god who had to wait and see — knowing in advance the free acts of every one of the billions of his creatures — is described as the least of His properties. The theology of verse 3's four names (knowledge of all things) returns as the warrant for verse 22's bookkeeping.

**Two Misreadings the Sūrah Itself Blocks**

A doctrine of prior writing invites two errors from opposite directions, and the chapter has already argued against both. If all is written, some said, effort is theater — but this same sūrah commanded belief, spending, racing (7, 21) in the imperative, and the imperatives are not decoration around the fatalism; they are the world's own mechanism inside the written thing. If all is written, others said, the Owner of the record is a tyrant — but the record's contents were described, from verse 1, as a cosmos in praise and wisdom (al-Ḥakīm at 57:1), and the sūrah's next words (23) state the point of the doctrine: not despair, not pride, but the two feelings dismounted. The classical schools split over whether the writing compels or foresees; the text leaves the verdict to its purposes, and its purposes are moral, not metaphysical.

**Earth Without, Self Within: The Two Registers**

The verse locates the record's entries in two places: whatever strikes the earth, and whatever strikes "yourselves." The pairing is the Qur'an's stock envelope for total knowledge — the horizons and the souls — and its function here is personal address. A community that could accept the cosmos being written still asks about its own disasters: was my loss mine, was my gain decreed, was the man who died written twice — once in the book of years, once in the book of causes? The verse answers by refusing to separate the registers. What befalls the earth includes what befalls you; your story is not a marginal note in the cosmology but one of its entries. That is why verse 23 can move, without a seam, from doctrine to therapy: a world written as one book is a world you can stop grieving as a series of accidents.

**The Vocabulary of the Written: Kitāb Before Ink**

The noun for the record — kitāb — is the same one the sūrah will use, three verses later, for scripture sent down with the messengers, and its semantic field in classical Arabic reaches beyond paper: kataba is marking, ordering, appointing (the maktūb of a wage is its fixed amount; the ketāb of a treaty is its terms). When verse 22 says every muṣība is "in a book," the audience hears "in a contract with terms," not merely "in a scroll," and the sūrah's whole economic register rewards that hearing: fate in this chapter is not a mood the cosmos is in but a signed document whose clauses run to the smallest item — the estate divided, the loan repaid, the boast recorded. The Book's own image for the prior register is equally clerical: a "Preserved Tablet" (85:22) nothing escapes, and the near twins in Qaf call it a "well-guarded Record" (50:4). Reading verse 22 beside its revealed-book sibling, the sūrah implies one more thing: the scripture being recited to this assembly is the copy by which they may know the original — a mercy in a verse whose first readers could verify neither.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:23

> ˹We let you know this˺ so that you neither grieve over what you have missed nor boast over what He has granted you. For Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful—

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Doctrine's Purpose Is Written With It**

Verse 22 announced a complete record; this verse states, in its own first words, what the announcement is for — and the list is therapeutic, not metaphysical. "So that you neither grieve over what has escaped you nor exult over what He has given you." The two passions are the whole weather of a grasping life: loss experienced as theft, gain experienced as merit. The sūrah does not ask its audience to stop feeling either; the verbs are not "do not feel" but "so that you do not feel," with the doctrine proposed as the instrument. A belief whose stated function is the equilibrium of the heart cannot be, on this chapter's own account, a license for passivity or an excuse for cruelty — those would both re-import the two feelings through the back door. The purpose clause is a test case for every serious reading of verse 22: whatever interpretation leaves the reader more despairing about the missed or more swollen over the given has failed the sūrah's stated aim.

**"What Has Escaped You" — Grief's Grammar Repaired**

The Arabic for "what escaped you" is mā fātakum, from fūt — passing by, missing, the root used for a caravan that has gone ahead and for a deadline that has overtaken you. The image quietly corrects the grief: you did not lose something held; something moved past. The loss-language of bereavement and market-loss share this root elsewhere in the Book's eschatology — the ruined deniers at the harvest of their life will call themselves "the deprived" (maḥrūmūn, 56:67) — and the sūrah's repair runs the other way: if what escaped was written as escaping before it was yours, its escape is not a robbery. That is the only sentence in this passage which makes patience philosophically plausible rather than merely demanded. The one who is addressed here, remember, was just being taught to lend to God (11) and to race to forgiveness (21): grief and vainglory are named as the two fouls of the runner.

**"Nor Exult" — the Boiling Verb**

The second forbidden feeling gets the more graphic verb: tajbarū, from jabar — to burst, to break through, the language of a pot coming to the boil and of a bone jutting through its setting. Exultation, in the sūrah's idiom, is pressure that has lost its container, and the verse that follows the two feelings with a divine name-pair makes the diagnosis physical: what boils over in joy is the same substance as what hardens in grief — the self, swollen by misreading ownership. Read against the chapter's ledger, the "what He has given you" is itself the remedy: the gift is passive, uʿītum — "you were given" — with the giver expressed and the recipient's doing absent from the clause. Praise the Giver and keep your joy; that is the sentence's arithmetic, and the same arithmetic pays back the loan of verse 11 without letting the lender celebrate himself into the verse's next sin.

**"Allah Does Not Love the Arrogant Boaster"**

The clause arrives as a proverb the Qur'an quotes in almost identical words elsewhere — "Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful." (31:18) — and the pair of nouns is worth its weight in walking. Mukhtāl is the one whose gait swaggers, the body full of its own measuring (khayal, imagination and parade at once); fakhūr is the man full of wind (fakhār), his tongue finishing what his feet began. Feet and tongue: pride described as a performance with two stages, the strut and the story. Against a sūrah that has been asking people to move their wealth out of their chests, the two-stage performance is the precise counter-example — movement for an audience, accumulation made visible — and the divine response is stated, after all this accounting language, as love withheld. The chapter's economy runs on being loved back; the arrogant boaster is not fined, he is simply not in the transaction.

**Why This Verse Sits Between 22 and 24**

Structurally, verse 23 is the hinge the chapter's second half needs. Verse 22 wrote the record; verse 24 will indict the miser. Without 23, the move would be a leap from cosmology to ethics; with it, the record's purpose lands first, and the miser can then be shown as the man whose ledger-work of hoarding was itself the exultation the doctrine was meant to cancel. The verse's opening "so that" also reaches backwards past verse 22, to verse 20's anatomy of play and rivalry: the world's fifth disease, rivalry in increase, is exactly what the equilibrium of 23 forbids from both ends — do not grip the gain, do not rage at the loss, and the rivalry loses its fuel. The chapter is constructing a single person: lent without panic, raced without swagger, and now — verse 24 — the failure mode named, taught, and closed.

**The Believer's Balance — a Hadith with This Verse's Shape**

The hadith Suhaib transmitted about the believer's affair is, in miniature, verse 23 as a saying of the Prophet ﷺ: everything is good for the believer, and this is nobody's affair but the believer's — if ease comes to him, he is grateful, and that is good for him; if hardship comes, he is patient, and that is good for him (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, book of asceticism — wording as transmitted; number not re-verified this session). The two halves of that sentence are this verse's two verbs: neither exult, nor grieve — and the hadith supplies what the verse assumes, the engine of both restraints, which is tawḥīd of the outcome: if ease is a gift (uʿītum, passive) and loss is written (verse 22), neither event licenses the self the verse is protecting against. The pairing of verse and hadith is why classical commentators treated this verse as the chapter's psychology of qadar: not a belief to be argued into the reluctant, but a reflex to be trained — gratitude and patience, two muscles, built for the day when the ledger at the gate decides which of the two the whole life has been exercising.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:24

> those who are stingy and promote stinginess among people. And whoever turns away ˹should know that˺ Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.

**Expanded Commentary**

**Stinginess That Preaches**

The verse's second clause is the one that makes the sin industrial: those who are tightfisted and order the tightfisting upon people. Withholding alone, in this sūrah's psychology, is a private failure; what gets indicted here is stinginess with a school — the miser recruiting, normalizing, building the culture in which his grip looks like prudence. Arabic shares the verb-form with the imperative and the commandments of law (amara bi), and the phrase's gravity is that a community's customs of generosity are made, not given: a man who teaches withholding has legislated inside the wallet of everyone he persuades. Read forward from verse 23's equilibrium, the sin is the exultation clause's twin — grief at what escaped you hardens into grabbing, and boasting at what you were given hardens into propaganda for your own right to keep. The chapter closes its economics on this pair: the selfish hand and the selfish sermon.

**Whose Stinginess — and Whether It Matters**

The verse's occasions, as classical compilers list them, are local and social: hypocrites of Medina who paid charity to be praised and withheld to be safe (the 9:79 cohort behind every verse on spendthrifts and their mockers), and, in a second report, an entire family proverb — "what is in our hand we cannot part with" — said by a man asked to feed his kin. Some exegetes generalize further, reading stinginess with knowledge as the verse's real target: withholding not just coins but teaching. That breadth is what the chapter supports — it has addressed, since verse 7, a whole community's money, not a case file — and the occasional reports add the texture without narrowing the claim. The one reading that fails is the smallest: a rule for individuals, since the verse's second half speaks to "whoever turns away," and turning away, in this sūrah, is precisely the refusal of the public mechanism — giving — that its interiority was being tested by.

**"And Whoever Turns Away" — the Threat That Isn't**

The final clause, at first hearing, looks like a warning: Allah is the Rich, the Praiseworthy, and your defection will not hurt Him. Its logic is actually colder than a threat and more humane than a bribe. The sūrah declines to make God's need the leverage on the human wallet — no injury claim, no punishment clause, no loss imagined in the divine account. Two names do the whole sentence's work: ghaniyy, so self-sufficient that your giving adds nothing; ḥamīd, so praiseworthy that creation's praise — verse 1's chorus — already secures the standing ovation of the cosmos whether or not your hand joins it. Turning away therefore harms only the turner, which is exactly what a sūrah that taught the goodly loan as dignity would insist: the transaction was for you. An implicit mercy rides with it: the table stays open, since a host who needs nothing is not compelled to be angry at guests who leave.

**"Praiseworthy": the Chapter's Frame Snaps Shut**

Set the last word of this verse beside the first verse's last description of why anything praises: the cosmos glorifies "for He is the Almighty, the All-Wise" (1), and the human refusal ends "Allah is the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy" (24). Between those two closers lies the whole chapter on wealth, and the pairing says the fact does not depend on the audience. Praise is not extracted; it is owed, received, and complete without you — the sūrah has already enrolled every thing in its utterance (verse 1, verse 6's night and day obeying without opinion). A miser living inside a universe in doxology is not robbing God; he is failing to participate in the only activity the cosmos agrees on. That reading fits the moral temperature of these verses, which since verse 16 have moved from rebuke to diagnosis; and it prepares, tonally, the next verse's great turn: the sending down of messengers, Book, Balance, and iron — the instruments by which people, not God, are equipped to hold the line against the man who preaches the closed fist.

**Why the Stinginess Verse Is Also About Preaching**

The chapter could have ended its ethics with the miser's hand. Instead it indicts the miser's sermon, and the reason is the sūrah's own mechanism: it has been training a community, not auditing individuals, since verse 7, and a community is an inheritance of habits — the next generation learns its wallet from this one's proverbs. That is the quiet bridge to verse 25: messengers were sent with proofs, book, and balance "so that people may stand by justice," the same social verb (aqāma, to set upright) applied to justice as verse 24 applies to greed; what preachers of stinginess build downward, the sent-down instruments are sent to build upward. Iron, in this light, arrives in the argument exactly where it should: the might that defends the open hand from its armed abusers. And the verse's final names — Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy — leave no incentive unexamined: if giving is done for praise, the cosmos already holds the only praise worth having; if it is done for need, He is the Rich, and the miser's calculation was never about Him at all. The sūrah's economics is now complete — belief, loan, rank, light, wall, heart, ledger, stinginess — and its next sentence changes register to history.

**A Closing Note on the Verse's Tense**

One small grammar marks how final this verse wants to be: "whoever turns away" is in the present participle of refusal — yuwalli — a turning that can go on. The sūrah does not say "whoever turned," because its entire moral psychology since verse 16 has been about states that continue or stop; the miser's sin began as a grip and ends as a departure, and the verse gives both in a single clause. Against that, the two names it answers with are stated without tense at all: the Self-Sufficient, the Praiseworthy — nominal, permanent, exactly as the chapter's other closers have been since the first verse's Almighty, All-Wise. The contrast is the argument: human postures are verbs, divine properties are nouns. Stinginess is something you do, and turning away is something you keep on doing; being Rich and Praiseworthy is what He is, with or without the congregation at the open hand.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:25

> Indeed, We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and with them We sent down the Scripture and the balance ˹of justice˺ so that people may administer justice. And We sent down iron with its great might, benefits for humanity, and means for Allah to prove who ˹is willing to˺ stand up for Him and His messengers without seeing Him. Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Verse That Named the Sūrah**

A chapter of loans and lights is titled by one clause of metal, and the naming preserves an instinct worth recovering. Iron is the verse's hinge-word, and the sūrah's Arabic readers titled it al-Ḥadīd because the definite noun — "the iron" — is the only thing in the passage that arrives with the same verb as the Book and the Balance and belongs, unmistakably, to the physical world: what God "sends down" here is law, standard, and ore, three objects of one verb, and the ore is the proof the other two are meant to produce in history. The title thus announces the sūrah's deepest claim before the reader reaches it: revelation and reality are handled by the same descent, and justice is not a sermon without a workshop. Whatever else the chapter teaches about chests and ledgers, it is named for the material in which the ledger's verdicts are ultimately forged.

**Clear Proofs, Scripture, Balance: The Instruments of the Third Person**

The verse begins with the familiar mission formula — messengers sent with bayyināt, clear proofs, the same term used at verse 9 for the revelations sent down on the servant — and then adds two instruments with one purpose clause: the Book and the Balance, "so that people may administer justice." The Balance (mīzān) carries, in classical exegesis, both its literal sense — the weighing that makes commercial fraud a sin this Book legislates against — and its transferred one: the standard by which any claim is weighed, the law itself, since the same pairing appears elsewhere: "It is Allah Who has revealed the Book with the truth and the balance ˹of justice˺." (42:17) The purpose clause, though, is the verse's innovation: the instruments exist so that "people" stand upright with justice — not so that heaven administers it. The grammar of iqāma (to set upright) makes the community the builder and the revelation the blueprint, the one thing sent down for the sake of what people must raise.

**"And We Sent Down Iron" — Reading the Verb Honestly**

The verb is again anzala, sent down, governing iron as it governed Book and Balance, and readers ever since have asked whether the sentence means iron's origin is extraterrestrial or whether the idiom simply extends the divine gift-language to the smithy's raw material. The classical exegetes, who could see the meteorology of metals better than we can, overwhelmingly read the descent as the same broad usage the Qur'an applies to cattle — provision "brought down" and made — because the verse's interest is not where the ore formed but what it is for, and asking the first question of the second is to answer the second badly. That restraint is worth stating plainly in an age of harmonizing headlines: the verse claims iron is a divine send-down the way scripture is, and the sūrah's own grammar of "send down" (water, verses, the Book, iron — one verb across the chapter) shows the claim is about giver-gift relation, not geology.

**"Great Might, and Benefits" — The Two Sides of the Metal**

The next clause gives the metal its double résumé: "great might" (ba's, the same word used for war's severity in the description of God's punishing strength), and benefits for people. The pairing is not a concession bolted onto a war-verse; it is the Qur'an's usual way of describing power honestly — force exists, and its moral status is decided by which sentence follows it. Tools come first in the list of benefits: the ploughshare, the adze, the nail, the needle that mends the sail, classical commentaries name them; then the sword. The sūrah of a trading community states here, without euphemism, that its open-handed ethic (verses 7–24) requires the means to keep the open hand unrobbed, and that those means are themselves "for the benefit of mankind" before they are anyone's army. Might and benefit travel in one sentence so that neither is ever alone.

**"That Allah May Know Who Helps Him Unseen"**

The purpose clause of the iron is the sūrah's hardest one and its most personal: God brings the metal down so that He may know — a way of saying "so that it becomes manifest" — who will stand for Him and His messengers "while unseen." The two meanings of "unseen" (ghayban) are both in play and the sūrah refuses to pick: one who aids the cause without seeing its payoff, and one who aids it without seeing a visible God — since if He were seen, support would cost nothing and prove nothing. Read with verse 6's chests and verse 22's pre-written record, the clause completes the chapter's test-theory: what the Book, Balance, and iron together sort is not obedience but sincerity under invisibility. The closing names — Strong, Mighty — are the sūrah's way of saying the test is real because the One testing cannot be helped in any way He needs, which is exactly why the helper is being looked for at all.

**Justice as the Verse's Subject, Not Its Reward**

Notice what the instruments are not said to achieve. The Book is not sent down so that people be saved — that is the sūrah's earlier ledger's language — but "so that people may administer justice," an outcome in this world, stated in the verse's own purpose clause (li-yaqūma al-nāsu bi-l-qisṭ). Justice here is qisṭ, the noun of measured fairness the Qur'an shares with the Balance's root, and the verb of setting it upright is the same one used for prayer and for raising the markets of the believers: justice is not a state of affairs that descends; it is a building activity with divine materials. This is why the verse's three descents form a single toolkit — proofs so claim can be tested, scripture so law can be cited, balance so weight can be verified, iron so the verified can be defended — and why the sūrah's long argument about money has led here: the chapter's economics were a practicum in qisṭ. A community that cannot pay its own poor has no standing to hold the Balance, and a Balance held by the ungenerous is, on this verse's logic, not a Balance but a weapon, which is exactly what the iron clause goes on to discipline.

**"Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty" — the Seal**

The ending pair — qawiyy ʿazīz, strong, mighty — closes the only verse in this sūrah that speaks entirely in the first-person plural Majesty, and the shift from "We sent down" to "Allah is" pulls the camera back from the workshop of history to the name above it. Strength (quwwa) is the power that cannot be obstructed; might (ʿizza) the power that cannot be matched — and after a verse about the great might of iron, applying both nouns to God keeps the metal in its place: every forge in the sūrah borrows its "great might" from the One whose strength needs no tool. The same pair of names opened the chapter's first line in other clothing (al-ʿAzīz al-Ḥakīm), so that the verse of iron now rhymes with the verse of cosmic glorification — power framed, in both, by wisdom and self-sufficiency rather than by need. Whoever supports the cause "unseen" supports, on this rhyme, the One whom the whole cosmos has been assisting by the simple fact of existing. The test of iron, seen this way, is the test verse 1 already says the rest of creation passes without wages.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:26

> And indeed, We sent Noah and Abraham and reserved prophethood and revelation for their descendants. Some of them are ˹rightly˺ guided, while most are rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**From Metal to Genealogy — the Sūrah Changes its Proof**

The chapter has run on two proof-structures: the cosmos that praises and the wallet that tests. Verse 25 introduced instruments — messengers, Book, Balance, iron — and verse 26 now supplies the instruments' history, and the transition is the argument's: the Book did not drop out of the sky, it dropped into a lineage. "We sent Noah and Abraham" — the two names that bracket the covenant story, the survivor of the flood and the father of the prophets — and the sūrah compresses the rest of the chain under the word "prophethood." What is claimed for the lineage is precise and modest: prophethood and the Book were reserved among their progeny (the verb jaʿala here reads "placed, appointed"), which is an assertion about the distribution of revelation, not about inherited virtue. The next clause immediately blocks that second reading: "some of them are guided, most are transgressing." The line that carries the Book does not automatically carry its keepers.

**"Some Guided, Most Rebellious" — the Verse's Sharpest Word**

The formula — qawmun muhtadūn, wa-aktharuhum fāsiqūn — is this sūrah's signature realism (it will reappear at verse 27, almost word for word, for the followers of Jesus, and the same ratio presides over the donors of verse 10). The minority/goodness, majority/wrongness pairing is stated without grief and without scandal, and both features are the lesson: the Qur'an refuses both the pious fiction that institutions stay pure and the cynic's inference that the institution therefore failed. Fāsiq, "one who exits," describes the condition precisely — the heir steps out of the line, though the line continues — which is why the verse pairs the noun with the covenant vocabulary rather than abolishing the covenant. The reader is being trained in a judgment the earlier verses demanded of wealth and now demand of genealogy: a thing is honored by what it is for, not by who holds it.

**The Book Among the Descendants — and Which Descendants**

By "the Book" in this verse classical exegesis understands the scriptures plural — Torah, Psalms, Gospel, and this Qur'an, all of them Abrahamic in the sūrah's own census — and the choice of Abraham's progeny as the channel is the Qur'an's point of contact with its two rival congregations in the Hijaz. The Jews and Christians of the audience can be addressed so directly in the next verses (27–29) precisely because verse 26 has just acknowledged their pedigree: prophethood was, by God's act and not their claim, set in this family. That acknowledgment is then qualified twice in two clauses — reserved for the descendants, and most of the descendants rebellious — which is the sūrah's compressed answer to exclusivity: the channel is real, and the channel is not a franchise. Verse 29 will finish the thought with grace itself, "in the Hand of Allah, He gives it to whom He wills."

**The Same Verdict for Muslims — an Early Tradition**

That the "most are rebellious" clause is not anti-Jewish or anti-Christian, but anthropological, is secured by the way the earliest teachers applied it, and a saying transmitted on the Prophet's authority about his own community's late generations makes the transfer explicit: the upright depart one by one until nothing remains but folk light as the dregs of barley and dates. [UNVERIFIED: the wording of this eschatological tradition varies across the collections; it is referenced here from the tafsīr reports as circulating material, without a numbered citation.] The sūrah needs no such report to make its point — its own vocatives, "O you who believe," keep returning the rebuke to the assembly, as verse 16 proved — but the tradition's existence shows how the first readers heard it: a chapter on Israel that refused to become a chapter about Israel, in a community that could see its own wallet in the mirror and chose to look anyway.

**Why Noah and Abraham, and Why These Two Alone**

The sūrah's history lesson could have named the whole roll of prophets, and its choice of two is an argument in itself. Noah stands at the beginning of the story scripture tells after the first failure of humankind — the second father — and Abraham at the beginning of the story scripture tells about covenant and progeny, the father of the prophets the audience's own rivals claim. Between them lie, by the Qur'an's count, every sent Book and every law, so the two names function as an inclusio: the entire apparatus of revelation is bracketed without a list. The address gains its force from what the bracket excludes: no Israelite-only genealogy of grace survives these two names, since Ishmael's line runs beside Isaac's inside the same verse, and the final messenger of the sūrah's audience descends from the other twin. Verse 29's denial that anyone holds leverage over God's favor is therefore already seeded here — the lineage is named at the exact moment it is universalized, and the two prophets stand as the chapter's proof that covenant is a family with more branches than any branch will admit.

**The Verb of Reservation: What "Reserved" Means Here**

The phrase translated "reserved prophethood and revelation for their descendants" is, in the Arabic, lighter than the English makes it sound: wa-jaʿalnā fī dhurriyyatihima al-nubuwwa wa-l-kitāb — "and We placed, in their offspring, prophethood and the Book." The verb is placing, not promising, and the preposition "in" locates the gifts among a people rather than owed to them. That grammar matters for the sūrah's next three verses, which speak of what the placed gifts were answered with: some guided, invention and loss (27), an appeal to fear and believe (28), and finally the declaration that the favor is in God's hand (29). If the verse had said "covenanted," the People of the Book could read their scriptures as a bank balance; saying "placed," it lets them read them as a trust — the very category verse 7 built for wealth, now applied to revelation itself. Prophecy is the largest mustakhlaf fīhi in the chapter's economics, and the next verse's monks will show what communities do with trusts they were never given.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:27

> Then in the footsteps of these ˹prophets˺, We sent Our messengers, and ˹after them˺ We sent Jesus, son of Mary, and granted him the Gospel, and instilled compassion and mercy into the hearts of his followers. As for monasticism, they made it up—We never ordained it for them—only seeking to please Allah, yet they did not ˹even˺ observe it strictly. So We rewarded those of them who were faithful. But most of them are rebellious.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Chain Closes on a Name, and a Gift**

The genealogy of verse 26 gets its chronology here: messengers sent "in the footsteps" of the patriarchs, and at the end of the line, Jesus son of Mary with the Gospel named as a gift (ātaināhu, "We gave him") rather than an achievement. The phrasing keeps the sūrah's register of descent: books and prophets come down, are sent, are given — verse 25's anzala vocabulary continuing into history. For an audience of believers being taught that nothing they hold is theirs, the verse quietly makes the same claim about their rivals' holiest possession: the Gospel is a grant, which means it is honorable, which means rejecting it rejects God and not a sect. No polemic survives that grammar; the sūrah has simply placed Christianity where it belongs in the same account it gave for the Torah and for itself, and it has done it in the middle of a chapter about loans.

**"We Placed Compassion and Mercy in Their Hearts"**

Before it reproaches them for anything, the verse praises what God Himself did in the community of Jesus: raʾfa — tenderness, a word soft enough to describe hands over a child — and raḥma, the same root as the chapter's divine names, placed not in books but "in the hearts" of the followers. The placement is the point: the fruit of the Gospel is described as an interior fact, the exact category (chest-versus-row) this sūrah has been interrogating since verse 6. It is also the sūrah's answer to the suspicion that faithfulness requires a perfect law: the disciples of Jesus are credited with a grace no legislator can install. The verse can then criticize their later invention without contradiction, because its standard throughout has been the one God writes directly on hearts — compassion is the receipt revelation leaves, and everything else is bookkeeping. The same pairing appears in the description of this Prophet's own company: "And those with him are firm with the disbelievers and compassionate with one another." (48:29)

**Rahbāniyya: the Invention, and Two Ways to Read Its Censure**

Monasticism — rahbāniyya, from the root of "monk," rahib, the fearful one — is the only human religious innovation the Qur'an names and adjudicates inside this verse, and the adjudication has two axes: "We did not prescribe it for them," and "they did not observe it as its observance is." The grammar allows two verdicts, and the commentators split along them. On the majority reading, the invention itself is not the fault — they invented it "seeking God's good pleasure," and seeking is praiseworthy — the fault is the double failure that followed: they could not keep what they imposed, and they imposed what God never imposed. On the second reading, the praise attaches to the initial God-given disposition and the censure to the later alteration of it, so the verse charges the community not with asceticism but with inventing the terms of its own merit. The first reading is stronger — it explains "we did not prescribe it" as a boundary on obligation, not a prohibition of excess — but both agree on the verdict that matters in this chapter's ledger: supererogation that cannot be kept is worse than ordinary faithfulness honestly kept.

**"They Did Not Observe It as Its Observance Is" — the Measure the Verse Sets**

The clause's Arabic is an idiom of proper measurement: faqadūhā — they "lost" it, the verb of losing, not abandoning — as though the vow had slipped out of their hands by its own weightlessness, and the standard named for keeping ("its observance," as an internal cognate) is deliberately impersonal: not our measure, not your founders', the thing's own. A promise made to God has, on this verse, a real structure with real obligations — including the obligation to keep promises that outran one's capacity, which is the practical lesson any monk's rule has taught: severity adopted in a high hour becomes either sustained or quietly faked, and the sūrah says the second happened at scale. This is where the chapter's argument about wealth folds in: a vow is a loan you make to yourself, and a community that borrows sanctity beyond its margin ends, like the boastful rich of verse 23, owing more than it can show. The faithful among them are named next, because the sūrah has not finished being fair.

**Reward for the Kept, Rebellion for the Most — the Same Sentence as Verse 26**

The verse ends with the ratio of the previous verse almost unchanged — those who believed among them receive their reward, and most of them are transgressing — and the repetition is structural, not lazy: whatever the community's law, the Day divides by fidelity, not by affiliation. "So We rewarded those of them who were faithful" — awbaynā, "We gave them their wage," in the past tense the sūrah already used for the martyrs' settled account (verse 19) — settles the question the monastic paragraph raised about inventors: seeking God's pleasure even by a path no one prescribed still reaches the Sought. And the "most are rebellious" clause keeps the verse from becoming praise of the cloister at all: what is rewarded is sincerity, which is rare in every institution, including the ones with better rules. A tradition preserved in this sūrah's commentaries puts the chapter's whole ethic of excess into one sentence — every prophet has his monasticism, and this community's monasticism is striving in God's way — a saying whose wording circulates without a fixed chain, and which the sūrah itself, with its open ledger and its iron, has already argued. [UNVERIFIED: the "monasticism of this community is jihad" report — transmitted in the collections with varying chains and not numbered here.]

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:28

> O people of faith! Fear Allah and believe in His Messenger. ˹And˺ He will grant you a double share of His mercy, provide you with a light to walk in ˹on Judgment Day˺, and forgive you. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.

**Expanded Commentary**

**A Vocative With a Double Address**

"O you who believe" is, everywhere in the Qur'an, the vocative of the in-group, the summons to the congregation of Muhammad ﷺ — and yet this sūrah's final movement uses it for the people it has just been describing, the followers of earlier scriptures, since verse 29's purpose clause names them as the ones meant to learn something from the whole exchange. The commentators felt the tension and mostly resolved it by letting the vocative address, in priority, the People of the Book who already "believe" in the sense their own scriptures command: fear God and believe in His Messenger — an invitation, not a rebuke. The secondary sense, pressed by those who note how often this sūrah turns on its own assembly (verse 16 being the sharpest case), is that the verse says to Muslims: your faith is not a completed transaction; believe again, through the same two verbs. Priority belongs to the conversion address — the double reward below fits it and no other — but the sūrah plainly intends both audiences to feel the sentence, and the vocative's ambiguity is the mechanism.

**"A Double Share of His Mercy" — the Arithmetic of Sequence**

The difl, a twofold portion, is the sūrah's payment for a convert with a prior faith: reward for the old fidelity and reward for the new recognition, both in one name. The hadith literature gives the arithmetic its canonical case — among the three who receive double reward, on the Prophet's report recorded by al-Bukhārī, is "a man from the People of the Book who believed in his Prophet and then believed in Muḥammad" — and the sūrah's own prior verses do the theology: verse 27 has just rewarded the faithful among the Christians of the rahbāniyya passage, so the double share here is not a bonus for the clever but the settlement of two honest accounts. It is also the chapter's final word against the resentment verse 10's gradation could breed: precedence in one ledger pays nothing in the other; a late arrival who believed in everything he was given first, and then in what came, out-doubles careers built on unbroken attendance. The old covenant (verse 8) and the fresh one meet in one paycheck.

**A Light to Walk By — the Chapter's Central Image Returns**

Everything in this sūrah about light has been building to this quieter clause. Verse 12 gave the owners of light their triumph, verse 13 told the lightless to go find some, and here light is promised as a provision (yajʿal lakum nūran), given to be walked in — guidance with a floor under it. The eschatological detail the tradition layers on — the believers crossing the bridge over Hell by the light they earned — belongs to the hadith literature rather than to this verse, which is about a present capacity as much as a future crossing: a light in which to walk now, meaning a guidance visible enough to move by. The Book's warning on the other side of the promise is stark in the same idiom: "And whoever Allah does not bless with light will have no light!" (24:40) What the sūrah has been describing since verse 7 is the one transaction in which the buyer receives both the coin and the visibility to use it.

**"And Forgive You" — After the Ransom Clause**

The third gift on the list is the one the chapter proved impossible for others: forgiveness, maghfira, named here in a sentence whose partners are mercy and light, with no condition left but taqwā and belief. The structural ear hears verse 15 in this clause — "today no ransom will be accepted" — and the sūrah's sequence lets the two verses argue: what the Day will close, the address now opens, for precisely the same kind of past the hypocrites had, a history of outer presence and inner lag. That is also why the vocative could double onto Muslims: anyone whose row was not always matched by a chest is standing in the range of this offer. The closing names — All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, ghafūr and raʾūf — repeat the pair from verse 9's end (kind, merciful), so the revelation that led out of darkness now signs the payroll, and the sūrah's theology of giving arrives at the gift that cannot be borrowed, earned, or ransomed, only granted. The sentence after the address will explain why even the graced must not claim it.

**"Fear Allah" — the Chapter's Whole Ethics in Two Words**

The address opens with ittaqū Llāh, fear God, and the verb deserves the plain hearing it too often loses. Taqwā, in the Qur'an's own anatomy, is not dread of punishment but the posture of a man walking a narrow path while watching his step — the root w-q-y means to shield, to put something between yourself and harm, and the sūrah has spent its length describing the only shield that works in the case it is arguing: hearts that humble at remembrance (16), wealth that moves when the chest loosens (7–11), light that cannot be borrowed at any gate (13). In a chapter that closes on grace given "to whoever He wills," the imperative of taqwā is therefore not a condition smuggled back in to cancel the gift; it names the orientation of the recipient — the one who walks in the light he was given, rather than storing it. The double share, the walking-light, and the forgiveness are listed as futures ("He will grant"), and the two present imperatives — fear, believe — are the only verbs the verse allows its audience to own. That proportion, promise heavy and demand narrow, is the sūrah's final calibration of the very relationship it began by describing in the cosmos: everything glorifies, nothing is coerced, and the praise the chapter still wants is the kind that walks.

## Sūrah al-Ḥadīd 57:29

> ˹This is so˺ that the People of the Book ˹who deny the Prophet˺ may know that they do not have any control over Allah's grace, and that all grace is in Allah's Hands. He grants it to whoever He wills. For Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.

**Expanded Commentary**

**The Sūrah Ends on a Subordinate Clause**

This is one of the Qur'an's shortest long endings: the final verse begins with "so that" — li-taʿlamū, "that you may know" — and a chapter that opened with the cosmos in doxology and ran through kingdoms, loans, walls, hearts, ledgers, iron, and lineages does not stop with a verdict, a law, or even a command. It stops with a purpose: the whole sūrah is structured as the means to a piece of knowledge held by the People of the Book who rejected the Prophet. A closing subordinate clause says something about the Book's theory of revelation — the verse after the last verse is the one the reader writes with his life — and it fits this chapter precisely: a sūrah that spent twenty-eight verses insisting the heart's interior is the only real ledger cannot end by legislating; it ends by proposing an epistemology to people whose error was a claim about knowledge they did not have — knowledge of who gets grace.

**"No Control Over Any of Allah's Grace" — Dismantling a Franchise**

The idiom — lā yaḍillūna min shay'in min faḍli Llāh, literally "they have no hold on anything of God's favor" — is aimed at a concrete claim, and the sūrah's own audience makes it recoverable: if prophethood was, by God's act, placed in Abraham's progeny (verse 26), a descendant could be tempted to read the placement as property and the scripture as an inherited deed, so that the last installment — arriving through Ishmael's house — would be legally void. Verse 29 answers by relocating everything: the covenant's distribution is a grace, grace by definition is unowed, and a grant no one can veto cannot have been a franchise. The hadith parable the classical teachers cited against exactly this resentment — the laborers hired dawn to noon, noon to afternoon, and the last hour for double, who grumble when the final crew is paid first ("That is My grace, which I give to whom I will," Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2269) — is a saying of the Prophet ﷺ whose conclusion quotes this verse's words, and its existence shows how early the Hijazi congregations heard verse 29 as the settlement of the wages argument, not as abstract predestination.

**"Grace Is in Allah's Hand" — The Verse That Closes All Accounts**

Set the phrase beside the one the sūrah opened its own accountancy with: "to Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth" (2). The kingdom said ownership; the hand says disposition — the warm, personal image of favor "in" someone's hand, the idiom of a thing you hold when you give it, not a vault where it is stored. Between verse 2 and verse 29 the chapter has taught its readers to move money through metaphors — loan, purchase, inheritance, ransom refused — and the final noun is none of them: fadl, surplus, favor, the giving-without-owing that every other transaction was a school for. That is why verse 21's race ended on the same pair of words, "the Lord of infinite bounty" — dhū l-faḍl — and why the repetition rings the chapter shut: competition in forgiveness, then its explanation by grace; no merit survives to the end that was not always a gift, and no gift arrives that anyone's hand withheld. "Infinite," ʿaẓīm, is the only adjective on it, which is the sūrah's last word on human arithmetic.

**"He Gives It to Whom He Wills" — Sovereignty Without Arbitrariness**

The clause is the verse's most misread sentence, and the sūrah itself supplies its guardrails. Read baldly, "He gives it to whom He wills" sounds like a decree that makes seeking pointless; but this chapter has spent twenty-six verses on commands — believe, spend, lend, race, fear — issued in the most real grammar Arabic has, and verse 21 placed the same clause immediately after "race." The sūrah's own placement resolves it: the will in question is what makes the invitation unconditional, not what makes it ineffective. Grace cannot be held by a lineage (the verse's target), which is exactly why anyone, including a convert with a double share, can receive it; predestination read as veto power would abolish the very offer the verse exists to defend. The strong sense is the freeing one: the giving is not constrained by your history or your rival's pedigree, only by the Giver's wisdom — the first verse's last word, al-Ḥakīm, which the last verse now quietly answers.

**How the Sūrah of Iron Leaves Its Reader**

Endings in scripture are usually verdicts, laws, or lullabies, and this one is a permission. The chapter began by reporting that everything that exists already glorifies God without asking anyone's leave (1), and it closes by reporting that everything that is given is given by God without asking anyone's leave — the two "without your consent" clauses bracket a community's pride in both its worship and its wallet. In between stand the wall with its gate, the hearts humbled or hardened, the pre-written record that forbids grief and boast, and the metal sent down so that the unglamorous labor of justice can be done at all. A sūrah named for iron ends with a noun that means "favors," and its readers are sent out with the chapter's one settled doctrine: everything was always on loan, everything returns, and what is returned has always been held — in a hand that gives to whom it wills, and asks the rest only to want to be given.
