# Sūrah al-Ḥadīd (Chapter 57) — Expanded Verse-by-Verse Commentary

## Introduction to the Sūrah

**The Miracle of Iron and the Loan to the Sovereign God**

Al-Ḥadīd, "The Iron," is the fifty-seventh chapter of the Qur'an, comprising 29 verses of early-to-mid Medinan revelation. It takes its name from verse 25, which delivers a profound geological, technological, and metaphysical revelation: *“And We sent down iron (*wa-anzalnā al-ḥadīda*), in which is great military might and benefits for mankind, and so that Allah may make evident who supports Him and His messengers unseen. Indeed, Allah is Powerful and Exalted in Might.”* Classical and modern commentators note the precise wording *anzalnā* ("We sent down"): modern astrophysics has confirmed that the heavy element iron was not formed within the primordial earth, but was synthesized in the cores of exploding ancient supernovas and literally "sent down" to the cooling planet through meteoric bombardment! Positioned at the exact mathematical center of the Qur'anic codex, Sūrah al-Ḥadīd opens the sequence of the five *Musabbiḥāt* chapters (Sūrahs 57, 59, 61, 62, 64) that begin with the cosmic glorification of God. It is a monumental treatise on the mobilization of wealth and conviction, asking humanity the searching question: *“Who is it that will offer Allah a goodly loan (*qarḍan ḥasanan*) so that He will multiply it for him and grant him a noble reward?”* (57:11).

**The Structural Modulation and the Wall Between Believers and Hypocrites**

The architecture of Sūrah al-Ḥadīd moves across four distinct movements:

1. Verse 3 expresses the sublime quartet of Divine Attributes: *“He is the First (*al-Awwal*) and the Last (*al-Ākhir*), the Ascendant (*al-Ẓāhir*) and the Intimate (*al-Bāṭin*), and He is, of all things, Knowing.”* He creates the cosmos, knows what enters the earth and what emerges from it, what descends from heaven and what ascends into it, and He is with you wherever you are (*wa-huwa maʿakum ayna mā kuntum*).

2. The Challenge of Spending and the Bridge of Light (7–15): Commanding belief and spending from that over which God has made humanity temporary trustees (*mustakhlafīna fīh*). It stages the terrifying scene of the Day of Judgment: believing men and women running across the bridge of the Hereafter with their radiant light gleaming before them and on their right hands, while the hypocrites cry out in pitch-black darkness: *“Wait for us that we may acquire some of your light!”* They are told to turn back. A towering wall with a locked gate is dropped between them: on the inside is mercy, and on the outside is torment.

3. The Softening of Hearts and the Parable of Worldly Life (16–24): The piercing call to soften the heart before the remembrance of God, illustrated by the dead earth revived with spring rain. Verse 20 delivers the definitive Qur'anic diagnosis of worldly life: play, diversion, adornment, boasting among yourselves, and competition in increase of wealth and children—compared to a lush rain that delights the cultivators, then withers, turns yellow, and becomes dry chaff. Iron, Apostles, and Monasticism (25–29): The descent of the Messengers with clear proofs, the Scripture, and the Balance (*al-mīzān*) so that humanity may uphold justice, conjoined with the descent of Iron (*al-ḥadīd*). The sūrah concludes by calling the believers to mindfulness and faith in His Messenger, promising them a double portion of His mercy, a radiant light by which they will walk, and forgiveness, for *“grace is in the Hand of Allah; He gives it to whom He wills.”*

## Verse 57:1
> Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah, for He is the Almighty, All-Wise

**"Whatever," Not "Whoever": an Inclusive Grammar**

Arabic distinguishes two relative pronouns: man for rational persons, mā for everything else — and the verse uses the broader, non-personal form. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth: stones and stars, angels and planets, rain and the silence between them. Some commentators note that if the verse had said "whoever," the reader could have supposed the praise belonged only to minds, to the angels and the praying servants; "whatever" closes that exit. Even the inanimate is a worshipper, which means that materialism is refuted at the level of grammar before a single argument is offered. Matter is not dead weight in this picture; it is a tongue — an unarticulated one, but a tongue of the kind God understands.

**Two Tongues: Spoken Praise and the Praise of Being**

Exegetes distinguish what creation says with utterance from what it says with existence — a mountain glorifies by being what it was made to be. The distinction matters because the Qur'an itself insists the second kind is real, not poetic: "The seven heavens, the earth, and all those in them glorify Him. There is not a single thing that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot understand their glorification. He is indeed Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving." (17:44) The closing sentence is not decoration; the verse ends by naming patience and forgiveness precisely because creation's praise continues despite being incomprehensible to us, and despite our failure to match it. Whatever praises God in its own language, we are not qualified to audit it. What remains for us is forbearance toward the chorus and gratitude for our part in it.

**The Glorifiers and a Verse Better Than a Thousand**

This sūrah heads a family — 57, 59, 61, 62. 64, all opening on the same verb of glorification — known in the sciences as al-Musabbiḥāt, "the Glorifiers." Al-ʿIrbāḍ ibn Sāriya reported that the Prophet ﷺ would not go to sleep before reciting them. Said of them, "In them is a verse better than a thousand verses" (Sunan Abū Dāwūd 5057; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2921, graded ḥasan by al-Tirmidhī). Exegetes have generally pointed that report at 57:3 — *“He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near, and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”*, whose four names gather theology into one line. The report does not name the verse, so the identification stays a judgment rather than a text — but it explains why the earliest scholars gave these openings disproportionate weight, and why the sūrah's first verses are about God alone, before the world and its duties are mentioned once.

## Verse 57:2
> To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. He gives life and causes death. And He is Most Capable of everything

**Sovereignty First, Commands Later**

The sūrah's second statement establishes ownership before it introduces obligation. The kingdom — mulk — is total: the heavens, the earth, and everything between, with no clause of exception. That sequence is the argumentative spine of the whole chapter, because the verses that follow will ask for money, presence, and loyalty; the opening line makes sure the reader already knows whose money it is. A king who commands tribute from his own property has not dispossessed anyone. Read this way, verse 7's call to "donate from what He has entrusted you with" was prepared by verse 2's claim to the kingdom. The economics of the sūrah are not a tax but a reallocation of the owner's goods among trustees, and the moral pressure it exerts loses its sting once ownership is settled.

**Giving Life and Causing Death: a Claim Tested in Court**

The pairing yuḥyī wa-yumīt does more than list two biological events; it assigns both ends of existence to one agent. The Qur'an elsewhere stages the claim as a courtroom challenge: "˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.”" (2:258) The disputant there could not follow through, and the argument moved to a stronger example, the sun's rising. In this sūrah there is no dialogue, only assertion — the claim is stated as settled fact in a verse of praise, not offered for debate. The denier of resurrection elsewhere in the Book asks who can revive bones; the reply is that the One who produced them first "has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being." (36:79) The sūrah's phrasing assumes that same answer is already accepted by its audience.

**"Most Capable of Everything": the Attribute That Ends the Verse**

The closing phrase — ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr — is one of the Book's signature conclusions, and it is precise: capability over everything, not over many things. And He is Most Capable of everything." (5:120) Its placement here matters for what follows: a community being told to spend for an unseen return is first told that repayment is not difficult for the One who promises it.

**Two Fears, One Verse**

Read against the verses this one precedes, verse 2 is addressed to the two engines of human refusal. Fear of loss is answered by "to Him belongs the kingdom": what you are being asked to give was lodged with you, and the Owner has not gone bankrupt by honoring the loan. Fear of people is answered by "He is Most Capable of everything": the community that spent and fought before Mecca had every reason to look frightened, and this clause is the answer to dread that outlasts a single battle. Elsewhere the Book says the same thing with even blunter economics: "And surely to Us ˹alone˺ belong this life and the next." (92:13) If both homes belong to Him, the transaction the sūrah will propose involves no real transfer; the only genuine movement is the heart's, from holding to trust.

**The Two Names the Verse Ends On**

The sūrah's first two verses close on names rather than on arguments, and the commentary that runs alongside the text explains both before it moves on. Al-ʿAzīz is glossed there as the One to whom all things submit in humility, which turns the attribute from a claim about power into a claim about the posture of everything else; al-Ḥakīm is glossed with three objects at once, wise in His creating, in His commanding and in His legislating, so that the wisdom named is a specification of where it shows rather than a general excellence. The clause that follows is treated the same way: “He is Most Capable of everything” is unpacked as whatever He wills is, and whatever He does not will never be. That sentence removes the middle category, the thing that might happen anyway, and read back into the sūrah's argument about money it does quiet work: the community being asked to spend is told that the One who promises the return is not merely able to pay, but is the only party whose will decides what exists at all.

## Verse 57:3
> He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near, and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things

**First and Last: What the Pair Excludes**

To say God is the First is to say there is no "before" in which He was not; to say He is the Last is to say the series of things has an end that He outlasts. The Qur'an's own evidence for each half is distributed: perishability belongs to everything else — "Every being on earth is bound to perish. Only your Lord Himself, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain ˹forever˺." (55:26–27) — while the priority belongs to Him alone, the point of the old confession that there was nothing besides Him. The two names therefore do double duty: they describe Him, and they demote every rival. A dynasty, a mountain, an idol: each has a first moment, so each fails the test of the First; each has a last, so each fails the test of the Last.

**The Outward and the Inward: Manifest and Hidden**

The Arabic is al-Ẓāhir and al-Bāṭin, and their sense is not spatial in a crude way but evidentiary. He is the Outward because everything that appears is His sign; the faces of things are windows, not walls. He is the Inward because nothing reaches inside a creature the way its maker does: "No vision can encompass Him, but He encompasses all vision. For He is the Most Subtle, All-Aware." (6:103) And nearness is claimed in the first person plural: "Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them. We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein." (50:16) The pairing forbids two errors at once: searching for God only in the distance, and domesticating Him as an inner feeling. The most direct commentary on these words is a practice. ʿĀʾisha reported that when the Prophet ﷺ lay down at night he supplicated: "O Allah, You are the First, there is nothing before You; the Last, there is nothing after You; the Manifest, there is nothing above You; the Hidden, there is nothing below You. Relieve my debt and enrich me beyond poverty." (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2713) Two things stand out.

**Knowledge Seals the Verse**

The final clause — "and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things" — grounds the four names in account of knowledge. Owner of time and interior would be a distant abstraction if He did not actually know each particular; knowledge makes transcendence operational. That is also why the sūrah can move without transition in verse 6 to "He knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart": the seed is here, in the verse that the hypocrites' exposure will later grow from.

**The Verse Prescribed for Doubt**

Abū Dāwūd records that Abū Zāmil said: I mentioned to Ibn ʿAbbās that I felt something in my heart. He said, “Doubts?” and then laughed, and said, “No one escapes this. God the Exalted said (10:94 — *“If you ˹O Prophet˺ are in doubt about ˹these stories˺ that We have revealed to you, then ask those who read the Scripture before you. The truth has certainly come to you from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt”*).” Then he told him: when you feel any of this in your heart, recite (57:3 — *“He is the First and the Last, the Most High and Most Near, and He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”*). The exchange is worth its weight for two reasons. The Companion laughs at the report instead of rebuking it, which places doubt inside the normal range of a believing heart; and the treatment he prescribes is not an argument but a verse, one whose four names cover the before and the after, the manifest and the hidden, so that a mind turning over a difficulty is handed a frame larger than the difficulty.

**A Supplication Built Out of the Four Names**

The same four names are what the Prophet ﷺ turned into a bedtime prayer. Aḥmad collected from Abū Hurayrah that the Messenger of Allah used to say when he lay down: O God, Lord of the seven heavens and Lord of the Mighty Throne, our Lord and the Lord of everything, Revealer of the Torah, the Gospel and the Criterion, Splitter of the grain and the date-stone — I take refuge with You from the evil of everything whose forelock You hold. O God, You are al-Awwal and there is nothing before You, al-Ākhir and there is nothing after You, aẓ-Ẓāhir and there is nothing above You, al-Bāṭin and there is nothing below You. Relieve us of debt and free us from poverty. Muslim records the same text through Sahl, who said that Abū Ṣāliḥ used to order them to lie on their right side and say it, narrating it from Abū Hurayrah from the Prophet ﷺ. Two details deserve notice: each of the four names is given its own exclusion, which is the verse's content restated as speech; and the prayer ends by asking about debt and poverty, the two pressures this sūrah is addressing when it asks for money.

**How Many Readings the Four Names Have**

The commentary notes that something like a dozen explanations of this verse were collected from the scholars of tafsīr, and it preserves one of them in an unusually compact form. Al-Bukhārī is quoted as saying: Yaḥyā said, “aẓ-Ẓāhir — knowing all things; al-Bāṭin — knowing all things,” and al-Mizzī is then cited to identify Yaḥyā as Ibn Ziyād al-Farrāʾ, author of the book Maʿānī al-Qurʾān. The doubling is the point of the report: both names are read back onto knowledge, so that the manifest and the hidden differ in direction and not in the reach of the One who knows them. It is the oldest kind of exegesis, four words glossed by four words, and it sits beside the later theological argument over the same verse without contradicting any of it.

## Verse 57:4
> He is the One Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne. He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the sky and whatever ascends into it. And He is with you wherever you are. For Allah is All-Seeing of what you do

Four movements are compressed into one line: making, seating, knowing, seeing. The first two are the stock structure of the Qur'an's creation accounts — "Indeed your Lord is Allah Who created the heavens and the earth in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne." (7:54) — and the sūrah reproduces that structure exactly, which already tells us something: its purpose is not the order of the universe but authority. Six Days of making, then the throne: the God who built is the God who rules. The sūrah that will judge a community by how it handles wealth uses the verse with a single word for the whole of production and the whole of government in it.

**"He Is With You Wherever You Are"**

The turning point of the verse is also the most debated sentence in classical theology. The majority read the with-ness as God's knowledge and power reaching everywhere, not His essence located alongside bodies — a reading that protects transcendence and fits the immediate context, which closes with "Allah is All-Seeing of what you do." Others, especially within devotional and Sufi commentary, allowed a stronger nearness, an accompanying of the servant Himself, anchored in verses like the jugular-vein claim (50:16 — *“Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ created humankind and ˹fully˺ know what their souls whisper to them, and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein”*). The majority position is stronger here for a textual reason: the verse moves from knowledge of all movement to knowledge of all conduct; the with-ness is introduced as surveillance-with-care, not spatial co-location. Both sides agree on the pastoral effect the clause exists to produce: the reader is not unattended.

**The Throne and the Question of the "How"**

The list's rhetorical force is in its boring completeness: nothing crosses any border of the world without being in view, and the same wording reappears verbatim in Saba (34:2 — *“He knows whatever goes into the earth and whatever comes out of it, and whatever descends from the sky and whatever ascends into it. And He is the Most Merciful, All-Forgiving”*), a repetition that marks the formula as fixed liturgical material, not an ad hoc image.

**What the Verse Names**

The verse gives the creation of the heavens and the earth, the seating of God on the Throne, and the knowledge of what enters the earth and what comes out of it and what comes down from the sky and what ascends, and it closes with the statement that God is with the community wherever they are, seeing what they do. The Book combines the creation with the knowledge in several places, and the Book brings the same knowledge and the same throne together in the verse of the Throne (2:255 — *“His Seat encompasses the heavens and the earth, and the preservation of both does not tire Him.”*).

**The Word for the Closeness**

The sentence that God is with you wherever you are is one of the Book's clearest statements of the nearness of God, and the commentators read it as His knowledge and His providence rather than His presence in a place, since the verse has just named the high and the low ends of the creation. The Sūrah of the Groaning gives the community a nearer statement when it says that God is with them wherever three people are speaking (58:7 — *“Do you not see that Allah knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth? If three converse privately, He is their fourth. If five, He is their sixth. Whether fewer or more, He is with them wherever they may be. Then, on the Day of Judgment, He will inform them of what they have done. Surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”*). The passage in front of us puts the two halves side by side, the throne and the company, and the Book's manner is to state both.

**Where the Sūrah Is Standing**

The chapter is addressed to a community that has become comfortable, and its opening verses deal with the kingdom and the decree. The verse after this one asks the men who claim the kingdom of the heavens and the earth to listen to what is said to them, and the sūrah then moves to a long passage on spending. The verse in front of us gives the reason for the whole arrangement: the One who made all of it and knows all of it is present with the people who are called to spend.

**What Enters, What Comes Out, What Descends, What Ascends**

The four movements of the verse are given contents in the commentary rather than left as abstractions. What enters the earth is counted as the seeds and the drops of water that pass below its surface; what comes out of it is the plants, the vegetation and the fruit; what comes down from the sky is the rain, the snow and the hail, and with them the decrees and commandments the honourable angels bring down; what ascends into it is the angels and the deeds. The claim behind the list is that nothing crosses a border of the world uncounted, and the same point is made elsewhere with the finest possible units: (6:59 — *“Not even a leaf falls without His knowledge, nor a grain in the darkness of the earth or anything—green or dry—but is ˹written˺ in a perfect Record”*). The ascent of the deeds is not a figure the commentators invented either. There is a report in the Ṣaḥīḥ in which the Prophet ﷺ said that the deeds of the night ascend to Him before the day falls, and the deeds of the day before the night falls — an accounting with a twice-daily collection, which is the verse's surveillance rendered as procedure.

## Verse 57:5
> To Him belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth. And to Allah all matters are returned

**The Return of Affairs: What "Matters" Means**

The word for "matters" is umūr — affairs, concerns, the running business of existence — not "days" or "lives." The claim is therefore administrative: the conduct of everything now underway is heading toward a decision point that belongs to God. The same phrase closes one of the Book's most concentrated passages on trust: "And to Him all matters are returned. So worship Him and put your trust in Him." (11:123) The logic there is instructive for here: because the affair ends in God's court, it is rational to worship and entrust now. The sūrah of Iron uses the clause identically, as the engine of obedience, not as metaphysics for its own sake. Every deferred injustice has a deadline; every paid bill of the world is on a conveyor.

**"All Matters," Down to the Smallest Affair**

The word translated "matters" is plural and ordinary — umūr, affairs — which is its scope. Not "the great events" but every transaction the word covers: a loan made in secret, a grudge nursed for a decade, a child's burial, a treaty broken, the price of iron in the market at Taifa. The verse does not promise that these affairs end dramatically, only that they end at a particular place: "to Allah," the same preposition that opens the verse on ownership ("to Him belongs the kingdom"). Direction and destination match. This is why the parallel verse in Hud pairs the return with trust rather than fear: "And to Him all matters are returned. So worship Him and put your trust in Him." (11:123) Return to an owner is not annihilation of the affair but its delivery to judgment — and a judgment this sūrah will describe, at verses 12–15, in the calm language of receipts, lights, and walls.

**The Judge the Affairs Are Returned To**

The verse says where the affairs go; the commentary is concerned with who receives them. God is described there as the King and Owner of this world and the next, and then as the Most Just, who never falls into injustice by so much as the weight of a speck of dust. Attached to that justice is an arithmetic: a single good deed is multiplied up to ten times, and (4:40 — *“And if it is a good deed, He will multiply it many times over and will give a great reward out of His grace”*). The scene itself is fixed in the Book's own language: (21:47 — *“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least”*). The two belong together, and this verse's return of the affairs is the hinge between them. The conveyor carrying every transaction ends at a set of scales, and the scales are the reason a sūrah can ask for money without promising that anyone will see the receipt.

## Verse 57:6
> He merges the night into day and the day into night. And He knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart

**A Verb for the Overlapping of Hours**

The single verb behind "merges" is wlj, to enter — the night enters the day and the day enters the night, each slipping into the other and displacing it inch by inch. Translators render this as "causes to overlap" or "makes to merge," and the image is not the swing of a switch but the slow subtraction and addition of light: the lengthening of one share is the shrinking of the other. The Qur'an uses the same verb at the end of the Pilgrimage sūrah as evidence of sustained divine control: "That is because Allah causes the night to merge into the day, and the day into the night. Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing." (22:61) That pairing of hearing and seeing with the astronomical claim shows the genre of both verses: natural constancy is presented as an attribute of a knowing agent, not of a machine.

**"He Knows Best What Is in the Breasts"**

The second half changes scale from the horizon to the sternum. The Arabic says ṣudūr — chests or breasts — while the English translation's "heart" names the resident rather than the room. The physical image should be kept: the old Arabic mind located the seat of intention and secret thought behind the breastbone, and the phrase means that the contents of every chest are as present to God as the passing of dawn. The comparative in "knows best" is the phrase of God's superlative knowledge rather than a race among knowers; the same expression ends a verse elsewhere that ties the claim to exposure on the Last Day: "He knows whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And He knows whatever you conceal and whatever you reveal. For Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart." (64:4) The two verses are near twins: chest-knowledge here, concealment-and-revelation there, and in both cases the point of the knowledge is disclosure.

**God's Verbs, the Sky's Obedience**

Count the verbs in the first half: merges, one subject, no partners. The same verb-family is used elsewhere of subjection: "He merges the night into the day and the day into the night. Has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term." (35:13) The alternation of light and dark is so a proof by submission — a cosmos that obeys without choice is the courtroom in which a creature with choice will be judged. That is also why the clause lands the way it does: "He merges…" is physics stated as worship; "He knows…" is worship witnessed by omniscience.

**Lengths Apportioned, Not Merely Alternated**

The verb of merging is explained in the commentary as an act of measurement rather than of machinery: God alternates the night and the day and apportions them by His wisdom as He wills, making the night longer than the day at one season and the reverse at another, and at another making them equal, and turning winter into spring, spring into summer and summer into autumn. The seasons are named because they are the slowest version of the same act, and a cosmos whose hours are portioned out by decision is the setting the verse needs for its second half: He has full knowledge of what is in the breasts — He knows the secrets, however concealed they are. The two statements stand side by side without argument, as though the second followed from the first.

## Verse 57:7
> Believe in Allah and His Messenger, and donate from what He has entrusted you with. So those of you who believe and donate will have a mighty reward

**"From What He Has Entrusted You With"**

The single Arabic word behind "entrusted" carries the sūrah's anthropology: mustakhlaf fīhi, made to succeed others in holding a thing. The root is the one used for humanity's appointment in the first place — "˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.”" (2:30) — and the same idea returns with its test spelled out: "He is the One Who has placed you as successors on earth and elevated some of you in rank over others. He may test you with what He has given you." (6:165) Read against that background, the verse does not command generosity; it commands the honest handling of trust property. Unequal wealth is itself part of the trial, not a sign of favor, which is why this sūrah will refuse to read success as approval when it reaches the boast of the rich (verse 20). The trustee's freedom is in how he manages what was never his.

**The Rebuke at the End of the Line**

This translation prints the interrogative "Why Do You Not Believe?" at the close of verse 7, while most classical arrangements begin the next verse with the same question expanded — the Messenger calling, the covenant invoked. The double question (why not believe, and, at verse 10, why not give) frames the whole passage on wealth: both halves of the transaction are defended by showing that no ground exists for refusing either.

**Whose Wealth It Was Before It Was Yours**

The word for what has been entrusted is explained in the commentary with a history attached. The wealth now in your hands was in the hands of those before you and was then transferred to you, which makes the command to spend a command about somebody else's property held on trust; and the same gloss draws out the consequence no trustee likes — whoever inherits it after you may obey God with it and be happier than you were, or may disobey with it, in which case you will have helped him to the evil. The report set beside the verse makes the point in a single image. Aḥmad collected from ʿAbdullāh bin ash-Shikhkhīr: I came to the Messenger of Allah while he was reciting the opening of Sūrat at-Takāthur (102:1) and saying, “The son of Ādam says, my wealth, my wealth — and what belongs to you of it except what you ate and used up, or wore and wore out, or gave in charity and sent ahead?” Muslim collected it with the addition: other than that, you will go away from it and leave it behind for other people. The verse's trust is therefore not a compliment about ownership but a statement about custody, and the custody has a term.

## Verse 57:8
> Why do you not believe in Allah while the Messenger is inviting you to have faith in your Lord, although He has already taken your covenant, if you will ever believe

**A Question That Lists Its Own Evidence**

This verse asks the people why they do not believe, then answers its own question in the same breath. It gives the two things that make disbelief impossible: a messenger inviting them, and a covenant already taken from them. The grammar piles the reasons onto the question. It is like asking a man why he cannot leave a room that has two open doors.

The Qur'an often argues this way. Instead of saying "believe because it is true", it asks the denier to explain why he has not. Unbelief is not defeated by a new proof in this verse. It is embarrassed by old ones.

**The Covenant Taken From Every Person**

The covenant mentioned is, on the reading most commentators take, the scene the Qur'an describes in the sūrah of the Heights, where the descendants of Adam were brought out and asked: am I not your Lord? and they answered that He is, and that they testify to it (7:172 gives the exchange — *“˹Allah asked,˺ “Am I not your Lord?”*). That testimony, given before a person had any history of his own, is why disbelief is incoherent in this verse's logic. Faith is not something foreign added to a person. It is honouring a confession the self already made.

A minority of commentators took the covenant here to be the one given to the People of the Book through their prophets, a reading that fits a sūrah ending by addressing them. The context favours the wider one. The verse rebukes everyone, not one community with a scripture, and it says "your Lord" rather than "your Book", which fixes the content of the covenant to God's lordship, the very thing the scene in the Heights tested.

**The Faith That Astonished the Prophet ﷺ**

The question this verse asks is answered in the commentary by a report it had already gathered through several routes at the beginning of the Book of Faith in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. One day the Messenger of Allah asked his Companions: whom do you consider the most astonishing in faith among the believers? They said, the angels. He said, and what would keep them from believing when they are with their Lord? They said, then the prophets. He said, and what would keep them from believing when revelation comes down upon them? They said, then us. He said, and what would keep you from believing while I am among you? Then he named a further company: the most astonishing in faith are people who will come after you, who will find pages and believe in them. The rebuke in the verse is aimed at the generation with the least excuse — a messenger present, clear proofs being recited — and the report supplies the comparison that makes it land.

**Two Readings of the Covenant**

The covenant the verse invokes is read in two directions. One takes it as the pledge given to the Prophet ﷺ, the same thing the Book recalls when it says (5:7 — *“Remember Allah’s favour upon you and the covenant He made with you when you said, “We hear and obey.””*); the commentary prefers a wider referent, the covenant taken from mankind while they were still in the loin of Ādam, which is also Mujāhid's opinion and which Ibn Jarīr states as his own. The difference is not trivial. The first makes the verse an appeal to a pledge the hearers gave with their own mouths in Madīnah; the second makes it an appeal to a confession older than any of them. The commentary's own closing note — and God knows best — is the honest way to leave a question the verse does not settle.

## Verse 57:9
> He is the One Who sends down clear revelations to His servant to bring you out of darkness and into light. For indeed Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to you

**Revelation Described From the Sending End**

The verse continues the ground of belief by describing the mechanism of the invitation: God is "the One Who sends down" — the divine name-form munazzil, a heavy intensive participle, not a casual verb of delivery. Revelation in this sūrah is not an idea that occurred to a man. It is a descent, the same vocabulary the Qur'an reserves for the Book as for the rain, and the pairing is no accident: just as verse 20 will make the rain the figure of worldly growth, verse 9 makes descent the mode of what grows the interior. "Clear revelations," āyāt mubiynāt — the signs are characterized by clarity, not ambiguity, so that failure to believe can never be pinned on the evidence's obscurity, only on the will's refusal.

**From the Plural of Night to the Singular of Dawn**

In the Qur'an's phrase, the phrase "out of darkness into light" almost always keeps the darkness plural and the light singular, and this translation follows the Arabic exactly. The shape of that number is the whole sermon: error is many-hued — every delusion its own shade — while guidance is one. The same figure appears in Abraham's sūrah, where the Book's purpose is stated as leading "people out of darkness and into light, by the Will of their Lord." (14:1) The clause "by the Will of their Lord" is the companion here of "He is the One Who sends down": the exit is neither automatic nor self-caused. Light is given, darkness merely left, and the leaving must be renewed each day, which is why a community that has exited still needs a Messenger calling.

**"Clear Revelations": What āya Names**

The word translated "clear revelations" is āyāt — signs — in the plural and with the adjective mubināt, "making-distinct," from the root that means the sharp line between things. The same phrase, in the Book's telling, describes Moses's wonders — the signs "given" as proof to a court (17:101 — *“We surely gave Moses nine clear signs.”*) — and scripture itself; the overlap is the point. That is why the same root returns at verse 17: "We have certainly made the signs clear for you so perhaps you will understand" — the passage on hearts will reopen the same claim about evidence.

**What the Darkness and the Light Are Named**

The nouns of the verse are given contents in the commentary. The clear revelations are clear proofs, unequivocal evidences and plain attestations; the darkness is ignorance, disbelief and contradictory statements; the light is guidance, certainty and faith. The kindness and mercy at the end are then explained by their instruments — the sending down of the Books and the sending of the messengers, by which doubts are eradicated and confusion removed. The order of those glosses is the verse's own order: the evidence first, the transition second, the favour third, so that the reader is not told he has been favoured until he has been told what the favour consisted of. The commentary adds one structural observation that explains the verse's placement: after commanding belief and spending, God encourages belief again here and states that He has removed every barrier between the hearers and it, and then encourages spending again in the verse that follows.

## Verse 57:10
> And why should you not spend in the cause of Allah, while Allah is the ˹sole˺ inheritor of the heavens and the earth? Those of you who donated and fought before the victory ˹over Mecca˺ are unparalleled. They are far greater in rank than those who donated and fought afterwards. Yet Allah has promised each a fine reward. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do

**The Inheritor Argument**

The second "why not" of the passage now targets the hand instead of the heart, and its evidence is an attribute of God: He is the inheritor of the heavens and the earth. Inheritance assumes death of the owner — the title assumes that every current holder dies, and that what is "held" therefore lies, already legally, in an estate whose sole heir is named. The Qur'an elsewhere seals the same claim with the plainest possible verb sequence: "Surely it is We Who give life and cause death. And We are the ˹Eternal˺ Successor." (15:23) Spend what is His, the verse reasons, or decline and watch it pass to the same destination anyway; the only variable is whether the transfer earned anything. This is the most economical theology of wealth in the sūrah, and it converts stinginess from prudence into a legal misunderstanding.

**Hierarchy Without Despair**

Immediately after the distinction, the verse closes the gap it opened: "Yet Allah has promised each a fine reward." The gradation is real — ranks differ — and the promise is universal for both parties. This double sentence is the sūrah's model for every later comparison of the first and the second generation: measure acts, do not measure people's end. The same pairing of favor and fairness governs the closest parallel in the Book, where those who stayed home are promised "that which is most beautiful" even as the strivers receive a great reward above them (4:95 — *“Allah has promised each a fine reward, but those who strive will receive a far better reward than others”*). Exegetes who worried the verse discourages latecomers misread the order of the clauses: the "yet" comes after the distinction for exactly this reason — hierarchy is stated last only after hope is secured.

**The Two Groups the Verse Compares**

The verse asks why people do not spend, since God holds the inheritance of the heavens and the earth, and then states that the believers are not all equal: those who spent and fought before the victory hold a higher rank than those who came afterwards. The victory meant is the conquest of Makkah, and the jurists and commentators discussed whether the line was the pledge at Ḥudaybiyyah or the opening of the city itself; either way the verse is about a window of time that no later generosity can enter. The first Muslims who put their money into a community that had nothing are the ones the Book singles out, and it names them again in the Sūrah of Repentance as the forerunners, the first of the Emigrants and the Helpers (9:100 — *“As for the foremost—the first of the Emigrants and the Helpers—and those who follow them in goodness, Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him.”*).

**The Promise to Both**

The verse does not leave the later believers behind. It says God has promised the best to each — to both groups. The Book uses the same structure at 4:95 — *“Those who stay at home—except those with valid excuses—are not equal to those who strive in the cause of Allah with their wealth and their lives. Allah has elevated in rank those who strive with their wealth and their lives above those who stay behind ˹with valid excuses˺. Allah has promised each a fine reward, but those who strive will receive a far better reward than others”*, where those who strive with their wealth and lives are ranked above those who sit at home, and then states that God has promised the best to all. What the verse sets out to correct is not the generosity of the second group but the complacency that measures a person by what he does rather than by what he was given the chance to do. The chapter's own answer follows two verses later, where lending to God is repaid and multiplied (57:11 — *“Who is it that will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply ˹many times over˺ for them, and they will have an honourable reward”*), and the rest of the sūrah spells out where the money comes from in the first place.

**The Line Drawn, and the Argument It Settled**

The majority take the conquest named in this verse to be the conquest of Makkah; ash-Shaʿbī and several others take it to be the treaty at al-Ḥudaybiyyah, and the commentary produces a report that supports them. Aḥmad collected from Anas that Khālid bin al-Walīd and ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān bin ʿAwf fell out, and Khālid said to him: you boast about days you were present at before us. When the words reached the Prophet ﷺ he said: do not trouble my Companions, for by the One in whose hand is my soul, if you were to spend gold equal to Uḥud you would not reach their deeds. Khālid had come over to Islam between the treaty and the conquest, which is what makes the report evidence for ash-Shaʿbī's reading. The quarrel had a history of its own: the Prophet ﷺ had sent Khālid to Banū Jadhīmah after the conquest and they said “we have changed religion” instead of “we have submitted,” so he ordered their execution, and ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān bin ʿAwf and ʿAbdullāh bin ʿUmar opposed him — that is what the two men were still arguing about. In the Ṣaḥīḥ the same warning is put in stronger terms: none of you should revile my Companions, for by the One in whose hand is my soul, if one of you spent gold equal to Uḥud it would not reach a mudd of one of them, or half of it.

**Why the Second Group Is Not Left Behind**

The promise the verse makes to both parties is explained with a saying of the Prophet ﷺ that raises exactly the difficulty it answers: the strong believer is better and more beloved to God than the weak believer, and in both there is good. The commentary then explains the shape of the sentence — the Prophet ended by naming the good in the second type so that its qualities would not be forgotten while the first was being preferred, and so that the second would not be dismissed as degraded. The same care governs the distinction this verse draws. The ranking is then tied to something other than the amount given: God's awareness is what separates the two groups, His knowledge of the intention of the first, their sincerity, and their spending in times of hardship, poverty and narrow means. A report to the same effect is quoted: one dirham outstrips a hundred thousand. And it is a settled judgement among the people of faith that Abū Bakr aṣ-Ṣiddīq holds the largest share under this verse, because he spent his whole property seeking the face of God, and did it voluntarily — not to repay a debt, and not to return a favour anyone had done him.

## Verse 57:11
> Who is it that will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply ˹many times over˺ for them, and they will have an honourable reward

**The Metaphor That Should Be Impossible**

The Qur'an asks for a loan from the One who owns everything — and the ask is the most startling commercial image in the sūrah. Every element of the figure therefore breaks under scrutiny except its purpose, and the purpose is dignity: God did not say "give," because giving positions the giver below; borrowing positions him as the one doing the favor. The same phrase appears as an open invitation in the Coward's sūrah elsewhere — "Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺." (2:245) — and there the borrowing verb is bracketed by the assertion that He controls both directions of wealth's flow, so the risk of the loan is nil.

**What Makes a Loan "Good"**

Commentators did not leave the adjective qaraḍ ḥasan, a "goodly loan," undefined, and the conditions they compiled read like a manual. Al-Rāzī's ten-point list is the fullest: the money must be lawfully earned; it must be the best of what one owns, not the offcuts, which is the plain sense of "O believers! Donate from the best of what you have earned." (2:267). One must give while loving what is given, since "You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish." (3:92). It must reach the most deserving; secrecy is preferred; no reminders of favor may follow the gift; the giver must not seek to be seen; the gift must be counted small however large it feels; and the giver must not think himself better than the recipient. Strip the list to its nerve and it says: the "goodness" of the loan is completely about what the money does to the lender, not the borrower.

**Multiplication at Divine Rates**

The return on the loan is not interest — the word for it is ḍiʿf, doubling. The sūrah explains the rate by parable elsewhere: "The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills." (2:261) Seven hundred, "and Allah multiplies even more" — the arithmetic is offered precisely to be abandoned mid-sentence; the promise is growth without a ceiling, which no loan agreement has ever contained. The verb "multiply" here also governs the sūrah's next appearance of the same figure (verse 18), where the doubling is promised to men and women equally, the one detail the commercial metaphor needed most to survive intact.

**"Who Is It That Will…" — the Grammar of the Challenge**

The interrogative opening — man dhā alladhī, "who is it that will lend" — is one of the Qur'an's rare formulas, used of this loan in three other places (2:245 — *“Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over?”*; 64:17 — *“If you lend to Allah a good loan, He will multiply it for you and forgive you. For Allah is Most Appreciative, Most Forbearing”*; 73:20 — *“And ˹continue to˺ perform ˹regular˺ prayers, pay alms-tax, and lend to Allah a good loan.”*), and always attached to a demand for expenditure. Rhetorically it functions as scarcity made audible: the question pretends to count hands, and every believer who does give knows the question was about him. There is also, in classical preaching, a note of urgency — God does not say "many will lend"; He asks, as if the desk were open and the line short. Read next to verse 10's account of first-generation donors, the formula sharpens: those who spent before the conquest are exactly the answer to this question in its first years, and everyone after is being asked the same question with the same short line, minus the danger. Honour, in the verse's final clause, is the reward the risk cannot buy later.

**What the Loan Covers**

ʿUmar bin al-Khaṭṭāb read this verse as being about spending in the cause of God; others took it to be about spending on one's children. The commentary's own judgement is that the sense is more general than either, so that everyone who spends in God's cause with a sound intention and a sincere heart falls under it — a widening that matters, because the audience was being asked for money in several directions at once. The reward is then described in the verse's own terms: a handsome reward and a great provision, Paradise on the Day of Resurrection. The generosity of that reading is the point of the verse's interrogative form: the question is open, and so is the list of what qualifies as an answer.

**The Man Who Handed Over His Garden**

Ibn Abī Ḥātim records from ʿAbdullāh bin Masʿūd that when the verse on the handsome loan came down, Abū ad-Daḥdāḥ al-Anṣārī said: Messenger of Allah, does God ask us for a loan? He said, yes, Abū ad-Daḥdāḥ. He said, give me your hand — and the Prophet ﷺ put his hand in his. Then Abū ad-Daḥdāḥ said: I have lent my garden to my Lord. It was a garden of six hundred date-palms, and his wife and children were living in it. He went and called out, Umm ad-Daḥdāḥ! She said, here I am. He said, leave the garden, for I have lent it to my Lord. She said, that is a profitable bargain, Abū ad-Daḥdāḥ — and she moved out her goods and her children. The Messenger of Allah said: how many sweet date-clusters Abū ad-Daḥdāḥ has in Paradise. In another narration he said: how many a date-palm there is in Paradise, its clusters hanging low, full of pearls and gems, for Abū ad-Daḥdāḥ. The story is the verse acted out — the loan offered as a question, answered with a handshake, and paid at once by a family with nowhere else to sleep.

## Verse 57:12
> On that Day you will see believing men and women with their light shining ahead of them and on their right. ˹They will be told,˺ “Today you have good news of Gardens, under which rivers flow, ˹for you˺ to stay in forever. This is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph.”

**The Day Seen From Inside**

The verse's construction is disorienting on purpose: "On that Day you will see" — the seeing happens on the Day it describes, so the reader is pulled into the crowd of spectators at the very moment being foretold. Two genders are named explicitly, believing men and believing women, which the sūrah will repeat verbatim in verse 18 for charity; this pairing marks one of the Qur'an's consistent refusals to let the reward for giving run through the male line alone.

**Ahead of Them and on Their Right**

The direction is not decorative. The record of deeds arrives in the right hand, and the right side is where the recipients of that scroll are gathered, so the light's geometry is the geometry of acquittal: in front, what the future holds; on the right, what the account shows. The same coordinates appear in the Prophet's taught supplication for the believers' exit from it all — "Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right." (66:8) — a duʿāʾ that asks not for the absence of darkness but for light within it. Adds the one petition that belongs in the scene: "Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us." (66:8) The completion requested there is the detail this verse omits: light can be given, and it can be perfected. Its size, tradition says, is proportional to the deeds that earned it.

**"Good News" Addressed to the Already Saved**

The speech act inside the verse — "Today you have good news" — answers a question the scene would otherwise leave open: who greets them? The glad tidings are delivered to men and women whose light is already blazing, which means the announcement is confirmation, not information. The Qur'an elsewhere reserves the phrase "the ultimate triumph" for exactly this register — forgiveness and Gardens in exchange for lives and wealth: "So rejoice in the exchange you have made with Him. That is ˹truly˺ the ultimate triumph." (9:111) The trading vocabulary is why this sūrah's economics mattered: a triumph is the closing of a contract, and the good news reads like a receipt.

**Lights Measured in Mountains and Fingers**

The commentary reports from ʿAbdullāh bin Masʿūd, on the authority of Ibn Abī Ḥātim and Ibn Jarīr, an explanation of the light that answers the question of proportion: they will pass over the Ṣirāṭ according to their deeds — some with a light like a mountain, some like a date-palm, some like a man standing, and the least of them with a light the size of his index finger, lit at times and extinguished at others. The last of those images is the one to hold, because it is the only one that flickers; the smallest light in the scene is not steady, and the prayer for the completion of the light that the Book puts on the believers' tongues (66:8) is precisely a request that the flickering stop. Ad-Ḍaḥḥāk adds a detail to the other hand: on the right are their books of record, the sense the Book itself uses when it says (17:71 — *“So whoever will be given their record in their right hand will read it ˹happily˺ and will not be wronged ˹even by the width of˺ the thread of a date stone”*). He also explains why the prayer is needed at all — everyone is given a light on that Day, and when they reach the Ṣirāṭ the light of the hypocrites is put out, and the believers, seeing it, fear that theirs will be put out too.

## Verse 57:13
> On that Day hypocrite men and women will beg the believers, “Wait for us so that we may have some of your light.” It will be said ˹mockingly˺, “Go back ˹to the world˺ and seek a light ˹there˺!” Then a ˹separating˺ wall with a gate will be erected between them. On the near side will be grace and on the far side will be torment

**"Turn Back and Look for Light"**

The reply — turn around and look for a light — is delivered with the Book's customary savage politeness. Some exegetes take it as direction to the place where the believers first got their light: return to the source of revelation, the teaching, the covenant, and see if you can earn what you asked to borrow. Others hear it as dismissal to the world they abandoned — go back to the life where lights are made, and note that the road home from the Day is closed. Both readings converge on the deadline logic of verse 14: the time for seeking was the span before the Command came. The mockery is not divine cruelty added to the scene; the scene is what the choice looked like from the other side, and the sentence merely names the closed door they themselves built.

**The Wall, Its Gate, and the Early Commentary on 7:46 — *“There will be a barrier between Paradise and Hell. And on the heights ˹of that barrier˺ will be people who will recognize ˹the residents of˺ both by their appearance. They will call out to the residents of Paradise, “Peace be upon you!” They will have not yet entered Paradise, but eagerly hope to”***

A wall with a gate is erected between the parties — ṣakhra in one reading, the barrier in the other. Mujāhid, among the oldest exegetes, identified this barrier with the veil mentioned in al-Aʿrāf: "There will be a barrier between Paradise and Hell." (7:46) The identification is worth keeping: on the heights of that barrier stand people who recognize everyone by their marks — the same intermediate vision the hypocrites of this verse get, able to see the mercy they cannot enter. The gate in an otherwise seamless wall is the detail that turns geography into irony: an opening exists, and it faces away from them. Separation is not enforced by thickness but by direction; they stand on the far side of a door that was built, in this sūrah's account, out of their own un-borrowable minutes.

**The Divine Passive and the Absent Executioner**

It will be said to them: turn back. In the Qur'an's Last-Day dialogues the damned are habitually answered by voices that are present, not by the throne directly: the companions of the Fire are addressed by the keepers of Gehenna, by their former allies, by their own limbs in the chapter that catalogues excuses (40:47–48 — *“˹Consider the Day˺ when they will dispute in the Fire, and the lowly ˹followers˺ will appeal to the arrogant ˹leaders˺, “We were your ˹dedicated˺ followers, will you then shield us from a portion of the Fire?”*), and here by whoever stands at the gate.

**The Light the Believers Have**

The scene assumes that the believers are carrying light on that day, and the sūrah told us a verse earlier where it came from: their light runs before them and on their right hands as they ask God to complete it for them (57:12 — *“On that Day you will see believing men and women with their light shining ahead of them and on their right.”*), which is the same prayer the Book records on the tongue of the believers in the sūrah of Prohibition, when they say, keep our light burning and forgive us (66:8 — *“They will say, “Our Lord! Perfect our light for us, and forgive us.”*). The light is the Book and the faith the Book produces in a person's life; the Book calls itself a light (4:174 — *“O humanity! There has come to you conclusive evidence from your Lord. And We have sent down to you a brilliant light”*; 42:52 — *“You did not know of ˹this˺ Book and faith ˹before˺.”*), and it describes the believer as a man who walks among people by the light his Lord gave him, in contrast to the man who is all darkness (6:122 — *“Can those who had been dead, to whom We gave life and a light with which they can walk among people, be compared to those in complete darkness from which they can never emerge?”*). The hypocrite had heard the words and had even stood in the same rows; what he had never done was carry the light home. The verses that follow finish the picture with the exchange between the two groups: the believers will be asked whether they were not with them, and the answer they give is that they were, but the hypocrites tempted themselves, waited, doubted and were deceived by hopes until the command of God came (57:14 — *“But you chose to be tempted ˹by hypocrisy˺, ˹eagerly˺ awaited ˹our demise˺, doubted ˹the truth˺, and were deluded by false hopes until Allah’s decree ˹of your death˺ came to pass.”*). The light was theirs for the asking in the years when asking was still possible.

**The Scene the Verse Is Taken From**

Al-ʿAwfī and ad-Ḍaḥḥāk and others report from Ibn ʿAbbās the shape of the gathering this verse describes: when the people are assembled in darkness, God sends a light, and the believers walk towards it, and it is their guide from God to Paradise; and when the hypocrites see the believers following the light they follow them, and God puts out the light for the hypocrites, and they say, wait for us, let us take something of your light; and the believers say, go back behind you, to the darkness you were in, and look for a light there. The wall is then located in two ways. Al-Ḥasan and Qatādah place it between Paradise and the Fire; ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān bin Zayd bin Aslam identifies it with the barrier the Book describes in al-Aʿrāf (7:46), which is also reported from Mujāhid and others and which the commentary states to be correct. Its two faces are named accordingly: inside it is Paradise and all that is in it, and outside it is the Fire, on the authority of Qatādah, Ibn Zayd and others.

## Verse 57:14
> The tormented will cry out to those graced, “Were we not with you?” They will reply, “Yes ˹you were˺. But you chose to be tempted ˹by hypocrisy˺, ˹eagerly˺ awaited ˹our demise˺, doubted ˹the truth˺, and were deluded by false hopes until Allah’s decree ˹of your death˺ came to pass. And ˹so˺ the Chief Deceiver deceived you about Allah

**The Credential of Proximity**

"Were we not with you?" is the argument of the co-attendant: we stood in your rows, marched in your lines, bled at your sides — so share the payout. The verse does not deny the premise. The reply is "Yes" — you were — and the sūrah refuses the comfortable heresy that association with the righteous earns their wages. Presence was real, and it was everything the hypocrite had; that is precisely the indictment. The Book makes this claim elsewhere in the same terms, quoting the men who waited to see which way the wind blew and then claimed a seat in both victories: "So if Allah grants you victory, they say ˹to you˺, “Were we not on your side?”" (4:141) Attendance is cheap in this economy. The wall between the two camps exists because the sūrah's God has priced companionship by chest, not by coordinates.

**The Deluder, or the Delusion**

The last phrase of the reply carries a famous ambiguity. Read with one vowel as "the Deceiver" (gharūr), it is Satan — the chief liar whose trade is to make God's forbearance look like approval. Read with another as "delusion" (gharūr), it is the substance of wishful thinking itself: the man who sins and counts on amnesty, the community that hears "Most Merciful" and files the warning. Both readings are in the apparatus, and the sūrah rewards whoever keeps both, because the sentence works exactly like the mechanism it describes: the external tempter and the internal alibi are never found apart. The Qur'an uses the same formula as a warning to all humanity: "So do not let the life of this world deceive you, nor let the Chief Deceiver deceive you about Allah." (35:5) — deception "about Allah" is the one sin against God directly, a false report filed about His character.

**What They Say and What They Are Told**

The verse records the call of the men in the darkness to the believers, their claim that they were with them, and the answer: yes, but you led yourselves into trial, you waited, you doubted, and your hopes deceived you until God's command came. The Book then names the outcome: the deceiver deceived you about God. The scene follows the verse before it, where the believers' light runs ahead and the men are left in the dark, and the passage is the sūrah's account of the separation that happens on that day.

**The Word for the Hope That Deceived**

The Book names the wishful thinking with the same root it uses for the deceptive hope of the Sūrah of the Women, where the deceiver promises and the promise of Satan is a delusion (4:120 — *“Satan only makes them ˹false˺ promises and deludes them with ˹empty˺ hopes. Truly Satan promises them nothing but delusion”*), and the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān has the same word for the men whose hopes were false (3:185 — *“Every soul will taste death. And you will only receive your full reward on the Day of Judgment. Whoever is spared from the Fire and is admitted into Paradise will ˹indeed˺ triumph, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment”*). The deception in front of us is about God, which the commentators explained as the confidence that His mercy would cover a life that had been spent otherwise. The Book's sentence is one of the most sober statements about presumption in the whole of it.

**Where the Passage Sits**

The sūrah has been dealing with a community that had grown comfortable since its years of hardship, and the passage on the light and the darkness is placed after the account of the iron and the messengers. The verses that follow tell the believers to fear God and believe in His messenger (57:28 — *“O people of faith! Fear Allah and believe in His Messenger.”*), and the arrangement leaves the reader with the choice the passage describes rather than with the scene alone.

**What “With You” Consisted Of**

The question the verse quotes is given its content in the commentary: were we not with you in the life of this world, attending the Friday prayer and the prayers in congregation, standing with you at ʿArafah, fighting at your side, and performing every kind of worship with you? The clauses of the reply are then glossed one by one. On “you looked forward to destruction,” Qatādah said: to the destruction of the truth and its people. On “you doubted”: that resurrection follows death. On “you were deceived by false hopes”: that they said their sins would be forgiven, or that this life deceived them. On “the deceiver deceived you about God”: the deceiver is Shayṭān — and Qatādah added, they were deceived by Shayṭān, and by God, they went on being deceived until God threw them into the Fire. What the reply amounts to is then stated plainly: you were with us in bodies that had no hearts in them and no intentions, cast into doubt and suspicion, showing off to people and remembering God little. Mujāhid put the same point historically: the hypocrites were with the believers in this life, marrying among them and betraying them even while they mixed with them; they were dead; and both companies will be given a light on the Day of Resurrection, but the light of the hypocrites will be put out when they reach the wall, and that is when the two camps separate and part.

## Verse 57:15
> So Today no ransom will be accepted from you ˹hypocrites˺, nor from the disbelievers. Your home is the Fire—it is the ˹only˺ fitting place for you. What an evil destination!”

**"Nor from the Disbelievers" — the Collapse of a Social World**

The parenthesis makes the sūrah's most uncomfortable equation: the hypocrite and the open denier are now answered in the same breath, on the same terms. Nothing could be further from the chapter's earlier behavior, which kept the categories of Medina painfully distinct — ranks of donors, ranks of fighters, the wall of the preceding verses raised between two camps that attended the same funerals and markets. Here the distinction is retained and then voided: the hypocrites are addressed directly, "from you," and only then joined, "nor from them." The social world they tried to live in twice — inside and outside at once — is ended by a verdict that treats the outside as the only address that survived them. Their punishment, the sūrah implies, includes the loss of their own cleverness.

**The Ransom the Qur'an Always Refused**

"No ransom will be accepted" is not this sūrah's invention but the refrain of an entire Book-wide settlement about the Day's economy, and hearing the echo here sharpens the verse. The Cow that would be ransomed from the Fire by a lifetime on earth (2:96 — *“You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do”*), the wrongdoer who would offer all the earth's gold at the drowning moment (10:54 — *“If every wrongdoer were to possess everything in the world, they would surely ransom themselves with it.”*), the intercession and compensation both barred for the guilty (2:123 — *“And guard yourselves against the Day when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted, and no help will be given”*, 2:254 — *“O believers! Donate from what We have provided for you before the arrival of a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession. Those who disbelieve are ˹truly˺ the wrongdoers”*) — the Qur'an runs one consistent line across the legal language of blood-money and war-capture and applies it about the Last Dayly: the Day has no fidyā because it has no second-hand goods. No creditor who can be satisfied by anything the bankrupt party still holds. Verse 15 closes exactly that door, mid-sentence: "no ransom will be accepted from you, nor from those who disbelieved." What is left, in the market that failed, is only what verse 11 bought while currency was valid — a goodly loan, repaid with honor by the only lender who could guarantee a return after the exchange desk shut.

**The Two "Todays"**

The passage keeps saying "Today," and it is worth hearing how many verdicts the word is asked to carry. Verse 12 put it in the mouth of the good news: today, the garden, forever. Verse 15 puts it on the ransom: today, nothing accepted from you, forever. The Day's tense is a single instant stretched over both announcements, and the sūrah is not being repetitive — this Book's most careful the teaching about the Last Day is its vocabulary for time. "Today" here has no tomorrow beside it, which is exactly why the ransom fails and the gladness holds: both are payments settled in a currency that no longer circulates. The reader of 57:15 is meant to feel the adjective the saved got and the denied did not: their "today" came with news addressed to them personally. The hypocrites' "today" comes as a closed desk. Verse 14's deadline had made this inevitable; verse 15 makes it permanent — and the chapter then turns, mid-breath, from their today to the believers' hour.

## Verse 57:16
> Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah and what has been revealed of the truth, and not be like those given the Scripture before—˹those˺ who were spoiled for so long that their hearts became hardened. And many of them are ˹still˺ rebellious

**The Most Frightening Verse in a Community's File**

"Has the time not yet come" is astonishment again, but turned against the inside of the congregation, and the sūrah preserves for it a place in the earliest accounts of the Muslim conscience. ʿAbdullāh ibn Masʿūd is reported to have said that only four years had passed between this community's entry into Islam and the arrival of this verse — that is, the rebuke came before the first generation could settle. It is a datum the chapter's own logic supplies: the wall of verse 13 and the dialogue of verse 14 are not propaganda about hypocrites of old; verse 16 announces that the same failure mode is live in believing hearts, only years from its first symptom.

**Hardness in the Qur'an's Anatomy**

The Book's psychology of the heart is a clinic, and hardening is only one of its ward names. The Qur'an's images for the diseased organ travel across several figures at once: a covering over the eye rather than in it (22:46 — *“Indeed, it is not the eyes that are blind, but it is the hearts in the chests that grow blind”*), a sickness that fluctuates (2:10 — *“There is sickness in their hearts, and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment for their lies”*), a pigment laid down sin by sin until the surface loses reflectivity (83:14 — *“But no! In fact, their hearts have been stained by all ˹the evil˺ they used to commit”*), a seal whose key was thrown with the act, padlocks on the doors of entry (47:24 — *“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts”*). What the figures share is that each leaves the heart active — stained things still stain, locked rooms still have tenants. Hardness is the sūrah's chosen word because it names the one state in which the organ can no longer be soft at all, and the sūrah's cure matches: not information, but remembrance, the repeated wet touch of the Name upon the surface. Isaiah's prayer against this very fate — that God would un-harden a heart that has strayed, turn back and repair the inheritance — is quoted in the Hebrew Bible almost as the Qur'an phrases it here (Isaiah 63:17 — *“O Yahweh, why do you make us to err from your ways, and harden our heart from your fear? Return for your servants’ sake, the tribes of your inheritance.”*), the same complaint from the inside rather than the verdict from outside.

**Where the Report Comes From, and What It Forbids**

Muslim records from ʿAbdullāh bin Masʿūd that only four years separated this community's acceptance of Islam from the revelation of this verse, in which God admonished them gently — and Muslim places the report just before the end of his book, while an-Nasāʾī collected it in the exegesis of this verse. The admonition has a second half, and the commentary states it as a prohibition: the believers are forbidden to imitate those who were given the Book before them. What those communities did is listed — as time passed they changed the Book of God they held and sold it for a small and miserable price, they threw it behind their backs, they were taken up with opinions and false creeds, and they made their rabbis and monks into gods beside God — and the consequence given is the hardening: their hearts grew hard and would not accept advice, and were not humbled by God's promises or His threats. “And many of them are rebellious” is then read as rebellion in action, so that their hearts are corrupt and their deeds invalid, the same connection the Book makes when it says (5:13 — *“But for breaking their covenant We condemned them and hardened their hearts. They distorted the words of the Scripture and neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold.”*). The prohibition is therefore not about customs but about a sequence: a book laid down, a price taken for it, and a heart that stops flinching.

## Verse 57:17
> Know that Allah revives the earth after its death. We have certainly made the signs clear for you so perhaps you will understand

**The Book's Standing Proof**

The dead-earth argument is fixed Qur'anic property, and its wording here is only one instance of a formula: "There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from them to eat." (36:33) The verse pairs its own signs with the same claim: "We have certainly made the signs clear for you so perhaps you will understand." What the formula shows, by repetition, is that resurrection in the Qur'an is never argued from metaphysics alone — it is argued from agriculture, from the water cycle, from the annual resurrection everyone had watched and nobody had interpreted. That is also the sūrah's rebuke to hard hearts: the signs are not scarce, they are seasonal, and a community that sees the green shoot each spring and draws nothing from it has not missed the evidence, only the analogy. Understanding is offered, not imposed — the purpose clause says "so perhaps," the Qur'an's modest grammar of hope.

**The Living and the Dead in the Commentators' Ear**

Early teachers heard verse 17's pairing of life and death in two registers at once. One of the Prophet's most-quoted similes confirms the ear was right: "The likeness of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is like the living and the dead." (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 6407) Remembrance is what the heart drinks; the dead land and the dry heart share one diagnosis. Read beside verse 16, the hadith supplies the missing clause: the verse asks "has the time not come for hearts to humble," and the simile answers that the difference between a soft and a hard heart is the difference between life and death — no third state, no intermediate condition of "functional but fallow." The sūrah itself, remember, had called God the Giver of life and the Causer of death in verse 2. Verse 17 lets that name do its work on the reader's chest.

**What the Reviving of the Earth Is Made To Do**

The verse is explained in the commentary as three reversals rather than one: God brings softness to hearts after they have hardened, guides those who were led astray after their confusion, and relieves hardship after it has intensified. The mechanism is stated as an analogy the reader has already watched — as He brings the dead, dry earth back to life with the abundant rain it needs, so He guides the hardened heart by the proofs and evidences of the Qurʾān, and the light of faith reaches a heart again after it had been closed, so that no guidance could get into it. The praise that closes the gloss is balanced in a way worth keeping: praise belongs to the One who guides whoever He wills after they were astray, and who lets go whoever He wills after they were guided. Verse 16 asked whether the time had not come; verse 17 answers that the means is already in hand, and that it works on hearts the way rain works on dust.

## Verse 57:18
> Indeed, those men and women who give in charity and lend to Allah a good loan will have it multiplied for them, and they will have an honourable reward

The participle translated "those who give in charity" is muṣaddiqīn. Arabic will not let the sūrah's readers keep it in one sense: the same form means both "givers of ṣadaqa" and "verifiers of the truth." Charity is called by the name of sincerity because, in this Book's ledger, it is the proof of sincerity — the one act of worship that costs what the chest loves.

**The Loan Formula, One Verse Shorter This Time**

"Lend to Allah a good loan" returns verbatim from verse 11 — qaraḍan ḥasanan — and its reappearance now completed is the verse's contribution: back in 11 the question was who would come forward; here the payment is settled, and to both sexes named in the same breath. The multiplier is God's, not the market's: "The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains. And Allah multiplies ˹the reward even more˺ to whoever He wills." (2:261) The parable's arithmetic (seven hundred, "and more") matches this verse's verb yuḍāʿif — doubling in installments — and both keep the rate open-ended on purpose: the sūrah wants the reader to stop calculating at the second sentence. What stops being calculated starts being trusted.

**The Conditions Inside the Two Verbs**

The verse's two acts are given conditions in the commentary. Giving charity is described as spending from one's wealth, whether the giver is a man or a woman, on the needy, the poor and the weak; and lending God a handsome loan is explained as giving with a sound heart, seeking the pleasure of God, not seeking a worldly return or the appreciation of the person given to. The multiplication is then given its range: the good deed is multiplied from ten times up to seven hundred times, and beyond. What the verse calls an honourable reward is unpacked as a generous and handsome reward, a good dwelling to return to, and an honourable place of arrival. The verse is short, but nothing in it is left general — both sexes are named, the intention is specified, the rate is bounded only from below, and the reward is described as a place.

## Verse 57:19
> ˹As for˺ those who believe in Allah and His messengers, it is they who are ˹truly˺ the people of truth. And the martyrs, with their Lord, will have their reward and their light. But ˹as for˺ those who disbelieve and reject Our signs, it is they who will be the residents of the Hellfire

**The Plural That Widens the Door**

Through verses 7, 8, and 28 the sūrah has said "Allah and His Messenger," singular — the address of this community to its own prophet. Here, and again at verse 21, the formula switches to "His messengers," plural, and the switch is the verse's first piece of argument. Whoever believes in all of them is counted among the truthful; whoever believes in God while rejecting one of His messengers has, on the Qur'an's own logic, mis-believed in the message as delivered (2:285 — *“They ˹all˺ believe in Allah, His angels, His Books, and His messengers.”*). The plural also widens the audience at the exact moment the sūrah is paying out reward: this class is not the earliest donors of verse 10, not the martyrs alone, not the miracle-workers — it is everyone who affirmed the whole chain, which in the Hijaz included Jews and Christians for whom belief in the messengers was already the shape of their faith. The verse recruits them by its grammar before verse 28 recruits them by name.

**The Mirror Clause: Denying Signs After the Signs Were Cleared**

The verse assembles a ladder — prophets, the truthful, witnesses, and the righteous — that appears complete in only one other place, at the end of the Women's chapter, where whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger is promised company with that company (4:69 — *“And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company”*).

**The Two Titles and What They Mean**

The verse gives the believers in God and in His messengers, all of them, two names: the truthful and the witnesses. The commentators read *al-shuhadāʾ* in two ways: the martyrs who die for the faith, and those who bear witness by their lives. Both readings were kept, and the Book uses the word for the martyr in the Sūrah of the Family of ʿImrān, where those killed in God's cause are named as living and provided for (3:169 — *“Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead. In fact, they are alive with their Lord, well provided for”*). The Sūrah of the Women lists the same company the verse is describing, naming the prophets, the truthful, the witnesses and the righteous as the ones God has favoured (4:69 — *“And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company”*). The Book gives a rank here to plain belief joined to the work of the faith, and it does not wait for death to award the title.

**Why Belief in All the Messengers Is the Test**

The verse puts belief in God and His messengers together, without singling any of them out, and the Book makes that omission into a rule. The Sūrah of the Cow states the creed of the community as belief in all of them without distinction (2:285 — *“˹They proclaim,˺ “We make no distinction between any of His messengers.”*), and the Sūrah of the Women warns that those who believe in God and disbelieve in His messengers, or who divide between them, are the ones who have taken a middle course (4:150-151 — *“Surely those who deny Allah and His messengers and wish to make a distinction between Allah and His messengers, saying, “We believe in some and disbelieve in others,” desiring to forge a compromise”*). The community addressed in this sūrah lived among Christians and Jews, and the Book's answer to the arguing was not to pick a favourite messenger but to require belief in the whole line.

**The Light and the Other Company**

The second half of the verse names what waits on the other side: those who disbelieve and deny the signs are the companions of the burning Fire. The Book describes the same division with a picture in the Sūrah of the Prohibition, where the light of the believers runs ahead of them on the Day and the hypocrites are left in the dark (66:8 — *“Their light will shine ahead of them and on their right.”*). The verse before it tells the believers to fear God and believe in His messenger so that He may give them a double share of His mercy, and the passage ends with the same offer made to the community that had been given the Book.

**Where the Sentence Stops**

The commentators divided over where this verse's first sentence ends, and the division changes the list. Al-ʿAwfī reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that the meaning does not run on into the next clause, and that the martyrs with their Lord begins a new statement; Abū aḍ-Ḍuḥā is reported to have paused after “they are the people of truth” and then begun a fresh recitation at “and the martyrs with their Lord,” and Masrūq, ad-Ḍaḥḥāk, Muqātil bin Ḥayyān and others said the same. Al-Aʿmash narrated from Abū aḍ-Ḍuḥā from Masrūq from ʿAbdullāh bin Masʿūd a gloss on the two clauses together: they are of three categories — those who give in charity, the people of truth, and the martyrs. The argument for keeping them apart is drawn from the Book itself: at (4:69 — *“And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous—what honourable company”*) God distinguishes the people of truth from the martyrs within a single list, which shows them to be two ranks and not one, and that the ṣiddīq stands above the martyr.

**The Lofty Rooms and the Four Ranks of the Martyrs**

The reward named for this class is illustrated with a report Mālik recorded in his Muwaṭṭaʾ from Abū Saʿdīd al-Khudrī: the Messenger of Allah said that the people of Paradise will look up at the inhabitants of the lofty rooms as one of you looks at a brilliant star far off on the eastern or the western horizon, because of the superiority of some of them over others; and the people said, Messenger of Allah, are those rooms for the prophets, that no one else can reach them? He said, no — by the One in whose hand is my soul, they are for men who believed in God and believed in the messengers. Al-Bukhārī and Muslim collected it too, and the answer is the verse's own phrase turned into a promise. The martyrs are then described in the Ṣaḥīḥ report of their souls living in the bodies of green birds that fly where they will in Paradise and return to nests in lamps, and who, when their Lord looks at them and asks what they want, ask to be sent back to the world to fight in His cause and be killed as they were killed the first time. Aḥmad records from ʿUmar bin al-Khaṭṭāb a ranking in four grades: the believer true in his faith, who meets the enemy, discharges his duty to God and is killed — the one people will look up to, and here the Prophet ﷺ raised his head until his cap fell off, and ʿUmar did the same; the believer who meets the enemy and is struck by a stray arrow and dies of it; the believer who has mixed good deeds with evil ones and is killed doing his duty; and the believer who has sinned much and is killed doing his duty. ʿAlī bin al-Madīnī said of this chain that it is an Egyptian chain and sound, and at-Tirmidhī graded it ḥasan gharīb.

## Verse 57:20
> Know that this worldly life is no more than play, amusement, luxury, mutual boasting, and competition in wealth and children. This is like rain that causes plants to grow, to the delight of the planters. But later the plants dry up and you see them wither, then they are reduced to chaff. And in the Hereafter there will be either severe punishment or forgiveness and pleasure of Allah, whereas the life of this world is no more than the delusion of enjoyment

**A Picture of This World**

The verse says that the life of this world is only playing, amusement, decoration, boasting among yourselves, and competing for more wealth and more children. Then it gives a picture: it is like rain that makes the crops grow, and the farmers are delighted with what comes up, and then the crops dry and turn yellow and are finally left as broken stalks. Then the verse says that in the last life there is either a severe punishment or forgiveness and approval from God, and that the life of this world is nothing but the enjoyment of a delusion.

**The Five Things the Verse Names**

The verse lists five activities in the order they take over a life: play, which has no purpose; amusement, which is play with laughter in it; decoration, which is the care spent on appearances; boasting, which is the competition of families and tribes comparing themselves; and the race for more wealth and more sons, which is the point where a person's life becomes a scoreboard. The Book names the same habits in other places: it calls the world's life play and amusement in the sūrah of the Cattle (6:32 states it — *“This worldly life is no more than play and amusement, but far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter for those mindful ˹of Allah˺. Will you not then understand”*), and it names wealth and children as the trial that distracts people from remembering God in the sūrah of the Hypocrites (63:9 — *“O believers! Do not let your wealth or your children divert you from the remembrance of Allah. For whoever does so, it is they who are the ˹true˺ losers”* describes that distraction and 64:15 — *“Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward”* names the trial).

**The Crop and the Water**

The picture the verse draws uses the same rain that the sūrah used a few verses earlier to prove that God can bring the dead earth back to life. The rain is not the problem; the difference is where the story is stopped. If the reader stops at the green field, he is watching the part that pleases the sower; if he waits a few weeks, he is watching the same field turn yellow and break into straw. The sūrah of the Cave uses the same image for a man who was proud of his garden and then saw it destroyed (18:45 describes the crop reduced to scatter — *“˹It is˺ like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky.”*), and the sūrah of the Pen tells the story of a garden whose owners lost everything in one night (68:17 begins the account — *“Indeed, We have tested those ˹Meccans˺ as We tested the owners of the garden—when they swore they would surely harvest ˹all˺ its fruit in the early morning”*). The point of the picture is not that pleasure is evil; it is that the pleasure has a season and the season ends.

**What Follows the Season**

The verse sets the two outcomes of the last life beside the picture: a severe punishment, or forgiveness and approval from God. The Book keeps the same pair in front of its readers in the sūrah of the Bee when it says that what is with God stays and what people have passes (16:96 states it — *“Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting. And We will certainly reward the steadfast according to the best of their deeds”*), and in the same sūrah it promises a good life to the man or woman who does right and believes (16:97 mentions the good life and the greater reward — *“Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds”*). The word the verse uses for the world's pleasure at the end is the same word it used earlier in this sūrah for the deceiver who made the hypocrites think they were safe. The verse therefore says plainly what the world is: a show that pleases for a while, and a choice that decides what comes after it.

**What the Verse Asks of the Reader**

The verse does not ask the reader to abandon his work or his family. It asks him to know what he is looking at. The sūrah opened with everything in the heavens and the earth declaring God's praise (57:1 states it — *“Whatever is in the heavens and the earth glorifies Allah, for He is the Almighty, All-Wise”*) and it goes on to speak about the men and women who lend to God and about the light that runs ahead of the believers, and the verse in front of the reader gives the reader the reason to spend rather than to hoard: the crop will dry up anyway, and what a person sends ahead of himself is the part that lasts. The sūrah of the Mutual Disregard names the same two things, wealth and children, as the trial that tests people (64:15 states it — *“Your wealth and children are only a test, but Allah ˹alone˺ has a great reward”*).

**The Rain That Comes After Despair**

The word for the rain in this verse is ghayth, and the commentary defines it by its timing: it is the rain that comes down on people after they have despaired, the same word the Book uses when it says (42:28 — *“He is the One Who sends down rain after people have given up hope, spreading out His mercy”*). The delight named next is then read as a likeness for attachment — the farmers admire the growth that comes after the rain, and just as the farmer admires the vegetation, the disbelievers admire this life, being the most eager of people for its characteristics and holding it dearest. The parable's last three stages are applied twice: once to the field, which goes from green to yellow to scattered dust, and once to a human life, which is young and strong and handsome at the start, then changes in manner and weakens, then grows old and feeble until movement is hard for it and easy things are beyond it. The Book states the same curve in its own order: (30:54 — *“It is Allah Who created you in a state of weakness, then developed ˹your˺ weakness into strength, then developed ˹your˺ strength into weakness and old age”*). The verse's comparison is therefore not between a garden and a soul but between two things that end the same way at different speeds.

**How Close Both Outcomes Are**

Aḥmad records from ʿAbdullāh that the Messenger of Allah said: Paradise is nearer to any of you than the strap of his sandal, and so is the Fire. Al-Bukhārī collected the same report through ath-Thawrī. The commentary draws the inference the verse needs: both good and evil stand close to a person, and that is why God urges people to hasten to righteousness and obedience and to leave what is forbidden, so that their sins are forgiven and they attain reward and a high station. Placed where it is, the saying turns the verse's ending from a description into an instruction — the two outcomes named in its second half are not far-off alternatives but adjacent doors, which is exactly what the race in the next verse assumes.

## Verse 57:21
> ˹So˺ compete with one another for forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those who believe in Allah and His messengers. This is the favour of Allah. He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty

**"Forgiveness from Your Lord," First in the Lane**

The runners are told to race to two things, and the order is the chapter's whole spiritual economy in miniature: first forgiveness, then the Garden. Forgiveness precedes Paradise because, in this sūrah's bookkeeping, the ledger closes before the estate opens — the ransom that fails on the far side of the wall (verse 15) is the same debt the race is meant to outrun while prices still move. "From your Lord," not "from Allah": the relational name appears mid-command so the runner races toward the one who fed and owned them, the Lord whose kingdom opened the chapter. The Garden then gets described in the only figure the audience could verify from its own horizon — the width of the heavens and the earth — and the verse pauses on that image long enough to be worth weighing carefully, because its geometry is deliberately wrong.

**Width Without Length: The Geometry of Excess**

"A Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth" — the verse states the breadth alone, and the commentators noticed what a poet would not miss. Measure a garden by its width when its length is left unspoken, and the field goes past speech; the image is built to break the reader's arithmetic exactly at the point the sūrah has spent a dozen verses insisting divine payment outruns calculation (seven hundred-fold, and more — 2:261 — *“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a grain that sprouts into seven ears, each bearing one hundred grains.”*). The near-identical summons elsewhere makes the choice visible: "And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺." (3:133) Two commands, one image, different footnotes — there, prepared for the pious. Here, "prepared for those who believe in Allah and His messengers," the same plural door verse 19 opened. The shift matters to a sūrah that ends by telling the People of the Book that the estate is not inherited by lineage: faith in the messengers, not prior tenancy, is the deed.

**The Race the Poor Emigrants Were Given**

The verse's closing clause is the answer to a complaint recorded in the Ṣaḥīḥ. The poor emigrants came to the Messenger of Allah and said: the people of property have taken the higher ranks and the lasting enjoyment. He asked, how so? They said, they pray as we pray and fast as we fast, but they give in charity and we cannot, and they free slaves and we cannot afford it. He said: shall I not tell you of something by which, if you do it, you will overtake those who have gone ahead of you, and no one will surpass you except one who does the same — say “glory be to God,” “God is most great,” and “praise be to God,” thirty-three times each after every prayer. They went away and did it, and then came back: our wealthy brothers heard what we did and did the same. The Messenger of Allah said: that is the favour of God, which He gives to whom He wills. The sūrah's last words are the Prophet's own words in that report, and the commentary places them here for that reason: the race is real, the prize is not earned by running it, and both statements are made in the same breath.

## Verse 57:22
> No calamity ˹or blessing˺ occurs on earth or in yourselves without being ˹written˺ in a Record before We bring it into being. This is certainly easy for Allah

**One Noun for Both Accounts of the World**

The verse's key noun, muṣība, comes from aṣāba, to strike a mark: what "hits," whether the shot lands as ruin or as relief, is the verse's subject, and this translation's bracketed "or blessing" preserves what the Arabic insists on. That choice is the doctrine. Had the verse said "no disaster," the reader could file it under grief-counseling; saying "no stroke of fate at all," it writes out of the world, in one sentence, the category of accident. Everything that befalls — harvest and blight, birth and burial, the loan repaid and the loan defaulted — has the same prior status in the same book. The sūrah's audience, traders who lived on the edge between caravan-profit and caravan-loss, receives the two extremes of their year described in one noun. Even the market's god, fortune, is demoted to a clerical entry: there is no such thing as luck, only reading and its arrival.

**"In a Record Before We Bring It Into Being"**

The preposition matters more than the noun: the writing is before the creating — not a report filed after the event, not divine surveillance keeping pace with the world, but a text the world then performs. The same kitāb is the word for this sūrah's own medium, the revealed Book sent down with the messengers (verse 25), and the chapter lets the two usages touch without explaining them: what God writes, happens; what He reveals, is meant to be read. The Qur'an elsewhere names the prior register with the sūrah's own adjectives — a "preserved Tablet" (85:22), a "well-guarded Record" that swallows nothing (50:4 — *“We certainly know what the earth consumes of them ˹after their death˺, and with us is a well-preserved Record”*) — and the shared vocabulary signals that the Book is not a second, lesser kind of knowledge but the accessible copy of the first.

**"Easy for Allah" — the Refrain That Closes an Argument**

The verse ends on the clause that, in the Qur'an, marks a claim too large for human arithmetic but trivial for its addressee: "that is easy for Allah." The exact formula seals the same doctrine of pre-written life elsewhere: "And no one's life is made long or cut short but is ˹written˺ in a Record. That is certainly easy for Allah." (35:11) The word the two verses share for life's length and its shortening — yuʿammar, yunqas — shows the claim's reach: lifespans themselves are counted before they run. "Easy" here is not a softening but a leveling. What would be impossible for a god who had to wait and see — knowing in advance the free acts of every one of the billions of his creatures — is described as the least of His properties. The theology of verse 3's four names (knowledge of all things) returns as the warrant for verse 22's bookkeeping.

**Famine, Disease, and What Is Forgiven**

Qatādah read the verse's two halves as two named calamities: what befalls the earth is famine, and what befalls yourselves is suffering and disease. He is also reported as saying: we were told that a person is not struck by the prick of a thorn, a twisted ankle or a bleeding vein except on account of his sins, and what God forgives is more. The commentary then draws the doctrinal line the verse exists to draw, against those who deny God's prior decree and His knowledge of things before they occur, and produces a report to fix the timescale. Aḥmad recorded from ʿAbdullāh bin ʿAmr bin al-ʿĀṣ: I heard the Messenger of Allah say that God ordained the measures of all things fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth. Muslim collected it with the addition, and His Throne was upon the water; at-Tirmidhī collected it and said, ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ. The two glosses sit at opposite ends of the doctrine — the thorn-prick at the smallest end of experience, the fifty thousand years at the furthest remove from it — and the verse is what holds them in one sentence.

**What “Easy for Allah” Unpacks**

The closing clause is explained as a statement about the kind of knowledge involved: God knows all things before they occur and records them exactly as they will occur when they occur, and this is easy for Him. The gloss then goes a step further than the verse, and it is the step that has done most of the work in later theology: God knows what happened, what will happen, what did not happen, and — had it happened — what shape and form it would have taken. A knowledge that includes the counterfactual is not a larger version of a record; it is a different order of thing, and to describe it as easy is the verse's way of refusing to turn the distinction into a difficulty for its addressee.

## Verse 57:23
> ˹We let you know this˺ so that you neither grieve over what you have missed nor boast over what He has granted you. For Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful—

**The Doctrine's Purpose Is Written With It**

Verse 22 announced a complete record; this verse states, in its own first words, what the announcement is for — and the list is therapeutic, not metaphysical. "So that you neither grieve over what has escaped you nor exult over what He has given you." The two passions are the whole weather of a grasping life: loss experienced as theft, gain experienced as merit. The sūrah does not ask its audience to stop feeling either; the verbs are not "do not feel" but "so that you do not feel," with the doctrine proposed as the instrument. A belief whose stated function is the equilibrium of the heart cannot be, on this chapter's own account, a license for passivity or an excuse for cruelty — those would both re-import the two feelings through the back door. The purpose clause is a test case for every serious reading of verse 22: whatever interpretation leaves the reader more despairing about the missed or more swollen over the given has failed the sūrah's stated aim.

**"What Has Escaped You" — Grief's Grammar Repaired**

The Arabic for "what escaped you" is mā fātakum, from fūt — passing by, missing, the root used for a caravan that has gone ahead and for a deadline that has overtaken you. The loss-language of bereavement and market-loss share this root elsewhere in the Book's the teaching about the Last Day — the ruined deniers at the harvest of their life will call themselves "the deprived" (maḥrūmūn, 56:67 — *“In fact, we have been deprived ˹of our livelihood˺.”*) — and the sūrah's repair runs the other way: if what escaped was written as escaping before it was yours, its escape is not a robbery.

**The Believer's Balance — a Hadith with This Verse's Shape**

The hadith Suhaib transmitted about the believer's affair is, in miniature, verse 23 as a saying of the Prophet ﷺ: everything is good for the believer. This is nobody's affair but the believer's — if ease comes to him, he is grateful, and that is good for him; if hardship comes, he is patient, and that is good for him (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, book of asceticism — wording as transmitted; number not re-verified this session). The two halves of that sentence are this verse's two verbs: neither exult, nor grieve — and the hadith supplies what the verse assumes, the engine of both restraints, which is tawḥīd of the outcome: if ease is a gift (uʿītum, passive) and loss is written (verse 22), neither event licenses the self the verse is protecting against.

The clause arrives as a proverb the Qur'an quotes in almost identical words elsewhere — "Surely Allah does not like whoever is arrogant, boastful." (31:18) — and the pair of nouns is worth its weight in walking.

**What Has Met You Could Not Have Missed You**

The purpose clause is explained in the commentary as the reason the doctrine was announced at all: we have told you of our encompassing knowledge, and of the recording of all things before they occur and their creation in a measure known to us, so that you may know that what has met you could never have missed you, and what has missed you could never have met you; so do not grieve for the fortune you have lost, for had it been decreed for you, you would have reached it. The clause on rejoicing is read the same way, and a variant reading is noted — nor rejoice over what came to you — with the commentary's judgement that both senses belong together. The prohibition on boasting is then given its ground: do not boast to people over what God has favoured you with, because you did not earn it by your own efforts; it came your way because God decreed it for you and provided it as your provision. ʿIkrimah is quoted summarising the whole verse in one sentence: every one of us feels joy and feels grief — so make your joy gratitude, and bear your grief with patience. The verse does not ask for the absence of either feeling; it asks for the two feelings to be answered differently.

## Verse 57:24
> those who are stingy and promote stinginess among people. And whoever turns away ˹should know that˺ Allah ˹alone˺ is truly the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy

**Whose Stinginess, and Whether It Matters**

The reports about the occasion of this verse are local and social. One says it concerns hypocrites in Madinah who gave charity in order to be praised and held back in order to stay safe, the same group behind the verses about people who mocked those who gave (9:79 names them — *“˹There are˺ those who slander ˹some of˺ the believers for donating liberally and mock others for giving only the little they can afford. Allah will throw their mockery back at them, and they will suffer a painful punishment”*). Another report gives a family saying, that what is in their hands they cannot let go of, spoken by a man asked to feed his relatives.

Some commentators widen the meaning further and read stinginess here as withholding knowledge as well as money, teaching as well as coins. That breadth fits the sūrah, which has been addressing a whole community's wealth from its early verses rather than one case file. The occasional reports add texture without narrowing the claim. The one reading that fails is the narrowest, that this is only a rule for individuals, because the second half of the verse speaks of whoever turns away, and turning away in this sūrah is precisely a refusal of the public act of giving that the inner life was being tested by.

**The Verse's Own Precedent for Its Last Two Names**

The names that close this verse are explained in the commentary by an earlier instance of the same sentence. The misers are glossed as those who commit evil and urge people to commit it, and whoever turns away is glossed as whoever turns away from keeping God's commands and obeying Him; and the answer to both — God is Rich, worthy of all praise — is set beside the words of Mūsā to his people: (14:8 — *“If you along with everyone on earth were to be ungrateful, then ˹know that˺ Allah is indeed Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy”*). The pairing does the work the verse leaves implicit. The withholding damages nothing in the One withheld from, and the self-sufficiency named at the end is the reason the command can be given without coercion. Stinginess is therefore not a loss to God but a loss to the one who turns away, which is why the sentence about Him reads as praise rather than as a threat.

## Verse 57:25
> Indeed, We sent Our messengers with clear proofs, and with them We sent down the Scripture and the balance ˹of justice˺ so that people may administer justice. And We sent down iron with its great might, benefits for humanity, and means for Allah to prove who ˹is willing to˺ stand up for Him and His messengers without seeing Him. Surely Allah is All-Powerful, Almighty

**The Verse That Named the Sūrah**

A chapter of loans and lights is titled by one clause of metal, and the naming preserves an instinct worth recovering. Iron is the verse's hinge-word, and the sūrah's Arabic readers titled it al-Ḥadīd because the definite noun — "the iron" — is the only thing in the passage that arrives with the same verb as the Book and the Balance and belongs, unmistakably, to the physical world: what God "sends down" here is law, standard. Ore, three objects of one verb, and the ore is the proof the other two are meant to produce in history.

**Clear Proofs, Scripture, Balance: The Instruments of the Third Person**

The verse begins with the familiar mission formula — messengers sent with bayyināt, clear proofs, the same term used at verse 9 for the revelations sent down on the servant — and then adds two instruments with one purpose clause: the Book and the Balance, "so that people may administer justice." The Balance (mīzān) carries, in classical exegesis, both its literal sense — the weighing that makes commercial fraud a sin this Book legislates against — and its transferred one: the standard by which any claim is weighed, the law itself, since the same pairing appears elsewhere: "It is Allah Who has revealed the Book with the truth and the balance ˹of justice˺." (42:17) The purpose clause, though, is the verse's innovation: the instruments exist so that "people" stand upright with justice — not so that heaven administers it. The grammar of iqāma (to set upright) makes the community the builder and the revelation the blueprint, the one thing sent down for the sake of what people must raise.

**The Three Things Sent for One Purpose**

The verse names three gifts given with the messengers: the clear proofs, the Book, and the Balance. The purpose is stated in the middle of the sentence: so that people may stand up in justice. The Balance is the measure that makes fairness possible in the market and in the court, and the Book returns to it in the Sūrah of the Most Merciful, where the sky is raised and the scale is set so that people do not cheat in weighing (55:7-9 — *“As for the sky, He raised it ˹high˺, and set the balance ˹of justice˺”*). Revelation and law arrive together, and the verse ties the worship of God to the way a person handles his neighbour's share.

**The Iron and the Word Used for It**

Then it says that God sent down iron, with great strength in it and benefits for people, and it gives a second purpose: so that God may know who helps Him and His messengers unseen. The Book calls the creation of a metal a sending down because it comes from the earth by God's arrangement, and the clause about strength is placed here because a just order sometimes needs to be defended. The jurists took the verse as one of the root texts on authority and force: law, measure, and the power to protect them. What it says about the users of that power is the hard part — the defender is promised nothing in the verse except that God sees him.

**Where the Passage Continues**

The next verses name Noah, Abraham, the line of prophets, and Jesus, and they say that the followers were given the Book and that some of them fell short (57:26-27 — *“Then in the footsteps of these ˹prophets˺, We sent Our messengers, and ˹after them˺ We sent Jesus, son of Mary, and granted him the Gospel, and instilled compassion and mercy into the hearts of his followers.”*). The whole passage is the sūrah's summary of religious history, and the point it makes with the iron is that the community receiving the message is also receiving the duty. The Sūrah of the Prophets states the outcome: the earth will be inherited by God's righteous servants (21:105 — *“Surely, following the ˹heavenly˺ Record, We decreed in the Scriptures: “My righteous servants shall inherit the land.”*), and the Book repeats the command to stand for justice in the Sūrah of the Women, even against oneself (4:135 — *“O believers! Stand firm for justice as witnesses for Allah even if it is against yourselves, your parents, or close relatives.”*). Iron is not the lesson; the lesson is what a person does with the strength he has been handed.

**The Balance, and What the Commentators Mean By It**

Al-mīzān in this verse is explained as justice on the authority of Mujāhid, Qatādah and others, and the commentary widens the sense to the standard by which claims are weighed: the truth to which sound and upright minds attest, against misguided opinions and ideas. The Book's own pairings are produced for the point — the clear proof from the Lord with a witness from Him following it (11:17), the natural religion God created people upon (30:30), and (55:7 — *“As for the sky, He raised it ˹high˺, and set the balance ˹of justice˺”*). The purpose clause is read with the same seriousness: that people may stand upright with justice means the truth and fairness found in obeying the messengers, in everything they conveyed from their Lord, and in following all that they commanded. What the prophets brought is the truth beyond which there is no truth, and the Book says so of its own word, (6:115 — *“The Word of your Lord has been perfected in truth and justice”*) — true in what it reports and just in what it commands and forbids. It is why the believers, when they take up their rooms in Paradise, say what the verse records of them: (7:43 — *“The messengers of our Lord had certainly come with the truth”*).

**Iron as a Deterrent, Iron as a Tool**

The iron clause is explained in two registers. In the first, God made iron a deterrent for those who refuse the truth and oppose it after the proof has been established against them; and the sequence is set out historically — the Messenger of Allah remained in Makkah for thirteen years while revelation came to him with the arguments against the idolaters and the detailed proofs of God's oneness, and when the evidence had been established against those who defied him, God decreed the migration and then ordered the believers to fight. Aḥmad and Abū Dāwūd recorded from ʿAbdullāh bin ʿUmar that the Messenger of Allah said: I was sent with the sword just before the Hour, so that God alone is worshipped without partner; my provision was placed under the shadow of my spear; those who defy my command are disgraced and humiliated; and whoever imitates a people is one of them. In the second register the same metal is a tool: the mighty power named is weaponry — swords, spears, daggers, arrows, shields — and the benefits for mankind are livelihood, the coins, hammers, axes, saws, chisels and shovels people use for tilling, sowing, cooking, kneading and making the rest of what they need. The test in the verse is then read as a test of intention: that God may know who helps Him and His messengers unseen means, whose intention in carrying the weapon is the defence of God's religion and His messenger. The gloss closes with a sentence that removes any pride from the command: God does not need the help of mankind, but He ordered the struggle in order to test people by means of one another.

## Verse 57:26
> And indeed, We sent Noah and Abraham and reserved prophethood and revelation for their descendants. Some of them are ˹rightly˺ guided, while most are rebellious

**From Metal to Genealogy — the Sūrah Changes its Proof**

The chapter has run on two proof-structures: the cosmos that praises and the wallet that tests. Verse 25 introduced instruments — messengers, Book, Balance, iron — and verse 26 now supplies the instruments' history, and the transition is the argument's: the Book did not drop out of the sky, it dropped into a lineage. "We sent Noah and Abraham" — the two names that bracket the covenant story, the survivor of the flood and the father of the prophets — and the sūrah compresses the rest of the chain under the word "prophethood." What is claimed for the lineage is precise and modest: prophethood and the Book were reserved among their progeny (the verb jaʿala here reads "placed, appointed"), which is an assertion about the distribution of revelation, not about inherited virtue. The next clause immediately blocks that second reading: "some of them are guided, most are transgressing." The line that carries the Book does not automatically carry its keepers.

**What the Two Names Bracket**

The commentary states the genealogy more precisely than the verse looks. Every prophet God sent after Nūḥ was from his offspring, and every revealed Book and every messenger who received revelation after Ibrāhīm, the friend of God, was from Ibrāhīm's offspring — the same reservation the Book makes in Ibrāhīm's own story, (29:27 — *“and reserved prophethood and revelation for his descendants”*). The chain is then closed at a particular point: the last of the prophets of the Children of Israel was ʿĪsā son of Maryam, who gave the good news of the coming of Muḥammad. Read forward, that closing is what makes verse 29 necessary. A distribution that runs through one family and ends with the announcement of a prophet from another branch of it is exactly the arrangement a claim of inherited right would be built on, and the sūrah dismantles the claim three verses later.

## Verse 57:27
> Then in the footsteps of these ˹prophets˺, We sent Our messengers, and ˹after them˺ We sent Jesus, son of Mary, and granted him the Gospel, and instilled compassion and mercy into the hearts of his followers. As for monasticism, they made it up—We never ordained it for them—only seeking to please Allah, yet they did not ˹even˺ observe it strictly. So We rewarded those of them who were faithful. But most of them are rebellious

**"We Placed Compassion and Mercy in Their Hearts"**

Before it reproaches them for anything, the verse praises what God Himself did in the community of Jesus: raʾfa — tenderness, a word soft enough to describe hands over a child — and raḥma, the same root as the chapter's divine names, placed not in books but "in the hearts" of the followers. The placement is the point: the fruit of the Gospel is described as an interior fact, the exact category (chest-versus-row) this sūrah has been interrogating since verse 6. It is also the sūrah's answer to the suspicion that faithfulness requires a perfect law: the disciples of Jesus are credited with a grace no legislator can install. The verse can then criticize their later invention without contradiction, because its standard throughout has been the one God writes directly on hearts — compassion is the receipt revelation leaves, and everything else is bookkeeping. The same pairing appears in the description of this Prophet's own company: "And those with him are firm with the disbelievers and compassionate with one another." (48:29)

The verse ends with the ratio of the previous verse almost unchanged — those who believed among them receive their reward, and most of them are transgressing — and the repetition is structural, not lazy: whatever the community's law, the Day divides by fidelity, not by affiliation. "So We rewarded those of them who were faithful" — awbaynā, "We gave them their wage," in the past tense the sūrah already used for the martyrs' settled account (verse 19) — settles the question the monastic paragraph raised about inventors: seeking God's pleasure even by a path no one prescribed still reaches the Sought. And the "most are rebellious" clause keeps the verse from becoming praise of the cloister at all: what is rewarded is sincerity, which is rare in every institution, including the ones with better rules..

**What the Verse Reproaches and What It Praises**

The reproach is aimed at the invention: the monks adopted a way that God had not prescribed for them. But the same sentence praises what was there before the invention, naming the tenderness and mercy God placed in the hearts of the followers of Jesus, and the Book states the failure of some to observe what they had taken on. The distinction is the sūrah's whole method: the goodness of the people is credited to the gift of God, and the fault is put on the practice that nobody was asked to invent. The Sūrah of the Table calls the monks and priests the ones closest in affection to the believers (5:82 — *“That is because there are priests and monks among them and because they are not arrogant”*), and it records why: some of them are learned men and priests who are not arrogant.

**What the Prophet Taught About Extremes**

The Prophet answered the same instinct when three men came to him intending to go beyond the law in worship. One said he would never marry, one said he would fast without breaking, and one said he would pray all night. He told them that he was the most mindful of God among them and still fasted and broke his fast, prayed and slept, and married. The report is in Bukhārī, and it gives the community the rule: a way of life that God has not prescribed is not a higher way. The jurists built on this passage the principle that the religious duty is the law, and that a person's vow may bind him only to something that is permitted.

**The Other Half of the Verse**

The verse adds a sentence about the believers later: those who believed and did good work, God will give them their reward. The next verse carries the same promise to the communities of the Book, telling them that if they fear God and believe, they will receive a double share of His mercy (57:28 — *“O people of faith! Fear Allah and believe in His Messenger.”*). The sūrah does not leave the passage on a note of rebuke alone. It states the fault of the monks and then holds the door open to the same people, and the last words of the verse before it name God as forgiving and merciful.

**How the Monasticism Started**

Ibn Jarīr and Abū ʿAbd ar-Raḥmān an-Nasāʾī record, in an-Nasāʾī's wording, from Ibn ʿAbbās an account of what lies behind this verse. There were kings after ʿĪsā who changed the Torah and the Gospel while there were still believers reciting them, and the kings were told that no people had ever confronted them with harsher criticism and abuse than these, who recite (5:44 — *“And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are ˹truly˺ the disbelievers”*) and accuse us of shortcomings in our deeds while they go on reciting; so summon them, and let them recite these verses in our way and believe in them in our way. The king gathered them and threatened them with death if they did not give up the original Torah and Gospel for the corrupted version. They asked to be left alone. One group said: build us a narrow tower we can climb, and give us a means of raising food and drink up to us, and you will be spared hearing us. Another said: let us go about the land and eat and drink as beasts do, and if you find us in your own land, kill us. Another said: build us houses in the deserts and the remote places, where we can dig wells and plant vegetables, and we will not oppose you or even pass by you. These groups had supporters among their own tribes, and it was over them that the verse came down — which makes the invention in the verse a retreat under threat, and the reproach a reproach of a bargain rather than of a longing.

**The Rahbāniyyah of This Community**

The verse's subject is given a counterpart in the reports the commentary gathers. Aḥmad recorded from Anas bin Mālik that the Prophet ﷺ said: every prophet has a rahbāniyyah, and the rahbāniyyah of this community is struggle in the cause of God; al-Ḥāfiẓ Abū Yaʿlā collected it with the wording, every community has a rahbāniyyah, and the rahbāniyyah of this community is struggle in the cause of God. Aḥmad also recorded that a man came to Abū Saʿdīd al-Khudrī asking his advice, and Abū Saʿdīd said that he had asked the same of the Messenger of Allah and was told: I advise you to keep to the fear of God, for it is the head of every matter; discharge the obligation of struggle, for it is the rahbāniyyah of Islam; and keep to the remembrance of God and the recitation of the Qurʾān, for it is your standing in the heavens and your good name on earth. Only Aḥmad collected this report. The two sayings answer the verse's reproach without reversing it — the instinct behind monasticism, a life given wholly to God, is not denied, but its form in this community is set by the law rather than invented against it.

## Verse 57:28
> O people of faith! Fear Allah and believe in His Messenger. ˹And˺ He will grant you a double share of His mercy, provide you with a light to walk in ˹on Judgment Day˺, and forgive you. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful

**A Vocative With a Double Address**

"O you who believe" is, everywhere in the Qur'an, the vocative of the in-group, the summons to the congregation of Muhammad ﷺ — and yet this sūrah's final movement uses it for the people it has just been describing, the followers of earlier scriptures, since verse 29's purpose clause names them as the ones meant to learn something from the whole exchange. The commentators felt the tension and mostly resolved it by letting the vocative address, in priority, the People of the Book who already "believe" in the sense their own scriptures command: fear God and believe in His Messenger — an invitation, not a rebuke. The secondary sense, pressed by those who note how often this sūrah turns on its own assembly (verse 16 being the sharpest case), is that the verse says to Muslims: your faith is not a completed transaction; believe again, through the same two verbs. Priority belongs to the conversion address — the double reward below fits it and no other — but the sūrah plainly intends both audiences to feel the sentence, and the vocative's ambiguity is the mechanism.

**"A Double Share of His Mercy" — the Arithmetic of Sequence**

The difl, a twofold portion, is the sūrah's payment for a convert with a prior faith: reward for the old fidelity and reward for the new recognition, both in one name. The hadith literature gives the arithmetic its canonical case — among the three who receive double reward, on the Prophet's report recorded by al-Bukhārī, is "a man from the People of the Book who believed in his Prophet and then believed in Muḥammad" — and the sūrah's own prior verses do the theology: verse 27 has just rewarded the faithful among the Christians of the rahbāniyya passage. The double share here is not a bonus for the clever but the settlement of two honest accounts.

**A Light to Walk By — the Chapter's Central Image Returns**

Everything in this sūrah about light has been building to this quieter clause. The Book's warning on the other side of the promise is stark in the same phrase: "And whoever Allah does not bless with light will have no light!" (24:40) What the sūrah has been describing since verse 7 is the one transaction in which the buyer receives both the coin and the visibility to use it.

**The Three Who Are Paid Twice**

The double share is explained by the report that gives it its canonical cases. From ash-Shaʻī, from Abū Burdah, from his father, from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, the Messenger of Allah said: three will be given their reward twice — a man from the People of the Book who believed in his own prophet and then believed in me; a slave who discharges God's rights and his master's duty; and a man who has a slave-girl, educates her well and teaches her good manners without harshness, then frees her and marries her. The report is in the two Ṣaḥīḥs. The reading it supports is the one an-Nasāʾī reports from Ibn ʿAbbās, that the verse concerns the People of the Book who accept Islam, and ad-Ḍaḥḥāk and ʿUtbah bin Abī Ḥakīm and others agreed with him, a preference Ibn Jarīr states as his own. The Book's parallel promise is (8:29 — *“O believers! If you are mindful of Allah, He will grant you a standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺, absolve you of your sins, and forgive you”*). A report of another kind is added for the arithmetic: Saʿdīd bin ʿAbd al-ʿAzīz said that ʿUmar bin al-Khaṭṭāb asked a rabbi of the Jews what the greatest increase of reward among them was, and he answered, a kifl, which is about three hundred and fifty good deeds; and ʿUmar said, praise be to God, who has given us two kifls — and Saʿdīd then quoted this verse, adding that the two kifls of Friday are of the same kind. Ibn Jarīr recorded it.

## Verse 57:29
> ˹This is so˺ that the People of the Book ˹who deny the Prophet˺ may know that they do not have any control over Allah’s grace, and that all grace is in Allah’s Hands. He grants it to whoever He wills. For Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty

**"No Control Over Any of Allah's Grace" — Dismantling a Franchise**

The phrase — lā yaḍillūna min shay'in min faḍli Llāh, literally "they have no hold on anything of God's favor" — is aimed at a concrete claim. The sūrah's own audience makes it recoverable: if prophethood was, by God's act, placed in Abraham's progeny (verse 26), a descendant could be tempted to read the placement as property and the scripture as an inherited deed, so that the last installment — arriving through Ishmael's house — would be legally void. Verse 29 answers by relocating everything: the covenant's distribution is a grace, grace by definition is unowed, and a grant no one can veto cannot have been a franchise. The hadith parable the classical teachers cited against exactly this resentment — the laborers hired dawn to noon, noon to afternoon. The last hour for double, who grumble when the final crew is paid first ("That is My grace, which I give to whom I will," Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 2269) — is a saying of the Prophet ﷺ whose conclusion quotes this verse's words. Its existence shows how early the Hijazi congregations heard verse 29 as the settlement of the wages argument, not as abstract predestination.

**Why the Verse Says It Twice**

The chapter closes by saying that the People of the Book should know that they have no control over anything of God's bounty, and that the bounty is in God's hand and given to whom He wills. The statement is aimed at two claims, and the passage names both. The first is the claim of the earlier communities that the favour of God belonged to them by line and by book, and the Sūrah of the Table answers it in the same terms when it tells the People of the Book that God does not wrong anyone and that the bounty is His to distribute (5:18 — *“The Jews and the Christians each say, “We are the children of Allah and His most beloved!” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Why then does He punish you for your sins? No! You are only humans like others of His Own making. He forgives whoever He wills and punishes whoever He wills. To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and everything in between. And to Him is the final return.”*). The second claim is the opposite one: that the newcomers could expect nothing because the promise went elsewhere, and the verse before this one has just promised those who believe among the people of the Book a double share of mercy (57:28 — *“O people of faith! Fear Allah and believe in His Messenger.”*).

**What the Community Was Meant to Learn**

The sūrah addresses the believers with the same lesson in its opening verses, telling them not to be like those who were given a book and had their hearts harden (57:16 — *“Has the time not yet come for believers’ hearts to be humbled at the remembrance of Allah and what has been revealed of the truth, and not be like those given the Scripture before—˹those˺ who were spoiled for so long that their hearts became hardened.”*), and it ends by taking away the ground of any community's pride. The Sūrah of Friday gives the sharpest form of the argument, telling the People of the Book who claimed to be God's friends to the exclusion of others to wish for death if they were truthful (62:6 — *“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Jews! If you claim to be Allah’s chosen ˹people˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death, if what you say is true.”*). The Book's rule is the same everywhere: the favour is real, the hand that holds it is God's, and no line of descent or possession of a scroll keeps it in a family.

**The Gift Named in the Passage**

The chapter's last verse names what the bounty is for those who receive it, telling the believers to fear God and believe in His messenger so that He may give them a double portion of His mercy and give them a light to walk by and forgive them (57:28 — *“O people of faith! Fear Allah and believe in His Messenger.”*). It then states its purpose in the verse in front of us: so that the people of the earlier books may know that they are not the gatekeepers. The sūrah ends on the two names that carry the whole arrangement, and it leaves the reader with a favour that is given rather than inherited.

**The Second Telling of the Day's Work**

The labourers' parable is recorded in two forms and the commentary keeps both. In the version Aḥmad collected from ʿAbdullāh bin ʿUmar, which al-Bukhārī also collected, a man hired workers for a qīrāṭ each from the dawn prayer until midday, and the Jews worked; then from the noon prayer until the afternoon prayer, and the Christians worked; then from the afternoon prayer until sunset for two qīrāṭ each — and you are the ones who did that work. The Jews and the Christians were angry and said, we worked more and were paid less; and God said, have I wronged you in anything of your wage? They said, no. He said, then it is My favour, which I give to whom I will. Al-Bukhārī records a second version from Abū Mūsā, and he alone recorded it: workers who quit at midday, and then others who quit at the afternoon prayer, each forfeiting the wage they had been promised, and a last company working until sunset and receiving the wages of both earlier groups — an image, the Prophet ﷺ said, of them, and of those who accepted this light. The verse's purpose clause is what both tellings end on: so that the People of the Book may know — that is, may be certain — that they can neither prevent what God gives nor give what God prevents.
