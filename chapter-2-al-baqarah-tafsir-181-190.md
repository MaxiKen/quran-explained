# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 181–190

This batch completes the bequest ruling, gives the law of the fast, and then turns to three separate matters that all turn on the same principle. The integrity of the will is protected, and the unjust will is made correctable (181–182). Then the fast is prescribed, with its purpose named as *taqwa*, its concessions given, and Ramadan identified as the month of the Qur'an (183–185). Then, in the middle of the fasting passage, the verse on supplication — in which God answers a question about Himself in His own voice with no "say" between (186). Then the detailed rulings of the fast, including one of the most instructive occasions of revelation in the whole Qur'an (187). Then the prohibition of consuming wealth unjustly and of bribing those in authority (188). Then the crescents, and the second negation of false righteousness in thirteen verses (189). And finally the opening of the fighting passage, whose first clause restricts it and whose second clause forbids transgression (190).

---

## Verse 181

**"But whoever changes the will after hearing it, the blame will only be on those who made the change. Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: whoever alters it after having heard it — the sin of it is only upon those who alter it. Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

*Fa-man baddalahu ba'da ma sami'ahu* — "so whoever alters it after having heard it."

*Baddalahu* is form II of the root meaning to exchange and substitute: he changed it, replaced it with something else.

And note the circumstance: *ba'da ma sami'ahu* — **after having heard it**. The aggravating factor is knowledge. This is not a person who never knew the terms. It is a person who heard the dying person state what he wanted and then substituted something else.

**Which makes this one of the most commonly violated and least-discussed rulings in the religion.** The alteration of wills after death — by an executor, by a relative who takes charge of the papers, by a family that decides the deceased "really meant" something else, or that a particular beneficiary "doesn't deserve it" — is not a grey area. The verse names it and names who bears it.

*Fa-innama ithmuhu 'ala alladhina yubaddilunahu* — "so the sin of it is **only** upon those who alter it."

***Innama*** — only — and the restriction is doing real work. The sin falls on the changers and **not on the deceased**.

That is a comfort the verse deliberately supplies. A person who made a fair will in accordance with the law, and whose will was then tampered with by someone after his death, is not answerable for the tampering. His account closed at his death; the sin is the living person's. The deceased is not held to a document he did not write.

*Inna Allaha sami'un 'alim* — "indeed Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."

**And note how exactly the two Names fit.** *Al-Sami'* — the All-Hearing: He heard the bequest as it was actually spoken, at the deathbed, whether or not anyone else remembers it correctly. *Al-'Alim* — the All-Knowing: He knows what was done to it afterwards.

The two Names are the two things the situation requires. The original statement had a Hearer who did not need witnesses. The alteration has a Knower who was not deceived. The same pair closed 2:127, where Abraham and Ishmael prayed *taqabbal minna* — "accept from us" — invoking the Hearing that receives the prayer and the Knowledge that sees the intention.

For practice, two things.

The first is the ruling itself, which is worth stating plainly because it is so often quietly broken. A will is a trust. Altering it — by pressure, by substitution, by "interpreting" it to mean what the survivors prefer, or by simply not carrying it out — places the sin on the person who altered it. And the *innama* means the deceased is discharged of it. A family that quietly reshapes what someone asked for on his deathbed is not doing him a favour and is not resolving an ambiguity. It is committing the thing this verse names.

And the second is the *sami'un 'alim* as a comfort to anyone who has been on the receiving end. A person who was cut out of a will, or short-changed by an executor, or who watched a document he knew the contents of get replaced — the verse says the original was heard by someone who does not forget and the alteration is known by someone who was not fooled. The remedy may or may not come in this life. The record is complete either way.

## Verse 182

**"Whoever suspects an error or an injustice in the will and brings about a ˹fair˺ settlement among the parties will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: but whoever fears from a testator deviation or sin, and sets things right between them — no sin upon him. Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.

*Fa-man khafa min musin janafan aw ithman* — "so whoever fears from a testator deviation or sin."

The crux is the pair, and the commentators distinguished them precisely.

***Janaf*** is from a root meaning to incline and to deviate. Here it denotes **error, deviation, a mistake** — an unintentional departure from what is right: the testator got the shares wrong, or exceeded the third without realising, or was not in full possession of his faculties, or wrote something he did not understand the effect of.

***Ithm*** is **sin** — a deliberate injustice: disinheriting someone from malice, bequeathing something that was not his to give, using the will to punish.

So the two cover the unintentional defect and the intentional wrong, and *khafa* — "fears," has reasonable grounds to suspect — is the standard: not certainty, but a grounded concern.

*Fa-aslaha baynahum* — "and sets right between them."

**And note the verb, which is the second condition of repentance at 2:160:** *tabu wa aslahu wa bayyanu* — they repented, they set right, they made clear. The same word. Here it is rectification between heirs: mediating a settlement that repairs the defect in the bequest rather than enforcing it.

*Fa-la ithma 'alayhi* — "no sin upon him."

**And this is the ruling with the widest reach in the pair: an unjust will may be corrected.**

The principle underneath is one of the most important in the law: **the purpose of the bequest is justice, not the literal enforcement of whatever was written.** A will is not sacrosanct in itself; it is an instrument for a just distribution, and where the instrument produces injustice, rectification is permitted and carries no sin.

That has an immediate practical consequence that most families get wrong. It is very common for heirs to enforce a will they privately know to be unfair, on the grounds that "it is what he wrote" or "it is his right to do what he wants with his money." The verse says otherwise. A person with a grounded concern that the will is mistaken or unjust may intervene, mediate a fair settlement among the parties, and is not sinning by departing from the document's terms.

*Inna Allaha ghafurun rahim* — "indeed Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."

And note the change of Names from verse 181. There the pair was *sami'un 'alim* — Hearing and Knowing, the attributes that answer the secret alteration. Here it is *ghafurun rahim* — Forgiving and Merciful, the attributes that answer the good-faith intervenor who may have got the rectification imperfectly. The one who alters a will for gain is met with the record. The one who intervenes to repair an injustice is met with forgiveness for the imperfection.

For practice, three things.

The first is the distinction between *janaf* and *ithm*, which has a direct bearing on how families talk about a disputed will. A testator who made an error is in a different position from one who acted vindictively, and the response should differ: the first needs correction, the second needs correction too but the heirs also need to be told plainly what happened rather than having it papered over in the name of family peace.

The second is the licence to intervene, which most people do not know they have. A person who suspects a will is unjust is not required to stand by and watch it executed. He may mediate, and he is not sinning. What he may not do is intervene for his own benefit under cover of the concern — the verse's whole framing is *aslaha baynahum*, setting right **between them**, not for himself.

And the third is the *ghafurun rahim* for the attempt. Rectifying a family's distribution is messy work and it is almost never done perfectly. The closing Names are for the person who tried.

## Verse 183

**"O believers! Fasting is prescribed for you—as it was for those before you—so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺."**

In simple terms: O you who believe, prescribed for you is the fast, as it was prescribed for those before you, that you may be God-conscious.

*Ya ayyuha alladhina amanu kutiba 'alaykumu al-siyamu* — "O you who believe, prescribed for you is the fast."

*Kutiba 'alaykum* is the same formula that opened the retaliation verse (2:178) and the bequest verse (2:180). Three consecutive legal prescriptions in the surah with the same opening, which is a way of saying: this is of a piece with what came before. It is law, and it is obligation.

*Al-siyam* is from the root meaning to abstain and to refrain. In the language it is abstention as such — the Qur'an uses the same word at 19:26 for Mary's vow of silence: "Indeed, I have vowed to the Most Merciful a fast [*sawman*], so I will not speak to anyone today." In the law it is abstention from the things that break the fast, from the true dawn to sunset, with the intention of worship.

*Kama kutiba 'ala alladhina min qablikum* — "as it was prescribed for those before you."

The majority of the commentators read the *kama* as comparing the **obligation itself**: fasting was prescribed for the communities before this one too. The transmitted accounts support this — the Prophet ﷺ came to Madinah and found the Jews fasting the day of 'Ashura, and he said, in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, that the Muslims have more right to Moses than they do, and he fasted it and commanded it to be fasted.

**And the pastoral function of the clause should not be missed.** The surah is about to lay a hard annual obligation on a community, and it pre-empts the complaint by naming two things in the same breath: this is difficult, and you are not the first. The verse does not pretend the fast is easy. It says: others have carried this, and it was prescribed for them, and it is prescribed for you. The shared burden is itself part of the gift.

*La'allakum tattaqun* — "that you may be God-conscious."

**And here is the deepest thing in the verse.**

The stated purpose of the fast is *taqwa* — the same purpose given for the worship of the Lord at 2:21 ("worship your Lord... that you may be God-conscious") and for retaliation at 2:179. So the purpose is not hunger, not weight, not solidarity, not even discipline as such. It is *taqwa*.

And the lexicographers' definition of *taqwa* is the key to why the fast is its instrument. *Taqwa* is from a root meaning to shield, to guard, to place a barrier between yourself and what you fear. And what does a fasting person do? **He places a barrier between himself and something that is entirely lawful at every other time of year.**

That is *taqwa* in its literal, embodied form. Food is lawful. Water is lawful. One's spouse is lawful. And for the length of a day, for the sake of God, they are walled off. **The fast is not a means to taqwa so much as taqwa performed physically** — the faculty of restraint exercised on material that offers no resistance at all, precisely so that it will be available when the material is desire, or anger, or money, or reputation.

The tradition's other emphasis belongs here too. Recorded by al-Bukhari from Abu Hurayrah: **"Whoever does not give up false speech and acting upon it, Allah has no need of his giving up his food and drink."** And a narration recorded by Ibn Majah and others states that perhaps a fasting person gets nothing from his fast but hunger and thirst.

For practice, three things.

The first is the purpose clause as a test. If the stated purpose of the fast is *taqwa*, then a fast that produces no increase in restraint has missed what it was for. The question to ask at the end of a Ramadan is not how many days were completed but whether the barrier is stronger.

The second is the false-speech hadith, which is the most practically corrective thing in the whole literature of fasting. The fast is abstention from what breaks it legally, and abstention from what defeats it spiritually, and the second list is longer than the first. A person who fasts from food and not from the lie, the backbite, the sharp word, and the dishonest transaction has done the easy half.

And the third is the *kama kutiba 'ala alladhina min qablikum* as consolation, applied more widely. Hard obligations in the religious life are almost never peculiar to the person carrying them. The surah's method here is worth imitating when handing anything difficult to someone else: name the difficulty, and name the company.

## Verse 184

**"˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days. But whoever of you is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺. But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them. And to fast is better for you, if only you knew."**

In simple terms: [a] counted number of days. So whoever among you is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. And upon those who can [hardly] bear it is a ransom: feeding a poor person. Whoever volunteers good, it is better for him. And that you fast is better for you, if you knew.

*Ayyaman ma'dudatin* — "[a] counted number of days."

*Ma'dudat* is numbered, limited, countable. And the word choice is a consolation: the days are **few** and they are **specified**, not open-ended.

Note that the same phrase was used at 2:80, where the People of the Book claimed the Fire would not touch them "except for a counted number of days." The surah used the phrase there for a false claim of limitation and here for a real one — which sharpens the consolation. This is a genuine limit, and it is stated in the same words.

*Fa-man kana minkum maridan aw 'ala safarin fa-'iddatun min ayyamin ukhar* — "so whoever among you is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days."

The concession is for **illness** and **travel**. The jurists then worked out the thresholds — what degree of illness qualifies (the majority: illness that fasting would worsen or whose recovery it would delay), and what distance of travel (the schools differ on the measure and on the conditions) — and those are genuine disagreements that a general summary should not flatten.

The narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Jabir ibn 'Abdullah is directly relevant, and it uses the surah's own key word: the Prophet ﷺ passed a man who was being shaded while travelling and was told that the man was fasting, and he said: **"It is not of righteousness [*al-birr*] to fast while travelling."** The word is the *birr* of 2:177 — the same concept the surah has just defined.

*Wa 'ala alladhina yutiqunahu fidyatun ta'amu miskin* — "and upon those who *yutiqunahu* is a ransom: feeding a poor person."

**And this is the crux of the verse, and one of the genuinely difficult phrases in the surah.** Two readings are held.

**The first reading** takes *yutiqunahu* in its straightforward sense: "those who are **able** to fast." On this reading the clause grants a temporary concession — those who could fast but found it very hard might instead feed a poor person per day — and **the majority hold that this concession was abrogated by the following verse**, 2:185's "so whoever witnesses the month, let him fast it."

**The second reading** takes it as "those who can **hardly** bear it" — those for whom fasting is a burden they can barely carry: the very elderly, the chronically ill, the one with a condition that fasting would genuinely endanger. A variant reading is transmitted which is understood in this sense. On this reading **no abrogation is needed**: the clause refers from the start to the permanently unable, and the ransom remains for them.

**And here is what is not in dispute across the schools:** the ransom (*fidyah*) remains the ruling for the person who will never be able to fast — the very old, the chronically ill with no prospect of recovery — who feeds a poor person for each day and does not make up the days. The disagreement is about how the clause got there, not about where it landed.

*Fidyatun ta'amu miskin* — a ransom: feeding a poor person. *Fidyah* is from the root meaning to ransom and redeem — the same word used at 2:196 for the pilgrimage ransom.

*Fa-man tatawwa'a khayran fa-huwa khayrun lahu* — "and whoever volunteers good, it is better for him."

**Note the exact repetition from 2:158:** *wa man tatawwa'a khayran fa-inna Allaha shakirun 'alim*. The same verb, the same construction. Whoever does more than the minimum — feeds more than one person for the day, or gives more than the bare measure — it is better for him.

*Wa an tasumu khayrun lakum in kuntum ta'lamun* — "and that you fast is better for you, if you knew."

**And this closing is the Qur'an's characteristic method, and it has appeared before in this surah.** The concession is given in full and in good faith, and then the superior option is named — without the concession being withdrawn. The reader is told what is easier and told what is better, and left to choose, with the knowledge that it is better appended: *in kuntum ta'lamun*, if you knew.

For practice, three things.

The first is the consolation in *ma'dudat*. The religion's hardest annual obligation is described with a word meaning *numbered* and *few*. A person facing a month of it is entitled to hold on to that.

The second is the concession, and the honesty needed about it. The concession is real and it is not a lesser piety. But the narration about travelling — "it is not of *birr* to fast while travelling" — is a warning against a particular misapplication: the person who refuses the concession out of a sense of rigour and ends up harming himself or failing at what he took on. Rigour that overrides a concession God gave is not extra piety.

And the third is the *yutiqunahu* case, which has a pastoral application that matters more than the legal one. There are people for whom the fast has become genuinely impossible — the very elderly, the seriously ill, those on medication that cannot be paused. The verse's ruling for them is not exemption-with-nothing; it is **feed a poor person for each day**. That is a real and doable substitute, and it means the person who cannot fast is not shut out of the month. He participates by feeding. That is worth saying to anyone who has had to stop fasting and feels they have lost Ramadan.

## Verse 185

**"Ramaḍân is the month in which the Quran was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear proofs of guidance and the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. So whoever is present this month, let them fast. But whoever is ill or on a journey, then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺. Allah intends ease for you, not hardship, so that you may complete the prescribed period and proclaim the greatness of Allah for guiding you, and perhaps you will be grateful."**

In simple terms: the month of Ramadan, in which the Qur'an was sent down — a guidance for people, and clear proofs of the guidance and the criterion. So whoever of you witnesses the month, let him fast it. And whoever is ill or on a journey, then a number of other days. Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship, and [wants] that you complete the number and magnify Allah for having guided you, and that you may be grateful.

*Shahru Ramadana alladhi unzila fihi al-qur'anu* — "the month of Ramadan, in which the Qur'an was sent down."

*Ramadan* is from a root meaning intense heat and scorched ground; the lexicographers connect the name either to the heat of the season in which the month originally fell, or — in a reading that is popular rather than linguistic — to the fast's burning away of sins. Both are transmitted; the first is the lexical one.

**And the crux: what does it mean that the Qur'an "was sent down in it," when the revelation came over twenty-three years?**

Three readings are transmitted and they are usually held together:

**(a)** The **beginning** of the revelation was in it — the first verses came in Ramadan, on the Night of Decree.
**(b)** The Qur'an was sent down **in its entirety** from the Preserved Tablet to the lowest heaven in a single act, on the Night of Decree, and was then revealed piecemeal over twenty-three years.
**(c)** The greater part of the revelation came in it.

The second is the reading most commonly given in the classical literature and it is what reconciles the verse with the twenty-three-year period. Its textual support is 97:1 — "Indeed, We sent it down during the Night of Decree" — and 44:3 — "Indeed, We sent it down during a blessed night" — both of which speak of a single sending, set against 17:106, which describes the gradual descent: "And [it is] a Qur'an which We have separated [by intervals] that you might recite it to the people over a sustained period."

*Hudan lil-nasi wa bayyinatin mina al-huda wa al-furqan* — "a guidance for the people, and clear proofs of the guidance and the *furqan*."

Note *lil-nas* — for **people**, for humanity — not "for the believers." The Qur'an's guidance is offered to humanity, and the offer is not withdrawn by the refusal of most of it.

***Al-furqan*** — and here is a thread worth pulling, because it ties the whole passage together.

From a root meaning to separate and distinguish, *al-furqan* is the criterion — that which distinguishes truth from falsehood. **And the surah used the same word at 2:53**, of what Moses was given: "And [recall] when We gave Moses the Scripture and the *furqan*." So the Qur'an is given the title that was given to the Torah, which places the two revelations in the same category.

And 8:29 makes the connection to this batch explicit: "O you who have believed, if you fear Allah [*tattaqu*], He will grant you a *furqan* [a criterion] and will remove from you your misdeeds and forgive you."

**Set that beside 2:183 and the shape of the month appears:** the fast is prescribed *la'allakum tattaqun* — so that you may have *taqwa* — and *taqwa* is what produces the *furqan*, and the *furqan* is the Qur'an, and the Qur'an was sent down in this month. The month of the fast is the month of the Criterion, and the fast builds the faculty that reads it. That is why the Qur'an and Ramadan belong together and why the tradition has always paired them — the night-long prayer of the month, and the recitation that fills it.

*Fa-man shahida minkumu al-shahra fal-yasumhu* — "so whoever of you witnesses the month, let him fast it." *Shahida* — witnesses, is present for, is resident. The jurists read into it the conditions of obligation: residence, sound mind, and having reached puberty.

And note the movement from verse 184: the "counted days" are now specified as **the month**, and the concession for illness and travel is repeated in the identical words. The commentators took the repetition as emphasis and as attaching the concession specifically to this month.

***Yuridu Allahu bikumu al-yusra wa la yuridu bikumu al-'usra*** — "Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship."

**This is one of the great texts of the law.** From it the jurists derived the maxim ***al-mashaqqah tajlib al-taysir*** — hardship brings ease — one of the five comprehensive maxims of *fiqh*, and the companion to the one derived at 2:173, that necessity is measured by its extent.

The supporting texts are consistent: 22:78 — "He has chosen you and has not placed upon you in the religion any difficulty [*haraj*]." And the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari: **"The religion is ease, and no one makes it harsh except that it overcomes him."** And the instruction recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: **"Make things easy and do not make them difficult; give good news and do not repel."**

*Wa li-tukmilu al-'iddata wa li-tukabbiru Allaha 'ala ma hadakum* — "and that you complete the number, and that you magnify Allah for having guided you."

*Tukabbiru* is form II of the root of *kabir*: to declare greatness — this is the textual basis of the *takbir* of 'Eid. And note what the *takbir* is for: *'ala ma hadakum* — **for having guided you**. The magnification is gratitude for guidance, not celebration at the end of a hardship. The jurists derive the ruling of the 'Eid takbir from this clause and differ on its precise form and on whether it runs from the sunset of the last day through the prayer.

*Wa la'allakum tashkurun* — "and that you may be grateful." The gratitude thread that runs from 2:152 and 2:172 closes this verse too.

For practice, three things.

The first is the descent of the Qur'an, and what it means for how the month is spent. If Ramadan is the month of the Qur'an's descent, then the characteristic act of the month is the Qur'an — recited, heard, studied, and acted on. The tradition's practice of completing the recitation in the month is not a custom attached to it from outside; it is what the verse identifies the month as being *for*.

The second is *yusr*, the ease. The maxim is a ruling, not a sentiment, and it has a direction: wherever a genuine hardship exists, the law bends. The application is to every person who has made the religion harder than it is, for himself or for others — the parent, the teacher, the imam, the spouse. The religion does not intend difficulty, and the person who manufactures it is working against the verse.

And the third is the *takbir* as gratitude for guidance. It is worth noting at the end of the month that what is being celebrated is not survival and not completion, but the fact of having been guided. Someone who was not guided to it has nothing to celebrate at its end however many days he completed.

## Verse 186

**"When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one’s prayer when they call upon Me. So let them respond ˹with obedience˺ to Me and believe in Me, perhaps they will be guided ˹to the Right Way˺."**

In simple terms: and when My servants ask you about Me — indeed I am near. I answer the call of the caller when he calls upon Me. So let them respond to Me and believe in Me, that they may be rightly guided.

*Wa idha sa'alaka 'ibadi 'anni fa-inni qarib* — "and when My servants ask you about Me, indeed I am near."

**And the first thing to notice is the grammar of the answer, and it is one of the most beautiful things in the Qur'an.**

The verse says: "when My servants ask **you** about Me." The question is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ. And in every comparable case, the answer comes with an intervening *qul* — "**say**: He is..." Here there is no *qul*. The reply comes directly, in the first person of God: *fa-inni qarib* — "indeed I am near."

**Allah answers the question about Himself in His own voice, with nothing between.** The commentators noted this and treated it as a mark of honour both to the messenger and to the one asking: a question about God is answered by God, unmediated.

The transmitted occasion, which the tafsir literature records, is that some of the companions asked whether their Lord was near so that they might whisper to Him, or far so that they should call out — and this verse came. It is transmitted and it fits the verse's content exactly.

***Qarib*** — near. And the crux is what nearness means.

The position held consistently by the commentators and the theologians, and the one maintained throughout this commentary at 2:115 and 2:153, is that **nearness here is not spatial**. It is nearness by knowledge, by hearing, by response and by care. The Salaf affirmed it as stated without asking how; later scholarship largely read it as nearness by His knowledge and His answering. What it is not, on any reading, is physical proximity or indwelling. The comparison is 50:16: "And We have already created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein."

*Ujibu da'wata al-da'i idha da'ani* — "I answer the call of the caller when he calls upon Me."

**And the crux here is what "answer" means**, because it is the question every person who has ever prayed has asked.

The answer given in the Sunnah is that the response is real but is not always the granting of the specific request. Recorded by Ahmad and others from Abu Sa'id: the Prophet ﷺ said that there is no Muslim who supplicates with a supplication containing no sin and no severing of kinship except that Allah gives him one of three — either He hastens his request for him, or He stores it for him in the Hereafter, or He averts from him an equivalent evil. And when the companions said they would then ask for more, he said: **Allah is more.** Its grading is discussed among the specialists and it is cited here for its meaning, which is the established position.

**And the hadith that governs the whole subject practically** is recorded by Muslim from Abu Hurayrah: the Prophet ﷺ mentioned a man who travels far, dishevelled and dusty, raising his hands to the sky and saying "O Lord, O Lord" — **while his food is unlawful, his drink is unlawful, his clothing is unlawful, and he has been nourished with the unlawful. How can he be answered?**

That hadith is the single most practically important thing in the literature of supplication, and it is worth stating plainly: **the obstacle to answered prayer is very often not the prayer.** It is what the person earns, eats, wears and transacts with. The raising of the hands and the length of the journey and the dust on the clothes are all in order. The unlawful income is what blocks it.

And also recorded by Muslim: the supplication of a servant continues to be answered as long as he does not ask for something sinful or for the severing of kinship, and **as long as he does not grow impatient**.

*Falyastajibu li wal-yu'minu bi* — "so let them respond to Me and let them believe in Me."

**Note that the reciprocity is in the word itself.** *Ujibu* — I answer. *Falyastajibu* — let them respond. Same root. The relationship is stated as mutual, and the mutuality is built into the vocabulary: answer for answer.

*La'allahum yarshudun* — "that they may be rightly guided." From the root of *rushd* — right course, sound judgement — the same word as 2:256's "the right course has become distinct from error."

**And on the placement: this verse sits in the middle of the fasting passage, and that is not accidental.** A narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah and others from Abu Hurayrah states that three supplications are not turned back, and names among them **the supplication of the fasting person when he breaks his fast**, alongside the just ruler and the wronged person. Its grading is discussed. On the transmitted reading, the verse on supplication is placed in the verses on fasting because the fasting person's supplication at the moment of breaking has a particular standing.

For practice, this is the most consoling verse in the batch and it gives three things.

The first is the removal of the *qul*. A person who wants to ask something of God does not need to route it through anyone. The verse's own grammar makes the point: the question about God is answered by God. That is a directness that most religious people underuse, usually because they feel unqualified to take it.

The second is the unlawful-income hadith, and it is the thing to act on. Before asking why a prayer has not been answered, the verse's own tradition says: look at the income, the food, the transactions. That is a harder and more useful exercise than repeating the request.

And the third is the reciprocity in *ujibu / falyastajibu*. The verse does not say "ask and receive." It says: I answer, so answer Me. Which means the person who wants his supplications answered has a corresponding obligation of response — obedience to what has already been commanded. A person praying fervently while knowingly persisting in something God forbade is asking the verse to operate in only one direction.

## Verse 187

**"It has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast. Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them. Allah knows that you were deceiving yourselves. So He has accepted your repentance and pardoned you. So now you may be intimate with them and seek what Allah has prescribed for you. ˹You may˺ eat and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night, then complete the fast until nightfall. Do not be intimate with your spouses while you are meditating in the mosques. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not exceed them. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to people, so they may become mindful ˹of Him˺."**

In simple terms: permitted for you on the night of the fast is intimacy with your wives. They are a garment for you and you are a garment for them. Allah knew that you were betraying yourselves, so He turned to you and pardoned you. So now be intimate with them and seek what Allah has prescribed for you. And eat and drink until the white thread becomes distinct to you from the black thread at dawn; then complete the fast until night. And do not be intimate with them while you are in retreat in the mosques. Those are the limits of Allah, so do not approach them. Thus Allah makes clear His signs to people, that they may be God-conscious.

*Uhilla lakum laylata al-siyami al-rafathu ila nisa'ikum* — "permitted for you, on the night of the fast, is *al-rafath* to your wives."

*Rafath* denotes sexual intimacy, and the commentators variously read it as the act itself or as the direct speech that leads to it.

**And the occasion of this verse is one of the best-attested in the Qur'an, and it establishes that the verse is a relaxation rather than a restriction.**

The initial ruling was harder: once a person broke his fast and then slept, or once he prayed the night prayer, he could not eat, drink or be intimate until the following sunset. The narration recorded by al-Bukhari from al-Bara' ibn 'Azib states that when a man was fasting and slept before eating, he would not eat that night nor the following day until evening — and that Qays ibn Sirmah al-Ansari, who was fasting, came to his wife at the time of breaking the fast and asked for food; she had none and went to look for some, and his eyes overcame him and he slept, and when she returned and found him asleep she said "deprived!" — and he fainted the following midday. The Prophet ﷺ was told, and this verse was revealed, and **the companions rejoiced greatly at it.**

*'alima Allahu annakum kuntum takhtanuna anfusakum fa-taba 'alaykum wa 'afa 'ankum* — "Allah knew that you were betraying yourselves, so He turned to you and pardoned you."

***Takhtanuna anfusakum*** — "you were deceiving your own selves." Note that the object is *anfusakum*: **yourselves**. The betrayal was not of God; it was self-harm, self-deception, people bringing hardship on themselves and some of them falling short in private.

And the mercy is doubled: *fa-taba 'alaykum* — He turned to you in acceptance (the same verb as 2:37 for Adam, 2:54, 2:128, 2:160) — **and** *wa 'afa 'ankum* — and He pardoned and effaced it (the same verb as 2:52, 2:109, 2:178). Acceptance **and** erasure.

*Hunna libasun lakum wa antum libasun lahunna* — "they are a garment for you, and you are a garment for them."

***LIBAS*** — and this is one of the most celebrated metaphors in the Qur'an, and the commentators and rhetoricians drew out what it carries:

**A garment covers** — so each conceals the other's faults and guards the other's honour. **A garment is closest to the body** — nothing is nearer to a person than what he wears, so the spouse is the nearest of people. **A garment adorns** — each is an adornment to the other. **A garment is worn constantly** — the nearness is steady, not occasional. **A garment protects from heat and cold** — each is shelter from what would harm. **A garment conceals what would otherwise be exposed.**

And note the **reciprocity of the phrasing**: it is stated both ways. Not "she is a garment for you" but *hunna libasun lakum **wa antum libasun lahunna***. The mutuality is the point and it is built into the sentence.

And note where the metaphor appears: inside a legal verse, at the point of granting permission. The word chosen for the most physical of relations is the word for clothing — which is itself the statement that the relationship is lawful, honourable, protective and mutual, rather than something requiring euphemism or shame. The comparison is 7:26, where *libas* is used for clothing and *libas al-taqwa* — the garment of God-consciousness — is called best.

*Fa-al-ana bashiruhunna wa-btaghu ma kataba Allahu lakum* — "so now be intimate with them, and seek what Allah has prescribed for you."

*Bashiruhunna* is from the root meaning direct skin contact — the same root as *bashshir*, to bring good news, because good news changes the face. And *wa-btaghu ma kataba Allahu lakum* — "seek what Allah has prescribed for you" — is read by the majority as children, or as the lawful pleasure of the relationship; some commentators read it as seeking the Night of Decree. The readings are transmitted.

*Wa-kulu wa-shrabu hatta yatabayyana lakumu al-khaytu al-abyadu mina al-khayti al-aswadi mina al-fajri* — "and eat and drink until the white thread becomes distinct to you from the black thread, at dawn."

**And this is the famous crux, and its occasion is one of the most instructive passages in the whole tradition.**

Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from 'Adiyy ibn Hatim: when this verse was revealed, 'Adiyy took an actual white thread and an actual black thread, put them under his pillow, and looked at them through the night, but could not distinguish one from the other. In the morning he went to the Prophet ﷺ and told him — and the Prophet ﷺ said to him: **"Your pillow is indeed broad! It is only the blackness of the night and the whiteness of the day."**

The methodological importance of that hadith is very hard to overstate, and it is why it has always been central in the literature. **It establishes that the meaning of the Qur'an is not fixed by a literal reading of the words in isolation, and that the Sunnah is the explanation of the Qur'an.** The words say "thread." The meaning is the horizon. And the Companion who read them literally was corrected by the Prophet ﷺ rather than commended for his literalism.

The jurists then distinguished the **true dawn** — *al-fajr al-sadiq*, the light that spreads horizontally along the horizon — from the **false dawn**, which rises vertically and fades. The supporting hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim is that the Prophet ﷺ said the pre-dawn meal is not to be abandoned at the first call, for that call is made at night, and that one should eat and drink until the second call.

*Thumma atimmu al-siyama ila al-layl* — "then complete the fast until the night."

*Ila al-layl* — **until nightfall**. The fast ends at sunset, not later. The hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from 'Umar: the Prophet ﷺ said that when the night comes from here and the day departs from here and the sun has set, the fasting person has broken his fast. And from Sahl ibn Sa'd: **"The people will remain upon good as long as they hasten the breaking of the fast."** And from Anas: **"Take the pre-dawn meal, for there is blessing in it."**

*Wa la tubashiruhunna wa antum 'akifuna fi al-masajid* — "and do not be intimate with them while you are in retreat in the mosques."

*'Akifun* is from the root meaning to cling and to devote oneself — the state of *i'tikaf*, retreat in the mosque for worship. **And note the thread with 2:125**, where the House was purified for "those who circumambulate and those who stay [*al-'akifin*] therein." The same word, the same state.

*Tilka hududu Allahi fa-la taqrabuha* — "those are the limits of Allah, so do not **approach** them."

***Hudud*** is limits, boundaries. And note the verb: *la taqrabuha* — do not **come near** them. Not "do not cross them." **Do not approach them.**

The commentators drew the rule from it directly: **the safe distance from a prohibition is greater than the prohibition itself.** A person who walks right up to a boundary is in danger of crossing it, and the verse forbids the approach rather than only the crossing. The comparison is 2:229, which says *la ta'taduha*, "do not transgress them" — the two phrasings occur, and this one is the stronger.

*Kadhalika yubayyinu Allahu ayatihi lil-nasi la'allahum yattaqun* — "thus Allah makes clear His signs to the people, that they may be God-conscious."

**And note the bookend.** The fasting passage opened at 183 with *la'allakum tattaqun* — that you may be God-conscious — and closes here with *la'allahum yattaqun*. The passage is bracketed by its purpose.

For practice, four things.

The first is the **occasion as theology**. The initial ruling was harder, the companions found it crushing, some of them failed at it privately, and Allah **lightened** it — and then told them He knew what they had done and had pardoned it. That is the religion's own account of how a difficult ruling is relaxed: not by the people demanding it and not by the law being soft, but by God seeing the hardship and lifting it, and by the lifting being accompanied by pardon for the failure rather than only by a new rule.

The second is the *libas*, applied. The metaphor is a standard for a marriage that is unusually demanding and unusually concrete: do you cover each other, or expose each other? Are you nearest, or is someone else? Do you adorn, or diminish? Do you protect, or is the harm coming from inside? The verse's test is mutual and it is stated twice, once in each direction.

The third is the **'Adiyy hadith**, and its methodological lesson extends far beyond the dawn. A person who reads a text literally in isolation, with great sincerity and great effort, can be entirely wrong about it and be corrected by the one who was sent to explain it. That is why the tradition holds that the Qur'an is explained by the Sunnah and by the understanding of the first generations, and why private reading untethered from them has always produced error.

And the fourth is *la taqrabuha* — do not approach. Applied generally, it is the answer to the question most people actually face, which is not "is this forbidden?" but "how close can I get?" The verse's answer is that the question is malformed. The safe distance from a limit is not its edge.

## Verse 188

**"Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly, nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property, knowing that it is a sin."**

In simple terms: and do not consume your wealth among yourselves falsely, and do not hand it over to the judges in order to consume a portion of people's wealth sinfully, while you know.

*Wa la ta'kulu amwalakum baynakum bi-al-batili* — "and do not consume your wealth among yourselves falsely."

*Batil* is from a root meaning null and void: by false means, without right. The prohibition is general — no taking of another's wealth by any means that does not carry a right.

The definitive parallel is 4:29: "O you who have believed, do not consume one another's wealth unjustly [*bi-al-batil*] **but only [in lawful] business by mutual consent**." The general prohibition paired with the general permission, in one verse.

And the Farewell Sermon, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, states the sanctity in the strongest available terms: **"Indeed your blood, your wealth and your honours are sacred to one another, like the sanctity of this day of yours, in this month of yours, in this land of yours."**

*Wa tudlu biha ila al-hukkam* — "and do not hand it over to the judges."

***TUDLU*** — and the image is worth recovering. The verb is from the root meaning to lower a bucket into a well. So the sense is: **do not lower your wealth down to the judges as a bucket** — do not use it as the means of drawing up a verdict. Wealth is the rope and the bucket; the judgment is what is drawn.

That is bribery, described with an image rather than a definition, and the image is more damning than a definition would be.

The supporting hadith, recorded by Ahmad, Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah from 'Abdullah ibn 'Amr: **"The curse of Allah is upon the one who bribes and the one who is bribed."** Its grading is discussed among the specialists.

*Li-ta'kulu fariqan min amwali al-nasi bi-al-ithmi* — "in order to consume a portion of people's wealth sinfully."

Note *fariqan* — **a portion**. The person addressed is not seeking the whole; he is seeking a part, which is how the thing usually works and why it is harder to see.

*Wa antum ta'lamun* — "while you know."

**And this is the aggravating circumstance, and the surah has used it before.** 2:22: "so do not make rivals to Allah while you know." 2:42: "and do not conceal the truth while you know." 2:75: "a party of them heard the word of Allah and then distorted it after they had understood it, while they knew." 2:146: "a party of them conceal the truth while they know."

**The knowledge is what makes it what it is.** Ignorance is error; knowing is the sin.

For practice, three things, and the third is the one that lands hardest.

The first is the general principle and the Farewell Sermon's formulation. Wealth and honour are in the same category as blood. That is the standard, and it is a much higher standard than most commercial and professional life operates at.

The second is the bribery clause, and its scope. The verse was revealed about bribing judges, and the tradition has always applied it to every form of using wealth or influence to obtain what one has no right to: the payment to the official, the facilitation fee, the gift that is not a gift, the commission routed through a relative, the contract awarded to the connected bidder. The briber and the bribed are both named in the hadith, which is worth noting — the person receiving is as much in it as the person giving.

And the third is the application the verse makes that most readers will not have considered: **winning in court is not the same as being right.**

The person addressed in this verse is not a thief and not a fraudster. He is someone who goes to court, and who **uses his wealth to obtain a judgment for something he knows is not his**. Which means he may well win. The judgment may be entirely valid in form and the procedure entirely correct. And he is, by this verse, consuming a portion of people's wealth *bi-al-ithm*, sinfully — **while he knows**.

That is a devastating and necessary thing for anyone with means, and it is why the tradition is so severe about the person who litigates knowing he is in the wrong. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: "I am only a human being, and you bring your disputes to me, and perhaps one of you is more eloquent in his argument than another and I judge in his favour on the basis of what I hear. **So if I judge in someone's favour something of his brother's right, let him not take it — for I have only allotted him a portion of the Fire.**"

## Verse 189

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, “They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage.” Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors. Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺. So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors, and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful."**

In simple terms: they ask you about the crescents. Say: they are appointed times for the people and for the pilgrimage. And righteousness is not that you come to houses from their backs — but righteousness is [the quality of] one who is God-conscious. So come to the houses by their doors, and be conscious of Allah that you may succeed.

*Yas'alunaka 'an al-ahillati* — "they ask you about the crescents." *Ahillah* is the plural of *hilal*, the new moon.

*Qul hiya mawaqitu lil-nasi wa al-hajj* — "say: they are appointed times for the people and for the pilgrimage."

**And note what the question was and what the answer is.** On the transmitted reading, they were asking about the **phases** — why the moon appears small and then grows, rather than staying constant. And the answer given is not an explanation of the mechanism. It is a statement of the **purpose**: they are for measuring time.

The lesson is worth stating precisely. The Qur'an is not hostile to investigating how things work — 2:164 invited observation and reasoning, and the tradition's scientific output is downstream of that. But the Qur'an's own answer here is functional rather than mechanical, and that in itself is an instruction about what matters most to ask. The parallel gives the same function: 10:5 — "It is He who made the sun a shining light and the moon a derived light and determined for it phases — **that you may know the number of years and the account [of time]**." And 36:39 describes the phases until the moon returns "like the old date stalk."

The practical consequence is the **lunar calendar**: Ramadan, the two 'Eids, the Hajj, the waiting periods, and the zakah year are all determined by lunar months. The governing hadith, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: **"Do not fast until you see it, and do not break the fast until you see it; and if it is obscured to you, then complete thirty."** And: "The month is twenty-nine days."

*Wa laysa al-birru bi-an ta'tu al-buyuta min zuhuriha* — "and righteousness is not that you come to the houses from their backs."

The crux is *min zuhuriha* — "from their backs." **And the transmitted occasion is this:** in the pre-Islamic period, some of the Arabs — the reports variously name the people of Madinah, or Himyar, or some of the Ansar — had a practice whereby a man who entered the state of consecration for the pilgrimage would not enter his house by its door, but would make an opening at the back and enter through that, treating the awkwardness as an act of devotion.

**So the practice was self-imposed hardship, performed as religion.**

**And note that this is the second time in thirteen verses that the surah defines righteousness by negating a ritual external.** At 2:177: "Righteousness is not that you turn your faces toward the east and the west." Here: "righteousness is not that you come to houses from their backs." The surah has now twice said, about two quite different externals — one a divinely commanded direction, one an invented austerity — that the external is not the thing.

*Wa lakinna al-birra mani ittaqa* — "but righteousness is [the quality of] one who is God-conscious."

**And note the grammar, which repeats 2:177 exactly.** Not "righteousness is that you fear God" but *mani ittaqa* — **he who** is God-conscious. Righteousness is predicated of a person. Twice in thirteen verses, in the identical construction.

*Wa'tu al-buyuta min abwabiha* — "and come to the houses by their doors."

**And this has become one of the most-quoted lines in the language, and rightly.** The immediate ruling abolishes the pre-Islamic practice. But the tradition has always read it as a comprehensive maxim as well: **approach a thing by the route that actually reaches it.** Seek knowledge by its path. Seek provision by its lawful means. Seek a goal by its door rather than by scrambling in at the back out of superstition, impatience, or the wish to avoid the effort of the entrance.

*Wa-ttaqu Allaha la'allakum tuflihun* — "and be conscious of Allah that you may succeed."

*Tuflihun* is from a root meaning to split the earth for cultivation, and hence to prosper: *falah* is the fruitfulness of worked land. **And this is the bookend:** the surah's second verse ended with *wa ula'ika humu al-muflihun* — "and those are the successful." The word returns here.

For practice, three things.

The first is the calendar. The religion runs on the moon, which means the months move through the seasons and no month has a permanent climate. That is not an inconvenience to be engineered away by calculation alone; on the reading of the majority it is the designed feature, and the hadith's instruction to sight the crescent — or to complete thirty — is the mechanism the law gives.

The second is the *laysa al-birr* pair, held together. At 177 the negated external was something genuinely commanded. At 189 it is something invented. The surah negates both, which means the principle is not "externals do not matter" but **"externals are not righteousness."** A person can perform every commanded external and every invented austerity and still not be the person the verse describes — because the verse describes a person, not a set of acts.

And the third is the maxim: **enter houses by their doors.** It is worth asking, in whatever one is currently pursuing — a qualification, a marriage, a livelihood, a position, a change in oneself — whether one is going in by the door or by the back. The back route is usually faster and usually involves avoiding something: the qualification, the honesty, the conversation, the waiting, the permission. The verse's instruction is not a moralism about shortcuts. It is the observation that the back route does not actually reach what the door reaches.

## Verse 190

**"Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you, but do not exceed the limits. Surely Allah does not like transgressors."**

In simple terms: and fight in the way of Allah those who fight you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not love the transgressors.

*Wa qatilu fi sabili Allahi alladhina yuqatilunakum* — "and fight in the way of Allah those who fight you."

*Qatilu* is form III of the root meaning to kill — the form of mutual engagement: to fight, to combat. And *fi sabili Allahi* is the phrase defined at 2:154 by the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: **whoever fights so that the word of Allah is supreme is in the way of Allah.**

**And the clause that governs everything is the one that follows: *alladhina yuqatilunakum* — those who fight YOU.**

The command is not to fight people because of what they believe, or who they are, or that they have rejected the message. It is to fight **those who are fighting you**. The restriction is in the verse's own words, and it has to be stated with full force, because this is the verse that both critics of the religion and apologists for violence most frequently quote while omitting its own limiting clause.

The jurisprudence of *jihad* is a specialised field built on the whole corpus of texts, and there are genuine and well-known differences among the schools and among contemporary scholars about its scope, its conditions and its application. **What is not in dispute, because the text states it, is that this verse restricts fighting to those who fight.** A general summary should not pretend to adjudicate the specialised questions, and no reader should derive a ruling on them from a commentary of this kind.

*Wa la ta'tadu* — "and do not transgress."

***I'tida'*** is form VIII of the root meaning to overstep, to go beyond the limit — the same word used at 2:178 for taking revenge after a settlement. And the prohibition is absolute and it sits in the same sentence as the permission.

**From this and the parallel texts the jurists derived the rules of conduct in war**, and the tradition is consistent and severe: non-combatants are not to be killed, mutilation is forbidden, treachery is forbidden, crops and livestock are not to be destroyed, and property is not to be laid waste.

The hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Ibn 'Umar gives the core: **a woman was found killed in one of the expeditions, and the Messenger of Allah ﷺ forbade the killing of women and children.** And the instruction transmitted from Abu Bakr to his commanders — recorded in the collections with discussion of its grading — adds: do not kill a woman, nor a child, nor an old man; do not mutilate; do not destroy palm trees or burn them; do not cut down fruit-bearing trees; do not slaughter livestock except for food; do not be treacherous.

*Inna Allaha la yuhibbu al-mu'tadin* — "indeed, Allah does not love the transgressors."

*La yuhibb* is a statement of divine disapproval, and the tradition affirms the attribute of love as real and not enquired into as to modality. The formulation recurs throughout the Qur'an: "and Allah does not love corruption" (2:205); "Allah does not love every disbelieving sinner" (2:276); and positively at 2:195, which follows this verse: "and do good; indeed, Allah loves the doers of good."

For practice, three things, and the third is a boundary.

The first is the restriction. **Those who fight you.** Any reading of this verse, or of the passages that follow it, that detaches the command from the clause that limits it has not read the verse. And that is true in both directions: it is true of the critic who quotes this verse as a charter for indiscriminate violence, and it is true of anyone within the community who reads it as licence.

The second is the prohibition of transgression, and what it says about means. In the one context where the taking of life is permitted, the very next words forbid excess. That is the pattern of this whole surah, seen again: every permission is given with its limit in the same sentence — the necessity clause at 173, the pardon at 178, the ease clause at 185. And it means that the conduct rules are not an optional chivalry code added to the permission. They are the boundary of it.

And the third is the boundary on the reader. **The jurisprudence of war and peace is among the most specialised areas of the law, and it is not to be derived from a general commentary or from isolated verses.** It requires the whole corpus, the conditions and objectives the jurists set out, and — in any concrete case — legitimate authority rather than individual judgement. The standing rule maintained throughout this work applies here with particular force: **no individual may declare another's blood lawful, and no person may act on his own reading of these texts against another human being.** Whatever the law permits in this area, it permits to a constituted authority under the conditions the scholars have laid down, and not to private individuals.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Three movements, and the third turns decisively.

**The will corrected (181–182).** Altering a bequest after hearing it places the sin on the one who altered it and not on the deceased, with Hearing and Knowing named as the attributes that answer it (181). And a will suspected of error or injustice may be rectified by a settlement among the parties, without sin — because the purpose of the instrument is justice and not the literal enforcement of whatever was written (182).

**The fast (183–187).** Prescribed, as it was for those before, for the purpose of *taqwa* — and the fast is *taqwa* embodied, a barrier placed around what is lawful (183). Counted days, with the concession for illness and travel, the disputed ransom clause for those who can hardly bear it, and the closing statement that the fast is better than the substitute (184). Then Ramadan, the month of the Qur'an's descent and of the *Furqan* — the same title given to what Moses received — with ease named as the divine intent and the *takbir* given as gratitude for guidance (185). Then, in the middle of it all, the supplication verse, in which God answers about Himself in His own voice with no "say" between, and in which the obstacle is so often the income rather than the prayer (186). And then the detailed rulings: the relaxation after a crushing initial ruling, the garment said in both directions, the thread that is the horizon, the fast ending at nightfall, the *i'tikaf* prohibition, and the limits that are not to be **approached** (187).

**Wealth, moons, and war (188–190).** No consuming of others' wealth by false means and no handing it to judges to draw out a verdict for what one knows is not his — while he knows (188). The crescents are for measuring time and pilgrimage, and not the back of the house but the door — the second negation of false righteousness in thirteen verses, with righteousness predicated of a person in the identical construction as 177 (189). And then the fighting verse: restricted by its own clause to those who fight you, bounded in the same sentence by the prohibition of transgression, and closed with the statement that Allah does not love those who exceed (190).

Three threads hold the batch. First, **righteousness is a person and never an external** — negated at 177 and again at 189, predicated of a person in both. Second, **every permission is given with its limit inside it** — the necessity clause (173), the pardon (178), the ease (185), the relaxation (187), the restraint (190); the pattern is the surah's signature. And third, **knowledge is what makes an act what it is** — after hearing it (181), while you know (188), the thread running back through 22, 42, 75 and 146.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: the two readings of *yutiqunahu* in verse 184 and whether the clause was abrogated; the three readings of the Qur'an's descent in verse 185; the reference of *ma kataba Allahu lakum* in verse 187; and the thresholds of illness and travel in verse 184.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the occasion of verse 186, the identification of the pre-Islamic practice in verse 189, and the instruction attributed to Abu Bakr on the conduct of war. The gradings of the narrations on answered supplication, on the three whose supplication is not turned back, on the briber and the bribed, and on the fasting person who gets nothing but hunger have all been noted as discussed rather than asserted.

Three boundaries have been observed. On verse 186: nearness has been stated as not spatial, per the position maintained at 2:115 and 2:153, and the obstacle of unlawful income has been given as the tradition's own primary answer to unanswered prayer. On verse 187: the 'Adiyy ibn Hatim narration has been presented with its methodological weight — that the meaning of revelation is not fixed by literal reading in isolation and that the Sunnah explains the Qur'an — because the opposite reading is a live and destructive error. And on verse 190: the restriction *alladhina yuqatilunakum* has been stated with full force against both the critic who detaches it and the reader who would ignore it, the jurisprudence has been named as specialised and not to be derived from a general summary, and the standing rule has been restated in its strongest form — that no individual may declare another's blood lawful and no person may act on his own reading of these texts against another human being.
