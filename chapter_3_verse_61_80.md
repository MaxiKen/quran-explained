## Verse 61

Verse 61 says: *Now, whoever disputes with you ˹O Prophet˺ concerning Jesus after full knowledge has come to you, say, “Come! Let us gather our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves— then let us sincerely invoke Allah’s curse upon the liars.”*

In simple terms, the verse gives the Prophet the final step of the dispute with the Christians: after the knowledge has come, whoever still argues about Jesus is called to the mubahalah — to gather the nearest of kin and families from both sides and sincerely invoke the curse of Allah upon the liars.

The verse is the culmination of the surah's argument with the delegation of Najran. The whole opening of Surah Ali 'Imran — from 3:1 through 3:61, more than eighty verses in the account of Muhammad bin Ishaq and Ibn Kathir — was revealed about the delegation's claims about 'Isa: that he is Allah, that he is the son of Allah, and that he is one of a trinity. The argument has been made step by step: the likeness of Adam (3:59), the truth from the Lord (3:60), and now, after knowledge has come, the mubahalah (3:61). The sequence is the theology of the final proof: first the evidence, then the testimony, then the invocation.

"whoever disputes with you concerning Jesus after full knowledge has come to you" — the verse's condition is exacting. The mubahalah is not offered at the start of the discussion, when people may be honestly seeking; it is offered after the knowledge has arrived and the evidence is complete. Ibn Kathir: the command came after "the knowledge that has come to you" concerning 'Isa's affair. Al-Jalalayn: the knowledge is "the knowledge of his affair" — that he is the servant and messenger of Allah, created by the word and born of the virgin — and whoever disputes after this has been made plain is not a seeker of the truth but a defier of it. The verse is thus a limit on the dispute: there is a point after which argument is no longer the issue and the matter becomes the sincerity of the disputant.

"Come! Let us gather our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves" — the summons is the family: the children, the women, and the selves, each side bringing those nearest to it. Ibn Kathir records the list of the mubahalah participants from the tradition: the Prophet set out with al-Hasan, al-Husayn, Fatimah, and 'Ali — the children, the woman, and the self of his household. The verse's "ourselves" and "yourselves" carries the sense of the leaders and the representatives, the ones who stand for the whole. The summoning of the families makes the mubahalah a matter of the whole household, not a private quarrel of scholars; the stakes are the children and the women and the selves.

"then let us sincerely invoke Allah's curse upon the liars" — *nabtahil* — to supplicate earnestly, to humble oneself in prayer; and the invocation: *fa naj'al la'nat Allahi 'ala al-kadhibin* — let us make the curse of Allah upon the liars. Al-Jalalayn renders the supplication: "Lord, curse the one that tells lies concerning the affair of Jesus." The mubahalah is not a duel of arguments but a prayer of truth: each side prays that the punishment of falsehood descend upon the side that lies. The Prophet and his household were willing to make the prayer; the delegation was not.

The story of the Najran delegation is the occasion of the verse. Ibn Ishaq's account, preserved in Ibn Kathir: the delegation consisted of sixty horsemen, including fourteen chiefs who made decisions — Al-'Aqib, also known as 'Abdul-Masih; As-Sayyid, also known as Al-Ayham; Abu Harithah bin 'Alqamah, of the family of Bakr bin Wa'il; and others. Three were the chiefs: Al-'Aqib, the leader to whom they referred for advice and decision; As-Sayyid, their scholar and leader in journeys and social gatherings; and Abu Harithah, their patriarch, priest, and religious leader — an Arab man who, when he embraced Christianity, was honored by the Romans, who built churches for him, supported him financially, and gave him servants, because of their confidence in the firmness of his faith. Abu Harithah knew the description of the Messenger from the earlier divine Books, but his position and honor in the Christian world held him back. They entered the mosque of the Prophet after the 'Asr prayer, robed and adorned, and the Companions said they had seen no delegation like them.

When the verses were revealed and the Prophet called them to the mubahalah, they asked for time to deliberate. They conferred with Al-'Aqib, and he counseled them: by Allah, you know that Muhammad is a Messenger and he has brought you the final word regarding your companion; no Prophet ever conducted mubahalah with a people and the old among them remained safe while the young grew up; it will be the end of you. If you have resolved to remain in your religion, then make a treaty with him and go back to your land. They returned and said: we cannot do mubahalah with you; you remain on your religion and we on ours; but send with us a trusted man to judge the monetary disputes between us. Al-Bukhari records the hadith of Hudhayfah: the two chiefs came seeking the mutual invocation, and one said to the other: let us not do that — by Allah, if he is truly a Prophet and we invoke the curse, we and our offspring will never succeed afterwards. The Prophet then said he would send a truly trustworthy man, and sent Abu 'Ubaydah bin al-Jarrah: "This is the trustee of this Ummah" — and on another occasion, "Every Ummah has a trustee, and the trustee of this Ummah is Abu 'Ubaydah bin al-Jarrah." Ibn 'Abbas reports: had those who sought the mubahalah gone ahead with it, they would have returned to find neither property nor family; another report says they would have been consumed by fire.

Maududi captures the significance: the real aim of the proposed procedure was to prove that the attitude of the delegation was deliberate stubbornness and intransigence. They had no sound arguments against the points made, no shred of evidence in their own scriptures with firm conviction, and their knowledge of the Prophet's character, teaching, and achievements had made them more or less convinced of his prophethood and at least made their disbelief waver. When told to pray that God's curse fall on the deniers of the truth if they were confident of the truth of their beliefs, none of them came forward. It became clear all over Arabia that the priests and leaders of Christianity in Najran, whose holiness was celebrated far and wide, followed beliefs in the truth of which they themselves were not fully confident.

For the reader, the verse teaches the etiquette of final disagreement. There is a point where the believers have delivered the knowledge, and the other side is not ignorant but recalcitrant; at that point the believer does not coerce, does not curse on his own behalf, and does not despair — he invites the sincere mutual invocation and lets the truth be judged. The mubahalah is a historical event of the Prophet's life, but its principle is permanent: after the truth is made clear, the sincere stance is the prayerful stance, and the lying stance is self-exposed. The delegation's refusal was not a triumph of their argument; it was the revelation of its absence.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 62

Verse 62 says: *Certainly, this is the true narrative, and there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah. And indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the Almighty, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse states the verdict of the whole preceding account: this is the true story, there is no god but Allah, and He is the Almighty, the All-Wise.

The verse follows the mubahalah challenge and gives the definitive statement of what the dispute was about. Ibn Kathir: "this is the true narrative" — what We narrated to you, O Muhammad, about 'Isa — the story of his creation from a mother without a father, his word from Allah, his miracles by Allah's leave, his speaking in the cradle, his being raised to Allah — "is the plain truth that cannot be avoided." The verse is the conclusion of the argument from 3:59 to 3:61: the likeness of Adam, the truth from the Lord, the mubahalah, and now the narrative itself declared true.

"this is the true narrative" — *al-qasas al-haqq* — the true story, the truthful narration. The word *qasas* carries the sense of tracing the story, following its traces — the root used of the accounts of the prophets throughout the Qur'an. The account of 'Isa in the Qur'an is not a legend or a controversial reading; it is the narrative that corresponds to the reality. The verse's verdict is the answer to the delegation's claims: what they said about him — that he is Allah, the son of Allah, or one of a trinity — is not the true narrative; the true narrative is that he is the servant and messenger of Allah, the word cast to Maryam and a spirit from Him.

"and there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah" — the creed that the whole section has been defending: *wa ma min ilahin illa Allah* — the negation and the affirmation. The verse does not merely refute the Christian claims; it replaces them with the positive creed. The uniqueness of Allah in worship is the root of the whole dispute: the Christians, in the Qur'an's presentation, corrupted the monotheism of 'Isa himself — the one who said, "Indeed, Allah is my Lord and your Lord, so worship Him" (3:51). The verse restores the creed: no god but Allah.

"And indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the Almighty, All-Wise" — *al-'Aziz al-Hakim* — the Mighty, the Wise. The pair is one of the Qur'an's most frequent divine names, and its appearance here is fitted to the context: the Almighty is the one who created 'Isa from a mother without a father, who raised him to Himself, who will punish the corruptors; the All-Wise is the one whose argument, whose narrative, and whose decree are perfectly ordered. Ibn Kathir: "And indeed, Allah is the All-Mighty, the All-Wise" — the One who cannot be resisted, whose wisdom is beyond the claims of the disputants. The verse's closing names answer the delegation's theology: the Almighty has no son, and the All-Wise requires no partner.

For the reader, the verse is the place where the surah's argument with the Christians crystallizes into the creed. The story is the narrative; the creed is the monotheism; the names are the divine Majesty who tells the story; and everything in between — the miracles, the birth, the cradle, the raising — belongs to the one who says "Be" and it is. The narrative of the Messiah is true because the one who narrates it is the Almighty, the All-Wise; and the reader who accepts the narrative accepts the One who narrates it. The verse also gives the believer the standard of the true story versus the false story: the true story ends in the worship of Allah alone; the false story ends in the worship of the created.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 63

Verse 63 says: *If they turn away, then surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the corruptors.*

In simple terms, the verse answers the possibility of rejection: if the disputants turn away from the truth, Allah knows exactly who the corruptors are.

The verse is the divine note appended to the call of 3:62. Ibn Kathir: "And if they turn away" — by abandoning this truth — "then surely, Allah is All-Aware of those who do mischief" — for those who abandon the truth for falsehood commit mischief, and Allah has full knowledge of them and will subject them to the worst punishment. The verse is the Qur'an's characteristic combination of the call and the warning: the messenger's duty is the call; the judgment of the refusal is Allah's.

"if they turn away" — *fa in tawallaw* — but if they turn their backs. The verb is the same used at the end of 3:64: "if they turn away, then say, 'Bear witness that we have submitted.'" The turning away is the rejection after the knowledge — the mubahalah has been offered and refused, the true narrative has been declared, and the refusal becomes a moral fact, not a cognitive one. The verse's grammar respects human freedom: they are not compelled, and the refusal is theirs.

"then surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the corruptors" — *fa inna Allah 'alimun bi al-mufsidin* — Allah is knowing of the corruptors, the spreaders of corruption. The word *mufsidun* is the opposite of *muslihun* — the reformers, the repairers: those who corrupt the earth and set wrong in place of right. In the context of the debate, the *ifsad* is both the corruption of the truth about Allah (the false deity, the false sonship, the false trinity) and the corruption of the people through the false teaching. Ibn Kathir notes both: the corruption of the truth for falsehood, and the full knowledge of Allah of them.

There is a subtlety in the verse's pairing with 3:62. The Qur'an says the narrative is true (62), and then says if they turn away, He knows the corruptors (63). The surah is not left in suspense about the outcome of the dispute: the divine knowledge is the guarantee that the injustice of the refusal is not lost. The teacher's limit is the call; the judge's knowledge is the verdict; and the two are joined in the verse: the message has been delivered and the judgment is with Allah.

For the reader, the verse teaches a crucial balance in the Qur'an's dealing with rejection. The call is not coercive and the messenger's task is not to force belief — but the refusal is not neutral either: it is classified as corruption and placed under the divine knowledge. Iman and ifsad, faith and corruption, are not two equal options: turning away from the truth after it has been made clear is not merely a difference of opinion, it is the act of the *mufsid*. The verse also closes the mubahalah episode properly: after the prayer of the curse has been declined and the narration has been declared true, the matter is not left hanging in debate; it is sealed with the divine accounting, so that the believers are not left with the anxiety of "did we say enough?" or "did we convince them?" — the call has been made, the account is with Allah.

The connection with the earlier verses is instructive. In 3:61 the mubahalah was offered; in 3:62 the true narrative was declared; in 3:63 the outcome is handed to Allah. The three verses are a complete model of the end of a religious debate: the invitation to the final arbitration (the invocation), the declaration of the truth (the narrative), and the release of the outcome (the divine knowledge). The believers are not commanded to keep arguing after the evidence and the supreme arbitration have been presented; they are commanded to state the truth, and then the case belongs to One who is All-Aware of the corruptors.

There is also a subtle teaching about the character of the falsehood-followers. The *mufsidun* are not called "the ignorant" or "the mistaken"; they are called the corruptors. The force of the word is deliberate: when people abandon the truth for falsehood, they are not merely wrong about a proposition, they are corrupting the world — because what is at stake in the dispute about 'Isa is not a scholarly footnote but the worship of the Creator, the account of the origin of the Messiah, and the direction of every person's devotion. The corruption of the creed is the corruption of the earth in the Qur'an's vocabulary (cf. 2:11-12, 2:30, 7:56), and the One who knows the corruptors is the One who will not let the corruption stand unreckoned.

The verse therefore gives the believer a settled mind toward the outcome of da'wah. The invitation to Islam is not followed by the demand that the hearer must accept; the believer's duty ends at the declaration, and the acceptance or refusal is the hearer's own verdict, which Allah knows. The one who turns away is not the believer's failure but the corruptor's choice. And the phrase "Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the corruptors" — with the perfect-known inserted by the translator — emphasizes that nothing escapes the divine knowledge: no interpretation of the scripture, no hidden agenda behind the refusal, no deceit in the debate. The believer can pray, call, and bear witness, and then leave the rest with the One who knows.

For the modern reader, the verse is also relevant to the way debates end. In interfaith and public discussions, there is often a pressure to continue arguing indefinitely, to concede the truth in order to keep the discussion "open," or to feel that a rejection means the argument has failed. The Qur'an models a different end: after full knowledge has been presented, the invitation to the sincere mutual invocation is the last arbitration, and if they turn away, the declaration of the narrative is the believer's part — with the accounting left to Allah. The believer is honest, thorough, and at peace; the refusal belongs to the refuser. This is the freedom of the verse: no coercion, no concession, and no anxiety about the verdict — for the verdict is with the All-Aware.

## Verse 64

Verse 64 says: *Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Let us come to common terms: that we will worship none but Allah, associate none with Him, nor take one another as lords instead of Allah.” But if they turn away, then say, “Bear witness that we have submitted ˹to Allah alone˺.”*

In simple terms, the verse invites the People of the Book to the common ground of all religions: to worship Allah alone, to associate nothing with Him, and to take no one as a lord besides Him; and if they refuse, the believers are to bear witness that they have submitted.

The verse is the Qur'an's great invitation to the common term — *kalimatin sawa'* — the word that is the same, the just word, the equitable word between the two sides. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "Every Person Knows about Tawhid": "Come to a word that is just between us and you" — an honest and righteous word that is fair to both parties. Al-Jalalayn: *sawa*' is the verbal noun meaning a word regarding which the matter is upright. The phrase is not a diluted pluralism; it is the identification of the ground that both communities actually share — the worship of the One — and the test of whether the claim of the community to be people of the Book bears the weight of the Book.

"that we will worship none but Allah, associate none with Him" — the first and second clauses are the heart of the matter: *an la na'buda illa Allah wa la nushrika bihi shay'an* — that we worship not except Allah, and we associate nothing with Him. Ibn Kathir: "we worship neither a statue, cross, idol, taghut (false gods), fire, or anything else. Rather, we worship Allah Alone without partners, and this is the message of all of Allah's Messengers" — citing 21:25: "We did not send any Messenger before you but We revealed to him: none has the right to be worshipped but I, so worship Me"; and 16:36: "And verily, We have sent among every Ummah a Messenger: worship Allah, and avoid taghut." The invitation is thus not a new claim but the very message of the prophets the People of the Book themselves honor: Moses, Jesus, Abraham, and all the messengers called to the same word.

"nor take one another as lords instead of Allah" — *wa la yattakhidha ba'duna ba'dan arbaban min dun Allah* — and let not some of us take others as lords besides Allah. The clause is the Qur'an's sharpest indictment of the religious authority of the late communities: the taking of rabbis and monks as lords — the obedience of the creature in place of the obedience to the Creator. Al-Jalalayn glosses: "as you have taken rabbis and monks." Ibn Jurayj, cited by Ibn Kathir: "we do not obey each other in disobedience to Allah." The phrase does not mean worshiping the rabbis and monks; it means the absolute obedience, the unquestioned authority, the placing of the human teaching above the divine teaching — the reverence that is due to Allah alone extended to the teachers. The verse declares the limit: there is no lord but Allah, and no deference that overrides His command.

"But if they turn away, then say, 'Bear witness that we have submitted ˹to Allah alone˺'" — the invitation is followed by the witness: if they refuse the common term, the believers are not left in doubt or hesitation; they declare their submission. Ibn Kathir: "if they abandon this fair call, then let them know that you will remain in Islam as Allah has legislated for you." The clause is the counterpart of 3:63: the turners-away are known to Allah; the believers bear witness. The witness is the completion of the call: the invitation was made, the refusal has occurred, and the believers' testimony is the record.

The verse's historical echo is the letter of the Prophet to Heraclius, which Ibn Kathir quotes: "In the Name of Allah, the Most Gracious, the Most Merciful. From Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah, to Heraclius, Leader of the Romans: peace be upon those who follow the true guidance. Embrace Islam and you will acquire safety; embrace Islam and Allah will grant you a double reward. However, if you turn away from it, then you will carry the burden of the peasants" — and the letter quotes the verse of the common word. The invitation to the common term was not confined to the disputing delegation; it was the message to the rulers and the nations.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'anic model of the invitation. It begins from the ground the other side claims to hold — the Book, the prophets, the worship of the One — and asks only for the consistency of the claim; it states the positive creed in terms that no believer of any of the revealed religions can deny; it refuses nothing of the truth and concedes nothing of the falsehood; and when the refusal comes, it does not curse, it does not despair, and it does not compromise — it bears witness that the believers have submitted. The verse is the foundation of the modern call to interfaith commonality, but its terms are strictly the Qur'an's: the common word is the worship of Allah alone, and the witness of the believers is the boundary of the common ground. Where the common ground is genuine, it is accepted; where it is refused, the believers are not required to abandon their creed — they are required only to bear witness to it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 65

Verse 65 says: *O People of the Book! Why do you argue about Abraham, while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him? Do you not understand?*

In simple terms, the verse challenges the People of the Book: why do you dispute about Abraham's religion when the Torah and Gospel came centuries after him? Do you not understand the contradiction?

The verse turns the argument from 'Isa to Ibrahim. The Najran Christians and the Jewish rabbis, in the account of Ibn 'Abbas via Muhammad bin Ishaq preserved by Ibn Kathir, gathered before the Prophet and disputed in front of him: the rabbis said Ibrahim was certainly Jewish; the Christians said Ibrahim was certainly Christian. The debate over 'Isa had become a debate over the claim to the ancestor: each community tried to annex the patriarch. Allah answered with the argument of chronology.

"Why do you argue about Abraham" — *lima tuhajjuna fi Ibrahim* — why do you dispute about Ibrahim? The dispute was a claim of ancestry in religion: each group asserted that the prophet of the one God belonged to its community. Ibn Kathir: "How is it that you, Jews, claim that Ibrahim was Jew, although he lived before Allah sent down the Tawrah to Musa? How is it that you, Christians, claim that Ibrahim was Christian, although Christianity came after his time?" The claims were anachronisms: religions named after the later revelation were retrojected onto the earlier prophet.

"while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him" — the argument from the order of revelation: *wa ma nuzzilat al-Tawrah wa al-Injil illa min ba'dihi* — the Torah and the Gospel were not sent down except after him. The Torah came to Moses; the Gospel came to Jesus; both came centuries after Ibrahim. If the religion of Ibrahim is defined by the Torah or the Gospel, then Ibrahim could not have followed either — his religion must be established by his own story, his own mission, his own obedience, not by the later laws. The verse's argument is the same method the surah used for 'Isa: the truth is determined by the revealed narrative, not by the later claims of the disputants.

"Do you not understand?" — *afala ta'qilun* — will you not use your reason? The question is the Qur'an's censure of the intellect that accepts the anachronism: the understanding that the Torah postdates Ibrahim is not obscure; it is the plain data of the two scriptures the disputants honor. The blame is not of the intellect but of the will that refuses to apply it — the same rhetorical question used in 3:38 for Zakariyya's reasoning and elsewhere in the surah (cf. 2:44, 2:73) for the refusal to think.

The deeper point of the verse: the claim to a prophet is a claim to his religion, and his religion is his own — the submission, the monotheism, the devotion — not the sectarian label of any later community. The people of the Book were holding the patriarch hostage to their own definitions, and the Qur'an's answer is that definitions cannot retrodate. The verse also implicitly establishes the category of the *hanif* — the upright monotheist before the laws — which the next verses will name explicitly.

For the reader, the verse is the lesson of the method: when two claims are made about a figure, the test is the order of events, the consistency of the claim, and the use of the intellect the claimant possesses. The verse is also a warning against the annexation of the great figures of the past by the present — the tendency to say that the patriarch, the prophet, or the ancestor was "one of us" in the sectarian sense, when his actual religion is known by the universal category: submission to the one God. The believers are not given the patriarch as a sectarian trophy; they are given him as the model of the *hanif* — the one who turned away from all false directions to the one direction.

## Verse 66

Verse 66 says: *Here you are! You disputed about what you have ˹little˺ knowledge of, but why do you now argue about what you have no knowledge of? Allah knows and you do not know.*

In simple terms, the verse points to the inconsistency of the argument: the disputants argued about things they knew something of, and now they argue about Abraham's religion, which they know nothing of — while Allah is the one who knows and they do not.

The verse is the continuation of the rebuke of 3:65. Ibn Kathir: "This Ayah criticizes those who argue and dispute without knowledge, just as the Jews and Christians did concerning Ibrahim." The structure of the argument: the People of the Book disputed over what they had knowledge of — their own religions, the laws given to them, the obligations they were under until the coming of the final Prophet — and that dispute, even if wrong, at least had subject matter within their competence. Then they disputed about what they had no knowledge of — the religion of Ibrahim, his confession, his spiritual condition — and that dispute had no basis in their possession. The verse's *ha antum ha'ula'* — "Here you are!" — is the Qur'an's pointed rhetorical turn, the equivalent of "there you go again": the same people who just finished disputing about what they know now turn to dispute about what they do not know.

"You disputed about what you have ˹little˺ knowledge of" — *hajajtum fima lakum bihi 'ilm* — you disputed over that which you had knowledge of. The word *'ilm* here, in the classical treatment, covers the disputed legal and religious matters in which the two communities were experts. Ibn Kathir: "Had they disputed about their religions, which they had knowledge of, and about the Law that was legislated for them until Muhammad was sent, it would have been better for them." The issue is not that all their dispute was worthless; it is that they moved from a defensible subject to an indefensible one.

"but why do you now argue about what you have no knowledge of?" — *fa lima tuhajjuna fima laysa lakum bihi 'ilm* — why do you argue about that which has no knowledge for you? The claim about Ibrahim's Jewishness or Christianness is a claim about a man's confession in a period with no Torah and no Gospel — a claim the sources of the disputants cannot support. Al-Jalalayn's treatment: the Jews and Christians dispute about Ibrahim and make claims for which they have no warrant. The Qur'an's answer is not a counter-claim by the Muslims; it is the question of the warrant.

"Allah knows and you do not know" — *wa Allahu ya'lamu wa antum la ta'lamun* — Allah knows and you do not know. The verse's two clauses express the epistemological limit: the disputants' knowledge does not reach the reality of the case; Allah's knowledge does. Ibn Kathir: "Allah commanded them to refer what they have no knowledge of to He Who knows the seen and unseen matters and Who knows the true reality of all things." The clause is the proper end of argument: the matter is either known or it belongs to the One who knows; and the One who knows is the witness of the narrative.

For the reader, the verse sets a standard for every disputation about religion. Dispute is not forbidden; the Qur'an engages in it constantly. What is forbidden is the dispute without knowledge — the confident assertion about what one has not learned, or the claim to know the state of people in ages one has not seen. The verse also shields the believer from the demand of false knowledge: when a matter is beyond the evidence, the honest answer is the referral of the matter to Allah, not the fabrication of a certainty. The verse applies to the sectarian claims of every age: the claims of a community to the exclusive ancestry of a prophet, a saint, or a scripture — and the intellectual humility that says what the evidence cannot say.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 67

Verse 67 says: *Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian; he submitted in all uprightness and was not a polytheist.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the truth about Abraham: he was neither a Jew nor a Christian; he was one who submitted to Allah in pure uprightness, and he was not an idolater.

The verse is the positive declaration after the two negative ones. Ibn Kathir: "Ibrahim was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was a true Muslim Hanifa, shunning Shirk and living in Iman, and he was not of the Mushrikin." Al-Jalalayn: "God, in order to dissociate Abraham from their claims, said: No; Abraham in truth was not a Jew neither a Christian but he was a Muslim professing the Oneness of God and a hanif who inclined away from all other religions towards the upright one; and he was never of the idolaters."

"Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian" — *ma kana Ibrahim yahudiyyan wa la nasraniyyan* — the claim of each community to the patriarch is denied by the name itself: the Jews are the followers of the law of Moses, which came after him; the Christians are the followers of the Gospel of Jesus, which came after him. The denial is not a denial of Abraham's virtue but a denial of the label: the category "Jew" and "Christian" are categories of the later communities, and the prophet could not belong to a category that postdates him. The verse's method is the same as the argument of 3:65 — the chronology — stated now as the declaration.

"he submitted in all uprightness" — *wa lakin kana hanifan musliman* — but he was a hanif, one who turned away from every false direction, a muslim, one who submitted. Maududi explains *hanif*: "one who turns his face away from all other directions in order to follow one particular course" — hence "a Muslim, wholly devoted to God." The word *muslim* here is the generic participle: the one who submits, surrenders — and it is used of Ibrahim as the archetype of the submission. The verse is the Qur'an's fundamental statement about the patriarch: his religion was not a label but a posture — the turning away from all false objects and the submission to the one object, Allah.

"and was not a polytheist" — *wa ma kana min al-mushrikin* — and he was not of those who associate partners with Allah. The clause is the negative of the *hanif*: the submission is not only to Allah but the exclusion of all partners; the religion of Ibrahim is not a mere devotion but a pure monotheism. Al-Jalalayn: "he was never of the idolaters." The verse thus establishes the category of the *hanifiyyah* — the primordial uprightness — which the Qur'an presents as the religion of Ibrahim and the religion of the believers: the submission to the one God, untainted by the association of partners and unlabelled by the later sectarian names.

The verse is also the surah's answer to the theological dispute of the two communities: each wanted the ancestor as a proof of its own claim; the Qur'an says the ancestor is a proof of neither — he is the proof of the monotheism both rejected or corrupted. Al-Jalalayn and Ibn Kathir both note the parallel with 2:135: "And they say: 'Be Jews or Christians, then you will be guided.' Say: 'Rather, the religion of Ibrahim, the hanif, and he was not of the mushrikin.'" The doctrine of the *hanif* is the continuously repeated teaching of the Qur'an: the religion of the one God, before and beyond the sectarian labels, is the religion of the submission.

For the reader, the verse is the foundation of the Qur'an's teaching on the universal religion: the religion of Ibrahim is not the possession of a community; it is the submission to the one God, in uprightness, without partners. The believer who calls himself a Muslim is not claiming a new name of his own invention; he is claiming the name of the submission — the same submission of Ibrahim, Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets. The verse also warns against the sectarian annexation of the prophets: the great figures of the divine history are not the trophies of the communities; they are the models of the submission. And the verse protects the doctrine of the unity from every mutation: the *mushrik* is the one who associates; the *hanif* is the one who turns away from association; and the religion of the patriarch is the turning and the submission.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 68

Verse 68 says: *Indeed, those who have the best claim to Abraham are his followers, this Prophet, and the believers. And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe.*

In simple terms, the verse identifies the true heirs of Abraham: those who followed him, this Prophet, and the believers — and Allah is the Guardian of the believers.

The verse answers the dispute of 3:65–67 with the positive claim: after the labels are denied, the genuine claim to Ibrahim is defined by the content of his religion, not the name of a later community. Ibn Kathir: "The people who have the most right to be followers of Ibrahim are those who followed his religion and this Prophet, Muhammad, and his Companions from the Muhajirin, Ansar, and those who followed their lead." The claim is by imitation and by continuation: the followers of the religion, the Prophet who restored it, and the believers who espoused it.

"those who have the best claim to Abraham" — *awla al-nas bi Ibrahim* — the people with the most right to Ibrahim, the most deserving of him. The phrase is the Qur'an's adjudication of the custody of the patriarch: the claim is not by blood alone but by religion. The Jews claim descent; the Christians claim the spiritual line; but the Qur'an says the claim is by the actual following of the religion — the ones who turned from all directions to the one direction, the ones who submitted, the ones who are not associates of partners. The criterion is the content of the faith.

"are his followers, this Prophet, and the believers" — the threefold identification: *alladhina ittaba'uhu* — those who followed him (the prophets and the upright of his line, and those who followed his religion in previous times); *wa hadha al-nabi* — this Prophet, Muhammad, the one who restored the religion of Ibrahim after its corruption; *wa alladhina amanu* — and the believers, the community of the followers of the Prophet. The verse is the Qur'an's genealogy of the faith: the same religion, the same submission, from the patriarch through the Prophet to the community. The three are not three religions; they are the three points of one religion.

Ibn Kathir records the hadith of Ibn Mas'ud: the Prophet said, "Every Prophet had a Wali (supporter, best friend) from among the Prophets. My Wali among them is my father Ibrahim, the Khalil (intimate friend) of my Lord, the Exalted and Most Honored" — and then recited this verse: "Verily, among mankind who have the best claim to Ibrahim are those who followed him." The hadith gives the Prophet's own reading of the verse: the claim to Ibrahim is the claim of the followers, and the Prophet himself is the follower of the claim — the intimate friend of his Lord being the father of his people.

"And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe" — *wa Allahu waliyyu al-mu'minin* — and Allah is the Wali, the Protector and Helper, of the believers. Ibn Kathir: "Allah is the Protector of all those who believe in His Messengers." The verse's close establishes the consequence of the claim: the one who follows Ibrahim and believes is not left to the guardianship of the community or the ancestor; the Guardian is Allah. The *wilayah* — the guardianship, the protection, the friendship — is the divine answer to the human claim of the sectarian custody. The patriarch is a model, not a guardian; the Guardian is Allah.

For the reader, the verse is the definition of religious inheritance. The claim to a prophet, a patriarch, or a scripture is established not by the label of the community but by the content of the religion: the following, the faith, the submission. The verse also teaches the Qur'an's view of the *ummah*: not a new community that severs itself from the previous religion, but the continuation of the submission — the same faith of Ibrahim, the same worship of the one God, the same prohibition of the partners. And the believer's ultimate claim is not the lineage of the ancestor but the faith in the One whose guardianship is the true protection — the Wali whose ward is the believer.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 69

Verse 69 says: *Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you ˹believers˺. They mislead none but themselves, yet they fail to perceive it.*

In simple terms, the verse names the intention of a group of the People of the Book: they wish to lead the believers astray — but they only mislead themselves, and they do not realize it.

The verse is the first of a series on the plots of the Jews of Madinah against the Muslim community. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that the Jews envy the faithful and wish they could misguide them. Allah states that the punishment of this behavior will fall back upon them, while they are unaware." The verse's occasion, per Ibn 'Abbas in the Tanwir tradition: Ka'b bin al-Ashraf invited some of the Companions — Mu'adh bin Jabal, Hudhayfah bin al-Yaman, and 'Ammar bin Yasir — to join their religion and leave Islam, after the Battle of Uhud. The invitation to misguidance was directed at the prominent and the committed — the ones whose defection would hurt the community most.

"Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you ˹believers˺" — *waddat ta'ifatun min ahl al-kitab law yudillunakum* — a faction of the People of the Book would love, wish, that they could make you stray. The verb *wadda* — to love, to long for — captures the emotional charge of the hostility: the wish is not a passing disagreement but a desire. Al-Jalalayn identifies the verses' context: "When the Jews called Mu'adh b. Jabal, Hudhayfa b. al-Yaman, and 'Ammar b. Yasir to join their religion..." — the wish was aimed at specific targets, the pillars of the faith.

"They mislead none but themselves" — *wa ma yudilluna illa anfusahum* — and they mislead none except themselves. The verse's paradox is the key: the one who calls others to misguidance first misguides himself — the sin of the call belongs to the caller, and the damage of the call, if the target does not follow, returns to the caller. Al-Jalalayn: "the sin for their leading others astray falls upon them while the believers do not heed them in this." The verse is the Qur'an's answer to every plot: the plotter is the first victim of his own plan, and the failure of the plan is its justice.

"yet they fail to perceive it" — *wa ma yash'urun* — and they do not perceive, do not know. The clause is the condemnation of the self-deception: the plotters believe they are harming the believers, while the harm of the plot — the sin, the moral failure, the eventual accounting — returns to themselves. The not-perceiving is the intellectual character of the envy: the mind that is consumed by the wish for the other's loss loses sight of its own. Ibn Kathir's note: the punishment of the envy falls on them "while they are unaware" — and the unawareness is the very form of the punishment's justice.

The verse's position in the surah is strategic. The section on the People of the Book moves from the high doctrinal disputes (3:61–68) to the low intrigues (3:69–77): the wishes, the whispers, the plots, the deceptions. The doctrinal dispute is met by the argument; the intrigue is met by the assurance: the misguidance does not touch the believers, and the plot's return is the plotter's own. The believers are not told to fear the plots; they are told the true accounting of the plots.

For the reader, the verse is a mirror for every form of envy and every attempt to lead others astray. It teaches that the harm of the call to misguidance is the caller's own: the sin, the corruption, and the accounting belong to the one who makes the call, whether or not the target follows. It also teaches the believers' security: the enemies' wish does not create their loss. And it teaches the blindness that accompanies malice: the one who is consumed by the desire to mislead others stops perceiving his own state — and the moral failure is the first deceit.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 70

Verse 70 says: *O People of the Book! Why do you reject the signs of Allah while you bear witness ˹to their truth˺?*

In simple terms, the verse exposes the contradiction of the People of the Book: they reject the signs of Allah while themselves bearing witness to the truth of those signs.

The verse is the rebuke of the cognitive contradiction. Ibn Kathir: "You know for certain that Allah's Ayat are true and authentic." The clause *wa antum tashhadun* — while you bear witness, while you are witnesses — is the key: the people of the Book are not ignorant of the signs; they are the ones who testify to the truth of the signs. The contradiction is not between the evidence and their knowledge but between their knowledge and their conduct.

"Why do you reject the signs of Allah" — *lima takfuruna bi ayat Allah* — why do you disbelieve in the Ayat of Allah? The *ayat* here are the verses of the Book and the signs of the revelation — the description of the final Prophet found in their own scriptures, the signs of the truth of the message, the clear proofs of the revelation. Al-Jalalayn's context: the Jews and Christians knew the description of the Prophet in their Books and still rejected him. The *kufr* is not the absence of faith but the rejection of the known truth — the refusal of the testimony.

"while you bear witness ˹to their truth˺" — the clause's force is the testimony of the objects known: the people of the Book bore witness to the truth of the previous revelations, to the prophethood of the prophets, to the oneness of Allah — and the same truths they testified to are the truths the final revelation confirms. The signs of Allah are not foreign to them; they are the very content of their witness. Ibn Kathir: they "know for certain" and still reject. The verse is the Qur'an's precise distinction between the honest seeker and the willful rejecter: the honest seeker follows the evidence; the willful rejecter testifies to the evidence and refuses its implications.

The psychological picture in the verse is of a divided self: the one who bears witness and the one who rejects; the testimony of the intellect and the refusal of the will. The Jews and Christians of the verse are not pagans with no knowledge of the one God; they are the heirs of the prophets, the keepers of the Books, the witnesses of the signs — and the accusation is not ignorance but the betrayal of their own testimony. The believer reading the verse is given the pattern of every self-serving rejection of the truth: the rejection that does not deny the evidence but refuses to act on it.

For the modern reader, the verse also speaks to the psychology of the rejection of the truth. The people of the Book in the verse were not secular or merely indifferent; they were heirs of a rich religious tradition, knowledgeable in the law, devout in their practice. Yet the Qur'an describes them as rejecting the signs while bearing witness. This is the phenomenon the classical exegetes call the self-divided heart: the knowledge that is not acted upon, the testimony that is not followed, the evidence that is acknowledged and then suppressed. The verse offers no excuse for this condition, because the excuse of ignorance is not available to one who bears witness.

The phrase "while you bear witness" may also be understood in the exegetical tradition as a reference to the covenant: the People of the Book bore witness to the prophecy of the Messenger in their own scriptures, they taught it, and they were themselves the custodians of the description of the final Prophet. Their rejection was thus a rejection of what their own tradition testified to — a self-refuting position. This is why the Qur'an's questions in 3:70-71 are so pointed: they are not the questions of someone asking for information, but the questions of a judge confronting a witness who contradicts his own testimony.

For the reader, the verse is therefore a call to intellectual and moral integrity. The believer is asked: do you bear witness to the truth and then act against it? Do you testify to the mercy of Allah and then despair? Do you acknowledge the revelation and then ignore its commands? The verse sets the standard that witness and conduct must agree, and that the rejection of the known truth — whether by the People of the Book of the past or the modern reader of the present — is a division of the self that Allah confronts directly. The only coherent response to the signs is the embrace of the signs; the only coherent response to the truth one bears witness to is the submission to it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 71

Verse 71 says: *O People of the Book! Why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide the truth knowingly?*

In simple terms, the verse gives the second accusation against the People of the Book: they mix truth with falsehood and they conceal the truth deliberately, while they know it.

The verse deepens the accusation of 3:70. The first accusation was the rejection of the signs while bearing witness; the second is the active manipulation of the signs: the mixing of the truth with the falsehood and the concealment of the truth. Ibn Kathir: "by hiding what is in your Books about the description of Muhammad, while you know what you do." The *lab* — the mixing, the intermingling — is the corruption of the message; the *kitman* — the concealing — is the corruption of the testimony.

"you mix the truth with falsehood" — *talbisun al-haqqa bil-batil* — you clothe, smear, mix the truth with the false. The verb *labasa* means to confound, to dress one thing in the appearance of another, to intermingle. Al-Jalalayn: "you mix the truth with the falsehood and conceal the truth while you know." The classical treatment identifies the mixing as the interweaving of the genuine text of the scripture with the forged additions, the interpretation, and the interpolation — the appearance of the false in the forms of the true. The mixing is the most dangerous corruption of the message because it leaves the core truth in place and surrounds it with the false, so the reader accepts the whole.

"and hide the truth knowingly" — *wa taktumun al-haqqa wa antum ta'lamun* — and you conceal the truth while you know. Ibn Kathir: the hiding of what is in the Books about the description of the Prophet — the description of the unlettered prophet the People of the Book found in their own Books, received from the earlier prophets, and concealed. The *kitman* is the deliberate suppression of the known truth — the same act of the scholars of the earlier communities who did not declare what they knew. The verse's two verbs are the two directions of the corruption: the mixing adds the false to the true; the hiding removes the true from the public — and between the two, the message is rendered unusable.

The verse's point about *'ilm* — knowing — is the linchpin of the whole critique. The accusation is not the ignorance of the people of the Book but their knowledge: the truth was known, the mixing was known, the concealment was known. Al-Jalalayn and Ibn Kathir both render the final clause with the force of "knowing what you do": the corruption is not an intellectual error but a moral act. The verse is the Qur'an's description of the dishonest scholar: the keeper of the truth who uses his knowledge to obscure the truth.

For the reader, the verse is a universal warning against the corruption of the message by its keepers. The two sins named are the two ways the religious message is betrayed: the addition of the false to the truth (the mixing) and the subtraction of the truth from the public (the concealment). The scholar who adds and the scholar who hides are the two shapes of the same betrayal — and both are acts of knowing, not of ignorance. The verse applies to every age: the religious authority that blends its own interest with the divine message, or that suppresses the plain truth for the sake of the position, is doing what the verse describes. The believer is given the standard: the truth is not what the authorities intermingle but what the revelation delivers — and the concealment of the known truth is one of the gravest acts against it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 72

Verse 72 says: *A group among the People of the Book said ˹to one another˺, “Believe in what has been revealed to the believers in the morning and reject it in the evening, so they may abandon their faith.*

In simple terms, the verse exposes a plot: a group of the People of the Book planned to pretend to believe in the morning and to reject the faith in the evening, so the believers would abandon their religion.

The verse is the narrative of the most detailed plot in the section. Ibn Kathir: "This is a wicked plan from the People of the Book to deceive Muslims who are weak in the religion. They decided that they would pretend to be believers in the beginning of the day, by attending the dawn prayer with the Muslims. However, when the day ended, they would revert to their old religion so that the ignorant people would say, 'They reverted to their old religion because they uncovered some shortcomings in the Islamic religion.'" The plan's target was not the strong believers but the weak ones — and its weapon was not the argument but the appearance.

"Believe in what has been revealed to the believers in the morning and reject it in the evening" — the theatrical profession: *aminu bi alladhi unzila 'ala alladhina amanu wajha al-nahar wa kfuru akhirahu* — believe in what was revealed to the believers at the face of the day and disbelieve at its end. The phrase *wajh al-nahar* — the face of the day, the beginning of the day — and *akhirahu* — its end, its last part. The plan's stagecraft is exact: the profession at dawn, the rejection at dusk, the visible pattern of the convert's retreat. Mujahid, reported by Ibn Abi Najih and cited by Ibn Kathir: "They attended the dawn prayer with the Prophet and disbelieved in the end of the day in order to misguide the people. This way, people would think that they have uncovered shortcomings in the religion that they briefly followed."

"so they may abandon their faith" — *la'allakum yarji'un* — so that they (the believers) would turn back, come back, apostatize. The verse's *la'alla* — perhaps, so that — is the express purpose of the plot: the aim of the profession and the rejection was the apostasy of the weak. The plot is the Qur'an's precise account of the deception: the adversary is not the opponent who argues; the adversary is the one who performs the false conversion to stage the false apostasy. The believers were not being attacked by the force or the argument; they were being played by the example.

The verse's significance in the surah's structure: the whole plot was an attempt to use the visible behavior of the respected figures to create an impression about the religion — the impression that the religion had been tried and found wanting. The plot is the ancestor of every propaganda of the modern age: the staged experiment, the planted doubt, the manufactured example. The Qur'an names it and exposes it: the morning faith and the evening rejection were not a conversion and an apostasy; they were a performance.

For the reader, the verse teaches the distinction between the outward profession and the inward sincerity that the believer must maintain. The plot does not change the truth of the religion; it manufactures an appearance against it. The believers' defense is the solidity of the faith: the one who knows the religion is not shaken by the staged departure of the one who never truly entered it. The verse also warns the believer against building his faith on the example of the fickle: the profession of the morning is not the proof of the commitment of the evening; and the religion is not judged by the apostates but by the truth. The verse teaches the believers the vigilance against the false witness — the one whose conversion and apostasy are both instruments of the enemy's design.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 73

Verse 73 says: *And only believe those who follow your religion.” Say, ˹O Prophet˺, “Surely, ˹the only˺ true guidance is Allah’s guidance.” ˹They also said,˺ “Do not believe that someone will receive ˹revealed˺ knowledge similar to yours or argue against you before your Lord.” Say, ˹O Prophet˺, “Indeed, all bounty is in the Hands of Allah— He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.”*

In simple terms, the verse reports the second counsel of the plotters — do not trust anyone except your own religion, and do not believe that anyone else can receive revelation like yours or use it as an argument against you — and the two divine answers: true guidance is only Allah's guidance, and all bounty is in Allah's hands, granted to whomever He wills.

The verse is the continuation of the plans of 3:72. Ibn Kathir: the plotters said, "do not trust anyone with your secret knowledge, except those who follow your religion. Therefore, they say, do not expose your knowledge to Muslims in order to prevent them from believing in it and, thus, use it as proof against you." The counsel is the sectarian monopoly of the revelation: the knowledge of the divine message was to be kept within the community, and no one outside it was to be believed capable of receiving the like or using it in argument.

"And only believe those who follow your religion" — *wa la tu'minu illa liman tabi'a dinakum* — do not believe, do not trust, except the one who follows your religion. The verb is the same root as faith; the counsel is the restriction of the trust to the sectarian boundary. Al-Jalalayn: the exclusion clause — "Do not affirm that anyone should be given this unless they follow your religion." The counsel is the theological closure: the community's claim to be the sole possessors of the divine knowledge, and the refusal of any outside admission. The plotters counseled both the secret and the monopoly — the same two instruments of the sectarian corruption the surah has been exposing.

"Say, ˹O Prophet˺, 'Surely, ˹the only˺ true guidance is Allah's guidance'" — the first divine answer: *inna al-huda huda Allah* — verily, the guidance is the guidance of Allah. Ibn Kathir: "Allah guides the hearts of the faithful to the perfect faith through the clear Ayat, plain proofs and unequivocal evidence that He has sent down to His servant and Messenger Muhammad. This occurs, O you Jews, even though you hide the description of Muhammad. The unlettered Prophet whom you find in your Books that you received from the earlier Prophets." The answer is the nullification of the monopoly: guidance is not the possession of a community; it is the gift of Allah, and He gives it to whom He wills.

"'Do not believe that someone will receive ˹revealed˺ knowledge similar to yours or argue against you before your Lord'" — the plotters' second counsel: *an yu'ta ahadun mithla ma utitum* — do not believe that anyone will be given the like of what you were given. Ibn Kathir: "They say, 'Do not disclose the knowledge that you have to the Muslims, to prevent them from learning it and thus becoming your equals. They will be even better because they will believe in it or will use it against you as evidence with your Lord, and thus establish Allah's proof against you in this life and the Hereafter.'" The fear is the loss of the advantage: if the Muslims receive the like of the revelation, they become equals; if they use it in argument, they become the superior. The counsel of the exclusion is the counsel of the fear.

"Say, ˹O Prophet˺, 'Indeed, all bounty is in the Hands of Allah— He grants it to whoever He wills'" — the second divine answer: *inna al-fadl bi yadi Allah yu'tihi man yasha'* — the bounty is in the hand of Allah; He gives it to whom He wills. Ibn Kathir: "all affairs are under His control, and He gives and takes. Verily, Allah gives faith, knowledge and sound comprehension to whomever He wills." The plotters' monopoly of the revelation is answered by the divine economy of the gift: the revelation, the prophethood, the guidance are not the property of a community; they are the bounty of Allah, dispensed by His will. And the verse's close names the divine attributes answering the two counsels: *wa Allahu wasi'un 'alim* — Allah is All-Bountiful (Wasi' — vast, encompassing) and All-Knowing. Maududi's note on *wasi'*: the word occurs in three contexts — the narrow-mindedness of people in contrast to Allah's not being narrow; the miserliness in contrast to Allah's generosity; and the ascription of finite limits to God in contrast to His infinity. Here the plotters' limited conception — the monopoly of the gift — is answered by the divine vastness: the bounty of Allah cannot be enclosed by a community's fear.

For the reader, the verse is the most complete answer to the politics of the religious possession. The plotters' psychology is the psychology of the scarcity: the revelation as a limited good, to be guarded, hoarded, and used as the advantage; the fear of the others' receipt; the fear of the others' argument. The Qur'an's answer is the theology of the abundance: the guidance is Allah's, the bounty is Allah's, and the giver dispenses it not by the sectarian boundaries but by the divine will. The believer is taught the open hand: the truth is not a possession to be guarded but a bounty to be received; and the One who gave the earlier prophets their knowledge gives the knowledge to whom He wills — including the unlettered Prophet and the believers who followed him. The verse also destroys the monopoly of the sectarian argument: the one who receives the truth can argue with the truth, before the Lord — and the argument of the truth is the argument against the falsehood.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 74

Verse 74 says: *He chooses whoever He wills to receive His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.*

In simple terms, the verse states the divine prerogative: Allah selects for His mercy whomever He wills, and He is the Owner of boundless bounty.

The verse is the conclusion of the divine answer to the plotters of 3:72–73. Ibn Kathir: "He has endowed you, O believers, with tremendous virtue, in that He honored your Prophet Muhammad over all other prophets, and by directing you to the best Shari'ah there is." The selection is the divine answer to the sectarian monopoly: the plotters claimed the exclusive possession of the divine knowledge; Allah answers with the election — He chooses whom He wills.

"He chooses whoever He wills to receive His mercy" — *yakhtassu bi rahmatihi man yasha'* — He singles out, distinguishes, selects for His mercy whom He wills. The verb *ikhtass* — to single out, to give a special property — is the divine prerogative behind every prophecy and every guidance: the choice of the prophets, the choice of the communities, the choice of the recipients of the mercy. The verse's grammar is the answer to the plotters' "do not believe that anyone will receive the like of what you received": the choosing is Allah's; the community's exclusion has no standing against it.

"And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty" — *wa Allahu dhu al-fadl al-'azim* — and Allah is the Owner of the great bounty. The phrase *dhu al-fadl al-'azim* — the Owner of the great grace, the Lord of the vast favor — is the divine name that closes the section. The *fadl* is the same word as 3:73's *fadl*: the bounty, the grace, the favor. The plotters feared the receipt of the bounty by the others; the verse declares the Owner of the bounty, and the Owner gives as He wills. The believer who received the final revelation is the beneficiary of the bounty: the very existence of the Muslim community, the Prophet, and the Qur'an is the evidence of the divine selection.

The verse's position is the hinge of the section: 3:69–74 is the account of the envy, the plots, and the counsels of the exclusion; 3:74 is the divine verdict. The sequence of the section: the wish to mislead (69), the rejection and mixing and concealment (70–71), the plot of the false conversion (72), the counsel of the monopoly (73), and the divine answer of the election (74). The whole section is the Qur'an's correction of the religious politics of the scarcity: the truth is not the property to be defended; it is the mercy to be received; and the receiver is chosen by Allah.

For the reader, the verse also teaches the two responses to the divine mercy. The first is the surrender of the claim of exclusivity: the believers do not possess the mercy by virtue of their community alone; the mercy is the possession of the One who gives it, and He gives to whom He wills. This is the theological foundation of the Muslim's humility toward the followers of the earlier revelations: the Jews and Christians are not outside the mercy by definition, and their rejection of the final Messenger is the individual choice of each, not the communal fate of all. The verse cuts against both the sectarian arrogance of the plotters and any inverse arrogance that would consign entire communities to the divine wrath as a matter of identity.

The second response is the gratitude for the received mercy. The believers of the Qur'an's audience — the followers of Muhammad — are the ones who received the mercy in the final revelation, and the verse's framing of the divine selection is a call to gratitude, not to pride. Ibn Kathir makes this explicit: Allah has endowed the believers with tremendous virtue by honoring the Prophet over all other prophets and directing them to the best Shari'ah. The verse's placement after the plots and the counsels of the exclusion is therefore the surah's way of saying: the plotters calculated the mercy as a limited good and tried to guard it; the truth is that the mercy is the divine freedom, and the recipients of it are the grateful, not the guardians.

There is also a doctrinal point in the word *yakhtassu* — to single out, to select for a special quality. The mercy spoken of here is not only the general mercy that encompasses all creation (the *rahmaniyyah* of provision, health, and life) but the *ikhtisas* — the special mercy of guidance, prophecy, and the final religion. This special mercy is the prerogative of Allah; no community can claim it by descent, by scholarship, or by the length of its tradition. The verse's close — "And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty" — gives the believers the reason for their security: the Lord of the bounty is the one who chooses, and the one who chooses is not subject to the plotters' counsels.

For the believer, the verse is therefore a lesson in the divine sovereignty and human humility. The identity of the believer is not the possession of the truth as a trophy but the reception of the mercy as a gift; the community is not the guardian of the revelation but the recipient of it; and the response to the selection is not the exclusion of others but the gratitude of the chosen. The verse closes the section on the plots of the People of the Book with the note that the believers' security is not in their own preservation but in the divine choice — the choice of the Lord of the infinite bounty.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 75

Verse 75 says: *There are some among the People of the Book who, if entrusted with a stack of gold, will readily return it. Yet there are others who, if entrusted with a single coin, will not repay it unless you constantly demand it. This is because they say, “We are not accountable for ˹exploiting˺ the Gentiles.” And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly.*

In simple terms, the verse distinguishes two types among the People of the Book: some are trustworthy with a great amount of wealth, while others will not return even a single coin unless pressed — because they claim no accountability toward the Gentiles, and so they attribute lies to Allah knowingly.

The verse turns from the doctrinal plots to the practical ethics of the community: the honesty of the People of the Book in their dealings with the Muslims. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that there are deceitful people among the Jews. He also warns the faithful against being deceived by them, because some of them, if entrusted with a Qintar (a great amount) of money, will readily pay it back; and among them there is he who, if entrusted with a single silver coin, will not repay it unless you constantly stand demanding, and insisting on acquiring your rightful property. If this is what he would do with one Dinar, then what about what is more than a Dinar?"

"if entrusted with a stack of gold, will readily return it" — the first type: the trustworthy. Al-Jalalayn names the individual: 'Abdullah bin Salam, to whom a man entrusted 1200 plates of gold, which he then returned to him; Ibn 'Abbas (Tanwir): "the reference is to 'Abdullah Ibn Salam and his companions." The verse is scrupulous in its honesty: the People of the Book are not one mass of treachery; there are those who return the great trust, and the Qur'an names the virtue. The distinction is the Qur'an's fairness: the indictment of the section is not of the community but of its particular persons and the doctrine that enabled them.

"there are others who, if entrusted with a single coin, will not repay it unless you constantly demand it" — the second type: the treacherous. Al-Jalalayn names the individual: Ka'b bin al-Ashraf, to whom a man from Quraysh entrusted a dinar and later denied it. The verse's image — *illa ma dumta 'alayhi qa'iman* — unless you keep standing over him, demanding, watching — is the image of the defaulting debtor: the one who will not return the smallest trust without the constant presence of the creditor. The verse's logic is the *a fortiori*: the one who betrays the coin betrays the fortune; the one who will not return the small trust cannot be trusted with the great.

"This is because they say, 'We are not accountable for ˹exploiting˺ the Gentiles'" — *dhalika bi anna hum qalu laysa 'alayna fi al-ummiyyin sabil* — that is because they said: there is no path, no way of blame, upon us regarding the unlettered. The *ummiyyin* — the unlettered, the gentiles — is the designation of the Arabs, and the claim is the exemption from the moral law toward them: the property of the outsider may be taken, the promise to him may be broken, the trust to him may be denied, because the accountability does not extend beyond the community. Ibn Kathir: "There is no harm in our religion if we eat up the property of the unlettered ones, the Arabs, for Allah has allowed it for us." Al-Jalalayn: "they considered it lawful to be unjust towards any person of a different religion."

"And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly" — *wa yaquluna 'ala Allahi al-kadhiba wa hum ya'lamun* — and they say the lie against Allah while they know. The verse's judgment is the attribution: the doctrine of the exemption was not their own invention of convenience; it was attributed to Allah — claimed as the divine permission, as the legal dispensation of their religion. The attribution of the lie is the deepest corruption: the falsehood is not merely committed; it is given the authority of the divine command, and the liegiver knows it is a lie.

Maududi shows the doctrine was not the mere misconception of the ignorant: it was the religious teaching of the accepted authorities and jurists, reflected in the Bible's distinction between the Israelite and the non-Israelite (Deuteronomy 15:1–3; 23:20) and in the Talmudic rulings — the bullock of the Israelite injuring the bullock of the non-Israelite carries no penalty, while the reverse does; the unclaimed article found among the Israelites is announced, among the non-Israelites it may be kept; Rabbi Samuel Ishmael's judicial instruction to decide by the law favorable to the Jew; Rabbi Samuel's ruling that benefit should be derived from every mistake the non-Israelite makes. The verse's indictment is thus not of a folk prejudice but of a legal doctrine — a doctrine that placed the outsider outside the moral law of the covenant.

Ibn Kathir records the application of the verse to the Muslims: Abdur-Razzaq relates that Sa'sa'ah bin Yazid asked Ibn 'Abbas about capturing the property of the people of the dhimmah, such as chickens and sheep, in battle, on the ground that there was no sin in it; Ibn 'Abbas said: "That is what the People of the Book said — 'There is no blame on us to betray and take the properties of the illiterates (Arabs).' Verily, if they pay the Jizyah, then you are not allowed their property, except when they willingly give it up." The verse was a warning against importing the doctrine of the exemption into the Muslim community: the property of the protected people is protected, and the "no blame" doctrine is the lie attributed to Allah.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's most precise treatment of the ethics of the trust and the theology of the exception. It teaches that the trustworthiness is individual, not communal: the community is judged by its types, and the honest member is named as honestly as the treacherous one. It teaches that the moral law has no sectarian boundary: the accountability toward the outsider is not exempted by the religion, and the claim of the exemption is the lie attributed to Allah. And it teaches the believer the vigilance of the small things: the one who betrays the dinar betrays everything; the one who refuses the small trust cannot be trusted with the great. The verse's standard for the believer: the trust is returned without the constant demand, and the property of the protected is protected, whoever holds it.

## Verse 76

Verse 76 says: *Absolutely! Those who honour their trusts and shun evil—surely Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺.*

In simple terms, the verse answers the excuse of the treacherous: on the contrary, whoever fulfils his pledge and fears Allah — Allah loves those who are mindful of Him.

The verse opens with *bala* — yes, absolutely, on the contrary — the Qur'an's emphatic negation of the preceding excuse. Ibn Kathir: "'Yes, whoever fulfills his pledge and fears Allah' — fulfills his promise and fears Allah among you, O People of the Book, regarding the covenant Allah took from you to believe in Muhammad when he is sent, just as He took the same covenant from all Prophets and their nations. Whoever avoids Allah's prohibitions, obeys Him and adheres to the Shari'ah that He sent with His Final Messenger and the master of all mankind." The *bala* is the correction of the doctrine of the exemption: there is no exemption, but there is a reward — the fulfillment of the covenant and the fear of Allah.

"Those who honour their trusts" — *man awfa bi 'ahdihi* — whoever fulfils his covenant, his pledge. The word *'ahd* — covenant, pledge, promise — spans the whole of the verse's context: the pledge of the trust (the property entrusted and the promise to return it), the covenant of Allah with the community (the belief in the Messenger), and the general covenant of the moral life. Ibn Kathir glosses the covenant in the specific sense of the divine covenant with the People of the Book, and the general sense of the fulfillment of the promise. The verse's criterion is the fulfillment: not the claim of the exemption but the performance of the pledge.

"and shun evil" — *wa ittaqa* — and he fears, is mindful, guards himself. The *taqwa* — the mindfulness, the God-fearing — is the second criterion: the one who fulfils the covenant and fears Allah. The two together are the Qur'an's definition of the trustworthy person: the performance of the obligation (the fulfillment) and the inward guard (the fear). The *taqwa* is not the absence of the temptation but the resistance of it — the consciousness of the presence of Allah that prevents the betrayal of the trust and the false claim of the exemption.

"surely Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺" — *fa inna Allaha yuhibbu al-muttaqin* — verily, Allah loves the God-fearing, the mindful. The verse's close is the reward of the path: the love of Allah. The doctrine of the exemption had claimed the absence of accountability; the verse replaces it with the love of Allah for the fulfillers. The alternative is not the absence of the divine attention but the presence of the divine love. Ibn Kathir: "Whoever avoids Allah's prohibitions, obeys Him and adheres to the Shari'ah that He sent with His Final Messenger and the master of all mankind" — the love of Allah is the reward of the covenant-keeping and the God-fearing.

For the reader, the verse is the counter to every moral exception. The doctrine of the exemption says the outsider is outside the moral law; the verse says the covenant and the fear are the standard, and the love of Allah is the reward. The believer's integrity is not the exemption from the moral law but the fulfillment of the covenant and the mindfulness of Allah; and the standard applies to the dealings with everyone — the outsider as well as the insider, the stranger as well as the kin. The verse's two criteria are the two halves of the moral life: the external performance (the pledge kept) and the internal guard (the fear of Allah); and the love of Allah is the convergence of the two. The Muslim who reads the verse is given the definition of the excellent character: not the one who is accountable only to his own community, but the one who fulfils his pledge to all and fears Allah in all.

## Verse 77

Verse 77 says: *Indeed, those who trade Allah’s covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain will have no share in the Hereafter. Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them on the Day of Judgment. And they will suffer a painful punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse warns of the most severe consequence: those who sell the covenant of Allah and their oaths for a small gain will have no portion in the Hereafter; Allah will not speak to them or look at them, and He will not purify them — and their punishment will be painful.

The verse is the strongest warning of the section. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that whoever prefers the small things of this short, soon-to-end life, instead of fulfilling what they have promised Allah by following Muhammad, announcing his description from their books to people and affirming his truth, then, they shall have no portion in the Hereafter. They will not have a share or part in the Hereafter's rewards." The verse's subject — *alladhina yashtarun bi 'ahd Allahi wa aymanihim thamanan qalilan* — those who buy with the covenant of Allah and their oaths a small price — is the act of the trade: the divine covenant and the solemn oath exchanged for the trivial gain.

"trade Allah's covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain" — the *thaman qalil* — the little price, the small gain — is the measure of the trade's folly. Al-Jalalayn: the Jews distorted the descriptions of the Prophet and the covenant of Allah in the Torah, and its reference to the false oath when bearing witness or selling merchandise; the price was the small things of this world, and the exchange was the covenant and the oath. Ibn Kathir's triple hadith collection on the verse covers the false oath in commerce, the false oath in litigation, and the false oath for property — the three trades of the verse.

"will have no share in the Hereafter" — *la khalaqa lahum fi al-akhirah* — there is no lot, no portion for them in the Hereafter. The word *khalaq* — lot, portion, share — is the positive good: the portion of the Hereafter, the share of the reward. The verse's first deprivation is the total loss of the portion: the trade has purchased the worldly gain and forfeited the eternal share.

"Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them" — the triple denial is the most severe divine verdict: *la yukallimuhum Allahu wa la yanzuru ilayhim yawma al-qiyamati wa la yuzakkihim*. Ibn Kathir: "Allah will not speak words of kindness nor look at them with any mercy, nor will He purify them from sins and impurities. Rather, He will order them to the Fire." The three negations are the deprivations of the three modes of the divine mercy: the addressing (the word of the grace), the looking (the regard of the mercy), and the purifying (the cleansing of the sin). Maududi's note: the reason is that, despite their worst crimes, they still thought they alone would be honored with God's favor on the Day of Judgement, and that toward them alone He would turn His gracious attention; they also believed that if they were stained by any trace of sin, it would be washed away by the grace of their pious elders; the verse warns that the treatment meted out to them will be contrary to their expectations.

The hadiths of the verse, collected by Ibn Kathir, give the three shapes of the trade:
- Abu Dharr: the Prophet said — "Three persons whom Allah will not speak to, look at on the Day of Resurrection or purify, and they shall taste a painful torment: the Musbil (man whose clothes reach below the ankles), he who swears while lying so as to sell his merchandise, and the one who gives charity and reminds people of it" (Imam Ahmad; Muslim; the Sunan).
- 'Adi bin 'Amirah al-Kindi: Imru' al-Qays bin 'Abis of Kindah disputed with a man of Hadramut before the Prophet over land. The Hadramite had no evidence; the Prophet required Imru' al-Qays to swear. The Hadramite exclaimed that his land was lost; the Prophet said: "Whoever swears while lying to acquire the property of others will meet Allah while He is angry with him" — and recited this verse. Imru' al-Qays asked what he would gain by forfeiting the dispute; the Prophet answered, "Paradise." Imru' al-Qays said, "Bear witness that I forfeit all the land for him" (Ahmad; an-Nasa'i).
- Al-Ash'ath bin Qays: "By Allah! This verse was revealed concerning me. I owned some land with a Jewish man who denied my right, and I complained against him to the Messenger of Allah. The Prophet asked me, 'Do you have evidence?' I said, 'I don't have evidence.' He said to the Jew, 'Take an oath then.' I said, 'O Allah's Messenger! He will take a false oath immediately, and I will lose my property.' Allah revealed the verse" (the Two Sahihs).
- Abu Hurayrah: the three — the man who withholds the surplus water from the wayfarer, the man who swears falsely after the 'Asr to complete a sale, and the man who pledges allegiance to an Imam and fulfills the pledge only when the Imam gives him something (Ahmad; Abu Dawud; at-Tirmidhi, hasan sahih).

"And they will suffer a painful punishment" — *wa lahum 'adhabun alim* — the fourth element of the verdict: after the deprivations, the torment. The verse's structure is the complete inversion of the expectation of the traders: they traded the covenant for the small price, and the price returned as the denial of the speech, the look, and the purification, and the pain.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's warning against the false oath and the sell-out of the truth. The verse's reach is not only to the People of the Book: the hadiths of the Prophet apply it to the Muslim — the trader of the false oath, the litigant of the false oath, the giver of the charity who reminds the recipient, the one who withholds water, the one whose allegiance is conditional, and the one whose piety is the outward claim without the inward guard. The believer is taught the value of the covenant and the oath: nothing is cheap enough to be worth the loss of the share in the Hereafter; the tongue that swears and the hand that sells are the instruments of the most costly bargain. And the verse teaches the mercy of the alternative: the one who forfeits the dispute, like Imru' al-Qays, gains Paradise; the one who keeps the oath and fears Allah receives the love of Allah (3:76). The two verses are the two paths of the trade: the small gain and the eternal loss, or the forfeited gain and the eternal gain.

## Verse 78

Verse 78 says: *There are some among them who distort the Book with their tongues to make you think this ˹distortion˺ is from the Book—but it is not what the Book says. They say, “It is from Allah”—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly.*

In simple terms, the verse names the corruption of the recitation: some of them twist the Book with their tongues, so you think what they say is from the Book when it is not; they say it is from Allah when it is not — and they attribute the lie to Allah knowingly.

The verse is the Qur'an's most precise description of the corruption of the scripture. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that some Jews, may Allah's curses descend on them, distort Allah's Words with their tongues, change them from their appropriate places, and alter their intended meanings. They do this to deceive the ignorant people by making it appear that their words are in the Book of Allah. They attribute their own lies to Allah, even though they know that they have lied and invented falsehood."

"who distort the Book with their tongues" — *yalwuna alsinatahum bil-kitab* — they twist their tongues with the Book. The verb *lawiya* — to twist, to bend, to distort — is the image of the oral corruption: the recitation that changes the pronunciation, the accent, the emphasis, the placement — so the words appear to say what the text does not say. Al-Jalalayn names the individual: "like Ka'b b. al-Ashraf, who twist their tongues with the Book, altering it by reciting it not according to the way in which it was revealed but according to the way in which they have distorted it, as in the case of the descriptions of the Prophet and other similar matters." The twisting is not the change of the text but the change of the reading — the oral alteration that leaves the written word in place and corrupts its meaning in the mouth.

"to make you think this ˹distortion˺ is from the Book" — *li tahsabuhu min al-kitab* — so that you might suppose it is of the Book. The verse's aim is the deception of the listener: the twisted reading produces the false impression that the twisted words are the words of the Book. The instrument is precisely the speaking of the corrupt version — the one who can hear only the words cannot verify the original, and the reciter's authority is the vehicle of the corruption.

"they say, 'It is from Allah'—but it is not from Allah" — the second stage of the corruption: after the twisting, the attribution. The verse's parallel structure is the anatomy of the lie: the distortion (the twisting of the tongue), the implication (so you think it is from the Book), the assertion (they say it is from Allah), and the refutation (it is not from Allah). The attribution is the lie's completion: the falsehood is not merely recited but given the name of the divine word.

The classical positions on the extent of the corruption, recorded by Ibn Kathir:
- Mujahid, Ash-Sha'bi, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, Ar-Rabi' bin Anas: "they alter them" — the change of the words and the readings.
- Ibn 'Abbas (Al-Bukhari): they alter and add, although none among Allah's creation can remove the Words of Allah from His Books; the alteration is of the apparent meanings and the additions.
- Wahb bin Munabbih (via Ibn Abi Hatim): "The Tawrah and the Injil remain as Allah revealed them, and no letter in them was removed. However, the people misguide others by addition and false interpretation, relying on books that they wrote themselves. Then, they say: 'This is from Allah,' but it is not from Allah. As for Allah's Books, they are still preserved and cannot be changed." Ibn Kathir's balance: if Wahb meant the books currently in the hands of the People of the Book, there is no doubt that they altered, distorted, added to, and deleted from them — the Arabic versions contain tremendous error, many additions and deletions and enormous misinterpretation; if he meant the Books of Allah that He has with Him, they are preserved and were never changed.

Maududi's workable gloss: the meaning of the tongue-twisting is that when, during their reading of the scriptures, they encounter any word or sentence which goes against their interests and the beliefs they cherish, they distort the meaning by deliberately twisting their tongues. And his example shows the corruption is not confined to the People of the Book: some people who stress the superhuman character of the Prophet misread 18:110 — *innama ana basharun mithlukum* (I am nothing but a human being like you) — by replacing *innama* with *inna ma ana*, and translate it: "I am not a human being like you." The twisting of the tongue that changes the small word and the whole meaning is the same corruption in the Muslim community.

For the reader, the verse is the lesson of the recitation and the reading. The text of the revelation is not the only site of the corruption; the reading, the interpretation, and the attribution are the sites equally. The verse teaches the believer the vigilance of the *tajwid* and the *tafsir*: the words of the Book must be recited as they were revealed, not as the reciter's interest requires; and the meanings of the Book must be taken from the Book, not imposed upon it. The verse also names the two forms of the false claim: the false claim that the human interpretation is the divine text — "this is from the Book" — and the false claim that the human fabrication is the divine word — "this is from Allah." Both are the lie attributed to Allah knowingly; and the believer's standard is the comparison with the Book: what the Book says is what the Book says, and the twist of the tongue cannot alter it.

## Verse 79

Verse 79 says: *It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to say to people, “Worship me instead of Allah.” Rather, he would say, “Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺”—in accordance with what these prophets read in the Scripture and what they taught.*

In simple terms, the verse states the impossibility of the prophet's self-worship: it is not fitting for one who received the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to command the worship of himself; rather he commands the devotion to the Lord alone — in accordance with the Scripture they taught and studied.

The verse answers the Christian claims about 'Isa — that he commanded the worship of himself — and the Muslim request to prostrate before the Prophet. Al-Jalalayn: "When the Christians of Najran claimed that Jesus had commanded them to take him as a Divinity, and some Muslims asked that they should be permitted to prostrate themselves before him, the Prophet, the following was revealed." Ibn Kathir: "It is not for a person whom Allah has given the Book, knowledge in the Law, and prophethood to proclaim to the people, 'Worship me instead of Allah,' meaning, along with Allah. If this is not the right of a Prophet or a Messenger, then indeed, it is not the right of anyone else to issue such a claim."

"It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood" — *ma kana li basharin an yu'tiyahu Allahu al-kitab wa al-hukm wa al-nubuwwah* — it is not for a human being that Allah would give him the Book, the judgment (the understanding of the law), and the prophethood. The three gifts are the highest divine grants: the Book (the revelation), the Hukm (the judgment, the wisdom, the authority of the law), and the prophethood (the office of the messenger). The verse's *ma kana* — it is not, it is not fitting, it is impossible — is the denial of the possibility: the very gifts preclude the corruption of the office. The person who received the Book knows the Book's content: the worship of Allah alone. The person who received the judgment knows the law's boundary: no lord but Allah. The person who received the prophethood knows the office's nature: the messenger, not the lord.

"to say to people, 'Worship me instead of Allah'" — *thumma yaqula lil-nasi kunu 'ibadan li min dun Allahi* — then he would say to the people: be servants/worshippers of me besides Allah. The verse's grammatical point: the *thumma* — then, after — links the gifts to the impossible act: the one who received the three gifts, then would say the act of the falsehood? The verse's answer is the categorical denial. Ibn Kathir: "Whoever calls to worshipping other than Allah will have called to Kufr."

"Rather, he would say, 'Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺'" — *wa lakin kunu rabbaniyyin* — rather: be rabbaniyyin — the devotees of the Lord. The word *rabbaniyyin* is the key of the verse. Al-Jalalayn: "masters, scholars, laboring — rabbaniyyun — 'those of the Lord' is derived from rabb, lord, with the extra alif and nun as a superlative of rabbiyyun." Ibn 'Abbas, Abu Razin, and several others: "wise, learned, and forbearing." Maududi: the term denotes the Jewish religious scholars and functionaries supposed to provide true religious guidance, establish rites of worship, and implement religious laws — the same word in 5:44 and 5:63; in the Christian tradition, "divine" is used as the equivalent. The *rabbani* is not the object of the worship but the devoted one — the scholar of the Lord, the learned of the Lord, the one whose knowledge and devotion are directed to the Lord alone.

"in accordance with what these prophets read in the Scripture and what they taught" — *bima kuntum tu'allimuna al-kitab wa bima kuntum tadrusun* — because of what you teach the Book and what you study. The verse's two activities are the reader and the teacher: the teaching and the studying of the Book. The source of the command is the Book itself: the prophets taught what the Book says, and what the Book says is the worship of the Lord alone. Ad-Dahhak: "Whoever learns the Qur'an deserves to become a Faqih (learned)." The *rabbaniyyin* are the learned of the Book — learned of the Book's teaching, and the Book's teaching is the worship of the Lord.

The parallel verses of the section: Ibn Kathir quotes 21:25 ("We did not send any Messenger before you but We revealed to him: none has the right to be worshipped but I, so worship Me"), 16:36 ("We have sent among every Ummah a Messenger: worship Allah, and avoid taghut"), and 43:45 ("Did We ever appoint gods to be worshipped besides the Most Gracious?"); and for the angels, 21:29 ("And if any of them should say: 'Verily, I am a god besides Him,' such a one We should recompense with Hell"). The prophets and the angels are equally incapable of the claim: the prophet does not command his own worship; the angel does not claim the divinity.

For the reader, the verse is the foundation of the doctrine of the worship's exclusivity and the correction of every cult of the prophet. The prophets are the greatest of the creation — the recipients of the Book, the wisdom, and the prophethood — and the very greatness of the gifts is the refutation of the claim to the worship: the greater the gift, the greater the knowledge of the giver; and the knowledge of the giver commands the worship of the giver alone. The believer is taught the *rabbaniyyah* — the devotion of the knowledgeable — the learned one who teaches what the Book teaches, who studies the Book, who is devoted to the Lord — and the love of the Prophet is the following of his command, not the worship of his person. The verse's application to the Prophet's own community (the request to prostrate before him) and to the later cults of the saints and the scholars: the ones who received the Book and the wisdom command the worship of the Lord; and the devotees of the Lord are the ones who teach and study the Book.

## Verse 80

Verse 80 says: *And he would never ask you to take angels and prophets as lords. Would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted?*

In simple terms, the verse closes the argument with the rhetorical question: the prophet would never command the taking of angels and prophets as lords — and would he command you to disbelieve after you had submitted to Allah?

The verse is the conclusion of the section on the possible corruption of the prophetic office. Ibn Kathir: "The Prophet does not command worshipping other than Allah, whether a sent Messenger or an angel." The verse extends the impossibility from the worship of the prophet to the worship of the angels and the prophets: the one who received the revelation does not command the worship of the creation — neither the angel nor the prophet — and the command to worship them would be the command to the *kufr*.

"And he would never ask you to take angels and prophets as lords" — *wa la ya'murakum an tattakhidhu al-mala'ikata wa al-nabiyyina arbaban* — nor would he command you to take the angels and the prophets as lords. The verse's list — the angels and the prophets — covers the two objects of the worship beyond Allah: the highest of the creation above the human (the angels) and the highest of the creation within the human (the prophets). The Christian claims about 'Isa and the angels; the Jewish and Christian veneration of the prophets and the priestly figures — the verse's answer is the refusal of the office: the prophet's own message contains the prohibition, and the prophet's own office cannot command the opposite of his message.

"Would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted?" — *aya'murukum bil-kufri ba'da idh antum muslimun* — would he command you to the disbelief after you have become Muslims, submitted? The question is the rhetorical completion. Ibn Kathir: "he would not do that, for whoever calls to worshipping other than Allah will have called to Kufr. The Prophets only call to Iman which commands worshipping Allah Alone without partners." The *muslimun* — the submitted — is the state of the believer: the one who has submitted has already accepted the command of the worship of Allah alone; and the command to the worship of the prophet or the angel after the submission is the command to the conversion — the *kufr* — which the office of the prophet cannot issue. The question's answer is the impossibility: the one who has submitted cannot be commanded by the messenger to unsubmit; the messenger's own office is the submission.

The verse's context includes the Muslim request to prostrate before the Prophet (the Jalalayn occasion of 3:79): the believers asked the permission to prostrate to the Prophet, and the verse's answer is the denial. The prostration to the prophet — as the prostration of the angels to Adam, the *sujud* of honor, the prostration of the human to the human — was not the worship of the creation as the lords. The verse's *arbaban* — as lords — marks the distinction: the taking of the angels and the prophets as lords — the worship, the obedience, the reverence that belongs to Allah alone. The section's close: the office of the messenger is the call to the worship of Allah alone; the *muslimun* has submitted; and the messenger's command and the believer's submission are the same direction — the worship of the One.

For the reader, the verse is the boundary of the reverence and the foundation of the *tawhid*. The believers are to honor the prophets and the angels — the greatest of the creation — but the honor is not the worship: the *tawhid* excludes the taking of any of the creation as a lord, and the *tawhid* is the very content of the message of every prophet. The one who has submitted ("muslim") has accepted the direction: whatever the veneration of the prophets, the direction is the One — and the one who claims the prophet's command of the worship of himself has attributed to the prophet the opposite of his message. The verse's answer to the Christian claims about 'Isa, to the cults of the saints and the scholars, and to every request for the human intermediary as the lord: the prophets command nothing but the worship of Allah alone; the one who has submitted is asked nothing but the submission; and the question "would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted?" is the rhetorical refutation of the false claim for all time.
