## Verse 141

Verse 141 says: *"That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done."*

In simple terms, the verse closes the long argument with the People of the Book about Abraham and his descendants: that was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned, and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done.

The verse repeats, almost word for word, the statement of 2:134 — and the repetition is deliberate. The whole argument about the patriarchs was an argument over entitlement: each community was claiming Abraham and his sons as its own, and the claim was being used as the ground of a religious inheritance — "we are the heirs of the prophets, therefore we are right." The verse says the inheritance does not exist in the form the claimants used it. The patriarchs were a community that passed away before; what belongs to them is the record of what they earned; what belongs to the later claimants is the record of what they themselves earn. Nobody inherits a verdict. Nobody inherits a religion by descent from a father who lived before the religion's divisions.

The verse is also the closure of the personal liability principle that runs through the whole surah: "And you will not be accountable for what they have done." The verse is the answer to every claim that rests on lineage — the claim that the merit of the ancestors transfers, that the sin of the ancestors transfers, or that the religious standing of the ancestors is the standing of the descendants. The Qur'an has already established the principle in 2:134, and the verse here says it again at the point of the argument's conclusion: the accounts are separate.

The classical commentators note the context: when the Jews said "we are the children of Abraham," or when the Muslims themselves wondered, in the matter of the qibla change (2:143), about the standing of the believers who had died praying toward Jerusalem, the answer was the same. The community that passed is not a certificate; the account that is judged is the account of the person being judged.

In modern life, the verse is the correction of every inherited identity that has been mistaken for a verdict. The child born into a religious community often stands on the claim of the fathers: the ancestors were righteous, the community has the truth, therefore the standing is inherited. The verse says the account does not transfer: for the fathers is what they earned; for you is what you have earned. The person is not saved by the record of his ancestors and not condemned by the record of his ancestors — he is accountable for his own.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 142

Verse 142 says: *"The foolish among the people will ask, "Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?" Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "The east and west belong ˹only˺ to Allah. He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.""*

In simple terms, the verse opens the great episode of the change of the qibla — the direction of prayer. The foolish among the people will ask, "Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?" The answer is given in the words of the command: the east and west belong only to Allah. He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.

Paul of the surah's history: for sixteen or seventeen months after the migration to Medina, the Prophet and the believers had prayed facing Jerusalem — Bayt al-Maqdis — at the command of Allah. The Prophet, according to the narrations recorded by al-Bukhari and others (from al-Bara' ibn 'Azib), wished that he could pray facing the Kaaba, the direction of Abraham. The command came. When the direction was changed, the opposition was heard immediately. The verse records it: "The foolish among the people will ask, 'Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?'"

The verse identifies the questioners with the word *sufaha'* — "the foolish" — and the commentators explain who they were: the Jews, the hypocrites, and the idolaters (Ibn Kathir). The identification matters. The objection was not a neutral inquiry; it was mockery, the mocking of a community whose obedience to the previous command was now being used as the ground of the accusation. The Muslims had obeyed the command of Allah when they faced Jerusalem; they now obeyed the command of Allah when they faced Mecca. The objectors read the obedience as inconsistency; the verse reads it as exactly the opposite: the obedience, and the change, both belong to the command of the same Lord.

"The east and west belong only to Allah" — the answer to the objection is the ownership of the directions. The Jerusalem objection rested on the idea that one direction was more holy, that God resides in one place, that the turn from it was a turn from God. The verse says: the direction of prayer is not the location of Allah; the east and the west belong to Allah, the command and the authority belong to Allah. The direction is a command to obey, not a territory to own.

"He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path" — the answer's second clause: the change of the direction is not a wandering; it is a guidance. The same Lord who commanded the one direction commanded the other, and the one who obeys in each is being guided on the path — the straight path — by the One who owns the east and the west.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to the mockery of the faithful who change. A person or community that obeys a command, and then is commanded to change, and obeys again, will be mocked by the ones who watch: "Why did they turn from the old way?" The verse's answer stands: the command belongs to the One who owns the east and west; the change belongs to the guidance; and the mockers are the foolish — not because they ask, but because the question is the mockery of obedience.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 143

Verse 143 says: *"And so We have made you ˹believers˺ an upright community so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you. We assigned your former direction of prayer only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith. It was certainly a difficult test except for those ˹rightly˺ guided by Allah. And Allah would never discount your ˹previous acts of˺ faith. Surely Allah is Ever Gracious and Most Merciful to humanity."*

In simple terms, the verse states the rank of the Muslim community and the purpose of the qibla change. And so We have made you an upright community, so that you may be witnesses over humanity, and that the Messenger may be a witness over you. The former direction of prayer was assigned only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith. The change was a difficult test — except for those rightly guided by Allah. And the verse closes with the assurance: Allah would never discount your previous acts of faith.

"An upright community" — the Arabic is *ummatan wasatan* — the middle community, the just and balanced community (Ibn Kathir records that the Prophet explained *wasat* as *'adl* — justice: "The wasat means the just"). The rank is not a boast; it is a function: the community is made a middle community so that it may be witnesses over humanity. The middle is the position of the witness: neither extreme, the community stands between the ages, between the communities, between the extremes, and its testimony is the testimony of the balanced one. The Prophet gave the illustration: you will be summoned to testify that the prophets conveyed their messages, and the Messenger will testify for you (recorded by al-Bukhari).

"That the Messenger may be a witness over you" — the testimony is two-directional. The community testifies over humanity — that the message reached it, that the prophets were true, that the religion is the one religion. The Messenger testifies over the community — that the community received the message, that it believed, that it obeyed. The verse's structure is the community's dignity and the community's accountability: the witness is a function, and the function is judged.

"We assigned your former direction of prayer only to distinguish those who would remain faithful to the Messenger from those who would lose faith" — the verse states the purpose and turns the change into a test. The direction toward Jerusalem had been a trial: when the command to face it was given, the believers obeyed; when the command to change came, the test was the readiness to obey the new command. The direction is the *qibla*, the direction of the obedience, and the test is the obedience itself: the ones who were ready to face the Messenger's command in any direction — those are the ones who remained faithful. Ibn Kathir records the occasion: the Muslims who had prayed toward Jerusalem worried about the standing of their earlier prayers, and the believers who had died before the change; the verse's final clause answers — "And Allah would never discount your previous acts of faith."

"It was certainly a difficult test except for those rightly guided by Allah" — the verse is honest about the difficulty. The change of the command is one of the hardest tests of faith: the person who has made the obedience to an old command the ground of his confidence finds that the ground has moved. The verse says the guidance is from Allah: the one who is rightly guided by Allah obeys the command, however the direction moves, because the obedience is to the Commander, not to the direction.

In modern life, the verse is the definition of the faithful community and the definition of the test of the faithful community. The community's rank is the witness: the middle, the just, the balanced — the community whose life testifies that the message is true. And the test of the community is the readiness to obey when the command moves: the change of the direction is the change of the situation, and the faithful person is the one who obeys the Commander in both.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 144

Verse 144 says: *"Indeed, We see you ˹O Prophet˺ turning your face towards heaven. Now We will make you turn towards a direction ˹of prayer˺ that will please you. So turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque ˹in Mecca˺—wherever you are, turn your faces towards it. Those who were given the Scripture certainly know this to be the truth from their Lord. And Allah is never unaware of what they do."*

In simple terms, the verse is the announcement of the change: "We see you, O Prophet, turning your face towards heaven." Allah is the One who sees the longing: the Prophet, facing Jerusalem at the command, was not content — his heart was turned toward the House of Abraham, the Kaaba. The verse says: We see it. Now We will make you turn towards a direction that will please you; so turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque in Mecca — wherever you are, turn your faces towards it.

The commentators emphasize the first words: "We see you turning your face towards heaven" — the Prophet could not command the change himself; he could not ask for a direction of his own preference; he turned his face to the heaven, waiting for the command. Al-Jalalayn: he wished for the Kaaba because it was the prayer-direction of Abraham and would be more conducive to the submission of the Arabs; and the verses come as the response to the longing that was not uttered. Tafhim al-Qur'an (Maududi) states that even before the revelation the Prophet was expecting something of this nature — the words "We see" and "We will turn you to a direction that will please you" show that the expectation was known to Allah.

"So turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque — wherever you are, turn your faces towards it" — the command is universal: not only in Mecca, not only in the Sacred Mosque, but wherever the believer is on the earth. Ibn Kathir (reporting from 'Ali ibn Abi Talib) notes that the command to face the direction of the Sacred Mosque — the general direction — applies from east, west, north, south; the exception is the voluntary prayer of the traveler, who may pray in the direction his mount faces while his heart intends the Kaaba (per the well-known narration).

"Those who were given the Scripture certainly know this to be the truth from their Lord" — the verse states that the change was known to the People of the Book: the Jews who opposed the change knew from their own Books that the Messenger was the one described there, that the description included the reorientation to the Kaaba, and that the Muslims were destined for it (Ibn Kathir; Al-Jalalayn notes the description in the Scriptures of how the Prophet would re-orient himself). The opposition was not ignorance; it was the resistance of the ones who knew.

"And Allah is never unaware of what they do" — the verse closes with the watch of Allah. The concealment and the opposition were known: the ones who knew the truth and hid it were doing it under the knowledge of the One who is never unaware.

In modern life, the verse is the model of the longing that is answered by the command. The believer is not the one who decides the direction of his own worship; he is the one whose longing is seen by Allah and whose direction comes from the command. The verse also teaches that the command is not the obstruction of the desire — the desire toward the House of Abraham and the command toward the House of Abraham are the same desire, seen and answered.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 145

Verse 145 says: *"Even if you were to bring every proof to the People of the Book, they would not accept your direction ˹of prayer˺, nor would you accept theirs; nor would any of them accept the direction ˹of prayer˺ of another. And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, then you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers."*

In simple terms, the verse states the futility of the debate over the direction. Even if you were to bring every proof to the People of the Book, they would not accept your direction of prayer — nor would you accept theirs — nor would any of them accept the direction of another. And the verse warns: if you were to follow their desires after all the knowledge that has come to you, you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers.

The verse's first clause is the honesty about the proof: the People of the Book were not going to be convinced by evidence, because the issue was not the evidence. Their rejection of the Kaaba — despite the fact that their own Scriptures described the change, and despite the fact that the Kaaba was the qibla of Abraham — was a rejection of the command, not a disbelief in the evidence. Ibn Kathir titles the section "The Stubbornness and Disbelief of the Jews": bringing every proof would not make them accept the direction.

The verse then states the mutual incompatibility: "nor would you accept theirs, nor would any of them accept the direction of another." The verse is the record of the religious fragmentation: the Jews had their direction, the Christians theirs, the pagans theirs — and none of them accepted the direction of the others. The divisions of the communities are not merely divisions of belief; they are divisions of direction, of the face turned toward worship. The verse's point is the absurdity of the claim that the communities could be brought together by negotiation on the direction: the direction of worship is the command of Allah, not the subject of the compromise.

"And if you were to follow their desires after all the knowledge that has come to you" — the verse's warning is addressed in the first instance to the Prophet. The commentators read the "knowledge" as the revelation: after the knowledge has come — after the command of Allah has been given — the one who follows the desires of the others is among the wrongdoers. The verse is the boundary of the debate: the debate with the People of the Book is permitted, the proof is to be brought, but the knowledge is the line that cannot be crossed for the sake of the agreement. The one who changes the command to please the opposition has committed the wrong.

In modern life, the verse is the boundary of the religious debate. The believer is commanded to bring the proof and to argue — but the verse states the limit: the proof does not compel acceptance; the opposition's rejection does not make the proof false; and the one who abandons the command that has come to him in order to follow the desires of the opposition is the wrongdoer. The verse forbids both the illusion of the universal conversion and the betrayal of the command.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 146

Verse 146 says: *"Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺ as they recognize their own children. Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly."*

In simple terms, the verse returns to the knowledge of the People of the Book: those We have given the Scripture recognize this Prophet as they recognize their own children. Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly.

The recognition "as they recognize their own children" is one of the Qur'an's most powerful descriptions of certainty. The scholars of the People of the Scripture knew the truth about what the Messenger was sent with — they knew his description, his coming, the qibla change, the whole matter — the way a parent knows a child: immediately, unmistakably, without needing to be told. Ibn Kathir notes that this is a parable the Arabs use to describe what is very apparent: the recognition of the child is the recognition of the most evident, most intimate, most undeniable fact of a person's life. The scholars of the People of the Book had that level of certainty about the Messenger — it was written in their Books, read by them, known by them.

The verse's structure is the pairing of the knowledge with the hiding: "they recognize this Prophet, yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly." The verse is the same pattern established in the whole surah — the knowledge and the concealment; the recognition and the suppression; the certainty of the scholars and the failure to act on it. The word *fariq* ("a group") identifies that not all of them hid it: the Believers among the People of the Book believed (2:121); the group that hid it — the scholars who knew and suppressed — is distinguished from the ones who accepted.

The commentators note the many references to the fact that their Books contained the description of the Messenger: his name, his lineage, his resting place, the qibla, the religion of Abraham (cf. 7:157). The recognition was not the recognition of the Prophet personally; it was the recognition of the description in the Book. The verse says the group did it "knowingly" — *wa hum ya'lamun* — hiding the truth, while they know it.

In modern life, the verse is the distinction between the knowledge and the confession. The people who know are not the same as the people who acknowledge: the person can recognize the truth with the certainty of the father recognizing the child — and still suppress it, still hide it, still act as if he did not know. The verse's lesson is the one the whole surah has been teaching: the knowledge is not the criterion; the criterion is what the one who knows does.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 147

Verse 147 says: *"˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord, so do not ever be one of those who doubt."*

In simple terms, the verse is the confirmation of the whole matter: this is the truth from your Lord — so do not ever be one of those who doubt. The verse is addressed to the Prophet in direct speech, and it is the command of certainty in the face of the opposition.

The commentators read the verse as the fortification of the Prophet and the believers: the Jews knew the truth and hid it; the opposition of the ones who know is one of the hardest forms of the test — the believer begins to wonder: if they know and they reject, perhaps the truth is not what I think? The verse says: the matter is the truth, from your Lord; the opposition's knowledge and concealment do not change the truth; do not be among the doubters.

Al-Jalalayn notes the eloquence: "be not among the doubters" — the verse says be not of such a disposition, which is more eloquent than merely saying "do not waver." The prohibition is of the character of doubt, not merely the act of doubting. The verse set the standard: the believer is not the person who never has questions, but the person whose character is not the character of the doubter — the one who does not live in the hesitation, the one who has the truth from the Lord and stands on it.

The passage also answers the practical questions: after the qibla change, the Muslims who had died facing Jerusalem, the prayers performed toward the old direction, the standing of the community before the command of the change — the verse says: this is the truth from your Lord, do not doubt. The verse's "truth" covers the whole passage: the qibla change was true; the acceptance of the previous prayers was true; the knowledge of the People of the Book was true; the standing of the believers was true.

In modern life, the verse is the answer to the doubt that comes from the opposition of the knowledgeable. The person who knows the truth and sees the educated, the learned, the powerful reject it, is tempted to doubt. The verse says: the truth does not depend on the number of its opponents; the truth is from the Lord; and the believer is commanded to not live in the doubt — to keep the character that stands on the given truth.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 148

Verse 148 says: *"Everyone turns to their own direction ˹of prayer˺. So compete with one another in doing good. Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together ˹for judgment˺. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything."*

In simple terms, the verse states the plurality of the directions and the unity of the destination: everyone turns to their own direction of prayer. So compete with one another in doing good. Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together for judgment — for He is Most Capable of everything.

The commentators note that the verse is connected to the statement in 5:48: "To each among you We have prescribed a law and a clear way" (the commentators quote this in the tafsir of 2:148 — the communities had different laws and directions, each prescribed by Allah). The verse's first clause is the recognition of the plurality: every community had its own direction — the Jews theirs, the Christians theirs, the Muslims theirs. The plurality is not the chaos; it is the divine decree: the directions were commanded, each community was given its own law and its own way.

"So compete with one another in doing good" — the verse turns the plurality into the competition. The religious communities were not competing over directions — the verse says the directions were each their own; the real competition is the *khayrat* — the good deeds. The verse's shift is the whole point: the believers were told to compete in the good, not to dispute the directions. The direction is the obedience; the competition is the good by which the obediences are measured.

The verse's relation to the Muslims is also the relation within the Muslim community: the differences of the communities — the Schools, the communities, the ways — apply; the competition that matters is the competition in the good. The differences are the matters of the command; the good is the matter of the account.

"Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together for judgment" — the verse states the destination of all the directions. The directions diverge in the world; the gathering is one: the Day of Judgment. The verse states the fact — *Allah will bring you all together* — and the ground: He is Most Capable of everything. The gathering of the scattered on the Day is the proof of the power: the one who can gather all the directions and all the people is the One who is Most Capable.

In modern life, the verse is the instruction for the community in the face of the divisions. When people differ over directions — over the details, the schools, the ways — the verse says: everyone has a direction; the direction is the command; the competition is the good. The person who turns the difference of the direction into a competition of the direction has missed the verse's point: the competition is in the good, and the gathering is with Allah, the Most Capable.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 149

Verse 149 says: *"Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque. This is certainly the truth from your Lord. And Allah is never unaware of what you ˹all˺ do."*

In simple terms, the verse repeats the command of the direction for the Prophet in the strongest form: wherever you are, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque — this is certainly the truth from your Lord; and Allah is never unaware of what you all do.

The verse's repetition is the emphasis. The qibla command was given twice, three times in the passage (2:144, 2:149, 2:150), and the commentators note the repetition is deliberate: Al-Jalalayn notes that the repetition is intended to point out that the stipulation applies equally to being on a journey or otherwise — the command of the direction is not limited to Mecca, not limited to home, not limited to one location. Wherever you are — *min haythu kharajta* — from wherever you set out — the face is turned toward the Sacred Mosque. Ibn Kathir records the earlier discussion: "and wheresoever you are, turn your faces in that direction is a command from Allah to face the Kaaba from wherever one is on the earth: the east, west, north, or south."

"This is certainly the truth from your Lord" — the verse attaches the truth to the command. The qibla change was not a political adjustment, not a concession to the Arabs, not a strategic change — it was the truth from the Lord. The verse says it to the Prophet in the face of the objectors: the change is true, the direction is true, the command is true.

"And Allah is never unaware of what you all do" — the verse's closing clause returns to the watch. The People of the Book were doing — hiding, opposing, mocking — and the believers were doing — obeying, changing, turning. The verse says: the One who is never unaware sees all: He sees the opposition and the obedience, the concealment and the command-following.

In modern life, the verse is the teaching about the command's scope. The believer's religion is not confined to the times and places where the worship is convenient: the command of the direction follows the believer — wherever he is, the face is turned. The verse also teaches the source of the command: the direction is the truth, not the preference; the Muslims were not turning to the Kaaba because it pleased them, but because it was the truth from their Lord — and the same standard governs every command the believer obeys.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 150

Verse 150 says: *"Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque. And wherever you ˹believers˺ are, face towards it, so that people will have no argument against you, except the wrongdoers among them. Do not fear them; fear Me, so that I may ˹continue to˺ perfect My favour upon you and so you may be ˹rightly˺ guided."*

In simple terms, the verse now extends the command to the believers: wherever you are, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque — so that people will have no argument against you, except the wrongdoers among them. Do not fear them; fear Me — so that I may perfect My favour upon you and so you may be rightly guided.

The verse's address is to the whole community: the command of the Prophet is repeated, and then the command of the believers. The direction is not a private matter of the Messenger; it is the communal command. The whole ummah faced the Sacred Mosque — the direction of Abraham, the House to which the pilgrims turn.

"So that people will have no argument against you" — the verse states the positive purpose of the command: the change to the Kaaba closed the argument of the People of the Book. Ibn Kathir explains: the Jews had disliked the command and asked what had turned the Muslims away from the direction they used to face (the objection of 2:142); the command to face the Kaaba now met the matter directly — the Kaaba was the qibla of Ibrahim, more respected and honored than the qibla of the Jews, and the People of the Book knew from the description of the Muslim Ummah that they would be ordered to face it — so the change closed their argument. The Arabs — the polytheists of Quraysh — had the opposite objection: "This man claims that he follows the religion of Ibrahim; if his facing Bayt al-Maqdis was a part of the religion of Ibrahim, why did he change it?" The verse answers: the direction that pleased the Lord — the direction of Abraham — was the command, and the objection was answered.

The "wrongdoers among them" — the ones who would still argue: Ibn Kathir identifies them as the Mushrikin of Quraysh, whose unsound statement was the one quoted above. The verse anticipates: even the perfect command and the perfect answer will not stop the wrongdoers from arguing; the argument of the wrongdoers is the argument of the ones who do not want the truth.

"Do not fear them; fear Me" — the verse's command is the heart of the matter. The believers were facing not only the theological argument but the social pressure: the Jews were a powerful community, the Quraysh were the masters of the Kaaba, and the change of the direction was met with the social opposition. The verse says: fear them not — the opposition's ridicule, the opposition's argument, the opposition's power — fear Me. The fear of Allah is the command that replaces the fear of the people.

"So that I may perfect My favour upon you and so you may be rightly guided" — the verse states the purpose of the command: the perfecting of the favor and the guidance. The qibla change was not a burden; it was the completion of the favor: the religion of Abraham, the direction of the Kaaba, the full measure of the nation's guidance. The command of the direction is the completion of the religion; the obedience to it is the guidance.

In modern life, the verse is the two-fold teaching: the command and the fear. The command is the direction of the community — the direction that the believer obeys wherever he is, whatever the situation, whatever the cost. The fear is the standard: the one who fears the people more than Allah will turn the command to please the people; the one who fears Allah will obey the command and let the argument of the wrongdoers stand unanswered. The favor is completed by the obedience; the guidance is the fruit of the fear.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 151

Verse 151 says: *"Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves—reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you never knew—"*

In simple terms, the verse sums up the favor that the qibla passage has been building toward: Since We have sent you a messenger from among yourselves — reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you never knew.

The verse is the continuation of the perfecting of the favor (2:150): the command of the direction of prayer was not the whole favor; the favor is the messenger. The verse lists the four works of the messenger, and the list is the definition of the religion's education: he recites the revelations, he purifies, he teaches the Book and the wisdom, and he teaches what was not known.

"Reciting to you Our revelations" — the first work is the recitation: the Qur'an was not sent as a book to be read alone; it was recited to the believers by the messenger, heard from the messenger, transmitted by the messenger. The recitation is the first level: the encounter with the words of Allah through the voice of the Prophet.

"Purifying you" — the second work is *tazkiyah*: the purification. Ibn Kathir explains: purifying and cleansing them from the worst types of behavior, the ills of the souls, and the acts of Jahiliyyah — pre-Islamic era. The verse's order is significant: the recitation comes first, the purification second. The knowledge of the revelation is the means of the purification; the words of Allah cleanse the soul that receives them. The English translation renders the sequence clearly: the messenger recites and purifies.

"Teaching you the Book and wisdom" — the third work: the Book — the Qur'an — and the *hikmah* — the wisdom. The commentators read the wisdom as the Sunnah of the Prophet: the life, the interpretation, the application, the jurisprudence. The Book without the wisdom is the religion in writing; the Book with the wisdom is the religion in practice. Ibn Kathir identifies the Hikmah with his Sunnah. Teaching the Book is teaching the words; teaching the wisdom is teaching the meaning and the life that embodies the meaning.

"And teaching you what you never knew" — the fourth work: the knowledge that was beyond the community's reach: the knowledge of Allah, of the Hereafter, of the unseen, of the true history, of the pure faith of Abraham — the knowledge the community could not have discovered by itself and did not have before the messenger. The verse's phrase is the testimony of the religion's origin: it was not the invention of the people; it was the teaching of the One who sent the messenger.

In modern life, the verse is the description of the education of the believer. The religion is not a book to be read and interpreted by each reader; it is the recitation, the purification, the Book, the wisdom, and the teaching — the whole of the message, received through the messenger. The believer who reads the Book without the purification and without the wisdom has received only part of the favor; the favor is the full transmission, and the education of the believer is the education of the whole list.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 152

Verse 152 says: *"remember Me; I will remember you. And thank Me, and never be ungrateful."*

In simple terms, the verse states the reciprocity of the remembrance: remember Me; I will remember you. And the verse adds the second command: thank Me, and never be ungrateful.

The verse is the pairing of the two manners of the relation between the servant and the Lord: the remembrance and the gratitude. The first clause — "remember Me; I will remember you" — is the verse that the commentators connect with the hadith qudsi recorded by al-Bukhari (Sahih al-Bukhari 7405, from Abu Hurayrah) and by Muslim, in which Allah says: "I am just as My slave thinks I am, and I am with him if He remembers Me. If he remembers Me in himself, I too, remember him in Myself; and if he remembers Me in a group of people, I remember him in a group that is better than they; and if he comes one span nearer to Me, I go one cubit nearer to him; and if he comes one cubit nearer to Me, I go a distance of two outstretched arms nearer to him; and if he comes to Me walking, I go to him running."

"Remember Me; I will remember you" — the remembrance is the human act; the response is the divine act. The verse is the exchange of the forms of the remembrance: the believer remembers Allah — with the tongue, with the heart, in the prayer, in the praise, in the remembrance of the commands — and Allah remembers the believer: with the favor, with the guidance, with the mercy, with the response to the call. The hadith qudsi makes the reciprocity explicit: the remembering is not the one-way act; the One who is remembered remembers, and the remembrance of the Lord is the response and the reward of the remembrance of the servant.

"And thank Me, and never be ungrateful" — the verse's second command: the gratitude follows the remembrance. The believer who remembers Allah is the one who sees the favor; the one who sees the favor gives the thanks. The verse pairs the two: the remembrance of the world's Giver and the gratitude for the world's gifts. The prohibition — "never be ungrateful" — is the warning against the *kufr* of the denial: the word used emphasizes the opposite of the gratitude. The one who receives the favors and does not give the thanks is the one who has closed the relation that the verse opens.

In modern life, the verse is the formula of the believer's inner life. The two commands are the whole of the practice: the remembrance, which is the heart turned toward Allah in every state — in the prayer and in the work, in the difficulty and in the ease; and the gratitude, which is the heart that sees the blessing and acknowledges the Giver — not only with the tongue, but with the use of the blessing in the way He commanded. The verse's promise is the reciprocity: the one who remembers is remembered; the one who thanks is met by the One who gives.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 153

Verse 153 says: *"O believers! Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the means of the believers in the difficulty: the believers are commanded to seek the comfort in the patience and the prayer, with the assurance that Allah is truly with those who are patient.

The word *ista'inu* — translated "seek comfort" (or "seek help") — means the seeking of the assistance. The believers had been tested: the change of the qibla, the opposition of the People of the Book, the ridicule of the fools — and the verse comes as the instruction of the two means of the assistance: the patience (*sabr*) and the prayer (*salah*). The patience is the restraint of the self: the bearing of the difficulty without the complaint, without the rebellion, without the despair. The prayer is the connection with the Helper: the turning to the One who helps.

The verse places the instruction in the address "O believers!" — the address of the whole community. The two means are the community's means: the patience and the prayer are not the private consolation of the individual; they are the practice of the ummah. The verse's phrase appears again in 2:45 ("seek help through patience and prayer"), and the commentators note that the same instruction is given repeatedly in the Qur'an because the means are the foundation of the believer's life under the test.

"Allah is truly with those who are patient" — the verse's clause is the ground of the instruction: the One who helps is with the patient. The "with" is the divine accompaniment: the help, the support, the nearness of the Lord to the one who bears and prays. The verse does not say the trial is removed from the patient; it says the Lord is with the patient — and the presence of the Lord in the trial is the means by which the trial is borne.

The commentators also connect the verse to the doctrine of the *ma'iyyah* (the divine accompaniment): the presence of Allah with the believers — in 57:4 ("He is with you wherever you are"), in 8:19, in 9:40 — is the presence of the knowledge, the help, and the support; and here the accompaniment is specified for the patient. The verse's two means — the patience and the prayer — are the two relations with the two dimensions: the patience is the relation with the trial; the prayer is the relation with the Lord.

In modern life, the verse is the simple, universal instruction for the hours of the difficulty. The believer is not told to be strong on his own; he is told to seek the assistance in the two means: the patience — the deliberate bearing, the refusal to be broken — and the prayer — the turning to the One who is with the patient. The verse is the answer to the modern problem of the crisis with no help: the help is the patience and the prayer, and the One who is with the patient is the Helper of the helper.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 154

Verse 154 says: *"Never say that those martyred in the cause of Allah are dead—in fact, they are alive! But you do not perceive it."*

In simple terms, the verse states the reality of the martyr: never say that those martyred in the cause of Allah are dead — in fact, they are alive! But you do not perceive it.

The verse's first command is the prohibition of the saying: "Never say that they are dead." The prohibition is not of the fact that the martyr has died physically — the body has died, the life of this world has ended — the prohibition is of the judgment: the word "dead" as the final verdict on the martyr. The Qur'an says the martyr is alive — in fact, alive — and the verse's statement is the reality that the physical death has not ended the existence.

The commentators explain the life as the life of the *barzakh* — the intermediate realm: the martyrs are alive with their Lord, receiving provision, in a state of real life that the living of this world cannot perceive. The verse associates the same with 3:169: "Do not think of those who were killed for Allah's cause as dead — they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision." The life is not metaphorical: the Qur'an says "in fact, they are alive!" — the emphatic *bal* — the declaration of the reality.

The Prophet's narrations describe the state in the images of the unseen: the souls of the martyrs are inside green birds in Paradise, moving wherever they wish, taking refuge in lamps hanging under the Throne (recorded in Sahih Muslim). The verse's final clause — "But you do not perceive it" — is the limit of the human perception: the life of the martyr is real; the perception of the living is confined to the life of this world; the believer is commanded to say what the revelation says — alive — and not what the perception says — dead.

The verse's context is the trial: the believers were being tested with fear, hunger, the loss of property and lives; and the martyrdom was the form of the loss that the opposition inflicted. The verse says: the one who is killed in the cause of Allah has not lost: he has been transferred to the life that is more real. The verse is the comfort for the believers over the martyrs and the definition of the loss: the martyr is alive; the martyrdom is not the loss of the life but the entry into the life.

In modern life, the verse is the teaching of the believer's view of the death in the cause of the truth. The world says the martyr has died; the revelation says he is alive. The verse's instruction is the instruction of the perception: the believer is not to judge the death of the martyr by the perception of the physical world, which does not perceive the existence of the reality beyond the physical. The comfort of the believers over the martyrs, and the meaning of the sacrifice, are in the verse's short declaration: in fact, they are alive — but you do not perceive it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 155

Verse 155 says: *"We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. Give good news to those who patiently endure—"*

In simple terms, the verse states the divine test in its forms: We will certainly test you with a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops. And the verse ends with the announcement: give good news to those who patiently endure.

"We will certainly test" — the verse's opening *wa lanabluwannakum* is the emphatic declaration: the test is certain, it is divine, it is the decree of the Lord. The believer is not being told "you may be tested if you are weak" — the believer is told: the test is the law of the life of faith; We will certainly test you. The certainty of the test is the certainty of the decree; the believers are not tested by chance or by the cruelty of the world but by the wise plan of the Lord.

The verse lists the forms of the test: "a touch of fear and famine and loss of property, life, and crops." The list covers the whole of the human vulnerability: the fear — the threat from without; the famine — the lack of the provision; the loss of property — the possessions; the loss of life — the loved ones, the martyrs; the loss of crops — the produce, the fruit of the labor. The commentators read the list in the context of the early Muslims — the fear of the enemies of Medina, the famine (cf. 2:155 and the same listing elsewhere in the Qur'an), and the losses of the battles; and the list is also the universal list of the vulnerabilities of every human life.

The word translated "a touch" — *bi-shay'* — is "with something": the test is not the complete loss but the touch, the portion, the measure: the One who tests is the One who measures the test. The verse's forms of the test are the forms of the attachment: the attachments of the human life — the safety, the food, the wealth, the loved ones, and the livelihood — are the sites of the test: the one who is attached to the world is tested by the loss of the world's attachments; the patience is the detachment that keeps the faith through the loss.

"Give good news to those who patiently endure" — the verb *bashshir* is the announcement of the good news: the good news is the reward of the patience: the blessings and mercy of 2:157, the guidance, the acceptance, the standing with Allah. The verse's pause — the comma, the continuation — holds the announcement until the next verse: the good news is delivered to the patient, and the next verses give the content of the patience: the response of the calamity with the statement of the return, and the reward that follows.

In modern life, the verse is the honest teaching about the life of faith: the believer will be tested; the test is certain; the forms of the test are the forms of the losses. The verse's message is not the promise of the exemption from the test — the promise is the good news for the patient through the test. The believer who looks at the world and sees the fear, the famine, the losses, and concludes that the faith has failed, has misunderstood the test: the test is the design of the One who measures it, and the good news is for the one who bears it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 156

Verse 156 says: *"who, when faced with a disaster, say, "Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.""*

In simple terms, the verse defines the patient ones: those who, when faced with a disaster, say, "Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will all return."

The verse's definition of the patience is the content of the good news: the patient ones are not the ones who do not feel the disaster — they are the ones who respond to the disaster with the statement of the return. The verse gives the believer's response to the calamity: *inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un* — "Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will return."

The statement's first half is the declaration of the ownership: "to Allah we belong." The disaster is the loss of something that was never the believer's own. The verse says: the property, the life, the crop, the loved one — all of them belonged to Allah; He gave them, and He took them; the believer's statement is the recognition of the ownership. The statement is the confession that the person has no absolute property over anything: the person is the servant of the Owner, the things are the trust of the Owner, and the taking of the trust is the decree of the Owner.

The statement's second half is the declaration of the return: "to Him we will return." The disaster is not the end: the believer is returning to the One who gave; and the return is the destination of all. The verse's statement is the answer to the grief: the one who has lost has returned to the One to whom the lost one belonged — and the return of the death is not the annihilation but the return to the Lord.

The commentators note the doctrine in the statement: the patient person is the one who, at the moment of the disaster, remembers that the disaster belongs to the decree of Allah and responds with the acceptance. Ibn Kathir links the verse with the hadith of Umm Salamah, recorded in the collections: the Prophet said that when a calamity strikes, the one who says "Inna lillahi wa inna ilayhi raji'un, Allahumma ajirni fi musibati wa akhlif li khayran minha" ("O Allah, reward me in my calamity and give me something better than it") — Allah will reward him and give what is better. Umm Salamah's reading of the verse is the famous example: her husband died, she made the supplication, and Allah gave her what was better — the Prophet himself.

In modern life, the verse is the believer's grammar of the grief. The modern world describes the disaster as the loss and the grief as the end; the verse describes the disaster as the ownership and the return. The one who says the verse's words at the disaster has named the truth: nothing was the person's own; the return is the destination; and the grief is borne by the One who owns whatever was lost — and by the promise that He replaces the loss with what is better.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 157

Verse 157 says: *"They are the ones who will receive Allah's blessings and mercy. And it is they who are ˹rightly˺ guided."*

In simple terms, the verse gives the reward of the patient ones: they are the ones who will receive Allah's blessings and mercy. And it is they who are rightly guided.

The verse's first gift — *salawat* (rendered "blessings") — is the special mention of the Lord: the blessings of Allah upon the patient ones. The word is the same root used for the divine mention in the hadith qudsi of the remembrance; the commentators explain that the *salawat* from Allah are the praise, the mercy, the mention in the higher assembly: the Lord's own eulogy of the patient believer. The verse's blessing is not merely the reward of the Hereafter; it is the present favor: the One who is with the patient (2:153) now declares the blessings upon them.

The verse's second gift — "mercy" — is the *rahmah*: the compassion that covers the grief, the mercy that follows the acceptance. The pair — blessings and mercy — is the verse's summary of the reward of the patient: the mercy answers the loss; the blessings answer the test.

"And it is they who are rightly guided" — the verse's final clause is the third element: the guidance. The patient ones are guided: the guidance in the disaster — the understanding that the loss belongs to Allah, that the return is to Allah; the guidance in the path — the right, the straight path that the qibla passage has been describing; the guidance in the reward — the increase of the faith through the trial. The verse's "they" is emphatic: the ones who receive the blessings and mercy are exactly the ones who are guided; the patience is the mark and the guidance is the fruit.

The verse also closes the teaching that began at 2:153: "Seek comfort in patience and prayer. Allah is truly with those who are patient" — and the verse here gives the conclusion: the patient ones receive the blessings and the mercy and are the guided ones. The test was the instrument; the patience was the path; the reward and the guidance are the end.

In modern life, the verse is the completion of the believer's hope. The one who has borne the disaster with the statement of the return is told: you have not lost — you have received the blessings of Allah and His mercy, and you are among the rightly guided. The verse does not say the loss is restored in this world; it says the reward is given: the mention of the Lord, the mercy, and the guidance — the rewards that the modern world cannot see and cannot measure, and that the patient believer holds as the true result of the test.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 158

Verse 158 says: *"Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing."*

In simple terms, the verse establishes the rite of the walking between Safa and Marwah: indeed, the hills of Safa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs the pilgrimage or the minor pilgrimage, let them walk between the two hills. And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing.

The verse was revealed in the context of the hesitation of the early Muslims. The commentators record (Ibn Kathir, from the hadith of 'Urwah from Aisha, reported in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim): at the time of Jahiliyyah, the Arabs had placed idols on the two hills — Isaf on Safa and Na'ilah on Marwah — and the people used to touch or kiss them. When Islam came, the Muslims hesitated to walk between the two hills, thinking the rite was the worship of the idols. Aisha's narration: "During the time of Jahiliyyah we used to hesitate to perform Tawaf between Safa and Marwah. Allah then revealed: 'Verily, Safa and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah...'" And Aisha said: "Allah's Messenger has made it the Sunnah to perform Tawaf between them, and thus no one should abandon performing it."

The verse answers the hesitation with the declaration: Safa and Marwah are among the *sha'a'ir Allah* — the symbols, the rites, the marks of Allah. The worship of the idols had been there, the idols had been placed there — but the hills themselves were not the idols; they were the symbols of the religion of Abraham, the places of the miracle of Hagar and Ishmael, the sites of the search for the water that became Zamzam. Ibn Kathir: the Tawaf between Safa and Marwah is among the acts that Allah legislated during the Hajj for Prophet Ibrahim; the origin is the Tawaf of Hajar between the two hills seeking water for her son, and Allah answered her prayer.

"So whoever performs the pilgrimage or the minor pilgrimage, let them walk between the two hills" — the verse commands the *sa'i*: the walking between the two hills is an integral rite of the Hajj and the Umrah. The Prophet, in the narration, began the rites by reciting the verse and then said: "I start with what Allah has commanded me to start with" — beginning the sa'i from Safa (the narrations of the sa'i and the wording recorded by the collectors).

"And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing" — the verse's closing clause is the larger principle: the voluntary good — beyond the obligatory rite — the one who walks the sa'i *with a willing heart*, or who adds to the rite the devotion, is met by Allah: the "Appreciative" — *Shakir* — who repays the good, and the "All-Knowing" — *'Alim* — who knows the intention behind the act. The verse's final words answer the whole question of the ritual: the rite is the command; the willingness is the heart; Allah appreciates and knows.

In modern life, the verse is the lesson about the rites and the hesitation. The believer is often hesitant about the acts that have been associated with the ignorance or the corruption of the past: the places that were defiled, the customs that were abused, the traditions that were misused. The verse says: the symbol belongs to Allah, and the rite belongs to the religion of Abraham — the defilement of the past does not cancel the symbol of the Lord; the command of Allah repossesses the place for the worship of the One. And the verse's closing: the good done willingly — with the heart, not only the body — is the good that Allah appreciates and knows.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 159

Verse 159 says: *"Those who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed— after We made it clear for humanity in the Book—will be condemned by Allah and ˹all˺ those who condemn."*

In simple terms, the verse states the condemnation of the concealment: those who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed — after We made it clear for humanity in the Book — will be condemned by Allah and all those who condemn.

The verse identifies the crime: the concealment of the clear proofs and the guidance — the signs and the evidences and the guidance — that Allah revealed, after He made it clear for humanity in the Book. The revelation was made clear: the proofs were given, the guidance was set, the Book was sent, the description of the Messenger was in the Scriptures of the People of the Book. The concealment is the act of the ones who had the clear proofs and the guidance and hid it. Abu al-'Aliyah, ar-Rabi' bin Anas and Qatadah said that the ayat were revealed about the People of the Scripture who hid the description of Muhammad (as recorded by Ibn Kathir); Al-Jalalayn notes the context: the Jews concealed the stoning verse and the description of Muhammad.

"Will be condemned by Allah and all those who condemn" — the verse's penalty is the *la'n*: the divine curse, the removal from the mercy. The verse names the cursers: Allah — the first curse; and "all those who curse" — the angels, the believers, and (as the commentators add, in the various statements of the tafsir) everything that curses, each in its own way. Ibn Kathir records the hadith: "Whoever was asked about knowledge that he has, but he hid it, then a bridle made of fire will be tied around his mouth on the Day of Resurrection" (recorded in the collections, with the warning against the concealment of knowledge); and the hadith of Abu Hurayrah: "If it were not for this verse in the Book of Allah, I would not have narrated a hadith for anyone" — the hadith of the verse's warning becoming the shield of the transmission of the knowledge. The commentators note the contrast: everything, including the fish in the sea, asks forgiveness for the scholars; and the ones who hide the knowledge are cursed by Allah and the cursers.

The verse's position in the surah is exact: the passage opened with the "fools" mocking the qibla change and the People of the Book who knew and hid; the passage closes with the concealment of the proofs and the guidance — the same people, the same crime: the knowledge and the suppression.

In modern life, the verse is the standing warning to the keepers of the knowledge. The scholar, the teacher, the editor, the authority — the one who holds the clear proofs and the guidance — is warned: the concealment after the clarity is the condemned act. The person who knows the truth and suppresses it for the fear, for the profit, for the favor, for the authority — the verse's curse is the measure of the crime. And the verse's counterpart: the transmission of the knowledge — the narration, the publication, the clarification — is the opposite of the concealment, and the ones who spread the knowledge with honesty have the forgiveness of the creation and the reward of the Lord.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 160

Verse 160 says: *"As for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known, they are the ones to whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the verse states the exception to the condemnation: as for those who repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known — they are the ones to whom I will turn in forgiveness, for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

The verse's three conditions are the way out of the condemnation: (1) *tabu* — they repent: the return to Allah from the concealment, the regret of the act, the turning in repentance; (2) *aslahu* — they mend their ways: the reform of the conduct, the correction of the behavior, the change of the practice; (3) *bayyanu* — and let the truth be known: the declaration, the publication of the truth they have hidden. The three are the complete repentance: the return of the heart, the reform of the deed, and the disclosure of the truth.

The third condition is the specific one — the truth that was concealed must be disclosed. Ibn Kathir: the ayat refers to those who regret what they have been doing and correct their behavior, and thus explain to the people what they have been hiding. The concealment of the truth is not repented by the private regret alone; the one who hid the truth is commanded to let the truth be known — the public disclosure is part of the repentance. The verse is the corrective of the concealment: the act of the hiding is undone by the act of the telling.

"They are the ones to whom I will turn in forgiveness" — the verse's response is the divine acceptance: the One who condemned the concealment now declares the acceptance of the repentance. The verb *atubu* — "I turn" — is the divine side of the repentance (tawbah): the servant turns to Allah and Allah turns to the servant; the acceptance is the divine turning. And the verse's final words give the ground: "for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful" — *al-Tawwab* — the One who accepts the repentance again and again — and *al-Rahim* — the Most Merciful.

The verse's lesson: the condemnation of the concealment (159) was not the closing of the door; the next verse opens the door: the repentance is accepted, the mend is required, the disclosure is commanded — and the One who condemns is the One who accepts: Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

In modern life, the verse is the structure of the honest correction. The person who has hidden the truth, suppressed the evidence, spoken against the clarity — the verse's way: repent, mend, and let the truth be known. The verse is the model of the correction for the institutions and authorities that have hidden what should have been public: the correction is the public disclosure, not only the private regret; and the verse's promise: the One who is the Accepter of Repentance and the Most Merciful turns to the one who turns, and discloses.

- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir*, commentary on 2:159–160 — Abu al-'Aliyah, ar-Rabi' bin Anas and Qatadah: revealed about the People of the Scripture who hid the description of Muhammad; Al-Jalalayn: the stoning verse and the description of Muhammad; the curse by Allah and the cursers (angels, believers, and everything that curses); the hadith of the bridle of fire for the one who hides knowledge; Abu Hurayrah's "if it were not for this verse, I would not have narrated"; the exception — those who repent, mend their ways and openly declare the truth — I turn to them; the indication that those who called to innovation or disbelief and repent are forgiven; the verse applies to the Jews' concealment. https://www.alim.org/quran/tafsir/ibn-kathir/surah/2/159/ and https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-160-sora-2.html
