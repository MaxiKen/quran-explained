## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:141

> That was a nation which has passed on. They will have what they earned, and you will have what you have earned. And you will not be asked about what they used to do.

**Commentary:**

### A Nation That Has Passed On

This āyah repeats the conclusion already given in 2:134. The repetition is purposeful. The preceding verses have just mentioned Ibrāhīm, Ismāʿīl, Isḥāq, Yaʿqūb, and the tribes of Israel, and the Qur’an now closes the discussion by preventing people from turning those noble ancestors into a substitute for faith. They were an ummah that has passed on—*tilka ummatun qad khalat*. Their history remains instructive, but their earthly opportunity to act has ended.

The wording does not diminish the prophets or the righteous people among them. Rather, it places their honour in its proper location. Ibrāhīm’s rank came through submission, sincerity, and obedience to Allah, not through a genealogical label that could be claimed without following his path. The same principle applies to every community. The best ancestry is a reason for gratitude and imitation, never a certificate that exempts someone from worship or accountability.

### Deeds Cannot Be Inherited

“They will have what they earned, and you will have what you have earned.” The Qur’an distinguishes between receiving the benefit of another person’s example and inheriting the reward of another person’s deeds. A believer may love a righteous parent, learn from a prophet, or benefit from a teacher, but he cannot borrow their īmān as though it were property. Conversely, a person is not condemned merely because an ancestor or fellow member of his group acted wrongly.

This is the Qur’anic principle of individual responsibility: “No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” (6:164; 17:15; 35:18; 53:38). It does not deny that people influence one another. Those who invite others to error share in the consequences of what they initiate, and those who establish a good practice share in its reward, as taught in the authentic hadith recorded by Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Yet the one who chooses, approves, and acts remains responsible for his own response.

The principle also corrects empty claims of religious inheritance. A person may say, “My forefathers were prophets,” “my family belongs to the learned,” or “my community is chosen,” while neglecting prayer, justice, repentance, and truthfulness. The Qur’an asks not only where a person comes from but what he has done with the guidance that reached him. Honourable lineage can increase a person’s duty to live honourably; it cannot replace that duty.

### Do Not Turn History into an Excuse

The final clause—“you will not be asked about what they used to do”—is addressed to those living after the earlier generations. They are not required to answer for the private deeds of prophets and ancestors. They will be questioned about their own faith, their own choices, and their own treatment of the truth. Historical admiration is valuable only when it becomes present obedience.

This protects Muslims from two opposite errors. The first is pride: claiming the greatness of earlier Muslims while making no effort to follow their sincerity. The second is despair: believing that the failures of earlier people make righteousness impossible today. Allah has placed every generation before its own test. A community may be inspired by the past without pretending to possess its reward automatically.

### The Ethics of Ancestral Claims

The verse is especially relevant to disputes over religious identity. The People of the Book argued about the true heirs of Ibrāhīm, while the Qur’an had already shown that he was neither a tribal possession nor a partisan of later labels. He was a *ḥanīf*, one who turned away from false worship toward pure devotion to Allah (2:135; 3:67). Whoever follows his submission shares in his way, regardless of ethnicity; whoever abandons submission cannot be rescued by a claim of descent.

The Prophet ﷺ made the same moral point in his warning that whoever is delayed by his deeds will not be advanced by his lineage, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith does not deny family ties or the rights of relatives. It denies the fantasy that a name, clan, or respected ancestor can perform the obedience that a person refuses to perform himself.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:134 and 2:141 on the passing of earlier communities and present accountability.
- Qur’an 6:164, 17:15, 35:18, and 53:38–39 on no person bearing another’s burden.
- Qur’an 3:67 and 16:123 on Ibrāhīm’s *ḥanīfiyyah* and following his way.
- Qur’an 49:13 on human diversity and the true measure of honour, namely taqwā.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that whoever is slowed by his deeds is not advanced by his lineage, and the hadith on sharing the consequence of initiating good or evil practice.
- For the relation of this repetition to the preceding controversy over Ibrāhīm’s religion, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:134 and 2:141.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:142

> The foolish among the people will say, “What has turned them away from their direction of prayer, which they used to face?” Say, “To Allah belong the east and the west. He guides whom He wills to a straight path.”

**Commentary:**

### The Question of the Foolish

The change of qiblah from Bayt al-Maqdis in Jerusalem to al-Masjid al-Ḥarām in Makkah was a test of obedience and an occasion for confused or hostile people to object. The Qur’an calls the objectors *sufahāʾ*, “the foolish.” This is not a condemnation of every person who asks a sincere question. It describes those who judge divine guidance by superficial appearance, mock what they do not understand, or allow tribal partisanship to replace submission.

A sincere believer may ask why a command has changed or how a ruling should be understood. Such a question can lead to knowledge. Foolishness appears when the question is used as a weapon: “If the first direction was correct, why change it? If the new direction is correct, why was the first used?” The objection assumes that a divinely appointed direction is sacred because of its physical orientation rather than because Allah appoints it.

### Direction Is a Sign of Obedience

“To Allah belong the east and the west.” Every direction is created by Allah and belongs to His dominion. No place or compass point possesses independent divinity. The qiblah is not worshipped; Allah is worshipped. Facing a prescribed direction unites the community and gives embodied form to obedience, but the direction itself does not limit the Lord to a location.

The same principle applies to sacred times, forms of prayer, and other revealed symbols. Their importance comes from *amr*, the command of Allah, and from the meanings Allah has attached to them. The believer does not ask whether one side of the earth is inherently more divine than another. He asks what his Lord has commanded and then obeys with trust. This is why the change was not a contradiction in divine knowledge. It was a change in the believers’ obligation and a disclosure of a new stage in the mission.

### Guidance Is Not Controlled by Human Expectations

Allah “guides whom He wills to a straight path.” Divine will is not arbitrary injustice. The Qur’an repeatedly explains that guidance is granted to those who seek truth with humility, while people who persist in arrogance and wrongdoing turn themselves away from its benefit (29:69; 61:5). Allah knows who is sincere, who is merely disputing, and what each person does with the signs that reach him.

The straight path—*ṣirāṭ mustaqīm*—is not a road chosen by custom or inherited political identity. It is the balanced way of tawḥīd, revelation, worship, and moral conduct. A person can possess sophisticated arguments yet be far from guidance if his arguments are designed only to protect pride. Another person may not know every answer but may be guided by a willingness to submit to clear truth.

### A Test of the Heart

The qiblah change exposed whether people followed the Messenger ﷺ or merely followed a familiar arrangement. The believers did not worship the earlier direction; they obeyed Allah when He commanded them to face it, and they obeyed Him again when He commanded a new direction. This is the meaning of *islām*: willing surrender to the One who gives the command.

A similar test occurs whenever a person’s religious practice is challenged by clear evidence. Sometimes the heart prefers a custom because it is old, familiar, or associated with respected people. Revelation may then require a correction. The question is not whether change feels comfortable but whether one’s allegiance is to Allah or to the emotional security of habit.

### The Qur’an’s Method with Objections

The reply is concise and principled. It does not chase every sarcastic remark or grant the objectors authority to define the issue. It establishes Allah’s ownership of all directions, then affirms His power to guide. This method teaches believers to answer confusion with the governing truth rather than becoming trapped in endless polemics.

At the same time, the verse does not command believers to despise people who do not yet understand. The label *sufahāʾ* identifies the quality of the objection, not a licence for personal abuse. Muslims should explain the wisdom of the qiblah, distinguish honest inquiry from mockery, and preserve good character in disagreement.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:115 on Allah’s ownership of every direction and His encompassing presence and knowledge.
- Qur’an 2:143–150 on the qiblah as a test and a mark of the Muslim community.
- Qur’an 29:69 and 61:5 on guidance and the consequences of turning away after knowing the truth.
- Qur’an 6:162–163 on directing prayer, worship, life, and death to Allah alone.
- For the reports surrounding the change of qiblah and the nature of the objection, see Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the narrations of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib, and Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī on 2:142.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:143

> And thus We have made you a middle community so that you may be witnesses over humanity and that the Messenger may be a witness over you. We appointed the direction which you used to face only to distinguish those who follow the Messenger from those who turn back on their heels. Indeed, it was difficult except for those whom Allah guided. And Allah would never let your faith go to waste. Surely Allah is Kind and Merciful to the people.

**Commentary:**

### A Middle and Upright Community

The Muslim community is called *ummatan wasaṭan*. The word *wasaṭ* means the middle, but in Qur’anic usage it also carries the meanings of balanced, just, excellent, and upright. This is not a declaration that Muslims are automatically virtuous merely by belonging to the community. It is a description of the vocation Allah has given them: to embody a balanced revelation and to bear it truthfully before humanity.

“Middle” does not mean lukewarm or indecisive. Islam is in the middle between excess and neglect, not between truth and falsehood. It joins spiritual devotion with responsible life in the world, individual accountability with social justice, and hope in Allah’s mercy with fear of His judgment. It rejects the excess that makes religion unbearable and the negligence that empties religion of obedience.

### Witnesses over Humanity

The community is made a witness over people—*shuhadāʾ ʿalā al-nās*. The primary witness is the Messenger ﷺ, who conveyed the revelation, explained it, and lived it. The believers witness by preserving, embodying, and communicating the message that reached them. This is an honour, but it is also a serious responsibility. A witness must know what he is witnessing, speak truthfully, and avoid corrupting the evidence through hypocrisy.

Classical exegetes explain that the community’s testimony includes its testimony on the Day of Resurrection that messengers conveyed Allah’s message, based on the revelation and the Messenger’s teaching. It also has a worldly dimension: Muslims should make the truth visible through justice, mercy, honesty, and worship. A community that calls people to tawḥīd while practising oppression undermines its own testimony.

The phrase does not authorize domination over every society. Being a witness is not the same as being a tyrant or forcing faith. The Qur’an says, “There is no compulsion in religion” (2:256), while also requiring believers to stand firmly for justice (4:135). Witness is conveyed through clarity and character; judgment belongs to Allah.

### The Messenger as Witness over the Believers

The Messenger is a witness over the Muslim community. He is the authoritative model through whom the Qur’an was explained in worship, ethics, family life, governance, patience, and mercy. Muslims do not decide what Islam means by preference alone. They return to the Qur’an and the authentic Sunnah, because the Messenger ﷺ is the one who taught the community how to live the revelation.

This relationship also protects the community from self-congratulation. Muslims may be witnesses over others only while remembering that they themselves stand under the Messenger’s testimony. Their claims are measured against his obedience, truthfulness, compassion, and courage. The one who invokes the Prophet’s name while abandoning his moral example has not understood the verse.

### The Earlier Qiblah as a Test

The phrase “We appointed the direction which you used to face only to distinguish” explains one wisdom of the earlier qiblah. The change was not a divine experiment based on uncertainty. Allah knew the response before the test occurred. The test made the hidden attitude visible in action: who would follow the Messenger when the command did not align with habit, and who would retreat into objection.

Every divine command can reveal this distinction. Obedience is easy when revelation agrees with a person’s desire, social environment, or immediate advantage. It becomes a sign of faith when the command asks for trust, sacrifice, or correction. The qiblah is physically simple, but it revealed whether people regarded the Messenger ﷺ as a true messenger or merely as a leader whose decisions could be accepted selectively.

### “Your Faith” Includes the Earlier Prayers

Allah promises that He will not let the believers’ faith go to waste. In the context of the qiblah, faith includes the prayers offered while facing Bayt al-Maqdis. When some Companions worried about those who had died before the change, the revelation assured them that sincere obedience to the command known at the time was accepted. Their previous prayers were not invalidated retroactively.

This teaches a broad principle about divine judgment. Allah does not punish a person for failing to obey a command that had not reached him, nor does He discard sincere worship performed according to the guidance available to him. The believer still seeks correction when new knowledge arrives, but he does not collapse into despair over acts done sincerely under an earlier understanding.

### Kindness and Mercy in the Test

The closing names of Allah, *Raʾūf* and *Raḥīm*, balance the language of examination. The test is real, but Allah is not eager to destroy His servants. He gives commands that cultivate them, clarifies what they need to know, and preserves the reward of sincere obedience. His mercy appears both in guidance and in the acceptance of imperfect servants who return to Him.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:142–150 on the qiblah and the distinction between sincere following and retreat.
- Qur’an 4:135, 5:8, and 16:90 on justice as part of the community’s public witness.
- Qur’an 2:256 and 10:99 on faith not being produced by coercion.
- Qur’an 4:41 and 22:78 on the Messenger’s testimony and the community’s responsibility.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports concerning the change of qiblah and the acceptance of earlier prayers; see also Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī on the Prophet’s teaching and practice as explanation of revelation.
- For *wasaṭ* as justice and excellence, and for the meaning of “your faith” as the earlier prayers, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:143.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:144

> We certainly see you turning your face toward the heaven, and We will surely turn you toward a direction that pleases you. So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Ḥarām. Wherever you are, turn your faces toward it. Surely those given the Scripture know that this is the truth from their Lord, and Allah is never unaware of what they do.

**Commentary:**

### The Prophet’s Longing

Allah had already commanded the Messenger ﷺ to face Bayt al-Maqdis, but the Prophet looked repeatedly toward the sky, awaiting a command that would establish the qiblah of Ibrāhīm and distinguish the final community. His longing was not dissatisfaction with Allah’s earlier command. It was a hopeful prayer for the direction he knew would better express the independent identity of the Muslim ummah and fulfil the promise associated with the Sacred House.

“We certainly see you turning your face toward the heaven.” The wording reveals Allah’s intimate knowledge of His Messenger’s state. The Prophet did not need to formulate a request in words; Allah knew the desire in his heart. The response is tender: “We will surely turn you toward a direction that pleases you.” Divine legislation here comes with recognition of the Messenger’s human concern and with honour for his sincere wish.

### Al-Masjid al-Ḥarām as the Qiblah

The command is direct: “Turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Ḥarām.” The sacred mosque is the central sanctuary in Makkah, built by Ibrāhīm and Ismāʿīl as a house dedicated to the worship of Allah (2:125–127). Facing it connects the final revelation with the original monotheistic mission. It also places the Muslim community’s ritual centre in the land where the Prophet Ibrāhīm called humanity to pilgrimage and where idolatry would eventually be removed.

The qiblah is not a claim that Allah is contained in the Kaʿbah. Allah is exalted above spatial limitation, and He says that He belongs to the east and west (2:142). The Kaʿbah is a created sign and a unifying orientation. Worship is directed to Allah, while bodies face the House because He has commanded them to do so.

### From One Individual to the Whole Community

The first command addresses the Prophet’s face in the singular, then expands: “Wherever you are, turn your faces toward it.” The Messenger ﷺ is the first obeyer, and the community follows him. This pattern appears throughout the Qur’an: the Prophet’s personal obedience becomes a public Sunnah, and the believers share in the command through their relationship to his mission.

Facing the qiblah in prayer is obligatory when a person is able and knows the direction. Those unable to determine it after sincere effort, or those in circumstances where facing it is genuinely impossible, are judged according to their capacity. The Qur’an’s legal commands are not designed to burden people beyond ability (2:286; 64:16). The qiblah unites worship without turning physical limitation into a cause of despair.

### The People of the Scripture and Recognition

Allah says that those given the Scripture know this command is truth from their Lord. Their earlier revelation contained signs pointing toward the final messenger and the restoration of Ibrāhīm’s sanctuary as the centre of the final community. The qiblah therefore was not an arbitrary political gesture, even though it had major social consequences. It was part of a revealed continuity that some knowledgeable people recognized.

Recognition does not guarantee submission. A person may know that a claim is true and still resist it because of status, fear, envy, or attachment to a group. The verse’s ending—Allah is not unaware of what they do—warns those who conceal recognition or turn knowledge into a tool of opposition. Knowledge increases responsibility when it is followed by deliberate refusal.

### Obedience and Communal Unity

The qiblah gives the ummah a visible centre. Muslims in different languages, cultures, and nations stand in rows facing one sanctuary. They do not face a ruler, race, or political capital. This unity does not erase legitimate diversity in law and custom, but it places all believers beneath one Lord and one revealed direction.

The prayer also disciplines the body. The heart may claim devotion, but the body is required to turn, stand, bow, and prostrate. In this way the qiblah teaches that faith is not merely an idea. It is a lived covenant expressed through regular and communal action.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:125–129 on the Sacred House, Ibrāhīm’s supplication, and the prayer for a messenger from among the people of Makkah.
- Qur’an 2:115 and 2:142–143 on Allah’s transcendence of direction and the qiblah as a test.
- Qur’an 3:96–97 on the first House established for humanity and the obligation of pilgrimage.
- Qur’an 2:286 and 64:16 on Allah not burdening a soul beyond its capacity.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the report of al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib about the Prophet’s prayer and the change of qiblah; see also the hadith reports on praying according to one’s best ability when direction is uncertain.
- For the historical setting, the significance of Bayt al-Maqdis, and the legal discussion of facing the Kaʿbah, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī on 2:144.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:145

> Even if you brought those given the Scripture every sign, they would not follow your direction; nor will you follow their direction, nor would they follow one another’s direction. If you were to follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you, then you would certainly be among the wrongdoers.

**Commentary:**

### When Evidence Meets Partisanship

The verse describes people whose opposition is not caused by a lack of evidence. “Even if you brought them every sign,” they would not follow the qiblah of the Prophet ﷺ. This is a warning about the difference between intellectual uncertainty and moral resistance. Evidence can guide a person who wants truth, but it cannot force humility upon someone determined to protect inherited allegiance.

The Qur’an is not saying that every individual from the People of the Book would reject every proof. Other verses praise people among them who recognize the Qur’an and respond with humility (5:82–85; 28:52–55). The pronouns here address the hostile opponents who had made the qiblah a pretext for dispute. The Qur’an’s precision prevents a legitimate criticism of a faction from becoming an unjust judgement on an entire population.

### The Prophet Does Not Trade Revelation for Approval

“Nor will you follow their direction.” The Messenger ﷺ is instructed to remain firm. His mission cannot be adjusted to make the truth acceptable to every audience. The qiblah is one clear example of religious independence: Islam honours the earlier prophets but does not surrender its own revelation to later communal preferences or political pressure.

This firmness is not arrogance. It is loyalty to Allah’s command. The Prophet interacted with Jews and Christians, ate with them in lawful circumstances, discussed scripture, protected covenants, and treated individuals justly. Yet respectful relations did not mean that he blurred the distinction between Islam and other creeds. A believer can show mercy to people while remaining clear about worship and revelation.

### Nor Do They Follow One Another’s Direction

The statement that they do not follow one another’s qiblah exposes the emptiness of the objection. Their dispute was not a unified search for a single sacred direction. Different communities possessed their own inherited practices and used them as markers of identity. The issue, therefore, was not simply geography; it was rivalry, group loyalty, and resistance to the Prophet’s authority.

The verse does not mock every difference in religious law. Allah sent different communities commands suited to stages of revelation, and the Qur’an says that He made a law and a way for each community while testing them in what He gave them (5:48). What is condemned is turning those distinctions into arrogant rejection of known truth.

### Desire after Knowledge

The warning is severe: “If you were to follow their desires after the knowledge that has come to you, then you would certainly be among the wrongdoers.” The Arabic *ahwāʾ* refers to desires that pull a person away from revealed truth. Desire can appear as fear of criticism, longing for social acceptance, attachment to ancestral practice, or the wish to avoid the cost of obedience.

The address to the Prophet ﷺ is also a lesson for the community. If even the Messenger is warned against compromising revealed knowledge, no scholar, leader, or ordinary believer can assume immunity. People may begin with a harmless desire to be liked and end by altering the religion. The remedy is to distinguish courtesy, which is praiseworthy, from theological surrender, which is not.

### Firmness without Injustice

The verse should not be used to justify hostility toward neighbours of other faiths. Its concern is fidelity to the qiblah and resistance to pressure that would make revelation follow human desire. The Qur’an separately commands Muslims to act with kindness and justice toward those who do not fight them because of religion (60:8), and to debate the People of the Book in the best manner (29:46).

The balance is important. A Muslim neither hides the truth to gain approval nor insults people in the name of defending it. Conviction protects the integrity of worship; justice protects the rights of people. Both are required by the same Lord.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:144–150 on the qiblah and the command to remain independent in obedience.
- Qur’an 5:48 on different revealed laws as a test, not a licence for arrogance.
- Qur’an 29:46 and 60:8 on respectful discussion and justice toward peaceful non-Muslims.
- Qur’an 3:149–150 and 6:116 on following others’ preferences and the danger of letting desire replace knowledge.
- The Prophet’s conduct in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, including his dealings with the People of the Book, demonstrates firmness in creed alongside justice in social relations.
- For the interpretation of the opponents’ persistence and the meaning of *ahwāʾ* after knowledge, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:145.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:146

> Those to whom We gave the Scripture recognize him as they recognize their own sons. Yet surely a group of them conceal the truth while they know it.

**Commentary:**

### Recognition as Clear as Family Knowledge

The pronoun “him” refers to the Messenger ﷺ, and the comparison is striking: some people of scripture recognized him as clearly as they recognized their own sons. This does not mean that every detail of the Prophet’s appearance was necessarily known to every individual. It describes confident recognition based on the signs, descriptions, and characteristics preserved in earlier revelation and confirmed by the circumstances of his mission.

A parent normally identifies a child through sustained familiarity, not by a single superficial feature. In the same way, these learned people saw a convergence of signs: the Prophet’s lineage from Ismāʿīl, his character, the content of the Qur’an, his call to tawḥīd, and the fulfilment of prophetic indications. Their recognition was moral and theological as well as descriptive.

### Knowledge and Submission Are Different

The verse distinguishes recognition from acceptance. A person may know that a message is true and still refuse to submit because truth threatens power, reputation, wealth, or inherited leadership. Knowledge is therefore not identical with guidance. Guidance includes the heart’s willingness to honour what it knows and to follow it.

This distinction appears elsewhere in the Qur’an: the people of Pharaoh rejected the signs inwardly while their souls were convinced of them, out of wrongdoing and arrogance (27:14). It also appears in the account of those who recognize the Messenger and weep when they hear the Qur’an (5:82–85). The Qur’an leaves room for different responses among the People of the Book rather than reducing them to one uniform category.

### Concealing the Truth

“A group of them conceal the truth while they know it.” The Arabic *yaktumūna al-ḥaqq* indicates deliberate concealment, not mere uncertainty or honest interpretive disagreement. Concealing revelation can occur by denying a clear sign, suppressing a relevant text, distorting its meaning, or keeping knowledge hidden when people need it in order to recognize guidance.

The sin is intensified by knowledge. Ignorance may require teaching; knowing concealment requires repentance and correction. Those who hold religious authority have a particular duty to speak accurately. They must distinguish what scripture says, what interpretation infers, and what remains uncertain. The Qur’an condemns the manipulation of revelation for worldly gain, not every scholarly difference.

### The Prophet’s Character as Evidence

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ was known among his people as al-Amīn, the trustworthy, before his prophethood. His honesty, mercy, courage, and consistency were not later inventions designed to support a claim. His opponents could not plausibly accuse him of habitual lying while relying on him in their transactions. The Qur’an invites people to consider the message, but it also directs them to reflect on the Messenger’s life and character (10:16; 68:4).

For those familiar with earlier revelation, this character was joined to signs in scripture. The final messenger did not call people to worship himself, tribal supremacy, or worldly wealth. He called them to the worship of the one God and to the moral obligations associated with revelation. Recognition therefore demanded a response to the whole pattern, not merely to a prediction extracted from a text.

### A Warning to the Muslim Community

Although the immediate context concerns some People of the Book, the warning reaches Muslims. A Muslim may know a ruling yet conceal it when it threatens a friendship, business interest, political alliance, or personal image. He may recite evidence for others while exempting himself from its demand. In that case, information has become an argument against him rather than a means of guidance.

The cure is *ikhlāṣ*, sincerity, and *amānah*, trustworthiness. Knowledge is a trust from Allah. It should be conveyed with wisdom, without exaggerating what is uncertain and without hiding what is clear. The scholar and the student alike should ask: do I want the truth to be known, or merely to win an argument?

- Qur’an 2:75, 3:187, and 7:157 on recognizing, conveying, and concealing revealed truth.
- Qur’an 5:82–85 and 28:52–55 on sincere people from earlier scripture who recognize and accept the truth.
- Qur’an 10:16 and 68:4 on the Prophet’s known character and integrity.
- Qur’an 27:14 on recognizing truth inwardly while rejecting it through arrogance.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim preserve reports of the Prophet’s character, dealings, and correspondence with the People of the Book; these illuminate the Qur’an’s appeal to recognition rather than coercion.
- For the comparison to recognizing one’s own sons and the distinction between knowledge and submission, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī on 2:146.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:147

> The truth is from your Lord, so never be among those who doubt.

**Commentary:**

### The Source of Certainty

After describing people who knowingly conceal the truth, Allah gives the Messenger ﷺ the foundation of certainty: “The truth is from your Lord.” The Qur’anic *ḥaqq* is not merely a private feeling or a conclusion based on social approval. It is what is established and true, and its ultimate source is Allah, the Lord who created, knows, and judges all things.

Calling Allah “your Lord”—*min rabbika*—joins truth to care and nurture. The One who revealed the command has been guiding the Messenger from the beginning of his mission. Revelation is not an isolated burden dropped upon humanity without explanation; it is part of Allah’s *rubūbiyyah*, His sustaining and educating lordship. Certainty grows when a believer recognizes that Allah’s commands arise from perfect knowledge and mercy.

### A Command to the Prophet and the Community

“Never be among those who doubt” is addressed first to the Prophet ﷺ, whose certainty was complete. Such Qur’anic addresses to the Messenger often teach the community through him. Believers should not interpret the verse as suggesting prophetic hesitation. Rather, it strengthens the Prophet against the objections of those who conceal what they know and instructs everyone who follows him not to let hostile argument unsettle established revelation.

Doubt can mean a genuine temporary uncertainty that a person resolves through learning, or it can mean an inward instability that repeatedly submits clear truth to every new pressure. Islam does not condemn sincere questions. It condemns the refusal to accept truth after it has become clear, or the habit of treating revelation as permanently subordinate to one’s shifting desires.

### Certainty Is Built on Evidence and Trust

The Qur’an’s certainty is not blind irrationality. It repeatedly calls people to observe creation, examine history, listen to revelation, and reflect upon their own nature (41:53; 52:35–36). *Yaqīn* is confidence grounded in truth, while *shakk* is hesitation that remains because the heart has not accepted the evidence or because desire keeps reopening a settled matter.

A believer may be uncertain about an interpretation, a historical detail, or the application of a general principle to a new situation. This is not the same as doubting Allah’s revelation itself. Intellectual humility allows a person to say, “I do not know,” while spiritual certainty allows him to say, “Allah has spoken truthfully, even where my understanding is incomplete.”

### Do Not Let Opposition Define Reality

The preceding verses mention objections to the qiblah and the concealment of prophetic signs. The instruction therefore has a practical setting: do not let the reaction of opponents become the measure of truth. A message does not become false because powerful people resist it, nor does it become true because a community applauds it. The qiblah is from Allah whether people welcome it or mock it.

This is especially important for religious teachers and young believers. Public controversy can create the illusion that every issue is equally uncertain. A thoughtful Muslim distinguishes between a real challenge requiring study and a loud objection requiring no surrender. He returns to sound evidence, qualified scholarship, and the Qur’an’s overall guidance rather than allowing the most confident voice to govern his faith.

### Certainty and Humility

Certainty does not authorize arrogance. The believer is certain of Allah and His revelation while remaining modest about his own interpretation. The Qur’an praises those who say, “Our Lord, let not our hearts deviate after You have guided us” (3:8). They possess conviction and still ask for protection. They do not confuse certainty in the Lord with infallibility in themselves.

The Prophet ﷺ also taught his Companions to seek beneficial knowledge and to ask Allah for firmness. A sound heart combines *yaqīn* with *tawāḍuʿ*. It is firm before falsehood, gentle toward sincere questioners, and ready to correct its own mistakes when evidence demands correction.

- Qur’an 2:146–147 on concealed knowledge and the source of revealed truth.
- Qur’an 3:7–9 and 41:53 on humility, reflection, and the foundations of certainty.
- Qur’an 10:94 and 52:35–36 on examining evidence rather than following assertion.
- Qur’an 49:6 on verifying reports and avoiding judgement based on unexamined information.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 2518, the hadith “Leave what makes you doubt for what does not make you doubt,” supports carefulness without treating every scholarly uncertainty as denial.
- For *ḥaqq*, *shakk*, and the rhetorical force of the address, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:147.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:148

> Everyone has a direction toward which he turns, so race toward all that is good. Wherever you may be, Allah will bring you all together. Surely Allah is capable of everything.

**Commentary:**

### Directions and Devotion

“Everyone has a direction toward which he turns.” The word *wijhah* can refer to a direction, orientation, or chosen course. In the immediate context, communities have their prescribed qiblahs. The verse acknowledges the visible diversity of religious orientations while directing believers away from sterile dispute. The existence of a direction does not make arguments over geography the essence of faith. What matters is whose command is being followed and what obedience produces.

The verse does not teach that all beliefs are equally true. The Qur’an has already declared the truth of the final revelation and commanded the Muslim qiblah. Rather, it teaches the community not to waste its life in competitive claims while neglecting righteousness. A direction is meaningful when it turns the heart and conduct toward Allah.

### Race toward Goodness

The central imperative is “race toward all that is good”—*fastabiqu al-khayrāt*. The verb *sabaqa* evokes competition and moving ahead. Believers are not told merely to perform good acts when convenient; they are encouraged to hasten, compete with themselves, and use their time before opportunity disappears. The competition is not against another person’s dignity. It is against one’s own laziness, delay, envy, and attachment to sin.

*Khayrāt* includes obligatory worship, voluntary devotion, truthfulness, justice, generosity, family responsibility, care for the vulnerable, seeking knowledge, reconciling people, and restraining harm. The plural suggests many forms of goodness. A person should not imagine that only public religious acts count while patience with a parent, honest work, or a private act of charity is insignificant. Allah knows the diverse ways in which servants seek His pleasure.

### From Debate to Action

The placement of this command after the controversy over the qiblah is instructive. Religious discussion has a place, but argument can become a substitute for obedience. People may spend hours proving that their side is correct while failing to pray, reconcile with a relative, pay a debt, or help someone in need. The Qur’an redirects energy from vanity to virtue.

This is not an invitation to abandon knowledge. Good deeds must be guided by revelation and sound understanding. But knowledge that never becomes action is incomplete. The Prophet ﷺ sought refuge from knowledge that does not benefit, and he taught that the most beloved deeds are those performed consistently, even if small, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

### Allah Will Gather Everyone

“Wherever you may be, Allah will bring you all together.” The promise of gathering points to resurrection and the final assembly. Distance, death, national borders, and differences of direction cannot prevent Allah from bringing humanity before Him. The verse therefore gives urgency to the command to race toward good: every deed will be brought forward, and no opportunity is lost from Allah’s knowledge.

The gathering also relativizes worldly rivalry. People may compete for status, wealth, or recognition, but all will stand together without those artificial distinctions. The true advantage will belong to the one who arrives with faith and righteous action. The Qur’an repeatedly describes the Day when people are assembled and each soul is shown what it sent ahead (18:47–49; 81:10–14).

### Capacity and Mercy

Allah is capable of everything—*inna Allāha ʿalā kulli shayʾin qadīr*. He can gather the scattered, reward hidden deeds, and bring justice where worldly systems failed. His power is also a source of hope for the person who feels too weak to improve. A servant takes the next good step, while relying on the Lord who can open paths no human being could create.

Goodness should be pursued with balance. The believer does not ruin an obligatory duty by chasing a voluntary deed, or neglect dependents in the name of public service. The Sunnah teaches that each right-holder has a right, and that consistent obedience is better than short-lived excess. Racing toward good means acting eagerly within the order and wisdom of revelation.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:143–150 on the qiblah, communal identity, and the priority of obedience over dispute.
- Qur’an 5:48 and 23:61 on competing in good works and striving for righteousness.
- Qur’an 3:133 and 57:21 on hastening toward forgiveness and the Garden.
- Qur’an 18:47–49 and 81:10–14 on the gathering and disclosure of deeds.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that the most beloved deeds are those done regularly even if small, and the hadith on each person’s rights.
- For the meanings of *wijhah* and *fastabiqu al-khayrāt*, and the relation of this āyah to the qiblah discussion, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī on 2:148.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:149

> From wherever you go out, turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Ḥarām. Surely this is the truth from your Lord, and Allah is never unaware of what you do.

**Commentary:**

### Repeated Emphasis of the Qiblah

The command to face al-Masjid al-Ḥarām is repeated after its first announcement in 2:144. Repetition in the Qur’an is not needless duplication. It establishes the ruling firmly, removes possible hesitation, and teaches that the qiblah applies wherever the Messenger ﷺ and his community travel. The believer does not treat the command as a temporary response to one dispute in Madīnah; it is the permanent orientation of the ummah.

The phrase “from wherever you go out” covers the circumstances of travel and movement. Wherever a Muslim finds himself, he seeks the direction of the Sacred House for prayer. This universal command joins a global community through a single act, whether the worshipper is in Makkah, Madīnah, Africa, Asia, Europe, or on the sea.

### Facing the House, Not Containing Allah

As in the preceding verses, the qiblah must be understood correctly. Muslims face the Kaʿbah, but they do not believe Allah is inside it or restricted by it. Allah is the Lord of the Kaʿbah, the heavens, and the earth. The House is a sign of unity and a place of worship, not an object of worship. This distinction separates tawḥīd from the worship of created places or objects.

For a person who can see the Kaʿbah, the obligation concerns facing the structure itself. For one far away, the obligation is to face its direction according to reasonable knowledge and effort. The legal schools discuss the details of exact alignment, but they agree on the underlying principle: Allah asks each servant to obey according to ability and honest effort, not to achieve impossible precision.

### “This Is the Truth from Your Lord”

The qiblah is described as *al-ḥaqq*, the truth. Its truth is not based on the material superiority of one compass point. It is true because Allah revealed and appointed it, because it restores the Abrahamic sanctuary to its role as a centre of pure worship, and because it shapes the final community’s identity under revelation.

A revealed practice can contain wisdom that the servant understands only partially. The proper response is neither to reject reflection nor to make human reasoning the judge over revelation. Muslims reflect on the wisdom of the qiblah—unity, remembrance of Ibrāhīm, distinction from rival identities—while accepting that the binding force of the command rests on Allah’s authority.

### Accountability in Ritual

The verse ends by reminding believers that Allah is not unaware of what they do. Prayer may look like a repeated physical routine, but every detail is seen: the effort to find the direction, the sincerity behind the turning, the carelessness of someone who could obey but refuses, and the hardship of someone genuinely unable. Ritual acts are never empty movements before the One who knows the heart.

This ending also prevents legal form from becoming a substitute for spiritual substance. Facing the correct direction is necessary, but the prayer must also produce humility, remembrance, and restraint from shameful deeds (29:45). A person should not use precision in external orientation to excuse arrogance, fraud, or injustice. Allah sees both the body’s direction and the life’s direction.

### Concessions in Hardship

The Sunnah shows how the command is applied with mercy. During travel, the Prophet ﷺ prayed voluntary prayers on his riding animal in the direction it was travelling, and he combined the practical concessions Allah granted to travellers. In danger, the form of prayer is adapted rather than abandoned. Those who cannot face the qiblah due to illness or compulsion are not held to what lies beyond their power.

These concessions do not weaken the qiblah. They show that the Lawgiver understands human circumstances. The fixed rule and the lawful concession both come from Allah. A believer does not invent hardship where ease is revealed, and does not use ease as an excuse where obedience remains possible.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:144 and 2:150 on the repeated command to face al-Masjid al-Ḥarām.
- Qur’an 2:115 and 22:32 on Allah’s transcendence and the reverence due to His sacred symbols.
- Qur’an 2:286 and 64:16 on ability and responsibility.
- Qur’an 29:45 on prayer’s ethical purpose.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports of the Prophet ﷺ praying voluntary prayers while travelling and the hadith “Pray standing; if you cannot, then sitting,” which establish the principle of capacity and concession.
- For the legal distinctions between the worshipper near the Kaʿbah and the one distant from it, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī, and the chapters on qiblah in the classical fiqh works.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:150

> From wherever you go out, turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Ḥarām. Wherever you are, turn your faces toward it, so that the people may have no argument against you—except those among them who act unjustly. So do not fear them; fear Me. This is so that I may complete My favour upon you and that you may be guided.

**Commentary:**

### The Final Repetition

This is the third connected command to face the Sacred Mosque. The repeated wording gives the qiblah a universal and settled character: the Prophet ﷺ, the believers in Madīnah, travellers, and Muslims in every region share the same orientation. Revelation is not leaving the community uncertain about whether the earlier direction remains an alternative. The change is complete.

Qur’anic repetition also trains the heart. A ruling that concerns daily prayer must be remembered daily. The qiblah is not merely a historical event; every ṣalāh renews the believer’s awareness that he belongs to a community gathered around Allah’s command.

### Removing a Pretext for Opposition

The command was also “so that the people may have no argument against you.” The opponents had used the change of direction to create confusion: some claimed that the Muslims were inconsistent, while others used the earlier qiblah as evidence that the Prophet ﷺ was following their tradition. The final command removed that particular pretext and made the community’s revealed identity clear.

The exception—“except those among them who act unjustly”—recognizes that not every opponent seeks a fair answer. An unjust disputant may continue arguing after the evidence has been clarified, not because the issue remains unclear but because argument serves anger or power. No amount of explanation can satisfy someone who has chosen injustice as a method.

The verse does not mean that Muslims must silence every questioner. It means that revelation should not be altered to satisfy people who are committed to disputing. Honest questions deserve patient answers; bad-faith demands do not acquire authority merely because they are repeated loudly.

### Fear Allah, Not Social Pressure

“So do not fear them; fear Me.” The command addresses the natural fear of isolation, ridicule, and opposition. A community that adopts a distinct qiblah may be mocked or pressured by groups with greater political or cultural power. Allah teaches the believers to place ultimate fear where it belongs: before the One who knows every deed and controls the final outcome.

This does not forbid ordinary caution or prudent planning. The Prophet ﷺ took precautions, made treaties, appointed guards, and considered worldly causes. The forbidden fear is the fear that makes a person disobey Allah in order to gain human approval. *Khashyah* of Allah produces courage, while panic before people makes a person surrender truth and justice.

### Completing the Favour

The qiblah is part of Allah’s favour because it gives the Muslim community a clear ritual centre and connects it with the legacy of Ibrāhīm. The larger favour is the complete guidance of the final revelation. Favour is not simply comfort or worldly success. It is being shown how to worship, how to distinguish truth from falsehood, and how to live under a covenant with Allah.

The expression “that I may complete My favour upon you” also teaches believers to recognize guidance as a gift. They did not invent the qiblah through political calculation. Allah taught them, tested them, and led them through the transition. Gratitude requires more than saying *al-ḥamdulillāh*; it requires using the favour in obedience and extending its mercy to others.

### Guidance Follows Obedience

The verse joins the completed favour to the hope “that you may be guided.” Guidance is both a gift and a path. Allah grants the light, and the servant walks by it. When believers obey a command despite social pressure, their understanding and certainty deepen. When they knowingly compromise, they place veils over their own hearts.

The qiblah therefore illustrates a general pattern. Allah clarifies the direction, the servant turns, and the turn itself becomes a sign of guidance. In every age, the believer must ask not only, “What do people expect from me?” but, “Where has Allah directed me?”

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:144–150 on the qiblah’s establishment and the removal of objections.
- Qur’an 3:175, 5:44, and 9:13 on fearing Allah rather than allowing human pressure to control obedience.
- Qur’an 5:3 and 16:81 on the completion of divine favour and the gratitude it requires.
- Qur’an 3:103 and 8:24 on unity through Allah’s guidance, not merely through shared worldly interest.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports concerning the change of qiblah and the Prophet’s practical balance between reliance upon Allah and taking precautions.
- For the relation of this command to the objections of the Jews, polytheists, and hypocrites, and for the meaning of the unjust exception, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Ibn Kathīr, and al-Qurṭubī on 2:150.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:151

> Just as We have sent among you a messenger from your own, reciting to you Our verses, purifying you, teaching you the Book and wisdom, and teaching you what you did not know.

**Commentary:**

### The Greatest Favour: A Messenger

After mentioning the completion of His favour, Allah reminds the believers of its greatest expression: the sending of Muhammad ﷺ from among them. He was human, spoke their language, shared their society, and could be known directly by those who lived with him. His humanity made him an accessible teacher and model; his revelation made him more than a cultural leader. He conveyed Allah’s words and demonstrated how those words become life.

The expression “from your own” (*min anfusikum*) carries closeness and mercy. The Messenger was not an unknown spirit or a distant philosophical idea. He was recognized for his trustworthiness and lived the hardships of the people he guided. The Qur’an describes the burden he felt for the believers and his compassion toward them (9:128). A messenger among humanity makes the divine command concrete without making the messenger divine.

### Four Interconnected Tasks

The verse lists four tasks: reciting the revelations, purifying the people, teaching the Book and wisdom, and teaching what they did not know. These are not separate projects. Recitation brings the revelation to the ear; teaching opens its meaning; purification removes the obstacles of disbelief and corrupt character; wisdom directs knowledge into sound action.

The order is also significant. The community first hears Allah’s verses, but hearing alone is insufficient. The message must transform the heart, correct the worldview, and produce wise conduct. A person may possess information about Islam while remaining arrogant, dishonest, or cruel. Prophetic education seeks *tazkiyah*, the cleansing and growth of the soul, not merely the accumulation of facts.

### Teaching the Book

“To teach you the Book” means to convey the Qur’an accurately, explain its meanings, identify its commands and prohibitions, and show how its principles govern worship and life. The Messenger ﷺ did not simply deliver a text and leave every question to personal invention. He recited the Qur’an, interpreted it through his words and practice, and applied it to real circumstances.

The community therefore receives the Qur’an through a chain of trustworthy transmission and prophetic explanation. This is why Muslims study Arabic, context, the occasions of revelation, the Sunnah, and the work of qualified scholars. Respect for the text includes respect for the method by which its first teacher explained it.

### Wisdom and the Sunnah

The scholars commonly explain *al-ḥikmah*, wisdom, here as the Sunnah, though wisdom also includes sound judgement and placing each matter in its proper place. The Prophet’s Sunnah specifies general commands, explains ambiguous expressions, demonstrates the forms of worship, and embodies the moral purposes of the Qur’an. The command to pray, for example, is taught in detail by the Prophet’s statement, “Pray as you have seen me pray,” recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī.

This does not make the Sunnah independent of the Qur’an. It is the Messenger’s authoritative explanation of the revelation and a source of guidance preserved through reliable reports. Muslims avoid two errors: treating personal opinion as equal to revelation, and claiming to follow the Qur’an while discarding the prophetic model that explains it.

### Purification before Power

The community was being given a qiblah and an independent identity, but Allah placed purification at the centre of that favour. A community cannot carry revelation well if its members are ruled by tribal pride, greed, envy, racism, or revenge. *Tazkiyah* includes purification from shirk and hypocrisy, as well as growth in patience, sincerity, justice, and mercy.

The Prophet ﷺ purified people through worship, truthful counsel, repentance, and example. He did not create moral transformation by humiliation or coercion. His teaching addressed the intellect and heart together. The Companions learned verses, acted upon them, and then proceeded to further learning—a method reported from several early authorities and consistent with the Qur’an’s call to reflect and practise.

### Learning What Was Unknown

The final phrase emphasizes humanity’s original ignorance. Revelation taught people realities they could not discover by speculation alone: the unseen, resurrection, the details of worship, the histories of prophets, and the moral purpose of life. It also corrected knowledge they thought they possessed, freeing them from superstition and inherited falsehood.

This is a source of humility for every student. Whatever one knows is a gift from Allah, and what one does not know is far greater. The proper response is gratitude, continued learning, and the willingness to say, “Allah knows best.” Knowledge should make a person more teachable, not more contemptuous.

- Qur’an 9:128 and 62:2 on the Messenger’s compassion, recitation, purification, and teaching.
- Qur’an 16:44 and 59:7 on the Prophet’s role in explaining revelation and the authority of his Sunnah.
- Qur’an 91:9–10 on the success of purification and the loss of corruption.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, “Pray as you have seen me pray,” and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports describing the Prophet’s teaching, worship, and character.
- For the meanings of *tazkiyah*, *taʿlīm al-kitāb*, and *al-ḥikmah*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:151.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:152

> So remember Me; I will remember you. Be grateful to Me and do not be ungrateful to Me.

**Commentary:**

### The Reciprocal Gift of Remembrance

After describing the Messenger’s work of reciting, teaching, and purifying, Allah gives the believers a concise command: “Remember Me; I will remember you.” *Dhikr* includes mentioning Allah with the tongue, recalling Him with the heart, obeying Him with the limbs, and keeping His presence in view when making decisions. It is not limited to a particular formula, although the prescribed forms of remembrance have special virtue.

Allah’s remembrance of a servant is not like a human being’s recollection after forgetfulness. Allah never forgets. His remembering means that He honours the servant, shows mercy, grants guidance, mentions him among the righteous, protects him, and responds to his supplication in the manner He knows best. The small act of a servant’s remembrance opens onto a divine favour infinitely greater than the act itself.

### Dhikr and the Whole of Life

The Qur’an praises those who remember Allah standing, sitting, and lying on their sides while reflecting on creation (3:191). This does not mean that a believer must withdraw from work or family responsibilities. Rather, remembrance accompanies every state. A trader remembers Allah by refusing fraud; a parent remembers Him by showing mercy; a judge remembers Him by refusing bribery; a student remembers Him by seeking knowledge with sincerity.

Formal prayer is the greatest regular institution of dhikr, and the Qur’an says that prayer is established to remember Allah (20:14). Reciting Qur’an, saying *subḥān Allāh*, *al-ḥamdulillāh*, *Allāhu akbar*, making istighfār, and sending blessings upon the Prophet ﷺ are among the well-known forms. Yet words detached from conduct can become habit without transformation. True remembrance restrains the heart from what Allah dislikes.

### Gratitude Is More Than a Statement

“Be grateful to Me” translates *washkurū lī*. *Shukr* is to recognize a blessing, praise the One who gave it, and use it in the way He approves. The tongue says *al-ḥamdulillāh*, the heart attributes the favour to Allah, and the limbs use the favour responsibly. Wealth is thanked through lawful earning and generosity; knowledge through teaching and humility; health through worship and service.

Gratitude does not require pretending that pain is pleasant. A believer can grieve, seek treatment, complain of hardship to Allah, and still recognize His gifts. The prophets made supplication in distress, and Ayyūb’s patience did not mean that he denied his suffering. Shukr is an orientation of trust: the servant sees that even in trial Allah has not ceased to be wise and merciful.

### Do Not Be Ungrateful

The prohibition of ingratitude—*wa-lā takfurūn*—includes denying Allah’s blessings, attributing them entirely to oneself, using them for sin, or refusing to acknowledge the Giver. Kufr in this context can mean ingratitude without necessarily referring to the full theological meaning of disbelief, though persistent ingratitude can lead the heart toward denial.

A society may possess technology, wealth, and education yet be ungrateful if it uses power to oppress the weak or destroy creation. An individual may recite praise while treating parents, employees, spouses, or neighbours unjustly. Gratitude is tested by behaviour. The Qur’an links it with increase: “If you are grateful, I will surely increase you” (14:7). The increase may be material, but it also includes blessing, contentment, wisdom, and nearness to Allah.

### Remembrance in Times of Stress

Dhikr is especially valuable when the mind is crowded by fear and distraction. Allah says that hearts find rest in His remembrance (13:28). This does not promise that every anxiety disappears immediately. It means that remembrance gives suffering a truthful framework: the servant is not abandoned, life is not meaningless, and the Lord who hears the whispered prayer controls the final result.

The Prophet ﷺ frequently remembered Allah in all circumstances. He taught concise supplications for waking, sleeping, travel, eating, fear, and difficulty. These practices turn ordinary transitions into opportunities for worship and protect the heart from living as though Allah were absent.

### Allah’s Remembrance and Human Honour

To be remembered by Allah is the greatest honour. Human beings often seek recognition from crowds, institutions, and online audiences, but such recognition is unstable and cannot save a person before Allah. The verse teaches a better ambition: remember Allah privately, and Allah will honour the servant whether people notice him or not.

This also corrects the idea that worship is a bargain between equals. The servant’s dhikr does not benefit Allah; Allah is free of all need. The benefit returns to the servant through guidance, forgiveness, tranquillity, and acceptance. Divine generosity makes the covenant astonishingly merciful.

- Qur’an 14:7 and 31:12 on gratitude, increase, and wise use of blessings.
- Qur’an 29:45 on prayer as remembrance that restrains indecency and wrongdoing.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 7405 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2675, the sacred hadith, “I am as My servant thinks of Me,” and Allah’s mention of the servant who remembers Him.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports that the Prophet ﷺ remembered Allah in all circumstances, and the hadith on the two phrases light on the tongue and heavy on the scale.
- For *dhikr*, *shukr*, and the meaning of Allah’s “remembering” His servants, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:152.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:153

> O believers, seek help through patience and prayer. Surely Allah is with the patient.

**Commentary:**

### Help Is Sought through Means of Worship

The believers are told to seek help—*istaʿīnū*—through *ṣabr* and *ṣalāh*. Islam does not teach passive resignation. A servant takes lawful practical means, asks people for appropriate help, and turns to Allah as the true source of ability and relief. Patience and prayer are not escapes from responsibility; they give the believer the inner strength and clear direction needed to face responsibility well.

The imperative is addressed to believers collectively. Hardship can isolate people, but worship reconnects them to Allah and to the community. A person in grief may need private prayer, sincere supplication, and the practical support of family and neighbours. The verse therefore supports both spiritual resilience and a culture in which believers help one another remain patient.

### The Many Dimensions of Patience

*Ṣabr* is often translated as patience, but it includes steadfastness, self-restraint, and perseverance. Scholars commonly describe three connected forms: patience in obeying Allah, patience in avoiding sin, and patience while enduring painful decree. The person who continues to pray despite exhaustion, refuses forbidden wealth despite need, and bears loss without accusing Allah of injustice is practising different aspects of ṣabr.

Patience does not mean that a person never feels sadness, anger, or fear. Yaʿqūb wept from grief, Mūsā experienced fear, and the Prophet ﷺ shed tears at the death of his son Ibrāhīm. What patience controls is the response: the tongue does not object to Allah’s wisdom, and the limbs do not pursue prohibited relief. A patient person may complain to Allah, seek treatment, protest injustice lawfully, and ask others for support.

### Prayer as Return and Renewal

Prayer is named alongside patience because it repeatedly returns the believer to the Lord. Five times each day, worldly pressure is interrupted by standing before Allah, reciting His words, bowing, and prostrating. In sujūd the servant is physically lowered but spiritually brought near. The prayer does not erase every problem, but it prevents the problem from becoming larger than Allah in the heart.

The Prophet ﷺ would turn to prayer when a matter distressed him. This was not a magical formula detached from action. Prayer clarified his purpose, strengthened reliance, and placed the matter under Allah’s judgement. The believer who prays in difficulty says through his body and words that he is a servant, not a self-sufficient master of outcomes.

### “Allah Is with the Patient”

Allah’s being “with” the patient—*maʿiyyah*—is a special nearness of support, care, guidance, and victory, not physical indwelling. Allah’s general knowledge and power encompass all creation, while this special companionship is promised to those who practise ṣabr and taqwā. It may appear as tranquillity, wise judgement, endurance, protection from sinful reaction, or reward reserved for the Hereafter.

The promise does not mean that patient people will experience no loss. They may still face poverty, illness, persecution, bereavement, or delay. It means they do not face these realities alone and that their endurance is never wasted. The Qur’an says that the patient will receive their reward without measure (39:10).

### Patience and Justice

Patience must not be distorted into an instruction to tolerate oppression without seeking justice. The Qur’an permits the oppressed to speak about wrongdoing and praises those who defend themselves within lawful limits (4:148; 42:39–43). The patient person can resist abuse, report a crime, leave danger, and defend the vulnerable. What ṣabr forbids is revenge that exceeds justice, despair that denies Allah, or abandonment of one’s obligations.

Likewise, prayer must not be used as a substitute for practical care. Someone facing hunger should be fed; someone ill should receive treatment; someone in danger should be protected. Seeking help through prayer includes asking Allah to make us instruments through which relief reaches His servants.

### A Discipline for Ordinary Struggles

The verse applies not only to dramatic crises. Sustained study, raising children, recovering from addiction, maintaining a marriage, earning lawfully, and resisting daily temptation all require ṣabr and ṣalāh. The small decision repeated for Allah’s sake can be more demanding than a single public act of courage.

A believer can build this discipline by guarding the obligatory prayers, making short supplications before difficult tasks, choosing good companions, and remembering the final meeting with Allah. Patience grows through practice, just as physical strength grows through repeated effort.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:154

> Do not say about those killed in Allah’s cause, “They are dead.” Rather, they are alive, but you do not perceive it.

**Commentary:**

### Correcting the Language of Loss

This verse follows the command to seek help through patience and prayer by addressing one of the deepest forms of loss: the death of those who are killed while striving in Allah’s cause. Allah forbids the believers from describing them as simply dead. The prohibition does not deny the physical reality that their bodies no longer live in this world. It corrects the conclusion that death has ended their existence or erased the honour and reward of their sacrifice.

The Qur’an uses careful language: they are alive, but the living do not perceive the nature of that life. This is life in the *barzakh*, the intermediate realm between worldly death and resurrection, not a return to ordinary earthly life. Muslims affirm what revelation states without inventing details that Allah has not disclosed.

### Who Are the Martyrs?

The phrase “killed in Allah’s cause”—*yuqtalu fī sabīl Allāh*—concerns those who die while sincerely striving under a legitimate cause sanctioned by Allah, not everyone killed in a conflict that bears a religious slogan. Intention, justice, lawful conduct, and the nature of the cause matter. Islam does not turn violence, aggression, terrorism, or the killing of innocents into martyrdom by attaching a pious phrase to it.

The Prophet ﷺ explained that martyrdom has categories beyond battlefield death, including some who die from particular illnesses or calamities, as recorded in authentic hadith. These reports expand hope in Allah’s mercy; they do not permit individuals to declare themselves martyrs or to treat death as a goal. Life is a trust, and the Prophet ﷺ prohibited wishing for death because of hardship.

### The Nature of Their Life

The next passage, 3:169–171, gives further detail: those killed in Allah’s way are alive with their Lord, receiving provision and rejoicing in Allah’s bounty. An authentic hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim describes the souls of martyrs as being in green birds that move freely in Paradise and return to lamps beneath the Throne. These are matters of the unseen. We believe them because the Qur’an and authentic Sunnah report them, not because we can observe or measure them.

The life of the martyr is therefore not annihilation, and death is not the final evaluation of his worth. The body may be buried, but Allah preserves the soul and its reward. This belief gives the community courage against tyranny, while the strict moral limits of Islamic law prevent courage from becoming recklessness or cruelty.

### “Do Not Say” Does Not Forbid Grief

The verse does not command the family of the deceased to feel no sorrow. The Prophet ﷺ wept when his son Ibrāhīm died and said that the eyes shed tears and the heart grieves, but the tongue speaks only what pleases Allah, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. Grief is compatible with faith. What is prohibited is speech that denies Allah’s promise, insults the divine decree, or treats the martyr’s sacrifice as pointless and forgotten.

The Muslim response to death combines tears with hope. The deceased is honoured, the family is supported, debts and rights are addressed, and supplication is made. The community does not romanticize bereavement or use the dead to manipulate the living. It remembers that the martyr is with Allah while the living remain responsible for justice and worship.

### A Protection against Despair

At a spiritual level, the verse expands the believer’s understanding of reality. Material sight is not the measure of all existence. The dead person is beyond our perception, not beyond Allah’s care. This protects people from despair when righteous servants are killed and from the illusion that oppressors have achieved the final victory.

The verse also dignifies sacrifice without glorifying war for its own sake. A person who gives life in a just cause for Allah’s sake has not lost everything; but a person who kills himself, kills innocents, or acts from tribal rage cannot claim the verse. The balance of revelation is essential: honour the sincere martyr, preserve innocent life, and leave final judgement to Allah.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:153–157 and 3:169–171 on patience, martyrdom, and life with Allah.
- Qur’an 4:74–76 on striving in Allah’s cause and the distinction between justice and false causes.
- Qur’an 17:33 and 5:32 on the sanctity of life and the prohibition of unlawful killing.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith describing the souls of martyrs in green birds; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, the Prophet’s grief at the death of Ibrāhīm.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports of martyrs who die in causes other than battle, interpreted with the conditions explained by the scholars.
- For *barzakh*, the limits of human perception, and the distinction between physical death and the life of the martyr, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:154.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:155

> We will certainly test you with something of fear and hunger, and a loss of wealth, lives, and fruits. Give good news to the patient.

**Commentary:**

### Trials Are Part of the Human Journey

After discussing prayer, patience, and those killed in Allah’s cause, Allah announces that believers will be tested. The verb *lanabluwannakum* is emphatic: testing is not an exceptional possibility reserved for a few people. It is part of life in this world. Faith is not a guarantee that a person will be protected from fear, hunger, loss, illness, or grief. It is a way of meeting those realities with guidance.

The word *balāʾ* refers to an examination that reveals what is within and develops the servant through experience. Allah already knows the hidden heart; the test does not give Him new information. It brings intention into action, establishes evidence for or against a person, and teaches the servant what he cannot learn through theory alone.

### “Something” of Fear and Hunger

The verse says “something of fear and hunger”—*bi-shayʾin min al-khawf wa-l-jūʿ*. The wording contains both warning and mercy. Allah may test with a portion, not necessarily the totality, of these hardships. The indefinite “something” reminds believers that trials vary in intensity and duration. One person faces insecurity, another food shortage, another the fear of illness or violence. No two tests are identical.

Fear can concern safety, livelihood, family, reputation, or an uncertain future. Hunger may be literal, as in famine or poverty, or part of a disciplined fast. In every case, the believer is permitted to take means: seek refuge, obtain food, treat illness, defend the vulnerable, and ask trustworthy people for help. Reliance on Allah does not mean refusing lawful means.

### Losses That Touch Every Life

Allah names loss of wealth, lives, and fruits. Wealth includes income, property, employment, and savings. Lives include the death or serious illness of loved ones, and the danger faced by a community. Fruits refers literally to crops and produce, and by extension to the results of labour: projects that fail, businesses that collapse, hopes that do not mature, and efforts whose benefits are taken away.

The list is comprehensive because human beings often measure security through possessions, relationships, and visible results. When these are lost, a person may feel that his entire identity has disappeared. Revelation teaches that these things are gifts and trusts, not permanent ownership. Their loss is painful, but it does not prove that Allah has abandoned the servant or that life has become meaningless.

### The Glad Tiding Is for the Patient

“Give good news to the patient.” The good news is not that every worldly loss will quickly be restored. Sometimes Allah replaces what was lost in this life; sometimes He gives a different form of relief; and sometimes the full recompense is reserved for the Hereafter. The promise is that patient endurance is seen, valued, and rewarded without waste.

Patience does not mean passivity or silence in the face of harm. A person can seek justice, move away from danger, mourn openly, consult a doctor, appeal for help, and work to rebuild. What makes these actions compatible with *ṣabr* is that the person does not let pain push him into disbelief, unlawful revenge, despair, or abandonment of obligation.

### Testing Is Not Always Punishment

A trial may be a consequence of wrongdoing, a means of purification, an elevation of rank, a warning, or a test of the community’s responsibility. It is unsafe to declare that every victim of hardship is being punished or that every prosperous person is favoured. The Qur’an criticizes precisely this simplistic equation (89:15–17). Allah tests through both deprivation and abundance.

The believer therefore asks two questions: “What must I do now?” and “What is Allah teaching me?” He repents if he has sinned, takes lawful means to repair the harm, and seeks the reward of patience. He does not claim to know the unseen wisdom of every event, but he trusts that Allah’s wisdom encompasses it.

### Community Responsibility

The plural address—“We will test you”—also places responsibility on the community. When people face hunger, loss, or fear, other believers must not merely quote patience at them. They should provide food, safety, financial support, medical care, and companionship. The Prophet ﷺ compared believers to one body and taught that whoever relieves a believer’s hardship will have a hardship relieved for him on the Day of Resurrection, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

A society that allows preventable hunger while praising patience has misunderstood the verse. Individual ṣabr and communal justice belong together.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:153–157 on patience, testing, and the correct response to loss.
- Qur’an 3:186, 29:2–3, and 67:2 on testing as a feature of faith and life.
- Qur’an 89:15–17 on wealth and deprivation not being simple signs of honour or humiliation.
- Qur’an 64:15 and 8:28 on wealth and family as tests.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on relieving a believer’s hardship and the hadith describing the believer’s good in both ease and adversity.
- For the categories of *balāʾ*, the relationship between testing and patience, and the communal duties created by hardship, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:155.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:156

> Those who, when disaster strikes them, say, “Surely we belong to Allah, and surely to Him we will return.”

**Commentary:**

### The Words of Return

The patient are identified by their response when calamity strikes: *innā lillāhi wa innā ilayhi rājiʿūn*. These words are known as *istirjāʿ*, the declaration of belonging and return. They do not deny the pain of the event. They place the pain within the truth that Allah owns us, sustains us, and will gather us back to Him.

“Inn ā lillāh”—we belong to Allah—means that our lives, bodies, families, wealth, and opportunities are trusts. We possess things temporarily and are responsible for how we use them, but absolute ownership belongs to Allah. “To Him we will return” means that death is not disappearance. Every servant returns for judgement, and every loss in this world is placed within the horizon of resurrection.

### Ownership Does Not Make Grief Unreal

Some people misunderstand surrender to Allah as a demand to feel no sorrow. The Qur’an does not teach that. Yaʿqūb said, “I only complain of my suffering and grief to Allah” (12:86). The Prophet ﷺ wept over the death of his son and acknowledged that the heart grieves. What *istirjāʿ* does is prevent grief from becoming rebellion, despair, or words that deny Allah’s wisdom.

A person may say these words with tears, trembling, and a heart that needs time to heal. Patience can be present even when emotions are intense. The measure is not whether the servant feels pain, but whether he continues to recognize Allah’s lordship and avoids prohibited responses. Seeking counselling, medical care, legal protection, or support from loved ones is compatible with this declaration.

### A Theology of Trust

The first half of the phrase gives the believer a secure identity. Before being an employee, parent, citizen, spouse, or owner, a person is a servant of Allah. When a role is lost, the core relationship remains. When a possession is taken, the Giver remains. When a beloved person dies, the servant trusts that both lives were always under Allah’s care.

The second half gives the event a destination. The believer may not know when relief will come or why the loss occurred, but he knows that the story does not end at the grave or in the hospital. Return to Allah means that injustice will be addressed, sacrifices will be rewarded, and no sincere tear or act of restraint will be forgotten.

### Saying It at the Moment of Calamity

The Sunnah encourages the declaration when calamity occurs and teaches a supplication after it: “O Allah, reward me in my calamity and replace it for me with what is better.” Umm Salamah رضي الله عنها reported that when her husband Abū Salamah died, the Prophet ﷺ taught this prayer; Allah later gave her better than she expected by marrying her to the Messenger ﷺ, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

The hadith does not promise that every replacement will resemble what was lost. “Better” may be forgiveness, a stronger heart, a protected faith, benefit to others, or reward in the Hereafter. The servant asks with hope while leaving the form and timing to Allah.

### A Shared Formula with Personal Meaning

The plural “we belong” reminds the speaker that calamity is also a human experience. The community should surround mourners with practical assistance and compassionate presence. Saying the formula together can comfort people, but it must not become a mechanical phrase used to dismiss their needs. The grieving person may need someone to listen, help with children, arrange food, settle urgent matters, or simply sit quietly.

At the same time, the words are deeply personal. No one can make another person’s return to Allah for him. Each servant must cultivate this recognition before crisis arrives, through prayer, gratitude, and regular remembrance, so that the heart has a truthful language when it is shaken.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:155–157 on calamity, *istirjāʿ*, blessing, mercy, and guidance.
- Qur’an 12:86 and 89:27–30 on grieving before Allah and returning to Him.
- Qur’an 3:185, 21:35, and 29:57 on death and the certainty of return.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 918, the report of Umm Salamah and the supplication for reward and replacement in calamity.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, the report of the Prophet’s words at the death of Ibrāhīm: the eyes shed tears and the heart grieves, but only what pleases Allah is said.
- For the meanings of *istirjāʿ*, ownership, and return, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:156.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:157

> They are the ones upon whom are blessings from their Lord and mercy, and it is they who are rightly guided.

**Commentary:**

### Three Divine Gifts

The patient people who respond to calamity with *istirjāʿ* receive three gifts: blessings, mercy, and guidance. The first, *ṣalawāt*, includes Allah’s praise of them, honour, forgiveness, and the elevation of their rank. It is a remarkable expression: the servants remember Allah in their trial, and Allah mentions them with favour in the highest assembly and before His creation in the manner befitting His majesty.

The second gift is *raḥmah*, mercy. Mercy may appear as strength to endure, comfort in grief, protection from sinful reaction, supportive people, eventual relief, or an immense reward in the Hereafter. The verse does not reduce mercy to the immediate removal of hardship. Sometimes the mercy is that the trial turns the servant back to Allah and saves him from a greater spiritual loss.

The third gift is guidance—*hudā*. Calamity can make a person disoriented, but the patient response gives him a map. He knows where he came from, to whom he belongs, and where he is going. Guidance does not answer every question about an event; it gives the correct relationship to the event.

### Patience Is Active Faith

The verse demonstrates that *ṣabr* is not empty endurance. It is faith acting under pressure. The patient person recognizes Allah’s ownership, controls his speech and conduct, seeks lawful help, and continues to fulfil obligations as much as possible. His patience is therefore rewarded with more than survival; it becomes a means of nearness to Allah.

This patience can be practised in small difficulties before a major disaster arrives. Delaying an angry response, accepting a correction, remaining honest when a shortcut is available, and continuing a good deed after enthusiasm fades all train the heart. When calamity comes, the established habit of turning to Allah becomes a source of stability.

### The Blessing of a Correct Response

Two people may experience similar losses and emerge differently. One becomes bitter, harms others, and rejects every reminder of Allah. Another grieves deeply but turns toward Allah, accepts support, and preserves justice. The event itself is not chosen by either person, but their response becomes a field of worship. This does not mean that suffering is easy or that those who struggle with grief are spiritually worthless. It means Allah sees the direction in which the heart is trying to move.

The verse therefore offers hope to a person who feels weak. Receiving divine blessing is not reserved for those who never cry. It is for those who return, again and again, to the truth even when their emotions fluctuate. A single sincere declaration in the moment of shock can be the beginning of a long journey of healing.

### Guidance Includes Practical Wisdom

Being guided through calamity includes knowing when to ask for help, how to protect dependents, and how to distinguish patience from helplessness. The Prophet ﷺ taught people to tie their camel and rely on Allah, and Islamic law preserves rights concerning inheritance, debts, mourning, and care for the vulnerable. Spiritual language must not be used to excuse negligence.

For communities, guidance means responding to the bereaved with mercy rather than judgement. A mourner should not be pressured to recover according to a timetable or accused of weak faith because tears continue. The Sunnah gives condolences, food, companionship, and prayer. It also forbids excessive lamentation that injures the dignity of the deceased or expresses rebellion against Allah.

### A Promise Beyond the Immediate Moment

The passive construction “upon them are blessings” indicates that the gifts descend upon the patient from their Lord. They are not earned through human strength alone. The servant takes the step of patience, but Allah creates the ability, records the reward, and sends mercy. This preserves humility: the patient person thanks Allah for helping him to be patient.

The final statement, “they are the rightly guided,” gives the highest evaluation. Guidance is not measured by a life free of trials. It is measured by whether a person responds to trials in a way that leads him nearer to Allah and preserves his moral obligations.

- Qur’an 9:99, 64:11, and 42:36–43 on mercy, contentment, and the spiritual response to adversity.
- Qur’an 3:146–148 on perseverance, repentance, and divine love in times of hardship.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith that a believer’s affair is good in prosperity and adversity, and the hadith on relieving grief and hardship.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports concerning patience, mourning, and the reward of restraining the tongue and conduct.
- For *ṣalawāt*, *raḥmah*, and *hudā* as the three consequences of *istirjāʿ*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:157.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:158

> Indeed, al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah are among the symbols of Allah. So whoever performs Hajj to the House or ʿUmrah, there is no blame upon him for walking between them. And whoever volunteers good, then surely Allah is Appreciative and Knowing.

**Commentary:**

### Sacred Symbols and Sacred Memory

Al-Ṣafā and al-Marwah are two hills near the Kaʿbah. Allah calls them *shaʿāʾir Allāh*, the symbols or distinctive rites of Allah. A symbol is a visible sign that directs the heart to a greater meaning. The two hills recall the devotion and urgency of Hājar when she searched for water for her infant son Ismāʿīl. Her trust and effort became part of the rites of Hajj and ʿUmrah for the worldwide Muslim community.

The rite does not worship the hills or make Hājar divine. It honours a servant of Allah and follows the ritual that Allah selected. The Muslim walks between Ṣafā and Marwah because Allah has made that movement an act of worship, just as Muslims face the Kaʿbah because Allah appointed it as the qiblah.

### “There Is No Blame”

The wording “there is no blame upon him” might sound as though walking between the hills were merely optional. In context, some early Muslims hesitated because the area had been associated with pre-Islamic idols and practices. The verse removes that anxiety and clarifies that the rite is a legitimate part of Hajj and ʿUmrah. The Sunnah and the practice of the Companions establish that *saʿy* is an essential rite according to the majority of jurists, with detailed differences in legal classification among the schools.

The Qur’an sometimes uses “no blame” to remove a false sense of sin, not to declare an act unimportant. A person should therefore study the rulings of Hajj and ʿUmrah carefully rather than infer legal status from one phrase in isolation. Worship is learned from the Qur’an, the authentic Sunnah, and qualified scholarship.

### Remembering Hājar’s Effort

Hājar’s running was an act of desperation joined to trust. She did not sit passively waiting for a miracle. She searched the nearby hills, climbed them, looked for travellers, and returned to the valley. Her movement was repeated seven times, and Allah preserved it as a rite. This shows that reliance on Allah, *tawakkul*, includes taking available means.

The story also gives women a central place in the memory of revelation. A rite performed by every pilgrim—men and women, rulers and the poor—recreates the urgent movement of a mother seeking life for her child. The Kaʿbah’s sacred history is not told only through kings or warriors. It includes a woman’s trust, labour, and perseverance.

### Hajj, ʿUmrah, and Intention

Hajj is the pilgrimage performed in its appointed season with its known rites, while ʿumrah may be performed at other times and has its own rites. Both require sincerity and obedience. The Prophet ﷺ said that deeds are judged by intentions, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. A person should undertake pilgrimage for Allah, not for social status, photographs, commercial prestige, or the praise of people.

The ritual is physically demanding and exposes character. Crowds, heat, delay, fatigue, and differences among pilgrims test patience. The Qur’an commands that there be no obscene speech, sinful conduct, or quarrelling during Hajj (2:197). Saʿy should therefore produce humility and remembrance, not pride in having completed a public religious achievement.

### Voluntary Good and Allah’s Appreciation

“Whoever volunteers good”—*man taṭawwaʿa khayran*—can refer to performing additional good deeds beyond the required minimum, or to willingly perform the rite with a heart that accepts it. Allah is *Shākir*, Appreciative: He magnifies the reward of a small sincere act. He is also *ʿAlīm*, Knowing: He knows the intention, effort, physical pain, and private devotion hidden from people.

This principle extends beyond pilgrimage. A person who offers extra prayer, helps a stranger, teaches a child, removes harm from a path, or spends time comforting the distressed should not think the act is too small for Allah. The appreciative Lord knows every step between the hills and every step taken for His servants elsewhere.

### Symbols Require Reverence and Understanding

Allah commands respect for His symbols (22:32), but reverence is not superstition. The pilgrim follows the rite precisely while understanding that its benefit comes from Allah’s command. He does not attribute independent power to stones, hills, garments, or locations. Sacred symbols train the heart to remember Allah, and their outward performance should lead to inward taqwā.

- Qur’an 22:32 on venerating Allah’s symbols through the heart’s taqwā.
- Qur’an 2:197 on avoiding obscenity, sin, and quarrelling during pilgrimage.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith of ʿĀʾishah explaining that Allah prescribed saʿy between Ṣafā and Marwah, and the hadith “Deeds are only by intentions.”
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī and Sunan al-Nasāʾī, reports on the rites of Hajj and ʿUmrah, read alongside the established practice of the Prophet ﷺ.
- For the historical account of Hājar, the meaning of *shaʿāʾir*, and the legal status of saʿy, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:158.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:159

> Surely those who conceal the clear proofs and guidance We sent down, after We made them clear to people in the Scripture, are cursed by Allah and cursed by those who curse.

**Commentary:**

### The Crime of Concealing Guidance

This āyah returns to the theme of knowledge and concealment. Allah sent *bayyināt*, clear proofs, and *hudā*, guidance that leads people to truth and right action. These were not obscure secrets intended for a private elite. They were made clear in scripture so that people could recognize Allah’s command. To conceal such guidance after it has been clarified is a serious betrayal of a trust.

Concealment can take several forms. A person may suppress a relevant verse, hide a prophetic teaching, misrepresent a clear ruling, refuse to teach what people need, or use technical ambiguity to prevent others from reaching the truth. It can also occur through selective quotation: presenting only the evidence that supports one’s interest while hiding the evidence that imposes a duty or restrains a desire.

### Knowledge Is a Trust

Religious knowledge is an *amānah*. Whoever learns something beneficial becomes responsible for using it honestly. This does not mean that every person must answer every question without expertise. A responsible teacher may say, “I do not know,” refer a matter to a qualified scholar, or explain that a question requires further investigation. Concealment is not the same as caution; the sin lies in knowingly withholding clear guidance when one has a duty and ability to make it known.

The Qur’an took a covenant from those given the Scripture that they would make it clear to people and not conceal it (3:187). The warning reaches Muslim scholars, institutions, parents, and anyone who speaks publicly about Islam. Authority, credentials, and a large audience increase the seriousness of misrepresentation. The more people rely on a person, the more carefully he must distinguish revelation from opinion.

### Clear Proofs and Responsible Interpretation

The phrase “after We made them clear” does not eliminate the need for scholarship. The Qur’an contains verses that are decisive and others that require careful interpretation. A jurist may differ from another jurist while both honestly seek the evidence. Such disagreement is not automatically concealment. The danger is claiming certainty where there is uncertainty, or hiding a clear proof because it is inconvenient.

Responsible interpretation considers Arabic language, context, the Sunnah, the practice of the Companions, the objectives of the Law, and the relationship between general and specific texts. It also acknowledges the limits of one’s method. A person who presents his preference as Allah’s unambiguous command may mislead even without consciously intending to lie; this is why humility and verification are essential.

### The Meaning of the Curse

“Cursed by Allah” means exclusion from His mercy and nearness because of the gravity of the betrayal. “Cursed by those who curse” has been explained by the exegetes as the condemnation of angels, prophets, believers, and all who are harmed by the concealment. When truth is hidden, injustice spreads, people are deprived of guidance, and society’s trust in knowledge is damaged. The wrong is therefore both vertical—against Allah—and social—against those who needed the truth.

The verse should not be turned into a tool for casually cursing named individuals. Final judgement belongs to Allah, and the conditions of knowledge, intention, and responsibility are known perfectly only to Him. The text warns about a described act; applying it to a person requires justice, evidence, and restraint.

### The Cost of Silence

Silence can be sinful when a person is the only reliable source of vital information and conceals it to protect power or profit. A scholar who hides a truth for a patron, a leader who suppresses evidence of wrongdoing, or a witness who refuses to disclose what is necessary to protect the innocent may all fall under the moral danger of the verse. The Qur’an commands believers to stand firmly for justice even when testimony is against themselves or relatives (4:135).

At other times, silence is wise. A person should not broadcast private faults, spread unverified claims, or speak beyond his knowledge. The Prophet ﷺ taught that whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should speak good or remain silent, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The duty is to make truth clear with wisdom, not to turn every conversation into exposure or dispute.

### Concealment and Public Harm

The revelation is meant to establish worship and justice. Hiding its guidance can produce exploitation in the name of religion, discrimination, abuse of family members, or neglect of the poor. It can also create the opposite reaction: people who encounter dishonest religious authority may reject the truth because it has been presented to them corruptly.

Therefore, teaching must include both doctrine and moral application. It is not enough to explain rituals while ignoring honesty, the rights of neighbours, the prohibition of oppression, and the sanctity of life. The clearest proof of knowledge is faithful practice and transparent service.

- Qur’an 3:187 and 5:15–16 on the covenant to make scripture clear and Allah’s guidance bringing people from darkness to light.
- Qur’an 4:135 and 5:8 on truthful testimony and justice even against one’s own interests.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:160

> Except those who repent, correct themselves, and make the truth clear. Those—I will accept their repentance, and I am the One who accepts repentance, the Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### The Door of Repentance Remains Open

The previous āyah described the severe consequence of concealing clear guidance, but this verse immediately opens the way back. Allah does not present the warning as a sentence from which no one can return. Even a person who hid truth can repent, repair the corruption, and restore clarity. This is the generosity of *tawbah*: the sinner is not trapped by his past when he turns sincerely to Allah.

The verse’s structure identifies three dimensions of repentance: *tābū*, they repent; *aṣlaḥū*, they correct themselves or make amends; and *bayyanū*, they make the truth clear. An inward statement of regret is essential, but in this case it is not enough. The harm was caused by concealment, so repentance must include public or practical clarification proportionate to the concealment.

### Repentance Is Return, Not a Slogan

The Arabic root *t-w-b* conveys returning. The servant turns away from the sin and returns to Allah’s obedience. Classical scholars describe sincere repentance as abandoning the sin, regretting it, resolving not to return, and restoring the rights of people where applicable. If the wrong involved another person’s property, honour, or access to guidance, repentance includes returning the property, seeking pardon when appropriate, and correcting the false impression.

For a teacher who hid a clear ruling, this may mean teaching it openly. For someone who spread a distortion, it may mean retracting it before the people who heard it. For someone who concealed evidence of injustice, it may mean giving truthful testimony and helping repair the harm. Repentance is not complete when a person asks Allah for forgiveness while preserving the worldly benefit gained from the sin.

### “Correct Themselves”

*Iṣlāḥ* means setting right, repairing, and reforming. It points to a sustained change in conduct, not a momentary emotional reaction. The person who once concealed knowledge must rebuild trust through honesty, careful speech, and accountability. He should learn what he previously misrepresented and place safeguards around the weakness that led him to conceal it.

The same principle applies to communities and institutions. If a religious organisation hid abuse, financial corruption, or a false teaching, its repentance requires investigation, protection of victims, transparent correction, and structural reform. Public piety without repair is not the *iṣlāḥ* named in the verse.

### “Make the Truth Clear”

The command to clarify—*bayyanū*—is especially important. Concealment often leaves people with a false understanding, and a false understanding does not disappear merely because the concealer has privately repented. The truth must be made visible in a way that reaches those affected, while respecting wisdom, privacy, and the prevention of further harm.

Clarification should be accurate rather than exaggerated. A person must not replace one distortion with another or use repentance as an excuse to expose private matters unnecessarily. Qualified scholars and trusted advisers can help determine what must be stated publicly, what can be corrected privately, and how to protect people from confusion.

### Allah Accepts Repentance

Allah names Himself *al-Tawwāb*, the One who repeatedly accepts repentance and guides servants back to Him, and *al-Raḥīm*, the Merciful. The repetition implied in *al-Tawwāb* gives hope to those who have fallen more than once. It does not make sin harmless; it shows that despair is not an acceptable conclusion while life remains and sincere return is possible.

The Qur’an repeatedly tells people not to despair of Allah’s mercy (39:53), while also warning that repentance cannot be postponed until death has arrived (4:17–18). A person should repent immediately, but he should also trust that Allah can transform a former wrongdoer into a source of good. The Companion ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb’s repentance from error and the later lives of many former opponents illustrate how Allah changes people through truth.

### Repentance and Rights of People

The verse has a public dimension because concealment harms the public. It therefore teaches that rights between people are not erased by private worship alone. The Prophet ﷺ warned that a person may come on the Day of Resurrection with prayer and fasting but have exhausted his good deeds by wronging others, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim’s hadith of the bankrupt person. Repentance must address both the right of Allah and the rights of His servants.

This does not require a repentant person to publicize every private sin in a way that creates new harm. Islamic ethics distinguish between restoring a right, correcting a lie, protecting victims, and exposing a concealed fault that Allah has covered. The aim is truth and repair, not humiliation.

### Hope with Responsibility

The final verse of this passage closes a warning with hope. Allah’s mercy does not cancel responsibility; it makes responsibility possible. The sinner is invited to return, repair, and clarify because Allah is willing to accept him. A community should therefore combine accountability with the possibility of reform. It should neither normalize concealment nor declare sincere repentance impossible.

The broader lesson reaches every Muslim. When a person discovers that he has misunderstood or misrepresented the Qur’an, the noble response is not defensiveness. He should return to Allah, correct himself, and make the truth clear. In this way a former failure can become a lesson in humility and a means of benefit to others.
