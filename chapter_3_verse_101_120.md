## Verse 101

Verse 101 says: *How can you disbelieve when Allah’s revelations are recited to you and His Messenger is in your midst? Whoever holds firmly to Allah is surely guided to the Straight Path.*

In simple terms, the verse asks the believers the impossible question: how could you fall into disbelief while the verses of Allah are being recited to you and the Messenger is among you? And it gives the way out: whoever holds firmly to Allah will be guided to the Straight Path.

The verse answers the warning of 3:100 with the counter-question. Ibn Kathir: "'And how would you disbelieve, while unto you are recited the verses of Allah, and among you is His Messenger?' — meaning, disbelief is far from touching you, since the Ayat of Allah are being sent down on His Messenger day and night, and he recites and conveys them to you." The believers of the Prophet's own time lived in the most privileged conditions of the whole history: the revelation descending, the Messenger present, the proofs in their hands. The question of the verse is not the accusation of the inevitable but the rebuke of the absurd: the disbelief of the one who has the evidence in his own assembly is the unreason of the evidence's self-contradiction.

"How can you disbelieve" — *wa kayfa takfurun* — and how could you disbelieve? Al-Jalalayn: "an interrogative of amazement and rebuke." The *kayfa* — how — is the question of the impossibility of the condition, not of the method: the believer who has the recitation and the Messenger has the means of the faith, and the disbelief of the one who has the means is the unjustified departure. The verse's parallel in 57:8: "And what is the matter with you that you believe not in Allah while the Messenger invites you to believe in your Lord, and He has indeed taken your covenant?" — the same combination of the invitation, the covenant, and the question of the absurd departure.

"when Allah's revelations are recited to you" — *wa antum tutla 'alaykum ayat Allah* — while the Ayat of Allah are recited to you. The *tilawah* — the recitation — is the continuous presence of the revelation: the verses descending day and night, read in the prayer, heard in the assembly. Ibn Kathir: "the Ayat of Allah are being sent down on His Messenger day and night, and he recites and conveys them to you." The believers of the early community did not need to seek the revelation; it came to them. The verse's argument is the argument of the presence: the revelation is not a distant tradition but the living word in the assembly.

"and His Messenger is in your midst" — *wa fikum rasuluhu* — and His Messenger is among you. The second source is the living presence of the Prophet: the one who received the revelation, who taught it, who embodied it, who was the witness of the command. The Believers of the first generation saw the Qur'an in action: the Messenger among them, the model of the faith, the interpreter of the word. The verse's double condition — the recitation and the messenger — is the perfection of the evidence: the word and its embodiment, the message and its messenger, the proof and its example.

The hadith of the most amazing faith, recorded by Ibn Kathir, is the verse's profound commentary: the Prophet asked his Companions, "Who among the faithful believers do you consider has the most amazing faith?" They said, "The angels." He said, "Why would they not believe, since they are with their Lord?" They mentioned the Prophets, and he said, "Why would they not believe while the revelation is sent down to them?" They said, "Then, we are." He said, "Why would not you believe when I am among you?" They asked, "Who has the most amazing faith?" He said, "A people who will come after you and who will find only books that they will believe in." The hadith is the mirror of the verse: the faith of the ones who have the Messenger among them is the faith of the evidence; the faith of the ones who come after with the books alone is the faith of the heart. The verse's question to the first generation is the honor of their condition; the hadith's answer is the honor of the later generations. And both are the faith: the believers of every time hold the rope.

"Whoever holds firmly to Allah is surely guided to the Straight Path" — *wa man ya'tasim billahi faqad hudiya ila siratin mustaqim* — and whoever holds firmly to Allah, then he has been guided to a straight path. The *i'tisam* — the holding, the clinging, the taking of the firm hold — is the verse's way out: the believers who face the risk of the departure are given the countermeasure — the clinging to Allah. The *sirat al-mustaqim* — the Straight Path — is the same path of the prayer of the Fatihah (1:6–7): the path of the prophets and the blessed, not of those who earned anger or went astray. Ibn Kathir: "trusting and relying on Allah are the basis of achieving the right guidance and staying away from the path of wickedness. They also represent the tool to acquiring guidance and truth and achieving the righteous aims."

For the reader, the verse is the surah's answer to the faith of the evidence and the faith of the absence. The believers are taught: the faith is not the passive reception of the proofs; it is the firm hold on the One who gives the proofs. The one who has the recitation and the Messenger and departs has no excuse; and the one who has only the books and the absence of the Messenger and believes has the firmest hold — the *i'tisam* of the heart. The verse's standard is the hold: the *i'tisam* — the clinging that does not let go, the dependence that does not waver — is the guidance to the Straight Path. And the believer of every generation is given the method: the recitation of the revelation, the emulation of the Messenger, and the firm hold on Allah — the three that make the disbelief of the impossible the disbelief of the abandoned.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 102

Verse 102 says: *O believers! Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺.*

In simple terms, the verse commands the believers to have true consciousness of Allah — to fear Him as He truly deserves — and to die only in a state of full submission to Him.

The verse is the surah's supreme command of the *taqwa* at the height of its address. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "Meaning of 'Taqwa of Allah'": the verse's *ittaqu Allah haqqa tuqatihi* — have Taqwa of Allah as is His due — is the fullest form of the divine consciousness. The command is the foundation of the whole section: the believers, warned against the disbelief of 3:100–101, are given the *taqwa* and the *islam* as the orientation of the life and the death.

"Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves" — *ittaqu Allah haqqa tuqatihi* — fear Allah, be conscious of Allah with the true consciousness. Ibn Abi Hatim records Ibn Mas'ud: "That He is obeyed and not defied, remembered and not forgotten, and appreciated and not unappreciated" (authentic chain; Al-Hakim's Mustadrak). The definition is the completeness of the *taqwa*: the obedience that does not defy, the remembrance that does not forget, the gratitude that does not deny. Anas's report: "The servant will not have Taqwa of Allah as is His due until he keeps his tongue idle" — the *taqwa* of the heart is the *taqwa* of the tongue: the one who controls the speech controls the fire. The *haqqa tuqatihi* — the due of His taqwa — is the command of the absolute: the consciousness of Allah that is not partial, not occasional, not convenient, but the whole of the life's orientation.

"and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺" — *wa la tamutunna illa wa antum muslimun* — and do not die except while you are Muslims, submitters. The command of the life is the command of the death: the believer is to live in the submission so that the death finds him in it. Ibn Kathir: "preserve your Islam while you are well and safe, so that you die as a Muslim. The Most Generous Allah has made it His decision that whatever state one lives in, that is what he dies upon and is resurrected upon. We seek refuge from dying on other than Islam." The verse's *illa* — except — is the condition of the life: the believer lives as a Muslim so that he dies as a Muslim; the submission is not the final act but the continuous state that the final act finds.

The Mujahid-Ibn 'Abbas hadith, recorded by Ibn Kathir (at-Tirmidhi, an-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah, Ibn Hibban, Al-Hakim — hasan sahih): the Prophet recited this verse and then said, "Verily, if a drop of Zaqqum falls, it will spoil life for the people of the earth. What about those whose food is only from Zaqqum?" The hadith is the teaching of the *targhib wa al-tarhib* — the promise and the warning: the *taqwa* is the consciousness of the reality, and the reality of the default is the severity the *taqwa* avoids. The hadith's question — what about those whose food is only Zaqqum? — is the rhetorical teaching of the stakes of the departure.

Imam Ahmad records Jabir: three nights before the Messenger's death, he was heard saying, "None of you should die except while having sincere trust in Allah, the Exalted and Most Honorable" (also Muslim). The hadith is the verse's completion: the *islam* of the death is the *husn al-zann* — the good opinion, the trust in Allah. The Two Sahihs record Abu Hurayrah: Allah said, "I am as My servant thinks of Me." The dying believer's trust is the reflection of the living believer's *taqwa*: the one who lived in the consciousness of Allah dies in the trust of Allah; the one who lived in the obedience dies in the hope; and the one who lived in the submission dies in the submission.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's definition of the faithful life and the faithful death. The believer is taught: the *taqwa* is not the abstract reverence but the concrete obedience — the obeying that does not defy, the remembering that does not forget, the appreciating that does not deny; and the *islam* is not the label but the state — the submission that finds the death. The verse's command of the death is the command of the life: the one who lives as a Muslim dies as a Muslim; the death is the snapshot of the life, and the resurrection is the snapshot of the death. The believer's practical program is in the verse: the continuous obedience, remembrance, and gratitude — the *taqwa* that fills the life and shapes the death — and the trust in Allah that the death meets.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 103

Verse 103 says: *And hold firmly to the rope of Allah and do not be divided. Remember Allah’s favour upon you when you were enemies, then He united your hearts, so you—by His grace—became brothers. And you were at the brink of a fiery pit and He saved you from it. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so that you may be ˹rightly˺ guided.*

In simple terms, the verse commands the believers to hold firmly to Allah's rope together and not to divide; it reminds them of the favor of unity — they had been enemies, He joined their hearts, and by His grace they became brothers; they were on the edge of the fire and He saved them.

The verse is the surah's most beloved command of the unity and one of the most recited verses of the Qur'an. Ibn Kathir titles the section "The Necessity of Holding to the Path of Allah"; Al-Jalalayn identifies the *habl Allah* — the rope of Allah — as "His religion," with the command "together" and the warning "do not scatter after submitting to Islam." The verse's occasion per Al-Jalalayn: the Aws and the Khazraj — the two tribes of Madinah who were enemies before Islam, until Allah joined their hearts through Islam so that by His grace they became brothers in religion and comradeship; and they were upon the brink of the pit of fire, such that to fall into it they only had to die in disbelief, but He delivered them through belief.

"hold firmly to the rope of Allah" — *wa i'tasimu bi habli Allahi jami'an* — and hold firmly to the rope of Allah, all of you. The *habl* — the rope — is the bond, the covenant, the religion — the same word used of the covenant of protection in 3:112 (*hablun min Allah* — a covenant from Allah). The *i'tisam* — the holding — is the same verb of 3:101's *i'tasam*: the firm grasp. The believers are commanded to hold the rope *jami'an* — all together — the unity as the form of the holding: the rope is held by the community, not the individual; the division is the release of the rope. Ibn Kathir: the rope of Allah — the Qur'an and His religion — is the single bond; the community's grasp of it is the community's preservation.

"and do not be divided" — *wa la tafarraqu* — and do not scatter, do not divide. The *tafarruq* — the splitting, the scattering — is the opposite of the *i'tisam*: the release of the rope, the sectarian division, the loss of the brotherhood. The command's *la* is the prohibition of the division: not the harmless difference of the scholars but the shattering of the community's unity. Ibn Kathir: the division of the *firqah* — the partisanship — is the release of the single rope; the *sunnah and jama'ah* — the way and the community — is the holding.

"Remember Allah's favour upon you when you were enemies, then He united your hearts, so you—by His grace—became brothers" — *wa udhkur nif'mata Allahi 'alaykum idh kuntum a'da'an fa'allafa bayna qulubikum fa'asbahum bi ni'matihi ikhwan* — the memory is the basis of the command: the believers were enemies — the *Aws* and the *Khazraj*, the tribes of the pre-Islamic blood-feuds — and Allah united their hearts; by His grace they became *ikhwan* — brothers. The *ta'lif* — the joining, the uniting — is the divine act: not the diplomacy, not the treaty, not the common interest, but the joining of the hearts. The brotherhood of the faith is the brotherhood of the heart: the *ukhuwwah* of the Muslims is the work of Allah, and the memory of it is the preservation of it.

"And you were at the brink of a fiery pit and He saved you from it" — *wa kuntum 'ala shafa hufratin mina al-nari fa anqadhakum minha* — and you were on the edge of a pit of the Fire and He rescued you from it. The *hufrah* — the pit — is the grave of the disbelief: the pre-Islamic state was the edge of the abyss, and the Islam was the rescue. Al-Jalalayn: "to fall into it you only had to die disbelieving; but He delivered you from it through belief." The *anqadh* — He rescued — is the divine deliverance: the believers of the early community were not the authors of their salvation; they were the rescued ones. The verse's picture is the picture of the mercy: the edge of the pit and the hand of the rescue; the abyss of the condition and the deliverance of the faith.

"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so that you may be ˹rightly˺ guided" — *kadhalika yubayyinu Allahu lakum ayatihi la'allakum tahtadun* — thus Allah makes clear to you His signs so that you might be guided. The *tabyin* — the making clear — is the method of the Qur'an: the signs of the unity, the memory, and the rescue are the evidences of the guidance. Ibn Kathir: the clear signs are the evidences of the divine favor; the *la'allakum* — so that — is the purpose: the guidance, the *huda*.

For the reader, the verse is the charter of the Muslim community's unity and the most powerful rebuke of its division. The believer is taught: the unity is not the political convenience but the divine gift; the brotherhood is not the common interest but the joined hearts; the community is not the tribal confederation but the *ikhwan* of the grace. The verse's command is the *i'tisam* — the holding of the rope together — and the memory is the preservation: the believers who remember the enemies they were and the brothers they became are the believers who hold the rope. The verse's warning is the *tafarruq* — the division — and the verse's rescue is the *anqadh* — the deliverance from the edge of the pit. The believer's practice: the holding of the Qur'an and the Sunnah together, the brotherhood of the hearts, and the memory of the rescue — the three that are the *habl* of Allah and the preservation from the pit.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 104

Verse 104 says: *Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful.*

In simple terms, the verse commands that a group among the believers should arise to call people to goodness, to command the right, and to forbid the wrong — and it is they who will be successful.

The verse is the Qur'an's foundational command of the *da'wah* and the *hisbah* — the call and the accountability. Ibn Kathir titles the passage "The Command to Establish the Invitation to Allah": "Let there arise out of you a group of people that calls to righteousness, enjoins all that is good and forbids evil in the manner Allah commanded," and "it is they who are the successful."

"Let there be a group among you" — *waltakun minkum ummatun* — and let there be from you a community, a group. The *ummah* here is not the whole community but the dedicated segment: the *ummah* within the *ummah*, the group of the callers. Ad-Dahhak: "They are a special group of the Companions and a special group of those after them, that is those who perform Jihad and the scholars." Ibn Kathir: "The objective of this Ayah is that there should be a segment of this Muslim Ummah fulfilling this task, even though it is also an obligation on every member of this Ummah, each according to his ability." The verse's structure is the Qur'anic balance: the collective obligation of the whole (*fard kifayah* — the communal duty) and the dedicated group that carries it.

"who call ˹others˺ to goodness" — *yad'una ila al-khayr* — calling to the good. The *da'wah* — the call — is the first function: the invitation to the good, the summoning to the faith, the call of the truth. The *khayr* — the good — is the comprehensive: the good of the faith, of the worship, of the conduct, of the community — the good that the religion identifies and the callers summon to.

"encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil" — *wa ya'muruna bil-ma'ruf wa yanhawna 'an al-munkar* — and they enjoin the ma'ruf and forbid the munkar. The *ma'ruf* — the known good, the recognized right — and the *munkar* — the denied evil, the repudiated wrong — are the Qur'an's categories of the moral command. The *amr bil-ma'ruf* and the *nahy 'an al-munkar* — the commanding of the good and the forbidding of the evil — are the defining functions of the Muslim community (3:110, 9:71, 22:41, 31:17). Ibn Kathir's hadith collection on the verse: Muslim records Abu Hurayrah — "Whoever among you witnesses an evil, let him change it with his hand. If he is unable, then let him change it with his tongue. If he is unable, then let him change it with his heart, and this is the weakest faith" (and in another narration, "there is no faith beyond that, not even the weight of a mustard seed"). Imam Ahmad records Hudhayfah — "By He in Whose Hand is my soul! You will enjoin righteousness and forbid evil, or Allah shall send down a punishment from Him to you. Then, you will supplicate to Him, but He will not accept your supplication" (at-Tirmidhi: hasan). The hadiths are the verse's practical method: the gradation of the hand, the tongue, and the heart — and the warning of the abandonment.

"it is they who will be successful" — *wa ula'ika humu al-muflihun* — and those are the successful ones. The *falah* — the success, the prosperity, the salvation — is the Qur'an's highest verdict (2:5, 23:1, 62:10): the success of the callers is the success of the *falah* — the salvation and the prosperity. The verse's *hum* — they — is the identity: the callers of the good, the commanders of the right, the forbidders of the wrong are the *muflihun*. The success is the success of the function: the community that calls and commands and forbids is the community that succeeds.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's command of the community's mission. The believer is taught: the faith is not the private piety; it is the communal mission — the call to the good, the commanding of the right, the forbidding of the wrong. The verse's structure is the balance of the collective and the specific: the group of the callers within the community of the believers, each member carrying his share according to his ability. The method is the hadith's gradation: the hand, the tongue, and the heart — the action of the power, the word of the speech, and the conviction of the heart; and the abandonment is the warning of the withheld prayer. And the verdict is the *falah*: the callers are the successful — not the successful of the world's measure but the successful of the divine verdict, the *muflihun* of the Qur'an.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 105

Verse 105 says: *And do not be like those who split ˹into sects˺ and differed after clear proofs had come to them. It is they who will suffer a tremendous punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse warns the believers: do not follow the pattern of those who split into sects and disagreed after the clear proofs had reached them — they will face a tremendous punishment.

The verse is the negative counterpart of 3:103–104. Ibn Kathir: "In this Ayah, Allah forbids this Ummah from imitating the division and discord of the nations that came before them. These nations also abandoned enjoining righteousness and forbidding evil, although they had proof of its necessity." The verse's *la takunu* — do not be — is the prohibition of the imitation; the *kalladhina tafarruqu* — like those who split — is the negative model; and the *ba'da ma ja'ahum al-bayyinat* — after the clear proofs came to them — is the forfeiture of the excuse.

"do not be like those who split ˹into sects˺ and differed" — *wa la takunu kalladhina tafarruqu wa ikhtalafu* — and do not be like those who split and differed. The *tafarruq* and the *ikhtilaf* — the splitting and the differing — are the two movements of the sectarian collapse: the splitting into the *firq* — the parties, the factions — and the differing in the religion. Ibn Kathir's context: the *ahl al-kitabayn* — the People of the Two Scriptures — divided; the difference is the difference after the proofs, not the legitimate *ikhtilaf* of the scholars but the sectarian *ikhtilaf* of the factions.

"after clear proofs had come to them" — *min ba'di ma ja'ahum al-bayyinat* — after the clear proofs came to them. The *bayyinat* — the clear proofs, the manifest evidences — are the same word of 3:86: the proofs the rejecters had and rejected. The qualification is the verdict's crux: the division of the ignorant is the error; the division of the proven is the rebellion. Ibn Kathir: the nations before "had proof of its necessity" and abandoned it — the division after the proofs is the division of the knowing. The *bayyinat* also includes the clear proofs of the unity: the message of the prophets, the evidence of the oneness, the proofs of the Qur'an. The sects of the two scriptures divided after the proofs: the truth was clear, and they divided about it.

Imam Ahmad records the hadith of Abu 'Amir 'Abdullah bin Luhay: "We performed Hajj with Mu'awiyah bin Abi Sufyan. When we arrived at Makkah, he stood up after praying Zuhr and said, 'The Messenger of Allah said: The People of the Two Scriptures divided in their religion into seventy-two sects, and this Ummah will divide into seventy-three sects — meaning the desires — all of them in the Fire except one — and it is the Jama'ah. And some of my Ummah will be guided by desire, like one who is infected by rabies; no vein or joint will be saved from these desires.' Then Mu'awiyah said, 'By Allah, O Arabs! If you do not adhere to what came to you from your Prophet, then other people are even more prone not to adhere to it'" (Abu Dawud records it from Ahmad bin Hanbal and Muhammad bin Yahya). The hadith is the verse's commentary: the division of the seventy-three, the one saved — the *jama'ah* — and the desires that run through the veins like rabies.

"It is they who will suffer a tremendous punishment" — *ula'ika lahum 'adhabun 'azim* — those are for them a great punishment. The *'adhab 'azim* — the tremendous punishment — is the verdict of the split after the proofs. The verse's *hum* — they — is the identity: the dividers, the differers, the sectarians of the proven truth are the ones of the great punishment. Ibn Kathir's treatment of the next verse: the faces of the followers of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah radiate with whiteness, and the faces of the followers of the innovation and the division are darkened — the punishment of the division is the darkness of the face at the gathering.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's warning against the sectarianism and the command of the unity's preservation. The believer is taught: the difference of the scholars is the mercy (the *ikhtilaf* of the *rukhsah*), but the division of the community after the proofs is the *'adhab 'azim* — the great punishment. The verse's standard is the *bayyinat*: the proofs were clear, and the division after them is the rebellion; the sectarianism of the proven is the sin of the knowing. The hadith of the seventy-three is the model: the one saved is the *jama'ah* — the community, the congregation of the truth — and the desires that infect like rabies are the desires of the faction. The believer's program is in the verse: the unity of the community, the avoidance of the sectarian division, the adherence to the *jama'ah* — and the preservation of the faith from the desires that would split it. The verse's warning is the warning of the face's darkness at the gathering — the recompense of the division after the proofs.

## Verse 106

Verse 106 says: *On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy. To the gloomy-faced it will be said, “Did you disbelieve after having believed? So taste the punishment for your disbelief.”*

In simple terms, the verse shows the Day of Judgment with a striking picture: some faces will be radiant and others darkened; the darkened-faced will be told, "Did you disbelieve after having believed? Taste the punishment for your disbelief."

The verse brings the warning of 3:105 to its conclusion: the division after the proofs (105) receives its recompense at the gathering (106–107). Ibn Kathir: "On the Day when some faces will become white and some faces will become black — on the Day of Resurrection. This is when the faces of followers of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah will radiate with whiteness, and the faces of followers of Bid'ah (innovation) and division will be darkened, as has been reported from Ibn 'Abbas." The *bayd* — the whiteness — and the *sawad* — the blackness — are the Qur'an's image of the state of the hearts reflected in the faces (80:38-42, 39:60, 75:22-26).

"On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy" — *yawma tabyaddu wujuhun wa taswaddu wujuh* — the day some faces become white and some black. The *yawm* — the Day, the Day of Resurrection — is the Day of the gathering of the division's consequence. Ibn Kathir: the faces of the followers of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah radiate with whiteness; the faces of the followers of the innovation and division are darkened (from Ibn 'Abbas). The verse's *ba'd* — some — is the division of the faces: the *awayd* — the white — and the *muswadd* — the black — are the two states of the two paths.

"To the gloomy-faced it will be said" — *fa amma alladhina iswaddat wujuhuhum* — as for those whose faces are blackened. The address is the rebuke: the darkened faces are the evidence, and the saying is the question. Al-Jalalayn: "these being the disbelievers who are thrown into the Fire and to whom it is said in rebuke." Al-Hasan Al-Basri: "They are the hypocrites." Ibn Kathir: the description "befits every disbeliever" — the blackened faces are the faces of the ones who had the faith and rejected it, and the rebuke is the rebuke of the apostasy.

"Did you disbelieve after having believed?" — *akafartum ba'da imanikum* — did you disbelieve after your belief? The question is the same rebuke of 3:86: the belief and the departure; the *iman* that preceded and the *kufr* that followed. Al-Jalalayn: "the day the covenant was made" — the address recalls the covenant of 3:81, the acknowledgment, and the rejection. The question is not the inquiry but the exhibition: the darkened faces are asked the question that records the sequence — you believed, and you disbelieved; the evidence of the belief is the beginning, the evidence of the disbelief is the darkening.

"So taste the punishment for your disbelief" — *fadhuqu al-'adhab bima kuntum takfurun* — then taste the punishment for what you used to disbelieve. The *dhuq* — the taste — is the experienced punishment: not the announcement but the test. Ibn Kathir: "Then taste the torment (in Hell) for rejecting faith." The *bima kuntum takfurun* — for what you were disbelieving — is the identification: the punishment is not for the single act but for the continuous state — the *kufr* of the life. The verse's *kuntum* — you were — is the habit: the disbelief was not the momentary lapse but the ongoing orientation.

The hadith of Abu Umamah, recorded by at-Tirmidhi (hasan), Ibn Majah, and Imam Ahmad: he saw the heads of the Khawarij hanging on the streets of Damascus and commented, "The Dogs of the Fire and the worst dead people under the cover of the sky. The best dead men are those whom these have killed." He then recited this verse until its end. Asked whether he heard it from the Messenger, he said: "If I only heard it from the Messenger of Allah once, twice, thrice, four times, or seven times, I would not have narrated it to you." The hadith is the verse's application: the sectarians — the ones of the division after the proofs — are the ones of the darkened faces; the Khawarij of the first community were the model of the *firqah* that split after the clear proofs, and the heads on the streets were the emblem of the division's end.

For the reader, the verse is the revealer of the recompense. The believer is taught: the inner state is the outward face; the harmony of the heart with the truth radiates and the disharmony darkens; and the Day of the gathering is the day of the exhibition. The verse's picture is the Qur'an's most vivid of the two ends: the radiant faces of the sunnah and the jama'ah, and the darkened faces of the innovation and the division; the address of the apostasy's question and the tasting of the disbelief's punishment. The believer's orientation: the *sunnah* and the *jama'ah* — the way of the Prophet and the community of the truth — which the light of the face reflects, and the avoidance of the *bid'ah* and the *firqah* — the innovations and the factions — which the darkness of the face announces.

## Verse 107

Verse 107 says: *As for the bright-faced, they will be in Allah’s mercy, where they will remain forever.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the other side: the bright-faced will be in Allah's mercy, dwelling in it forever.

The verse is the counterpart of 3:106: the two faces, the two verdicts. Ibn Kathir: "'And for those whose faces will become white, they will be in Allah's mercy (Paradise), therein they shall dwell forever' — in Paradise, where they will reside for eternity and shall never desire to be removed." The *rahmat Allah* — the mercy of Allah — is the Paradise: not the reward of the deed alone but the mercy that welcomes the deed's people.

"As for the bright-faced" — *wa amma alladhina ibyaddat wujuhuhum* — as for those whose faces became white. The *ibyaddad* — the becoming white — is the state of the radiant: the faces of the faithful, the followers of the sunnah, the believers of the one path. Ibn Kathir: the faces of the followers of the Sunnah and the Jama'ah radiate with whiteness. The *bayd* of the face is the light of the faith: the *nur* of the belief (57:12 — "their light will run before them"), the radiance of the submission, the brightness of the heart's orientation.

"they will be in Allah's mercy" — *fa fi rahmati Allahi* — then they are in the mercy of Allah. The *fi* — in — is the dwelling: the mercy is not the visitation but the abode; the believers are in the mercy as in a place. Ibn Kathir: "in Paradise, where they will reside for eternity." The *rahman* — the mercy — is the divine attribute that encompasses: the mercy of the creation, of the guidance, and of the abode; the faith of the believers is the faith of the mercy's acceptance.

"where they will remain forever" — *hum fiha khalidun* — they are in it forever, abiding. The *khulud* — the permanence, the abiding — is the same word of the negative verdicts (3:88, 3:116): the abiding of the believers in the mercy is the counterpart of the abiding of the deniers in the punishment. Ibn Kathir: "they shall never desire to be removed" — the abiding is the completion of the mercy: the believers not only dwell but dwell without the desire of departure; the perfection of the peace.

The verse's position in the surah: 3:106–107 are the culmination of the section that began at 3:100: the warning against the yielding to the People of the Book (100), the rebuke of the disbelief of the proven (101), the command of the taqwa and the islam (102), the command of the rope and the unity (103), the command of the call and the command and the forbidding (104), the prohibition of the division (105), and now the two verdicts: the darkened faces of the dividers and the radiant faces of the merciful. The section's arc is the arc of the community's choice: the unity and the submission and the call — or the division and the disbelief — and the faces at the gathering.

For the reader, the verse is the promise that balances the warning. The believer is taught: the division's end is the darkness, but the unity's end is the radiance; the disbelief's end is the tasting, but the faith's end is the mercy. The verse's *rahmat* is the believer's hope: the Paradise is the mercy — not the wage of the deed but the mercy that the deed's people receive; and the *khulud* is the completion: the eternal abiding without the desire of departure. The believer's orientation is in the verse: the faith, the sunnah, the jama'ah — the radiance of the face and the mercy of the abode — and the verse's *fi rahmati Allah* — in the mercy of Allah — is the believer's final destination: the dwelling in the divine mercy, forever.

## Verse 108

Verse 108 says: *These are Allah’s revelations We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. And Allah desires no injustice to ˹His˺ creation.*

In simple terms, the verse states the nature of the recitation: these are Allah's verses, recited to the Prophet in truth — and Allah does not will any injustice to the creation.

The verse is the bridge between the two verdicts and the conclusion of the section. Ibn Kathir: "'These are the Ayat of Allah. We recite them to you' — meaning, 'These are the verses of Allah, His proofs and signs that We reveal to you, O Muhammad' — 'in truth' — making known the true reality of this world and the Hereafter. 'And Allah wills no injustice to the 'Alamin' — for He never treats them with injustice. Rather, He is the Just Ruler Who is able to do everything and has knowledge of everything. Therefore, He does not need to treat any of His creatures with injustice."

"These are Allah's revelations" — *tilka ayat Allah* — these are the Ayat of Allah. The *tilka* — these — is the demonstration: the verses of the whole section — the covenant, the verdicts, the commands, the warnings — are the *ayat* of Allah. Ibn Kathir: "His proofs and signs." The *ayat* — the signs — of Allah are the evidences of His judgment: the recitation of the verses is the recitation of the divine testimony, and the testimony is the record of the truth.

"We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth" — *natluha 'alayka bil-haqq* — We recite them to you in truth. The *tilawah* — the recitation — is the divine recitation to the Prophet: the revelation conveyed; and *bil-haqq* — in truth — is the manner: the recitation is not the error, not the exaggeration, not the distortion — the truth of the reality of this world and the Hereafter. Ibn Kathir: "making known the true reality of this world and the Hereafter." The recitation's truth is the correspondence of the report and the reality: the verses say what is, and the recitation is the truthful saying.

"And Allah desires no injustice to ˹His˺ creation" — *wa ma Allahu yuridu zulman lil-'alamin* — and Allah does not will wrong for the worlds. The *zulm* — the injustice, the wrong — is the denial of the just; and the *la yuridu* — does not will — is the categorical. Ibn Kathir: "He never treats them with injustice. Rather, He is the Just Ruler Who is able to do everything and has knowledge of everything. Therefore, He does not need to treat any of His creatures with injustice." The verse's *lil-'alamin* — for the worlds — is the universal: not the single creature but the whole creation; and the *zulm* — the wrong — of the divine judgment is the denied: the judgment of the darkened and the radiant faces is the just judgment, and the justice is the truth of the recitation.

The verse is the answer to the possible misreading of the verdicts: the punishment of 3:106 is not the divine injustice but the consequence of the disbelief, and the mercy of 3:107 is not the arbitrary favor but the reception of the faith. Ibn Kathir's framing: the One who is able to do everything and knows everything does not need the injustice; the judgment of the creation by the creator's justice is the perfectly just. The *zulm* of the covenant-breakers and the dividers (their own wrong of 3:117) is their own; the *zulm* of the divine — the wrong of the Judge — is the denied.

For the reader, the verse is the surah's foundation of the divine justice and its denial of the wrong. The believer is taught: the reports of the Qur'an are the truth; the realities of this world and the Hereafter are as the verses say; and the judge of the realities is the Just One who does not will the wrong. The verse's *bil-haqq* — in truth — is the believer's confidence: the recitation is the truthful record, the judgment is the just verdict, and the destiny is the exact recompense. The believer's response: the acceptance of the recitation, the trust in the justice, and the removal of the doubt about the accounting — the doubt that the *zulm* of the divine could touch the creation.

## Verse 109

Verse 109 says: *To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺.*

In simple terms, the verse states the total ownership: everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah, and all matters are returned to Him for judgment.

The verse completes the doctrine of 3:108: the justice of the judge follows from the ownership of the Owner. Ibn Kathir: "and to Allah belongs all that is in the heavens and all that is in the earth — they are all His servants and His property — and all matters go back to Allah — for His is the decision concerning the affairs of this life and the Hereafter, and His is the Supreme Authority in this life and the Hereafter."

"To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth" — *wa lillahi ma fi al-samawati wa ma fi al-ard* — and to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is in the earth. The *li-Allah* — to Allah — is the ownership: the *mulkiyyah* of the whole. Ibn Kathir: "they are all His servants and His property." The verse's *ma* — whatever — is the exhaustive: everything, without exception — the angels, the heavens, the earth, the creatures, the humans — the whole of the reality is His. The ownership of 3:109 is the same ownership of 2:284, 3:29, 3:129: the *mulq* of Allah — the possession of the sovereign.

"and to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺" — *wa ila Allahi turja'u al-umur* — and to Allah the matters are returned. The *ruju'* — the return — is the return of the affairs: the end of the creation and the beginning of the judgment. Ibn Kathir: "His is the decision concerning the affairs of this life and the Hereafter, and His is the Supreme Authority in this life and the Hereafter." The *umur* — the matters — are the affairs: the individual and the communal, the worldly and the otherworldly; and the return is the return of the decision — the judgment of the affairs is the divine decision.

The verse's theology: the *mulkiyyah* and the *ruju'* are the two pillars of the final sovereignty. The ownership of 109a is the basis of the justice of 108: the Owner of the *ma fi al-samawat* judges the *ma fi al-ard*; the judge of the affairs is the Owner of the affairs. The *ruju'* of 109b is the completion: the matters are returned to the Owner, and the judgment is the Owner's. The verse's *turja'* — they are returned — is the passive: the matters are returned to Allah — not by the coercion but by the reality: the origin and the end are His, and the affairs return to the One from whom they came.

For the reader, the verse is the doctrine of the total divine authority and the believer's confidence in the final judgment. The believer is taught: the ownership of everything — the heavens, the earth, the affairs — is Allah's, and the return of the matters is to Him; the judge is the Owner, and the judgment is the just. The verse's *turja'* — they are returned — is the believer's hope: the disputes of the world, the deceptions of the enemies, the injustices of the earth — the matters return to the Owner, and the judgment of them is His. And the verse is the believer's submission: the affairs are not in the hands of the creatures but of the Creator; the *mu'min* trusts the return, and the trust is the peace.

## Verse 110

Verse 110 says: *You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah. Had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them. Some of them are faithful, but most are rebellious.*

In simple terms, the verse declares that the Muslim community is the best community raised for humanity because they enjoin good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah; had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them — some do believe, but most are rebellious.

The verse is one of the most celebrated of the surah: the divine declaration of the *khayr ummah* — the best community. Ibn Kathir titles the section "Virtues of the Ummah of Muhammad, the Best Nation Ever": "Allah states that the Ummah of Muhammad is the best nation ever." The verse's *kuntum* — you were, you are — is the declaration of the community's standing; the *ukhrijat lil-nas* — raised for the people — is the community's mission; and the three verbs — the commanding, the forbidding, and the believing — are the community's qualities.

"You are the best community ever raised for humanity" — *kuntum khayra ummatin ukhrijat lil-nas* — you are the best community brought forth for the people. The *khayr* — the best — is the superlative: the Ummah of Muhammad is the best of the communities. Al-Bukhari records Abu Hurayrah: "(You, Muslims, are) the best nation of people for the people — you bring them tied in chains on their necks (capture them in war) and they later embrace Islam." Similarly Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, 'Atiyah Al-'Awfi, 'Ikrimah, 'Ata', and Ar-Rabi' bin Anas: "the best of peoples for the people" — the best, most righteous, and most beneficial nation for mankind. The *lil-nas* — for the people — is the purpose: the best community is the community raised for the people — the community of the mission, of the benefit, of the guidance.

"you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah" — *ta'muruna bil-ma'ruf wa tanhawna 'an al-munkar wa tu'minuna billah* — the three qualities of the best community: the *amr bil-ma'ruf*, the *nahy 'an al-munkar*, and the *iman billah*. The three are the same three of 3:104 (the *ummatun* calling to the good) and 9:71 (the believers enjoin and forbid). Ibn Kathir: "whoever among this Ummah acquires these qualities will have a share in this praise." The best-ness of the community is the functional: the community that commands the good, forbids the evil, and believes in Allah is the best community — the quality is the criterion, not the label.

The hadiths on the virtues of the Ummah, recorded by Ibn Kathir: Imam Ahmad, at-Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah, and Al-Hakim record Hakim bin Mu'awiyah bin Haydah from his father: "You are the final of seventy nations, you are the best and most honored among them to Allah" (at-Tirmidhi: hasan; also from Mu'adh and Abu Sa'id). Ali: "I was given what no other Prophet before me was given… I was given victory by fear, I was given the keys of the earth, I was called Ahmad, the earth was made a clean place for me, and my Ummah was made the best Ummah" (hasan chain). The Two Sahihs record Abu Hurayrah: seventy thousand entering Paradise with faces like the full moon — Ukkashah bin Mihsan stood and asked to be among them; the Prophet made the supplication, and when an Ansari asked, he said, "Ukkashah has beaten you to it." Jabir: the Prophet hoped those following him would be one-fourth, then one-third, then one-half of the people of Paradise. Buraydah: the people of Paradise are 120 rows, and this Ummah takes eighty (at-Tirmidhi: hasan). The Two Sahihs: "We are the last to come, but the foremost on the Day of Resurrection, and the first people to enter Paradise… the Jews' day is tomorrow (Saturday) and the Christians' is the day after tomorrow (Sunday)." Qatadah: "'Umar recited this verse during a Hajj when he saw the people rushing and said, 'Whoever likes to be among this praised Ummah, let him fulfill the condition that Allah set in this Ayah'" (Ibn Jarir). The virtue of the Ummah is the virtue of its Prophet and its law: the perfect and complete Law, few deeds replacing many.

"Had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them" — *wa law amana ahl al-kitabi la kana khayran lahum* — and if the People of the Book had believed, it would have been better for them. The verse's *law* — if — is the conditional of the missed good: the People of the Book who believed in what was sent down to Muhammad would have had the better: the best of the two communities — the faith of the final revelation. Ibn Kathir: "in what was sent down to Muhammad — it would have been better for them." The verse's *khayr* — the better — is the comparative: the belief of the People of the Book in the final Messenger would have been better than their rejection — the better of the acceptance.

"Some of them are faithful, but most are rebellious" — *minhumu al-mu'minun wa aktharuhumu al-fasiqun* — among them are believers, but most are the rebellious. The verse's fairness: not all the People of the Book are the rejecters; there are the believers among them — Al-Jalalayn names 'Abdullah bin Salam and his companions. Ibn Kathir: "Therefore only a few of them believe in Allah and in what was sent down to you and to them. The majority of them follow deviation, disbelief, sin, and rebellion." The *mu'minun* — the believers — are the exceptions; the *fasiqun* — the rebellious — are the majority. The verse's *akthar* — most — is the measure: the majority, not the whole; and the *mu'minun* — the believers — are the honest recognition: the community of the Book is not a monolith.

For the reader, the verse is the declaration of the community's honor and the condition of the honor's preservation. The believer is taught: the best-ness is not the inherent virtue of the label but the functional quality — the community that commands the good, forbids the evil, and believes in Allah is the best community; and the one who does not acquire these qualities is like the People of the Scriptures whom Allah criticized (5:79 — "they did not forbid one another from the munkar"). The verse is the honor and the warning: the *khayr ummah* is the *kuntum* — the conditional — and the *Ummah* that abandons the *amr* and the *nahy* loses the honor. The verse's *law amana* — the conditional of the People of the Book — is the fairness: the believers among them are recognized, and the *fasiqun* are the majority. The believer's program: the *amr*, the *nahy*, the *iman* — the three qualities that make the *kuntum khayra ummah* real.

## Verse 111

Verse 111 says: *They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance. But if they meet you in battle, they will flee and they will have no helpers.*

In simple terms, the verse reassures the believers: the enemies can never truly harm them — only a little annoyance; and if they fight them in battle, they will flee and find no one to help them.

The verse follows 3:110 with the assurance. Ibn Kathir titles the section "The Good News that Muslims will Dominate the People of the Book": "Allah states that the People of the Scriptures will never be able to harm the believers, although they may do so by means of uttering foul words or fighting with the believers. He then states that when they fight the believers, they will turn their backs and run away." The verse's *lan yadurrukum* — they will never harm you — is the categorical promise; *illa adhan* — except annoyance — is the qualifier; and *yuwallun al-adbar* — they will turn their backs — is the declaration of the enemies' fate.

"They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance" — *lan yadurrukum illa adhan* — they will never harm you except with annoyance. The *darar* — the harm — is the real injury; the *adha* — the annoyance, the offense — is the superficial. Ibn Kathir: "they may do so by means of uttering foul words" — the tongue-severity of the enemies (the hatred of 3:118–119) is the *adha*. The verse's *lan* — never — is the categorical of the harm's prevention: the divine protection of the believers from the real harm; and the *illa* — except — is the allowance of the annoyance: the superficial offenses the enemies can inflict — the insults, the irritation, the provocations — but not the real harm. The verse's theology: the protection is the divine; the harm the enemies try is the *adha*; the real harm is the protected.

"But if they meet you in battle, they will flee" — *wa in yaqatilukum yuwallunukum al-adbar* — and if they fight you, they will turn their backs to you, they will flee. The *tawliyah* — the turning — is the fleeing: the defeat of the enemies in the battle. Ibn Kathir applies the verse's history: "the Companions of the Prophet defeated the Jews in every battle they fought against them, and this is exactly what occurred when the Jews of Madinah fought the Prophet — Banu Qaynuqa', Banu An-Nadir, and Banu Qurayzah. The Prophet marched against them and defeated them." The verse's promise of the *tawliyah* is the promise of the believers' victory: the enemies who fight do not fight to victory but to the turning of the backs — the route of the Khaybar, the exile, the humiliation (3:112).

"and they will have no helpers" — *thumma la yunsarun* — and then they will not be helped. The *nusrah* — the help — is the aid: the enemies fight without the allies; the defeat is the defeat without the support. Ibn Kathir: "the group remaining in ash-Sham until 'Isa descends ruling by the Law of Muhammad, breaking the cross, killing the swine, banishing the jizyah" — the verse's fulfillment in the succession: the Jews of Madinah defeated, the Christians of ash-Sham subdued, and the no-help of the enemies — the route of the conquests.

The verse's historical confirmation, per Ibn Kathir: the Jews of Madinah — Qaynuqa' (expelled), Nadir (expelled), Qurayzah (the consequences) — defeated; the Christians of ash-Sham — in the time of Abu Bakr and 'Umar — subdued; the group in ash-Sham remains until 'Isa descends. The verse's promise is not the abstract but the historical: the Muslims of the first communities saw the fulfillment — the enemies who fought and fled, who were not helped and were humiliated.

For the reader, the verse is the reassurance of the believers' security and the declaration of the enemies' defeat. The believer is taught: the real harm of the enemies is the prevented; the annoyance of the tongues is the tolerated; and the fight of the enemies is the flight — the turning of the backs and the no-help. The verse's *adha* — the annoyance — is the believer's patience-test: the offenses of the enemies are the superficial; the *lan yadurrukum* is the deep. The verse's *yunsarun* — they are not helped — is the believer's confidence: the enemies alone, without the allies, defeated. And the verse's history — the Qaynuqa', the Nadir, the Qurayzah, the ash-Sham — is the record of the promise's fulfillment: the believers who trust the verse trust the route of the conquests.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 112

Verse 112 says: *They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go, unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people. They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery for rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations.*

In simple terms, the verse states the state of the rejecters: disgrace strikes them wherever they go, unless they hold a covenant with Allah or a treaty with people; they have earned Allah's displeasure and been marked with misery — because they rejected His revelations and killed the prophets unjustly — a fair reward for their disobedience and violations.

The verse is the declaration of the *dhillah* — the disgrace, the humiliation — of the rejecters. Ibn Kathir: "This verse explains the state of the enemies of the believers — the Jews and the Christians — whom Allah humiliated and disgraced." The verse's *duribat 'alayhim al-dhillah* — disgrace was struck upon them — is the divine stamping; *ayna ma thuqifu* — wherever they are found — is the universal; *illa bi hablin min Allahi wa hablin min al-nas* — except with a covenant from Allah and a covenant from people — is the exception; and the causes — the *kufr* of the verses, the *qatl* of the prophets, and the *'isyan* and the *i'tida'* — are the accounting.

"They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go" — *duribat 'alayhim al-dhillah ayna ma thuqifu* — the disgrace was struck over them wherever they were found. The *dhillah* — the disgrace, the lowliness, the humiliation — is the divine stamp of the rejectors: the state of the punished. Ibn Kathir: "disgrace and humiliation will cover them whenever they are found" — not the occasional but the universal; the *duribat* — was struck — is the stamping: the disgrace is not the event but the condition; and the *ayna ma thuqifu* — wherever they are found — is the universality: in every land, every time, every state — the disgrace of the rejected.

"unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people" — *illa bi hablin min Allahi wa hablin min al-nas* — except with a rope/covenant from Allah and a rope/covenant from people. The verse's *habl* — the rope, the covenant — is the same word of 3:103: the protection of the believers' covenant. Ibn Kathir: "the dhimmah — the covenant and protection of the Islamic law — such as the *jizyah* that is taken from them, for if they are under the *dhimmah*, their persons and properties will be protected, and they will be given the covenant of Allah, His Messenger, and the believers, and the protection of the Muslims" (Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, and others). The *habl min Allah* — the covenant from Allah — is the *dhimmah* of the Islamic law; the *habl min al-nas* — the covenant from people — is the pledge of safety from the Muslims: the *aman*, the guarantee. The verse's exception is the recognition: the disgrace is not the state of the *dhimmi* — the Jew or Christian under the protection — but of the enemy who refuses the covenant; the *dhillah* — the humiliation — is lifted by the *dhimmah* — the protection — of the *jizyah* and the law.

"They have invited the displeasure of Allah" — *wa ba'u bi ghadabin min Allahi* — and they have returned with anger from Allah. The *ghadab* — the anger, the wrath — is the divine anger: the same *ghadab* of the Fatihah (1:7 — "those who earned Your anger"), of the Jews' sin. Ibn Kathir: "they earned the anger of Allah." The *ba'u* — they returned, they earned — is the recompense: the anger of Allah is the return of their choices; they brought it upon themselves. The verse's *ba'u* — they returned with — is the image of the merchants returning with a commodity: they earned the divine displeasure as the result of their trade.

"and have been branded with misery" — *wa duribat 'alayhim al-maskanah* — and poverty was struck over them. The *maskanah* — the poverty, the destitution, the wretchedness — is the second stamp: the disgrace of the condition and the poverty of the state. Ibn Kathir: "the poverty, destitution, and the humiliation." The *maskanah* of the People of the Book was the historical: the exile of the Jews, the loss of the lands, the humiliation of the centuries — the *maskanah* of the rejectors. The *duribat* — was struck, was branded — is the divine marking: the disgrace and the misery are not the accidents of history but the signatures of the divine judgment.

"for rejecting Allah's revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly" — *dhalika bi annahum kanu yakfuruna bi ayat Allahi wa yaqtuluna al-anbiya'a bi ghayri haqq* — that is because they were disbelieving in the Ayat of Allah and killing the prophets without right. The two causes are the historical: the *kufr* of the verses — the rejection of the divine signs, including the prophecies of the final Messenger (Ibn Kathir: "they disbelieved in the Ayat of Allah" and "they killed the prophets without right" — the killing of Zakariyya and Yahya per the traditions) — and the *qatl* of the prophets — the murder of the messengers sent to them. The *bi ghayri haqq* — without right — is the injustice of the killing: the prophets were not enemies but messengers; the killing was the crime of the wrong.

"This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations" — *dhalika bima 'asaw wa kanu ya'tadun* — that is because they disobeyed and were transgressing. The third cause: the *'isyan* — the disobedience — and the *i'tida'* — the transgression, the crossing of the limits. Ibn Kathir: "their disobedience and transgression" — the general cause after the specific: the *kufr* and the *qatl* were the acts; the *'isyan* and the *i'tida'* were the states. The verse's *kanu* — they were — is the continuity: the disobedience and the transgression were not the moments but the orientations.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's doctrine of the rejectors' state and the believers' protection. The believer is taught: the *dhillah* and the *maskanah* — the disgrace and the misery — are the divine consequences of the *kufr*, the *qatl*, and the *'isyan*; the *habl* — the covenant — is the exception: the *dhimmi* under the *jizyah* and the protection is not the state of the disgrace but of the covenant. The verse is both the history and the law: the history of the rejectors' humiliation, the law of the covenant's protection. And the verse is the warning: the causes of the *ghadab* and the *maskanah* — the rejection of the verses, the killing of the prophets, the disobedience and the violation — are the causes the believers avoid; the *dhillah* is the consequence of the *kufr*, and the dignity — the honor — is the consequence of the faith.

## Verse 113

Verse 113 says: *Yet they are not all alike: there are some among the People of the Book who are upright, who recite Allah’s revelations throughout the night, prostrating ˹in prayer˺.*

In simple terms, the verse corrects the generalization: the People of the Book are not all the same — among them is an upright community that recites Allah's revelations throughout the night, prostrating in prayer.

The verse follows the negative of 3:110–112 with the positive, and it corrects the possible reading of the condemnations. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that not all the People of the Book are alike; rather, they have good and bad people." The verse's *laysu sawa'* — they are not equal — is the correction; *ummatun qa'imah* — a community standing, upright — is the positive; and the *yatlu ayat Allah* — reciting the verses of Allah — *ana' al-layl* — in the hours of the night — *wa hum yasjudun* — while they prostrate — is the description of the upright.

"Yet they are not all alike" — *laysu sawa'an* — they are not equal, they are not all the same. The *sawa'* — the equality — is the denial: the People of the Book are not a monolithic deniers. Ibn Kathir: "not all the People of the Book are alike — rather, they have good and bad people." The verse's correction is the fairness of the Qur'an: the condemnations of 110–112 are the condemnations of the *fasiqun* — the majority, the rebellious; but the *ummah qa'imah* — the upright community — is the exception of the *mu'minun* (110).

"there are some among the People of the Book who are upright" — *min ahli al-kitabi ummatun qa'imah* — among the People of the Book is a community standing, upright. The *qawm* — the standing — is the uprightness: the community that stands in the prayer and in the truth. Al-Jalalayn: "implementing the Book of Allah, adhering to His Law, and following the Prophet." Ibn Kathir identifies the group: the clergy who embraced Islam — 'Abdullah bin Salam, Asad bin 'Ubayd, Tha'labah bin Sa'yah, Usayd bin Sa'yah — per Ibn Ishaq, and Al-'Awfi from Ibn 'Abbas — the scholars and the monks who implemented the Book and followed the Prophet. The *ummatun qa'imah* is the community of the Book that stood upright: the ones who saw the final Messenger and believed; the *qiyam* — the standing — of the truth.

"who recite Allah's revelations throughout the night, prostrating ˹in prayer˺" — *yatluna ayat Allahi ana'a al-layli wa hum yasjudun* — they recite the verses of Allah in the hours of the night while they prostrate. The *tilawah* — the recitation — is the *tahajjud* — the night prayer: the reciting of the Qur'an in the *ana' al-layl* — the hours of the night. Ibn Kathir: "reciting the verses of Allah at night, for the Tahajjud, and prostrating" — the night prayer of the believers of the Book: the *qiyam* and the *sujud* — the standing and the prostration — the worship of the night. The verse's *yasjudun* — they prostrate — is the prostration of the prayer; the *ana'* — the hours — the night's watches: the recitation throughout the night is the worship of the night — the *qiyam al-layl* of the upright.

The verse's context: the People of the Book who believed — 'Abdullah bin Salam and his companions — were the clergy who saw the evidence and embraced the faith. The verse's *yatlu* — they recite — is their practice: not the rejection but the recitation; not the *kufr* but the *tilawah*. Ibn Kathir: "these are the clergy of the People of the Book who implemented the Book of Allah, adhered to the Law of Allah, and followed the Prophet."

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's fairness and the recognition of the upright among the People of the Book. The believer is taught: the condemnations of the rejectors are not the condemnations of the whole — the *ummatun qa'imah* — the upright community — is the exception; and the upright are described by the recitation and the prostration — the worship of the night. The verse's *laysu sawa'* — not equal — is the correction of the stereotype: the People of the Book are not the monolith of the rejection; they have the believers and the upright — the 'Abdullah bin Salams and the Asad bin 'Ubayds. And the verse is the recognition: the night-worship of the upright is the worship of the Qur'an — the *tilawah* — and the prostration — the *sujud* — the same worship of the believers. The verse's *qa'imah* — the standing — is the uprightness of the community that stood in the truth.

## Verse 114

Verse 114 says: *They believe in Allah and the Last Day, encourage good and forbid evil, and race with one another in doing good. They are ˹truly˺ among the righteous.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the description: the upright among the People of the Book believe in Allah and the Last Day, encourage good and forbid evil, and race with one another in doing good — they are truly among the righteous.

The verse completes the description of 3:113. Ibn Kathir: "'They believe in Allah and the Last Day' — in their belief in the faith and the actions: they believe in Allah and the Last Day, enjoin good, forbid evil, and hasten in good works." The verse's four qualities — the *iman billah*, the *iman bil-yawm al-akhir*, the *amr* and the *nahy*, the *sibaq* — are the same qualities of the believers of the Ummah (3:104, 3:110): the upright of the Book are described by the believers' qualities.

"They believe in Allah and the Last Day" — *yu'minuna billahi wal-yawmi al-akhiri* — they believe in Allah and the Last Day. The *iman* of the two pillars: the belief in Allah — the Oneness, the Lord — and the belief in the Last Day — the judgment, the resurrection, the recompense. Ibn Kathir: "they believe in Allah and the Last Day — and this is the law of the faith: the belief in the One God and the final judgment." The upright of the Book believed in the truth of the final revelation: the *iman* in what was sent down to the Prophet — the completion of the *iman*.

"encourage good and forbid evil" — *wa ya'muruna bil-ma'ruf wa yanhawna 'an al-munkar* — and they enjoin the right and forbid the wrong. The same two functions of 3:104 and 3:110 — the *amr bil-ma'ruf* and the *nahy 'an al-munkar*: the upright of the Book are described by the same mission of the Muslim community. Ibn Kathir: "they enjoin the good and forbid the evil." The verse's repetition of the two commands — the *amr* and the *nahy* — is the Qur'an's identification: the community of the upright is the community of the mission — the commanding and the forbidding — whether of the Muslims or the believers of the Book.

"and race with one another in doing good" — *wa yusari'una fi al-khayrat* — and they hasten in the good works. The *sibaq* — the racing — is the hastening: not the reluctant but the racing to the good. Ibn Kathir: "they hasten in the good works and are first to perform them" — the *musara'ah* — the racing — of the good: the *sabq* — the precedence — in the deeds of the righteousness. The verse's *al-khayrat* — the good works — is the comprehensive: the worship, the charity, the good; and the *yusari'un* — they race — is the readiness: they do not wait or hesitate but hasten — the *sibaq* of the good, the belief made action.

"They are ˹truly˺ among the righteous" — *ula'ika mina al-salihin* — those are among the righteous. The *salihun* — the righteous, the good, the sound — is the verdict: the upright of the Book are *mina al-salihin* — among the righteous. Ibn Kathir: "these are the righteous, the believers" — the *salah* — the soundness, the righteousness — is the quality of the upright: not the *fasiq* of 110 but the *salih* of 114. The verse's *min* — among — is the membership: they are not the whole of the Book's people but among the righteous — the *salihun* of the community.

The verse is the second half of the positive description: 113's *yatlu* — the recitation, the worship — and 114's *iman*, *amr*, *nahy*, *sibaq* — the belief, the mission, the racing. The two verses are the portrait of the believer among the People of the Book — the *qa'imah* — the standing: the worship of the night (113) and the mission of the day (114); the recitation and the prostration (113) and the belief and the good (114). Ibn Kathir's parallel (3:199): the same type of the People of the Book — the believers — who are described in 3:199 as "some of the People of the Book who believe in Allah, in what was sent down to you, and in what was sent down to them."

For the reader, the verse is the completion of the portrait of the righteous among the People of the Book. The believer is taught: the upright are described by the believer's qualities — the *iman* in Allah and the Last Day, the *amr* and the *nahy*, the *sibaq* in the good; and the upright are *mina al-salihin* — among the righteous. The verse's *sibaq* — the racing — is the readiness the believer imitates: the good deeds not as the reluctant duty but as the racing; the *musara'ah* — the hastening — the believer's habit. And the verse's fairness is the Qur'an's: the upright of the Book are not the *fasiqun*; they are the *salihun* — and the believer recognizes the righteous of the Book as the righteous.

## Verse 115

Verse 115 says: *They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of those mindful ˹of Him˺.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the guarantee: the upright will never be denied the reward for the good they have done — and Allah has perfect knowledge of those mindful of Him.

The verse is the promise of the upright's reward. Ibn Kathir: "'And whatever good they do, nothing will be rejected from them' — God will award them the best reward for their deeds, because Allah accepts the good deeds from whoever does them, as long as the person believes in Him and His messengers. 'And Allah knows well the Muttaqin' — He knows those who have Taqwa, their actions, their intention, their statements and their work, and He will give them the complete reward." The verse's *la yukfarun* — they will not be denied — is the guarantee; *wa Allahu 'alimun bil-muttaqin* — and Allah knows the righteous — is the assurance.

"They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done" — *wa ma yaf'alu min khayrin falan yukfaruhu* — and whatever good they do will never be denied, never be rejected. The *kufr* here is the denial, the ingratitude — the same root as the *kufr* of the disbelief: the denial of the good. Ibn Kathir: "nothing will be rejected from them — God will award them the best reward." The verse's *ma yaf'alu* — whatever they do — is the comprehensive: every good deed, every act of the *salih*; and the *falan yukfaruhu* — will never be denied — is the guarantee: the good is not lost, not ignored, not denied. The *kufr* of the believer's good is the impossible: the denial of the righteous's deeds is not in the divine accounting.

"And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of those mindful ˹of Him˺" — *wa Allahu 'alimun bil-muttaqin* — and Allah is knowing of the righteous, the God-fearing. The *'alim* — the knowing — is the divine knowledge: the full knowledge of the *muttaqin* — their deeds, their intentions, their states. Ibn Kathir: "He knows those who have Taqwa, their actions, their intention, their statements and their work, and He will give them the complete reward." The verse's *al-muttaqin* — the righteous, the God-fearing, the conscious — is the same *taqwa* of 3:102: the consciousness of Allah; and the *'alim* — the Knower — is the guarantee: the righteous are not denied because they are not unknown; the Knower of the hearts and the deeds gives the complete reward.

The verse's place: the *salihun* — the righteous of the Book — are promised the *la yukfar* — the non-denial — and the *'alim bil-muttaqin* — the knowledge of the divine. The two clauses are the two sides of the guarantee: the good is not denied (115a), and the righteous are known (115b) — the non-denial follows from the knowledge: the deeds are not lost because the Doer knows; the reward is complete because the Knower is complete. Ibn Kathir's parallel: 3:199 — "So whatever good they do, they will not be denied it" — the same type of 3:113–115 is the same believers of 199.

For the reader, the verse is the guarantee of the good and the knowledge of the divine. The believer is taught: the good is never denied — the *yukfar* of the righteous's deeds is the impossible; and the righteous are known — the *'alim bil-muttaqin* is the assurance. The verse's *falan yukfaruhu* — never denied — is the believer's confidence: the good works are not lost, not overlooked, not unrewarded; the accounting of the good is the exact. And the verse's *'alim* — the Knower — is the believer's security: the hearts, the intentions, the hidden deeds are known — and the known are rewarded. The verse's *muttaqin* — the righteous — is the believer's program: the *taqwa* — the consciousness of Allah — and its deed — the good — that is never denied and always known.

## Verse 116

Verse 116 says: *Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.*

In simple terms, the verse states the futility of the disbelievers' worldly strength: neither their wealth nor their children will benefit them against Allah — they will be the residents of the Fire, there forever.

The verse returns to the disbelievers' condition after the portrait of the believing People of the Book (113–115). Ibn Kathir: "Beware, the wealth and children of the disbelievers do not avail them in the least — this is such that they will not save them from the punishment of Allah." The verse's *lan tughni* — will not avail — is the futility; *min Allahi shay'an* — against Allah, in the least — is the absolute; and *ashab al-nar* — the companions of the Fire — is the verdict.

"neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah" — *lan tughni 'anhum amwaluhum wa la awladuhum min Allahi shay'an* — their wealth and children will not avail them against Allah in the least. The *amwal* — the wealth — and the *awlad* — the children — are the two worldly strengths: the resources and the lineage, the property and the family. Al-Jalalayn: "the wealth with which they may ransom themselves, or the children who they may seek help from" — neither the payment of the ransom nor the assistance of the children. Ibn Kathir: "their wealth and children will not benefit them — they will not save them from the punishment of Allah." The verse's *min Allahi shay'an* — against Allah, anything — is the absolute: in the presence of the divine justice, the worldly strengths are nothing; the *tughni* — the avail — is the denial: the wealth of the disbelievers cannot redeem, the children of the disbelievers cannot save.

"It is they who will be the residents of the Fire" — *ula'ika ashabu al-nar* — those are the companions of the Fire. The *ashab al-nar* — the companions, the people of the Fire — is the same verdict of 2:217, 3:10, and the *hum fiha khalidun* — they will abide in it forever: the companions of the Fire are the permanent. The verse's *hum* — they — is the identity: the disbelievers — the ones of the *kufr* — are the companions of the Fire; and the *ashab* — the companions — is the dwelling: the Fire is the abode, not the visitation.

"They will be there forever" — *wa hum fiha khalidun* — and they will abide in it forever. The *khulud* — the permanence, the abiding — is the same word of 3:107: the abiding of the believers in the mercy and the abiding of the deniers in the Fire — the two permanences of the surah's parallel verdicts. Ibn Kathir: "they will abide in the Fire forever." The verse's *fiha* — in it — is the dwelling of the Fire; and the *khalidun* — abiding — is the permanence: the residents of the Fire are not the visitors; they are the dwellers.

The verse's parallels: 3:10 — "Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire" — the same statement in the opening of the surah's section of the disbelievers; and the same in 2:217, 58:17, 4:18. The *amwal* and the *awlad* of the disbelievers are the repeated futility: the strengths of the world do not avail against Allah. The *wud* — the fuel — of the Fire (3:10) is the same *ashab* — the companions — of 116: the disbelievers are not the visitors but the fuel — the *waqid* — the firewood of the Fire.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's critique of the worldly security. The believer is taught: the wealth and the children — the two human strengths — do not avail the disbeliever against Allah; the *amwal* of the world and the *awlad* of the family are not the currency of the accounting. The verse's *min Allahi shay'an* — against Allah in the least — is the absolute: no ransom, no help, no resource — the *tughni* of the world's strengths is the denial. And the verse's *ashab al-nar* — the companions of the Fire — with the *khalidun* — abiding forever — is the verdict: the disbelievers are the people of the Fire. The believer's lesson: the trust of the world's strengths is the trust of the disbelievers' — the *amwal* and the *awlad* are the means of the world, and the *iman* and the *'amal* — the faith and the deed — are the means of the Hereafter.

## Verse 117

Verse 117 says: *The good they do in this worldly life is like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind, destroying it ˹completely˺. Allah never wronged them, but they wronged themselves.*

In simple terms, the verse gives the parable: the good the disbelievers do in this worldly life is like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind and destroyed completely — Allah never wronged them; they wronged themselves.

The verse is the parable of the futile expenditure. Ibn Kathir: "'The example of what they spend in this life' — the disbelievers' spending in this life — 'is like the example of a freezing wind that strikes the crops of a people who wronged themselves and destroyed them' — meaning, their expenditure in the worldly life is not built on firm foundations; it will not benefit them in the Hereafter." The verse's *mathal* — the example, the parable — is the picture: the *sirr* — the bitter, freezing wind — and the *harath* — the harvest — and the *zulm* — the wrong — of the self.

"The good they do in this worldly life" — *mathalu ma yunfiquna fi hadhihi al-hayati al-dunya* — the example of what they spend in this worldly life. The *yunfiqun* — they spend — is the expenditure: the charity, the generosity, the good of the disbelievers — the *sadaqat* of the world; and the *fi hadhihi al-hayat al-dunya* — in this worldly life — is the sphere: the good of the world, not the good of the Hereafter. Ibn Kathir: "their expenditure in the worldly life is not built on firm foundations; it will not benefit them in the Hereafter." The verse's *ma yunfiqun* — what they spend — is the comprehensive of the disbelievers' giving: the charity of the world, the good of the life — the deeds without the foundation.

"is like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind, destroying it ˹completely˺" — *ka mathali rihin fiha sirrun asabat haratha qawmin zalamu anfusahum fa'ahlakat'hu* — like the example of a wind in which is *sirr* that struck the harvest of a people who wronged themselves and destroyed it. The *sirr* — the freezing cold, the frigid, the severe cold — is the key word of the verse. Ibn Kathir: "'sirr' — a frigid, freezing wind" — from Ibn 'Abbas, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, Qatadah, Ad-Dahhak, and Ar-Rabi' bin Anas: the *sirr* is the severe cold — the wind that freezes and burns; also from Ibn 'Abbas and Mujahid: "a fire" — the *sirr* is the fire/wind that burns — and the two interpretations agree: the extreme cold burns like the fire, destroying the harvest. The *harath* — the harvest, the crops — is the fruits of the effort: the produce that was struck; and *zalamu anfusahum* — they wronged themselves — is the state: the *zulm* — the wrong — of the owners of the harvest. The *fahlakat* — destroyed completely — is the ruin: the expediture of the disbelievers is the ruin; the good of the world is the wasted.

"It is not Allah who wrongs them, but it is they who wrong themselves" — *wa ma zalamahum Allahu wa lakin anfusahum yazlimun* — and Allah did not wrong them, but they wrong themselves. The verse's *ma zalamahum Allah* — Allah did not wrong them — is the exoneration of the divine; and *lakin anfusahum yazlimun* — but they wrong themselves — is the identification of the wrongdoer. Ibn Kathir: "Allah did not wrong them — the wind, the destruction, the loss — but they wronged themselves: their *zulm* — their disbelief, their evil, their rebellion — is the cause of the ruin." The verse's *zalamu anfusahum* — they wronged themselves — is the same of 3:117: the *zulm* of the disbelievers is the self-wrong; and the *ma zalamahum Allah* is the divine justice: the Lord did not strike the harvest of the innocent; the *zulm* of the self is the cause of the destruction.

The verse's parable, in the interpretation: the *sirr* — the bitter wind — is the calamity that strikes the *harath* — the harvest — of the people who wronged themselves: the *sadaqah* of the disbelievers — their charity, their generosity, their good — is destroyed by the *zulm* of their state: the *kufr* and the sin that strip the deeds' reward. The wind of the *sirr* is the divine permission; the *zulm* of the self is the cause; and the destruction of the harvest is the conclusion. The extremes — the cold and the fire — are the same: the *sirr* that burns by the cold is the *sirr* that burns by the fire — the destruction of the harvest in both.

For the reader, the verse is the parable of the futility without the foundation. The believer is taught: the *sadaqah* and the good of the disbeliever are like the harvest struck by the bitter wind — they are destroyed, not because Allah is unjust but because the *zulm* of the self — the disbelief, the rebellion — strips the reward. The verse's *sirr* — the bitter — is the divine permission; the *zalamu anfusahum* — they wronged themselves — is the cause. And the verse's *ma zalamahum Allah* — Allah never wronged them — is the justice: the Lord of the harvest is not the destroyer; the self's wrong is the destruction. The believer's lesson: the good of the faith and the *taqwa* — the foundation — is the harvest that stands; the good of the world without the foundation is the harvest struck by the wind. The *zulm* — the wrong — is the self's, and the justice is the divine.

## Verse 118

Verse 118 says: *O believers! Do not associate closely with others who would not miss a chance to harm you. Their only desire is to see you suffer. Their prejudice has become evident from what they say—and what their hearts hide is far worse. We have made Our revelations clear to you, if only you understood.*

In simple terms, the verse warns the believers: do not associate closely with those outside the faith — they would not miss a chance to harm you, their only desire is to see you suffer, their prejudice shows in what they say, and what their hearts hide is far worse; the revelations have been made clear if you understood.

The verse turns from the condition of the People of the Book (110–117) to the believers' relations with them. Ibn Kathir: "'O you who believe! Take not as your bitanah (advisors, helpers, and intimate friends) those outside your religion' — this is one of the greatest issues; Allah orders the believers not to take the supporters and the companions from among the disbelievers and the hypocrites, for they will not cease their assistance in the disbelievers' favor and will cooperate with them against the believers." The verse's *bitanah* — the intimates, the inner circle — is the key: the trusted advisors of the secrets, the allies of the heart; and the *min dunikum* — outside your circle — is the boundary.

"Do not associate closely with others who would not miss a chance to harm you" — *la tattakhidhu bitanatan min dunikum la ya'lunakum khabala* — do not take intimates from outside yourselves; they will not cease [to cause] you corruption. The *bitanah* — the inner, the intimate, the confidant — is the one of the secrets: the advisor, the companion of the trust; the *min dunikum* — from besides you, outside your circle — is the boundary: the one outside the community of the believers. Al-Jalalayn: "Jews, Christians, and the hypocrites — do not take them as counselors or intimate friends, and do not share your secret affairs with them." The *khabal* — the corruption, the harm, the ruin — is the continuous effort: the *la ya'lun* — they do not cease — is the continuous. Ibn Kathir: "they will not cease their assistance in the disbelievers' favor and will cooperate with them against the believers." Al-Jalalayn: "they are keen on your ruin and corruption."

"Their only desire is to see you suffer" — *waddu ma 'anittum* — they love what troubles you, what distresses you. The *wudd* — the love — of the *'anat* — the hardship, the distress — is the second: the outsiders love the believers' hardship; the *'anat* — toils, troubles — is the suffering, and the *wadd* — the love — is the desire. Ibn Kathir: "they love your hardship and distress." The verse's *'anittum* — you are distressed — is the believers' trial; the *waddu* — they love — is the outsiders' desire: the enemies love to see the believers harmed.

"Their prejudice has become evident from what they say—and what their hearts hide is far worse" — *qad badati al-baghda'u min afwahihim wa ma tukhfi suduruhum akbar* — hatred has appeared from their mouths, and what their breasts hide is greater. The *baghda'* — the hatred, the enmity, the prejudice — is the third: *badat* — appeared — from the mouths: the hatred of the speech. Ibn Kathir: "hatred has appeared from their mouths — their words, their insults, their mockery of your religion." The *sudur* — the breasts, the hearts — is the depth: the *tukhfi* — they conceal — is the hidden; and the *akbar* — greater, far worse — is the comparison: "what their breasts hide is greater — the hatred, the enmity, the deception — than what their mouths show." The verse's *akbar* — the greater — is the warning: the surface of the speech is the lesser; the depth of the hearts is the greater; the *baghda'* of the mouths is the sign, and the *baghda'* of the breasts is the reality.

"We have made Our revelations clear to you, if only you understood" — *qad bayyanna lakum al-ayati in kuntum ta'qilun* — We have made clear to you the signs, if you are reasonable, if you understand. The *tabyin* — the clarification — is the method: the signs of the *bitanah* — the prohibition, the harms, the comparison — are the clarified; and the *ta'qil* — the understanding — is the condition: the signs are clear to the ones who reason. Ibn Kathir: "We have explained the signs to you, if you understand." The verse's *in kuntum ta'qilun* — if you are reasonable — is the appeal: the warning is the addressed; the *'aql* — the reason — is the response.

The hadith and report of Ibn Kathir: Al-Bukhari and An-Nasa'i record Abu Sa'id — "Every Prophet and Khalifah has two types of *bitanah* — an advisor commanding him good and an advisor commanding him evil." The hadith is the illustration: the *bitanah* of the good and the *bitanah* of the evil — the believers are to take the *bitanah* of the good. And Ibn Abi Hatim records from Ibn Abi Ad-Dahqanah: 'Umar refused the Christian scribe of Hirah — "I would then be taking advisors from among the disbelievers" — after he heard this verse. The report is the application: the *bitanah* of the believer — the scribe, the advisor, the confidant — is the believer; and the *khabal* of the outsider — the corruption, the harm — is the avoided.

For the reader, the verse is the Qur'an's most direct command of the community's boundaries. The believer is taught: the *bitanah* — the inner circle, the trusted advisors, the confidants of the secrets — are to be of the believers; the outsider — the one outside the circle — is not the confident of the community. The reasons are the three harms: the *khabal* — the never-ceasing corruption; the *wadd* — the love of the distress; the *baghda'* — the prejudice of the mouths and the greater of the breasts. And the verse's *bayyanna* — the clarification — with the *ta'qilun* — the understanding — is the believer's responsibility: the warning is clear, and the reasoning is the response. The verse's practical: the believers' advisors, the community's secrets, the *bitanah* of the faith — the *bitanah* of the good, per Abu Sa'id's hadith — and the avoidance of the *bitanah* of the evil — per 'Umar's report: the believer's inner circle is the believer's.

## Verse 119

Verse 119 says: *Here you are! You love them but they do not love you, and you believe in all Scriptures. When they meet you they say, “We believe.” But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹May you˺ die of your rage!” Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart.*

In simple terms, the verse exposes the imbalance: here you are — you love them but they do not love you, and you believe in all the Scriptures; when they meet you they say, "We believe," but alone they bite their fingertips in rage; say, "May you die of your rage!" — Allah knows best what is hidden in the heart.

The verse is the exposure of the relationship's imbalance. Ibn Kathir: "Allah states that the believers love the People of the Book because of their kinship and their pretended friendship, and in their times the believers treated them with friendliness. But the People of the Book do not love the believers, because they oppose their religion." The verse's *ha antum* — here you are — is the rhetorical introduction; the *tuhibbunahum* — you love them — and the *la yuhibbunakum* — they do not love you — is the imbalance; the *tu'minun bil-kitab kullihi* — you believe in all the Books — is the believers' openness; the *idha khalaw 'addu al-anamil* — when alone they bite the fingertips — is the rage's display; and the *mutu bi ghayzikum* — may you die in your rage — is the return.

"Here you are! You love them but they do not love you" — *ha antum ula'i tuhibbunahum wa la yuhibbunakum* — here you are, you love them and they do not love you. The *ha antum* — here you are, behold — is the rhetorical presentation of the imbalance: the verse's *tuhibbun* — you love — is the believers' affection — the *mawaddah* — of the kinship and the friendship; and the *la yuhibbunakum* — they do not love you — is the denial. Ibn Kathir: "the believers love them because of kinship and pretended friendship, but they do not love the believers because they oppose the religion." The imbalance of 119a is the exposure: the *mawaddah* of the believers is the unrequited; the affection that is not returned.

"and you believe in all Scriptures" — *wa tu'minuna bil-kitabi kullihi* — and you believe in all the Scripture. The *tu'minun bil-kitab kullihi* — you believe in all the Books — is the believers' comprehensive faith: the *iman* in the books of Allah — the Torah of Moses, the Gospel of Jesus, the Psalms of Dawud, the Qur'an of Muhammad — the whole of the revelation. Ibn Kathir: "you believe in the Book that was sent down to you and to them, and you believe in the Torah, the Gospel, and the Qur'an." And the context of the imbalance: the believers believe in the whole revelation, and the outsiders do not return the faith — they do not believe in the believers' Book. Al-Jalalayn: "you believe in the whole Scripture — or all the Books — and they do not believe in yours." The imbalance of 119b: the believers' openness to the whole revelation and the outsiders' closure to the believers' — the comprehensiveness and the rejection.

"When they meet you they say, "We believe."" — *wa idha laqukum qalu amanna* — and when they meet you, they say "we believe." The *qalu amanna* — they say "we believe" — is the hypocrisy's surface: the profession of the faith in the meeting, the *aman* — the belief — of the tongue. Ibn Kathir: "when they meet you, they say 'we believe' — they pretend and lie." The verse's *idha laqukum* — when they meet you — is the occasion: the meeting, the encounter; and the *qalu amanna* — they say "we believe" — is the display: the profession of the hypocrites — the *aman* of the meeting, not the *aman* of the heart.

"But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage" — *wa idha khalaw 'addu 'alaykum al-anamila mina al-ghayz* — and when they are alone, they bite at you the fingertips from the rage. The *khalaw* — they are alone — is the occasion: the privacy, the absence of the believers; and the *'addu al-anamil* — they bite the fingertips — is the display of the *ghayz* — the rage, the fury: the gesture of the extreme anger. Ibn Kathir: "when they are alone, they bite their fingers because of the rage and the fury against you." Al-Jalalayn: "they bite their fingertips in extreme rage." The verse's *min al-ghayz* — from the rage — is the cause: the *ghayz* — the fury — is the rage of the *baghda'* — the hatred, the prejudice — of the hearts (118).

"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "˹May you˺ die of your rage!"" — *qul mutu bi ghayzikum* — say: may you die in your rage! The *qul* — say — is the address to the Prophet; the *mutu* — die — is the command: the *mut* — the die — of the rage; and the *ghayz* — the rage — is the thing. Al-Jalalayn: "remain in this state until you die — for you will never see what will please you; they will not be able to defeat you." The verse's *mutu* — die — is not the curse of the death but the command of the permanence: the rage that is the state — the *ghayz* of the enemies — is the rage they might die in; and the believers' response is the divine command: the *mutu bi ghayzikum* — the dismissal of the rage, the return of the fury.

"Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart" — *inna Allaha 'alimun bi dhati al-sudur* — indeed, Allah is knowing of what is in the breasts. The *'alimun bi dhati al-sudur* — the knower of the breasts' contents — is the divine knowledge: the *sudur* — the breasts, the hearts — and the *dhat* — the contents, the inner — are known. Ibn Kathir: "Allah knows what is in the hearts — the enmity, the hatred, the hypocrisy." The verse's *'alim* — the knower — is the assurance: the *baghda'* of the breasts (118) is not hidden from the divine; the *ghayz* of the hearts is the known — and the known is the recompensed.

For the reader, the verse is the exposure of the imbalance and the believers' instruction. The believer is taught: the love of the believers is the unrequited; the faith of the believers is the comprehensive — the *iman* of the whole revelation — and the outsiders' is the rejection; the "we believe" of the meeting is the hypocrisy's surface, and the rage of the alone is the reality. And the verse's "May you die of your rage" — is the believers' confidence: the rage of the enemies does not prevail; the *ghayz* of the fury is the enemies' own. And the "Allah knows best what is hidden in the heart" — the knowledge of the hearts — is the believers' security: the *baghda'* that is hidden is known; the known is the recompensed. The believer's program: the love of the faith — not the unrequited love of the enemies — and the *iman* of the whole revelation — not the partial — and the trust in the divine knowledge.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 120

Verse 120 says: *When you ˹believers˺ are touched with good, they grieve; but when you are afflicted with evil, they rejoice. ˹Yet,˺ if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺, their schemes will not harm you in the least. Surely Allah is Fully Aware of what they do.*

In simple terms, the verse describes the enemies' reactions: they grieve when good touches you and rejoice when evil afflicts you — but if you are patient and mindful of Allah, their schemes will not harm you in the least; Allah is fully aware of what they do.

The verse concludes the section of the relations with the People of the Book. Ibn Kathir: "'If a good befalls you, it grieves them' — the enemies are hurt when good comes to the believers; 'but if some evil overtakes you, they rejoice at it' — the enemies rejoice. 'Yet if you are patient and have Taqwa, their plotting will not harm you at all' — the believers are promised the protection of the divine." The verse's *in tamsaskum hasanah* — if good touches you — is the first; *tasu'hum* — it grieves them — is the reaction; *wa in tusibkum sayyi'ah* — if evil strikes you — is the second; *yafrahu biha* — they rejoice — is the reaction; and the *tasbiru wa tattaqu* — the patience and the taqwa — with the *kayd* — the scheming, the plotting — and the *ihatah* — the encompassing awareness — is the resolution.

"When you ˹believers˺ are touched with good, they grieve" — *in tamsaskum hasanatun tasu'hum* — if good touches you, it grieves them. The *hasanah* — the good — is the good of the believers: the success, the victory, the blessing; and the *tasu'* — it grieves, it harms — is the enemies' grief: the *su'* — the distress — of the good's arrival. Ibn Kathir: "the enemies are hurt when the good comes to the believers." The verse's *tamsaskum* — touches you — is the contact: the *hasanah* — the good — of the believers' affairs; and the *saw'* — the grief — of the enemies is the reverse: the good of the believers is the distress of the enemies.

"but when you are afflicted with evil, they rejoice" — *wa in tusibkum sayyi'atun yafrahu biha* — and if evil strikes you, they rejoice at it. The *sayyi'ah* — the evil, the bad — is the misfortune: the harm, the loss, the defeat; and the *yafrahu* — they rejoice — is the celebration: the enemies' joy at the believers' distress. Ibn Kathir: "the enemies rejoice when evil touches the believers." The verse's pair — the *hasanah* and the *sayyi'ah* — is the mirror: the good of the believers and the evil of the believers — the grief and the rejoicing of the enemies — the *saw'* and the *fara'a* — the distress and the joy — of the outsiders.

"˹Yet,˺ if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺, their schemes will not harm you in the least" — *wa in tasbiru wa tattaqu la yadurrukum kayduhum shay'an* — and if you are patient and have Taqwa, their scheming will not harm you in the least. The *sabr* — the patience — and the *taqwa* — the consciousness — are the two protections; the *kayd* — the scheming, the craft, the cunning — is the enemies' effort; and the *la yadurrukum shay'an* — will not harm you in the least — is the protection. Ibn Kathir: "the believers are promised: if they are patient and have Taqwa, the plotting of the enemies will not harm them." Al-Jalalayn: "their cunning and their plot will not harm you in the least." The verse's *sabr* — the patience — is the endurance: the good and the evil — the *hasanah* and the *sayyi'ah* — are met with the patience; and the *taqwa* — the consciousness of Allah — is the trust: the *kayd* of the enemies is the *kayd* of the created, and the protection is the divine.

"Surely Allah is Fully Aware of what they do" — *inna Allaha bima ya'maluna muhit* — indeed, Allah encompasses what they do. The *ihatah* — the encompassing — is the divine: the *muhit* — the surrounding, the full awareness — of their deeds. Ibn Kathir: "Allah encompasses their deeds — He knows them and He is able to recompense them." The verse's *bima ya'malun* — with what they do — is the deeds of the enemies: the *kayd* — the scheming, the *baghda'* — the prejudice, the *ghayz* — the rage — all are known; and the *muhit* — the encompassed — is the divine: the deeds are not beyond the awareness and the recompense.

For the reader, the verse is the conclusion of the believers' protection and the enemies' futility. The believer is taught: the enemies' reactions are the mirror — the grief at the good and the joy at the evil — and the believers' two protections are the *sabr* and the *taqwa* — the patience and the consciousness — and the *kayd* — the scheming — will not harm them. The verse's *la yadurrukum shay'an* — will not harm them in the least — is the absolute: the *kayd* of the enemies is the *kayd* of the created, and the *ihatah* of the divine is the full awareness. The believer's program: the *sabr* of the good and the bad, the *taqwa* of the conscious, and the trust in the *muhit* — the encompassed — the known and the recompensed. The verse's resolution: the believers' affairs — the good, the evil, the scheming — are all in the divine awareness; and the *sabr* and the *taqwa* are the believers' keys — the patience and the consciousness that make the enemies' scheming the harmless.
