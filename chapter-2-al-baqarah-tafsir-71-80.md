# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 71–80

These ten verses close one story and open a diagnosis. The cow is finally slaughtered, grudgingly, and the miracle it was commanded for is performed: a murdered man is struck with a piece of it and comes back to life, naming his killer, as a demonstration of the Resurrection itself (71–73). And then comes the verdict — after all of that, their hearts hardened until they were harder than stone, and the surah says something almost unbearable: rock is more responsive to its Creator than they were (74). From there the surah turns to the believers and tells them not to expect faithfulness from people who distort the word knowingly, conspire in private, and forget that nothing is hidden (75–77). And it ends with a complete anatomy of how a religious community rots: lay people who know only wishful thinking, leaders who forge scripture for profit, and a comfortable eschatology that shrinks the Fire to a few days (78–80). The whole passage is a case study, and the case study is not only about them.

---

## Verse 71

**"He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields; wholesome and without blemish.’” They said, “Now you have come with the truth.” Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly!"**

In simple terms: once the description was complete they acknowledged it, and they slaughtered the cow — but with such reluctance that they very nearly did not do it at all.

*Qalu al-ana ji'ta bi-al-haqq* — "they said: now you have come with the truth." That word *al-ana*, "now," is the problem in this sentence, and the commentators did not pretend otherwise. Read plainly, it implies that before this, Moses had *not* come with the truth — which is a serious thing to say to a prophet who has conveyed four successive revelations on the same subject, each of them true. The tradition handled it two ways. The more common reading is that they meant: now you have brought the complete, decisive clarification — the description is now full and unambiguous, so there is nothing left to ask about. On that reading *al-haqq* means the settled and fully specified matter rather than truth as opposed to falsehood. But a number of commentators, including al-Razi and al-Qurtubi, censured the statement outright, holding that whoever speaks to a prophet as though his earlier disclosures were not the truth has said something blameworthy, since every one of those answers was from Allah.

The tension is worth leaving visible, because it shows something real about them. What they meant, and what they said, are not the same thing. What they meant was "now you have told us what we can act on." What they said was "at last you are telling the truth." That gap between an acceptable intention and a careless expression is where most of our speech failures live.

There is a second observation that matters more. Notice when they finally acted. They acted the moment the specification was complete — which means the obstacle was never the vagueness of the command. Any cow would have done at verse 67. They had spent three rounds of questions manufacturing a difficulty that did not exist, and the instant it disappeared they obeyed. That tells you what the questions were for.

*Fa-dhabahuha wa ma kadu yaf'alun* — "so they slaughtered her, though they almost did not do it." The feminine pronoun is because *baqarah* is a feminine noun. The clause *ma kadu yaf'alun* is the crux: *kada* means "to be near to," and with the negation the natural sense is "they were close to not doing it" — they did it, but barely, and with the worst possible grace. There is a genuine grammatical discussion about where the negation attaches in constructions with *kada*, and the grammarians examined it closely; the overwhelming majority reading, and the one every translation follows, is that they performed the act with extreme reluctance, at the very edge of refusing.

So the story of the cow ends with an obedience that was technically complete and spiritually almost worthless. They did the thing. They found the animal, they paid for it, they slaughtered it. And the surah records the manner as a defect, because the manner is part of the deed.

That is the most useful thing in this verse for a reader today, and it is uncomfortable. Most religious failure is not apostasy. It is grudging compliance — the obligation performed at the last possible moment, with resentment, at the minimum standard, with the whole heart somewhere else. The Qur'an describes the same posture in the hypocrites: "And when they stand for prayer, they stand lazily, showing off to the people and not remembering Allah except a little" (4:142). And the Prophet ﷺ described it precisely, in the hadith recorded by Muslim from Anas: "That is the prayer of the hypocrite: he sits watching the sun until it is between the horns of Satan, then he stands and pecks four, remembering Allah only a little."

Two applications follow directly.

The first is for anyone who recognises in himself the "almost didn't do it" shape of obedience. The prayer prayed at the very edge of its time, the charity given after being asked three times, the apology extracted rather than offered, the obligation discharged with a visible sigh. In each of these the act was done, and in each of them something was withheld that the act was supposed to carry. The surah names the withholding. A deed performed while the will is holding its nose is a deed that has not yet reached the heart, and the gap between the two is exactly where the work is.

The second is the hope hidden in the verse, which should not be missed. They almost did not do it — and they did it. The threshold was crossed. Whatever else is true of that grudging slaughter, it is the thing that unlocked the miracle of the next two verses, the revival of a dead man and the resolution of a murder that had divided them. Immense goods have hung on far worse obedience than theirs. The person who drags himself to the act, resentfully, minimally, at the last moment, is still the person who did it — and the door that opens next opens for him too.

## Verse 72

**"˹This is˺ when a man was killed and you disputed who the killer was, but Allah revealed what you concealed."**

In simple terms: they had killed someone and then argued among themselves, each pushing the blame onto the other, while Allah was in the process of exposing what they were concealing.

*Wa idh qataltum nafsan* — "and when you killed a soul." Note the address: *qataltum*, "you" plural. Not "some of you." And that is worth pausing over, because the surah has been scrupulous elsewhere about attributing acts to subsets rather than to whole peoples — "those among you who transgressed" at verse 65, "those who wronged" at verse 59, "a party of them" at verse 75. Here the attribution is to the community as such.

Why? The commentators' answer is that the whole community was implicated, not in the killing but in what followed: the concealment and the mutual recrimination. A murder one person commits and a whole town covers up belongs to the whole town. The surah is precise about this throughout, and the precision runs in both directions — it refuses to blame a people for the crimes of their ancestors (2:134, 2:141), and it does hold a community accountable for a wrong it had the power to surface and chose instead to bury.

*Fa-iddara'tum fiha* — "and you disputed over it." The verb is one of the most vivid in the surah. It comes from a root meaning to push away, and the form indicates reciprocal action: each of them pushing the matter away from himself onto someone else. The word picture is not of people calmly disagreeing but of people shoving an accusation back and forth, each refusing to let it land. Every reader has seen this happen in a family, a workplace, or a community after something goes wrong, and the Qur'an named it in a single word fourteen centuries ago.

*Wa Allahu mukhrijun ma kuntum taktumun* — "and Allah was bringing out what you were concealing." *Mukhrij* is the active participle, and it carries a continuous or emphatic sense: Allah is the One who brings out, who extracts into the open. And *taktumun* is from the same root as the *kitman* — the concealment of the truth — that was condemned at verse 42. The two verses belong together: concealing what you know is one of this surah's standing charges, and here it takes the form of a buried murder.

The theological claim is simple and total: nothing is hidden. The Qur'an says it in every register — "He knows what is secret and what is more hidden" (20:7); "And with Him are the keys of the unseen; none knows them except Him" (6:59); and in the most intimate form: "And We have already created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein" (50:16). The killer in this story was unknown to the community and fully known to Allah, and the miracle of the next verse exists precisely to drag the hidden thing into daylight.

Now the applications, and there are three.

The first is about concealed wrong. Every person carries something that has never surfaced — the thing done in private, the money taken quietly, the lie that was never corrected because nobody ever asked, the harm nobody traced back. The instinct is to assume that because it is unknown to people it is safely out of the account. This verse is the correction, and it is stated as a fact about the structure of the world rather than as a threat: Allah is *mukhrij*, the One who brings out. Exposure is not a risk. It is the eventual condition — sometimes in this life, always in the next, and in either case the only escape from it is the one the Qur'an offers, which is to bring it out yourself.

The second is about the reflex of *iddara'tum*. Watch what a group does in the first hour after a failure. Do they establish what happened, or do they establish whose fault it was not? The shoving match described in this verse is the default human response, and the Qur'an records it as part of the problem rather than as an understandable reaction to it. The person who takes his own share first, without waiting to see whether the blame will be assigned to him, is doing something rare and immediately recognisable.

And the third is the hardest and the most ordinary. What are you hiding? Not necessarily a crime — often it is smaller than that and more persistent: the resentment that has never been named, the motive you would be ashamed to state aloud, the private contempt you feel for someone you praise in public. The verse says of such things that they are being brought out. The question it leaves with the reader is whether you will do the bringing, or wait.

## Verse 73

**"So We instructed, “Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs so that you may understand."**

In simple terms: Allah told them to strike the murdered man with a piece of the slaughtered cow; he came back to life. And the verse states the purpose plainly — this is how Allah resurrects the dead, and He is showing you His signs so that you will use your reason.

*Fa-qulna idribuhu bi-ba'diha* — "so We said: strike him with part of it." Which part? The commentators transmitted several opinions — the tongue, the tail, a thigh bone, a piece of the flesh — and they could not settle it. That disagreement is instructive rather than unfortunate, because the text is deliberately indefinite. *Ba'd* means "a part," any part. Had a specific organ been named, someone would eventually have attributed the revival to the organ. By leaving it open, the verse strips the miracle of every secondary cause and leaves only Allah.

And there is a quiet irony here that the surah has earned. For three verses these people demanded ever finer specifications — the age of the cow, its colour, its exact description. They got what they asked for, and it made everything harder. Now, at the moment of the miracle itself, they are given no specification at all: strike him with *a part* of it. Having insisted on precision when it was unnecessary, they are asked to act without it when it might have seemed essential. The test is the same in both directions, and it is the same test: obey what you have been given and stop negotiating.

*Kadhalika yuhyi Allahu al-mawta* — "thus does Allah give life to the dead." This is the verse's centre of gravity. The word *kadhalika*, "in this manner / thus," links a single historical event to a universal principle. The revival of this one man is not a curiosity; it is a demonstration of a standing rule. The Qur'an makes the same move with the other revivals it records — the people struck by the thunderbolt and raised (2:56), the sleepers of the cave (18:19, 18:25), the man who died for a hundred years (2:259), the birds of Abraham (2:260), the dead raised by Jesus with Allah's permission (3:49, 5:110). Each is a worked instance of the thing that is denied.

This matters because the standard objection to resurrection is empirical rather than logical — "Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated?" (36:78), and "Does man think that We will not assemble his bones?" (75:3). The Qur'an answers on two fronts. Reason: "He who produced them the first time will give them life" (36:79), and "Is He who created the heavens and the earth unable to create the likes of them? Yes, indeed He is the Knowing Creator" (36:81). And demonstration: here it is, done before witnesses. A community that had seen a dead man walk and name his killer had no remaining excuse for doubting the greater rising.

*Wa yurikum ayatihi la'allakum ta'qilun* — "and He shows you His signs so that you may reason." Note what the signs are for. They are not meant to overwhelm the intellect into surrender; they are meant to be *reasoned about*. *'Aqala* is to use the mind, to bind one thing to another and draw the conclusion — and it is the same word the surah will use at the very end of the cow passage in verse 75, where the failure is described as distorting the word *after having understood it*. The Qur'an's ideal is not credulity. It is a person who sees a sign and draws the inference.

For practice, three things carry.

The first is the deliberate vagueness of the command, which is a standing feature of the religious life. Most instructions we receive are not fully specified, and the waiting for a specification that is not going to arrive is one of the most successful forms of disobedience there is. Strike him with a part of it. Give charity. Establish the prayer. The details are worked out in the doing, and the doing is what was asked for.

The second is the evidential weight of the resurrection examples. A person who finds the afterlife hard to believe is not being asked to accept something without support. The Qur'an's method is to point at instances — a revived corpse, a hundred-year sleep, a dead heart brought back to life by remembrance — and ask whether the principle has ever actually been violated. It has not.

And the third, which is the one this verse most wants from a reader, is the inference. A dead man was brought back and identified his killer in front of a community that had been shoving the blame around for days. The conclusion the surah invites is not admiration for a miracle. It is: the things I have buried are not lost; there is a day on which they are produced; and the One who can bring a corpse back to testify can certainly bring me back to answer. That is what *la'allakum ta'qilun* is asking the reader to do.

## Verse 74

**"Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water; while others are humbled in awe of Allah. And Allah is never unaware of what you do."**

In simple terms: after everything they had witnessed, their hearts hardened until they were like rock — or harder — because rock at least yields water and falls in awe of Allah, while these hearts yielded nothing. And Allah is not unaware of anything they did.

*Thumma qasat qulubukum min ba'di dhalika* — "then your hearts hardened after that." The verb is from the root that gives *qaswah*, hardness, and it is used throughout the Qur'an for the heart that has lost its responsiveness (5:13, 6:43, 22:53, 39:22, 57:16). Note *min ba'di dhalika* — after that. After the sea. After the manna. After the covenant beneath the mountain. After a dead man walking and naming a murderer.

That is the single most important and most counter-intuitive fact in this verse: **the hardening did not come from a shortage of signs. It came from an excess of them.** The heart was not starved; it was overexposed. Sign after sign arrived, was received without response, and each unresponded-to sign left the heart a little less able to respond to the next one. This is the mechanism the surah has been describing since verse 7, and this is the verse that names it.

It is also the mechanism the Qur'an names as a warning to the believers, and the warning is explicit. "Has the time not come for those who have believed that their hearts should become humbly submissive at the remembrance of Allah and what has come down of the truth? And let them not be like those who were given the Scripture before, and a long period passed over them, so their hearts hardened; and many of them are defiantly disobedient" (57:16). That verse is addressed to Muslims, about this exact failure, and it names the cause in the same terms: a long period passed over them. Time plus exposure plus non-response equals hardness.

The tradition described the process at the level of the individual too. In the hadith recorded by al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah from Abu Hurayrah, the Prophet ﷺ said: "When the servant commits a sin, a black spot is placed upon his heart. If he desists and seeks forgiveness, his heart is polished. But if he returns, it increases until it covers his heart — and that is the 'ran' which Allah mentioned: 'No! Rather, the stain has covered their hearts of that which they were earning'" (83:14). The narration is graded *hasan* rather than *sahih* by some authorities, and it should be cited with that qualification; but it describes exactly the process this verse names, and the same process is attested in stronger narrations about the heart's sickness.

*Fa-hiya ka-al-hijarati aw ashaddu qaswatan* — "they are like stones, or even harder." The word *aw* here is generally read as "rather" — not expressing doubt about whether they are like stone, but escalating: rather, harder still.

Then three kinds of stone, in ascending order of responsiveness. *Wa inna mina al-hijarati la-ma yatafajjaru minhu al-anharu* — some stones from which rivers burst forth. Note the verb: *yatafajjar*, to gush out. It is the same root used thirteen verses earlier, at 2:60, for the twelve springs bursting from the struck rock. The surah is making the connection deliberately: rock yielded water for them, and they are harder than the rock that fed them. Then *wa inna minha la-ma yashshaqqaqu fa-yakhruju minhu al-ma'u* — some split open and water comes out. Then, most strikingly, *wa inna minha la-ma yahbitu min khashyati Allahi* — some fall down in awe of Allah.

Now that last clause raises a real question: do stones feel awe? The majority of commentators read it literally, and there is strong Qur'anic support for doing so. "There is not a thing except that it exalts [Allah] by His praise, but you do not understand their [way of] exalting" (17:44). "Do you not see that to Allah prostrates whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth — the sun, the moon, the stars, the mountains, the trees, the moving creatures" (22:18). And most directly: "If We had sent down this Qur'an upon a mountain, you would have seen it humbled and splitting apart from fear of Allah" (59:21). On this reading, creation has a real mode of response to its Creator, and rock falling from a mountainside is described as awe.

But a necessary boundary must be stated with it: this is the *tasbih* of creation, whose modality we do not know, and it does not imply that stones possess moral agency, awareness in the human sense, or accountability. The Ash'arite and Maturidi discussions of this are careful on exactly that point. A minority of commentators read the clause figuratively — that rock yielding water is *as though* it feared Allah — and that reading exists and should be acknowledged. What matters for the reader is that on either reading the argument works identically: creation responds to its Maker, and a human heart can become less responsive than geology.

*Wa ma Allahu bi-ghafilin 'amma ta'malun* — "and Allah is not unaware of what you do." The closing is a double negative: Allah is *not* heedless. Nothing is lost, nothing is overlooked, and the hardening itself — which is the one process nobody notices from the inside — is fully seen from outside.

For practice, this verse is the most personally urgent in the batch, and it turns on a distinction that is easy to miss.

The distinction is between a hard heart and a neutral one. There is no neutral state. The person who hears the Qur'an recited every week for thirty years and feels nothing is not in the same condition he was in thirty years ago; he has travelled somewhere, and the direction is set by what he did with what he heard. Emotional flatness in religion is not a personality type. It is a trajectory, and it has a cause named in this verse: repeated exposure without response.

The remedy is equally specific, and it is the remedy the Qur'an names wherever it names the disease. "Is one whose breast Allah has expanded to Islam and who is upon a light from his Lord [like one whose heart is hardened]? So woe to those whose hearts are hardened against the remembrance of Allah" (39:22) — the remedy is *dhikr*. "Unquestionably, by the remembrance of Allah hearts are assured" (13:28). And the Prophet ﷺ said, in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari from Abu Musa al-Ash'ari: "The example of the one who remembers his Lord and the one who does not is like the example of the living and the dead."

There is one more application that is harder to say but worth saying. The three stones in this verse yield rivers, yield water, and fall in awe. They produce. A hard heart produces nothing — no gratitude, no tears, no generosity, no change of direction. If you want to know whether a heart is hardening, the Qur'an's own test in this verse is not emotional. It is: what comes out of it?

## Verse 75

**"Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you, though a group of them would hear the word of Allah then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it?"**

In simple terms: Allah asks the believers whether they really expect these people to be sincere with them, when a party among them used to hear the word of Allah and then deliberately distort it after fully understanding it, knowing exactly what they were doing.

*A-fa-tatma'una an yu'minu lakum* — "do you then hope that they will be true to you?" The verb *tatma'una* carries a sense of eager expectation, of longing for something. And note the shift in address. For the whole preceding passage the "you" has been the Children of Israel. Here the surah turns and addresses the believers — the Muslims in Madinah who were hoping that the Jewish community would accept Muhammad ﷺ, many of whom had ties of kinship and neighbourhood with them.

That is a gentle but firm disappointment, and it has a purpose beyond the historical moment. The surah is protecting the Muslim community from a specific kind of collapse: the disillusionment that comes when people you love and expected better from do not respond to something you have found overwhelmingly true. Managing that expectation is not cynicism. It is a mercy, and it is what keeps a person in the work after the first decade of disappointment.

*Wa qad kana fariqun minhum* — "when a party of them used to..." Note the qualifier again: *fariqun minhum*, a party of them. Not all of them. This is now the fourth time in this passage that the surah attributes a fault to a subset rather than to the whole — "those who wronged" (59), "those among you who transgressed" (65), and here "a party of them." The discipline is consistent and it is being modelled for the reader, in a passage whose entire function is indictment. No one reading this surah is permitted to generalise where the Qur'an particularises.

*Yasma'una kalam Allah* — "hearing the word of Allah." There are two questions here that the commentators distinguished.

The first is what "the word of Allah" refers to. The dominant reading is that it is the Torah — the speech Allah addressed to them through Moses, which they heard. Some held it refers to the actual divine speech they heard at Sinai, connected to the episode of the seventy at 7:155 and the demand at verse 55 of this surah. On either reading the point is the same: they had direct access to the revelation, which removes every excuse of distance or mediation.

The second question is how the phrase was used in later theology. *Kalam Allah* — "the speech of Allah" — became one of the central terms in the classical debate over whether the Qur'an is created or uncreated, a dispute that convulsed the Muslim community in the third century and after. Ahl al-Sunnah held that the Qur'an is the speech of Allah and that His speech is an eternal attribute, uncreated; the Mu'tazilah held that it is created. This verse was cited in that debate. I note the disagreement without resolving it here, since it belongs to works of creed rather than to a verse commentary, and the practical content of this verse is unaffected by which side of that question a reader takes.

*Thumma yuharrifunahu min ba'di ma 'aqaluhu* — "then they distort it after having understood it." This is the crux, and it turns on what *tahrif* means. The word comes from a root meaning an edge or a border, and the form implies turning a thing away from its edge — deflecting it. The classical discussion divides into two positions, and the difference has real consequences.

The first is *tahrif al-ma'na* — distortion of the meaning. On this reading the text was preserved but deliberately misinterpreted: words taken out of their places, rulings explained away, plain statements allegorised into something harmless. The strongest support for this reading is that the Qur'an describes the distortion in exactly those terms elsewhere: "They distort the words from their [proper] places" (4:46; repeated at 5:13). *'An mawadi'ihi* — from their places — most naturally reads as displacement of meaning rather than alteration of text. Al-Razi argued for this position at length, as did others.

The second is *tahrif al-nass* — corruption of the text itself: words added, removed or changed. This position was held by a number of classical authorities including Ibn Hazm, and it is the more common position among contemporary Muslim apologists. Its strongest support is the next verse in this batch, 2:79, which describes people writing a book with their own hands and then claiming it is from Allah.

Both positions are held within the tradition, and they are not always mutually exclusive — a community can both misinterpret an existing text and circulate forged material alongside it. Three things should be said plainly about the question, because it is one where Muslims most often overreach.

First, the Qur'an simultaneously affirms the Torah as "guidance and light" (5:44) and commands its possessors to "establish the Torah and the Gospel and what has been sent down to them from their Lord" (5:66, 5:68). Any reading of *tahrif* must be able to hold those affirmations at the same time, not explain them away.

Second, this is a Muslim theological claim about the earlier scriptures, made on the basis of revelation. It is not a licence for Muslims to treat a physical Torah scroll or Gospel with disrespect, nor to attribute the distortion to Jewish people as such rather than to specific religious authorities acting knowingly for specific ends. The Qur'an's own charge is against a knowing act by a party among them, and it is framed that way on purpose.

Third, and this is the boundary most often crossed: attempts to settle the question from manuscript evidence are riskier than either side usually admits. The textual history of the Hebrew Bible and the New Testament is genuinely complex, showing both remarkable stability in some respects and real variation in others, and neither a claim of wholesale textual corruption nor a claim of pristine preservation is as simple as popular presentations make it. The Islamic claim is a revealed claim. It should be stated as such, and it should not be staked on a scholarly argument that can be overturned by the next manuscript find.

*Wa hum ya'lamun* — "while they know." That is the aggravating element, and the surah has used it before: mixing truth with falsehood and concealing the truth *while you know* (2:42, 3:71). This is not error, confusion, or an honest misreading. It is knowing distortion, and the difference between the two is the difference between a mistake and a lie.

For practice, this verse has a hard edge and it points in several directions at once.

The first is expectation. Anyone engaged in inviting others to anything — a religion, a cause, a reform — will face the moment when the people he most expected to understand it do not, and the moment after that, when he realises they understood it very well. The surah is preparing the believer for that so that he does not quit when it arrives.

The second is the knowing distortion, and it is the one that applies within every community including the Muslim one. It is the scholar who quotes the source and omits the qualifying sentence. It is the institution that suppresses the inconvenient passage. It is the debater who cites the data he knows are misleading. It is the preacher who says what his congregation wants and knows better. *Min ba'di ma 'aqaluhu* — after having understood it. The understanding is present. That is what makes it what it is.

And the third, softer, is about how a person handles truth that costs him something. Most people do not reject an unwelcome truth. They reinterpret it. They find the reading that lets them keep their position and their integrity at the same time, and they are usually sincere when they do it. The word *yuharrifunahu* is a warning about that reflex, and it is aimed at everyone.

## Verse 76

**"When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But in private they say ˹to each other˺, “Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you, so that they may use it against you before your Lord? Do you not understand?”"**

In simple terms: publicly they professed faith; privately they accused each other of giving the believers ammunition from their own scripture, warning that it would be used as proof against them on the Day of Resurrection.

*Wa idha laqu alladhina amanu qalu amanna* — "and when they meet those who believe, they say: we believe." The structure is the classic hypocrite's signature, and the surah has already drawn it in detail at verses 8 to 20: a statement in public that is contradicted in private. What is new here is the content of the private conversation, and that content is startling.

*Wa idha khala ba'duhum ila ba'din qalu a-tuhaddithunahum bima fataha Allahu 'alaykum* — "and when they are alone with one another, they say: do you tell them what Allah has disclosed to you?" *Fataha* means to open, to unveil, to grant — so the phrase is "what Allah has opened up to you," meaning what He has revealed or made known.

What was that? The dominant reading among the commentators is that it refers to the knowledge in the Torah concerning Muhammad ﷺ — the descriptions and proofs that some of them had been sharing with the believers, and that others wanted suppressed. The Qur'an refers to this elsewhere: "Those who follow the Messenger, the unlettered prophet, whom they find written in what they have of the Torah and the Gospel" (7:157), and "And when there came to them a Book from Allah confirming that which was with them — although before it they had been praying for victory over those who disbelieved — then when there came to them that which they recognised, they disbelieved in it" (2:89).

So the thing they were hiding was proof of the very message they were rejecting. That is the first devastating detail in this verse. They were not suppressing an argument they thought was false. They were suppressing one they knew was true.

*Li-yuhajjukum bihi 'inda rabbikum* — "so that they may argue against you with it before your Lord." Here is the second devastating detail, and it is the one that should stop a reader. The phrase *'inda rabbikum* — before your Lord — places the feared argument on the Day of Resurrection. They were worried that the believers would produce the Torah as a *hujjah*, a proof, against them *on the Day of Judgement*.

Think about what that reveals. These are not people who disbelieved in the Hereafter. They knew there would be a Day. They knew there would be an accounting. They knew the evidence would be produced there, and they knew their own scripture would tell against them. And their response was not to reconsider their position but to try to prevent the evidence from reaching the other side.

That is a level of bad faith the Qur'an exposes precisely, and it is a recognisable human posture. It is the person who believes, at some level he will not examine, that he will answer for what he has done — and whose strategy is not repentance but information management. He does not want the argument to reach the tribunal, so he suppresses it, avoids the conversation, changes the subject, and quietly hopes the day will not come. The surah's answer, in the next verse, is that the One before whom the argument would be made already has it.

*A-fa-la ta'qilun* — "do you not reason?" There is a genuine question about who is speaking. The more natural grammatical reading is that this is the continuation of their private speech — the rebukers saying to those who disclosed the knowledge, "have you no sense?" A number of commentators, however, read it as Allah's rebuke of them, which fits the Qur'an's habit of closing a reported conversation with His own rhetorical question. Both readings are held, and either way the substance is the same: the suppression was irrational, because the knowledge they were trying to keep from the believers was already fully known to the One they feared.

Now the applications, and the first is the most contemporary.

The suppression of inconvenient evidence is the characteristic intellectual sin of every professional class that holds a position. It is the internal memo that never leaves the building, the study that is not published, the data that does not fit and therefore does not appear. The verse's diagnosis of it is precise: the people doing this know. *Min ba'di ma 'aqaluhu* in verse 75, and here the whole private conversation is conducted by people who understand the situation perfectly. The problem was never information.

The second is about the public-private split, which is the mark this surah has been tracing since verse 8. A person who says one thing to the believers and another to his own circle is living in two rooms, and the Qur'an's consistent treatment of that condition is severe because the split is not a social accommodation — it is a division in the person. The modern world engineers this split on an industrial scale. The public persona and the private group chat are two different people, and most of us maintain the gap without thinking of it as a religious problem. This verse thinks of it as a religious problem.

And the third is the exposure, which is the quiet joke of the passage. They held this conversation *in private*. They were careful. And the Qur'an, revealed to a man who was not in the room, records their words verbatim. There is no private. That is the point of verse 77, and it lands with full force because of this one.

## Verse 77

**"Do they not know that Allah is aware of what they conceal and what they reveal?"**

In simple terms: do they not know that Allah knows what they keep hidden and what they make public?

*A-wa-la ya'lamuna anna Allaha ya'lamu ma yusirrun wa ma yu'linun* — "do they not know that Allah knows what they keep secret and what they make known?" The two verbs are deliberately paired. *Yusirrun*, from the root for a secret and for the interior of the heart. *Yu'linun*, from the root for publicity and openness. Between them they cover everything a human being can do with a thought: hide it, or show it. And Allah's knowledge covers both.

Note how the verse answers verse 76. Their whole strategy was premised on privacy: they waited until they were alone, *khala ba'duhum ila ba'din*, and then spoke. The plot depended entirely on the conversation not being overheard. And this verse does not say "Allah heard you." It says something colder and more complete: do you not *know*? The knowledge was never acquired by eavesdropping. There was never a moment when it was not present.

The pairing of the hidden and the manifest recurs throughout the Qur'an, always to the same effect. "I know what you reveal and what you concealed" (2:33), said to the angels about what was in their hearts before they spoke it. "And He knows what you declare and what you conceal" (6:3). "And if you speak aloud — indeed, He knows the secret and what is [even] more hidden" (20:7). And in the most searching form: "And We have already created man and know what his soul whispers to him, and We are closer to him than [his] jugular vein" (50:16).

Two applications follow, and they pull in opposite directions, which is exactly why the verse holds them together.

The first is the end of privacy as licence. There is no unwitnessed space. Not the locked room, not the deleted message, not the thought that never became an action, not the motive you would never say aloud. The person who sins only where nobody can see has made a calculation, and the calculation is false, because the category "where nobody can see" does not exist. This is why the Prophet ﷺ defined the highest station of the religion in exactly these terms. In the hadith of Jibril, recorded by Muslim, he was asked about *ihsan* and replied: "It is that you worship Allah as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, then He sees you." Not a feeling. A fact, held in mind while acting.

And this is precisely the cure for the double life that verse 76 described. The public-private split lasts exactly as long as a person believes the private room is unobserved. Collapse that belief and the two rooms become one, which is either a terrifying thought or the beginning of integrity, depending on what is in the private one.

The second application is the comfort, and it is the half of this verse that is always under-preached. Nothing good is hidden either. The charity given so quietly that the left hand did not know — "and if you conceal them and give them to the poor, it is better for you" (2:271) — is fully known. The restraint exercised when nobody would have blamed you for losing it is known. The prayer prayed at a hour when everyone else was asleep is known. The person who has quietly carried a difficult obligation for thirty years with no recognition and no audience is not, in fact, unobserved.

Held together, the two applications produce the state the Qur'an is always aiming at: a person who behaves the same in public and in private not because he is performing, but because the distinction has stopped being meaningful to him. That is what integrity literally is — a single, undivided self. And it is worth noticing that on the Qur'an's account it is achieved not by willpower but by a piece of knowledge: that you are seen.

## Verse 78

**"And among them are the illiterate who know nothing about the Scripture except lies, and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate."**

In simple terms: some of them are unlettered and know nothing of the scripture except their own desires and assumptions — so everything they believe about it is conjecture.

*Wa minhum ummiyyun* — "and among them are unlettered ones." The word *ummi* needs care, because it carries a weight in Islam that goes beyond this verse.

Its basic sense is someone who does not read or write, or who has no scripture. Here the commentators understood it as those among them who had no knowledge of their own Book — people who could not read it and did not know it, and who therefore followed whatever their leaders told them or whatever they hoped.

The word matters enormously elsewhere, because it is the description the Qur'an gives of the Prophet ﷺ himself: "the unlettered prophet, whom they find written in what they have of the Torah and the Gospel" (7:157), and "So believe in Allah and His Messenger, the unlettered prophet" (7:158), and "It is He who sent among the unlettered a Messenger from themselves" (62:2). For Muhammad ﷺ, being *ummi* was not a defect to be explained away. It was part of the argument. A man who could neither read nor write could not have copied the earlier scriptures — "And you did not recite before it any scripture, nor did you inscribe one with your right hand; otherwise the falsifiers would have had cause for doubt" (29:48) — and could not have composed the Qur'an. His unlettered state is cited in the classical works on inimitability as one of the proofs of the revelation.

The polemical use of the word against Islam by some non-Muslim writers therefore gets it exactly backwards. Within the tradition, *ummi* as applied to the Prophet is a credential.

*La ya'lamuna al-kitaba illa amaniyya* — "they do not know the Book except *amani*." Here is a genuine and interesting dispute over a single word, and it is worth setting out because the two readings, while different, converge on the same warning.

The first reading, and the one followed by most modern translations, takes *amani* as the plural of *umniyyah*, meaning a wish or a fond hope. On this reading the verse says: they know nothing of the scripture except their own wishes. What they imagine the Book says is a projection of what they would like it to say. That reading is confirmed by what follows in verse 80 — "the Fire will never touch us except for a few numbered days" — which is precisely a wish presented as doctrine.

The second reading, transmitted from Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid and others and discussed by the early grammarians, takes *amani* in the sense of recitation: they do not know the Book except by reading it aloud. On this reading the verse says: they recite the scripture without understanding it. The sounds are right, the meaning is absent.

Both readings are held in the tradition and both have early support. And notice that they land in the same place. Whether a person has replaced the scripture with his wishes, or recites it without comprehension, the outcome is identical: he has the Book and does not have its knowledge. One is religion as desire; the other is religion as sound. Both are extremely common, and both leave the person exactly as he was.

*Wa in hum illa yazunnun* — "and they do nothing but conjecture." *Zann* is assumption, opinion held without warrant. There is a nice point here that connects back to verse 46 of this surah. In that verse the Qur'an praised those who *yazunnuna annahum mulaqu rabbihim* — who are certain that they will meet their Lord. There *zann* was the highest station of conviction. Here the same word is condemned. The difference is entirely in the object. Conjecture attached to the meeting with Allah, grounded in His promise, is certainty. Conjecture about what Allah will or will not do, grounded in your own preference, is guesswork dressed as religion. Same word, opposite verdict, because the question is never how strongly you believe something. It is what your belief is resting on.

For practice, this verse describes a condition that is far more common among religious people than among the irreligious, and that is why it stings.

The condition is holding a theology assembled from wishes. It runs: God is merciful, so this will be fine. God knows my heart, so the omission does not count. God would not punish someone who is basically decent. I am from a good family. I have done more good than harm. Every one of those sentences may even be partially true. None of them is knowledge, and verse 80 will show exactly where that road ends.

The second application is the recitation-without-understanding reading, and for a Muslim audience it is unavoidable. The Qur'an is recited more than it is understood — by most people, most of the time, in most places. Recitation itself is enormously rewarded and is not the problem. The problem described in this verse is a person who has the Book and does not know it, who has recited a passage a thousand times and could not tell you what it asks of him, and who has never noticed that this is a condition the Qur'an names and warns about.

And there is a third, which is the specific failure of the community rather than the individual. The *ummiyyun* here are lay people who do not know their scripture and follow their leaders. The surah is careful: it faults them for wishful thinking and conjecture, but the far more severe condemnation is reserved for the leaders in the next verse, who knew and forged. A community whose lay people are ignorant while its scholars are corrupt is a community in a specific and diagnosable condition, and the surah spends the next two verses on it.

## Verse 79

**"So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say, “This is from Allah”—seeking a fleeting gain! So woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they have earned."**

In simple terms: severe destruction awaits those who compose a text themselves, then claim it is from Allah, in order to sell it for a trivial gain. Woe to them for the forgery, and woe to them for the profit.

*Fa-waylun lilladhina yaktubuna al-kitaba bi-aydihim* — "so woe to those who write the Book with their own hands." *Wayl* is a word of severe threat, used throughout the Qur'an for those under a declared doom. The commentators offered various explanations of what it denotes concretely — including reports describing it as a valley or a pit in the Fire — and those descriptions are transmitted reports rather than text, and should be cited as such.

Now notice the phrase *bi-aydihim*, "with their own hands." Writing is done with the hands anyway, so the phrase is redundant unless it is carrying force. The commentators were acute about this: the emphasis is that they wrote it *themselves*. It is their own composition. The redundancy is the point — the text they are about to attribute to Allah was manufactured by the people attributing it.

*Thumma yaquluna hadha min 'indi Allahi* — "then they say: this is from Allah." That is the crime, and it is the gravest element in the verse. Wrongly attributing something to Allah is not a minor fault in this Book. It appears in the lists of the greatest prohibitions: "Say: My Lord has only forbidden immoralities — what is apparent of them and what is concealed — and sin, and oppression without right, and that you associate with Allah that for which He has not sent down authority, and that you say about Allah that which you do not know" (7:33). And "And do not say about what your tongues assert of falsehood, 'This is lawful and this is unlawful,' to invent falsehood about Allah" (16:116).

For a Muslim reader there is a direct and unavoidable analogue, and the Prophet ﷺ stated it in one of the most severely worded narrations in the entire corpus: "Whoever lies about me deliberately, let him take his seat in the Fire." This hadith was transmitted by more than seventy Companions and is classified as *mutawatir* — mass-transmitted, and therefore certain — and it appears in al-Bukhari, Muslim and the other collections. Muslim also records the version: "Whoever narrates a hadith from me which he knows to be a lie is one of the liars."

That is the same crime this verse condemns, transposed into the Muslim community: forging an attribution to the revelation for the sake of an audience. And it is worth saying plainly that it is not a hypothetical crime. The fabrication of hadith is a documented historical phenomenon in Islam, which is exactly why the science of hadith criticism — *al-jarh wa al-ta'dil*, the examination of chains and transmitters — developed into the most rigorous discipline of its kind in the pre-modern world. The Muslim community did not ignore this verse. It built an entire science to enforce it.

*Li-yashtaru bihi thamanan qalilan* — "to purchase with it a small price." Note the phrase, because the surah has used it before. At verse 41 the command was: "And believe in what I have sent down confirming that which is with you, and be not the first to disbelieve in it. And do not exchange My signs for a small price." That phrase — *thamanan qalilan*, a small price — recurs in seven places in the Qur'an (2:41, 2:79, 2:174, 3:187, 5:44, 9:9, 16:95), and it is always the same transaction: the truth sold for something transient. What was the price here? The commentators described it generally as the worldly gain attached to religious office — status, revenue, the retention of a following that would have been lost if they had acknowledged what they knew. Specific historical instances are transmitted in the commentaries; they are reports, and the general category is what the verse establishes.

The word to notice is *qalilan* — small. Nobody sells the truth for everything. That is the tragedy and it is also the diagnosis. The sale is always for something visibly inadequate: a little status, a little income, a little peace, a moment's advantage in an argument. The disproportion is the point, and it is what makes the transaction so baffling to everyone watching it happen.

*Fa-waylun lahum mimma katabat aydihim wa waylun lahum mimma yaksibun* — "so woe to them for what their hands have written, and woe to them for what they earn." Two woes, and they are not repetitive. The first is for the forgery — what their hands wrote. The second is for the profit — what they earned. The deed and the motive are each condemned separately, which tells the reader that even if the forged text had been harmless and even if the profit had been given away, the two together would still be two crimes.

Now the applications, and this verse has more of them than any other in the batch.

The first is for anyone who teaches, writes, or speaks about the religion, which in an age of social media means almost everyone. To state a ruling as the ruling of Islam when you do not know it is, in the logic of this verse, to write with your own hand and say "this is from Allah." To invent a hadith, to attribute a view to a scholar who did not hold it, to cite a verse for a position the verse does not support, to present your preference in the accents of revelation — these are graded versions of the same act, and the tradition's severity about it is not fussiness. It is because a forged attribution to the revelation corrupts the thing the revelation exists to do, which is to tell people what is actually true.

The second is the "small price," and it is a test worth applying honestly. Whenever a person finds himself softening a truth, or omitting a qualification, or declining to say something he knows is right — the question to ask is what he is getting for it. The answer is almost always embarrassingly small: the approval of a room, a little standing with a group, the avoidance of an awkward conversation. *Thamanan qalilan*. Naming the price out loud, to yourself, is often enough to end the transaction.

And the third is a guardrail that must be stated with the verse, not after it. This verse condemns a specific act by specific people who knew what they were doing and did it for gain. It is not a description of Jewish scholarship, it is not a licence to dismiss the Torah — which the Qur'an calls "guidance and light" (5:44) and which it commands its possessors to establish (5:66, 5:68) — and it is not a warrant for any Muslim to accuse a Jewish person of forgery. Most importantly, its primary and intended readership is the Muslim community, whose own scholars inherit exactly the same warning and against whom the same *wayl* is pronounced if they do the same thing.

## Verse 80

**"˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, “The Fire will not touch us except for a number of days.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Have you taken a pledge from Allah— for Allah never breaks His word—or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?”"**

In simple terms: they claimed the Fire would only touch them briefly. The answer is a two-horned argument: either you have an actual promise from Allah — and His promises do not fail — or you are inventing claims about Allah without knowledge.

*Wa qalu lan tamassana al-naru illa ayyaman ma'dudatan* — "and they said: the Fire will never touch us except for a few numbered days." Two details deserve attention.

The first is the verb: *tamassa*, to touch. Not "enter," not "burn," not "punish." They minimised even in the grammar. The Fire will barely graze us. That is how self-serving theology always sounds — not a denial of the thing, but a shrinking of it to a manageable size.

The second is *ayyaman ma'dudatan*, numbered days — a few countable days. The same phrase is used in this surah for the fast (2:184), and the identical claim is recorded at 3:24: "That is because they say, 'Never will the Fire touch us except for a few numbered days.'" What was the basis of it? The transmitted reports give several: that they would only be punished for the forty days of the calf-worship, corresponding to the forty nights of verse 51; that their forefathers the prophets would intercede for them; that they were the descendants of the prophets and therefore safe. All of these are reports rather than text, and the Qur'an's response does not engage any of them on their merits. It goes underneath all of them.

*Qul a-ttakhadhtum 'inda Allahi 'ahdan* — "say: have you taken a covenant with Allah?" And here is the argument, which is one of the cleanest pieces of reasoning in the Qur'an and is worth learning as a tool.

The verse sets up a strict dichotomy with no third option. Either you have received a pledge from Allah, or you are speaking about Allah without knowledge. If the first, then produce it — and note the parenthetical: *fa-lan yukhlifa Allahu 'ahdahu*, "for Allah never breaks His pledge." That clause is not a reassurance to them; it is the trap closing. If Allah had truly promised them this, it would hold, because His promises do not fail. So if they can show the promise, they win. They cannot. Therefore the second horn: *am taquluna 'ala Allahi ma la ta'lamun* — or are you saying about Allah what you do not know?

And that second horn is not a soft landing. Speaking about Allah without knowledge is listed among the gravest of crimes in the Qur'an: in 7:33, alongside *shirk* and oppression; in 10:68, "Glory be to Him! He is far above what they describe"; and in 16:116, "do not say about what your tongues assert of falsehood, 'This is lawful and this is unlawful,' to invent falsehood about Allah. Indeed, those who invent falsehood about Allah will not succeed."

There is a doctrinal point inside the parenthetical that should be stated carefully. Ahl al-Sunnah hold that Allah does not break His promise, and that He is not obligated by anything outside Himself — He fulfils what He has promised out of His grace, and He fulfils what He has threatened out of His justice, except that He may forgive whom He wills. The Mu'tazilah held that it is rationally necessary for Allah to fulfil both promise and threat. The discussion is real and belongs to works of creed; it was noted briefly at verse 40 of the earlier batch and need not be reopened here. What every party to that discussion agrees on is the part that matters for this verse: nothing Allah has said will fail to happen, whether of promise or of threat.

For practice, this verse gives a transferable instrument and a hard warning, and both are needed.

The instrument is the two-horned question, and it applies to every claim anyone makes about what Allah will or will not do. "God would never punish a good person." "A loving God wouldn't send anyone to Hell." "He knows my heart, so it's fine." "I was born Muslim, so I'll be fine in the end." Each of these is a claim about the unseen, and this verse establishes the only two possible sources for such a claim: revelation, or invention. There is no third category. It is not harsh to ask which one it is. It is the most loving question available, because a person building his eternity on an invention deserves to be told before the day arrives.

The warning has a Muslim version, and it is the one that most needs saying to the readers of this surah. The claim "the Fire will never touch us except for a few days" has a direct descendant in the Muslim community: the assumption that being Muslim settles the matter regardless of what follows. The tradition is explicit that it does not settle it that way, and the balance the Qur'an and Sunnah maintain is between fear and hope, with neither permitted to collapse into presumption or despair.

That balance has to be stated in full, because stating half of it does damage. On the side of hope: "None of you will be saved by his deeds" (al-Bukhari and Muslim) sits alongside "Indeed, My mercy prevails over My wrath" (al-Bukhari and Muslim), and the hadith qudsi in which Allah says, "I am as My servant thinks of Me, so let him think of Me what he wishes" (al-Bukhari and Muslim), and the vast body of texts on the breadth of His forgiveness. On the side of fear: the warnings of this verse, and the fact that the people it addresses were people who had received revelation, had prophets among them, and were certain of their standing.

The difference between legitimate hope and the presumption this verse condemns is not the intensity of the hope. It is the ground. Legitimate hope rests on what Allah has actually promised — His mercy, His forgiveness, His acceptance of repentance, His willingness to replace bad deeds with good. Presumption rests on what we would prefer to be true. The verse's question sorts the two perfectly: did Allah say this, or did you?

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Verses 71 to 80 close the story of the cow and then perform an autopsy.

The story closes badly, though it closes. They slaughtered the animal, grudgingly, at the edge of refusal — and the miracle they had obstructed for three verses was then performed anyway: a murdered man struck with a piece of the cow came back to life and named his killer, as a demonstration of the Resurrection and an exposure of what the community had been hiding (71–73).

Then the verdict: after all of that, their hearts hardened until they were harder than stone, since stone at least yields rivers, splits with water, and falls in awe of its Lord (74). Hardening is not the absence of signs. It is the accumulated residue of signs received and not acted upon, and the same surah warns the believers of the same process in so many words (57:16).

Then the anatomy, in five parts. A party of them heard the word of Allah and distorted it after understanding it, knowingly (75). They said one thing to the believers and another in private, where they plotted to suppress the very proof they knew would be used against them on the Day they believed in (76). They forgot — or failed to draw the inference — that what is hidden and what is declared are equally known (77). Their lay people knew nothing of the scripture but their own wishes and guesses (78). Their leaders wrote a book with their own hands, called it revelation, and sold it for a small price (79). And the whole community settled into a comfortable eschatology: the Fire will barely touch us, and only for a few days (80).

Three conclusions hold the passage together. First, the failure is consistently described as occurring *after understanding* — after the sea, after the covenant, after the corpse walked, after hearing the word. Nobody in these verses lacks information. Second, the corruption runs from the top down: the scholars forge, the lay people believe what they wish, and the result is a community holding a confident theology that has no source. And third, the specific claim at the end is the one the whole catalogue was aimed at: a community that has been told ten favours and matched them with ten refusals, and has concluded that the accounting will be brief. The surah's answer is a single question with only two possible answers, and it is the same question that closes every self-serving theology in every age. Did Allah say this, or did you?

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators genuinely differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: whether *amani* means wishful thinking or recitation (both readings transmitted from early authorities, both presented, and their convergence noted); whether the clause "do you not reason?" in verse 76 belongs to the reported speakers or to Allah; whether the stones' falling in awe is literal or figurative; and, most consequentially, whether the distortion of verse 75 is of the text or of the meaning.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the identity of the murdered man and the circumstances of the killing, the part of the cow used in the striking, the historical specifics of what the forgers altered and for what payment, the reported bases of the "few numbered days" claim, and the descriptions of *wayl* as a place in the Fire. The hadith of the black spot on the heart is cited with its grading noted as *hasan* rather than *sahih*.

Four boundaries have been observed. On verse 74: the awe of creation has been presented with the explicit qualification that the *tasbih* of created things does not imply moral agency or accountability, and the modality of that praise is unknown to us. On verse 75: the distortion is attributed to a knowing act by a party among them, not to a people; the Torah's status as guidance and light (5:44) is held simultaneously with it; and the attempt to settle the question from manuscript evidence has been flagged as riskier than either side usually admits, with the Islamic claim stated as a revealed claim rather than a scholarly one. On verse 79: the verse has been applied primarily to the Muslim community and its scholars rather than outward, and it has been stated that it is not a warrant for dismissing the Torah or for accusing Jewish people of forgery. On verse 80: the balance between fear and hope has been stated in full, on both sides, so that the warning against presumption is not read as a licence for despair — no reader of this surah may conclude from it that anyone's case is hopeless, and no one may declare any individual among the inhabitants of the Fire.
