# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 41–50

We are continuing the long address to the Children of Israel that opened at verse 40, and these ten verses divide naturally into two halves. Verses 41 to 46 state what is being asked of them: believe in the revelation that confirms what they already hold, and do not be the first to reject it or sell it for a small price; do not blur the line between truth and falsehood or suppress what they know; establish the prayer, give the zakah and join the praying community; and stop the grotesque contradiction of preaching what they do not practise — seeking help instead through patience and prayer, which is heavy for everyone except those who are certain that they are going to meet their Lord. Then, at verse 47, the tone changes. The surah begins a catalogue of the favours Allah bestowed on them, a catalogue that will run to verse 60 and return again at verse 122. Verses 47 and 48 give the general call — remember the favours, and guard against a Day on which every escape route is closed — and verses 49 and 50 name the first two favours in detail: the deliverance from Pharaoh and the parting of the sea. The structure is deliberate and it is worth watching: the demand comes first, and then the evidence of why it is owed.

---

## Verse 41

**"Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures. Do not be the first to deny them or trade them for a fleeting gain. And be mindful of Me."**

In simple terms, Allah commands the Children of Israel to believe in the Qur'an, which He describes as confirming the scripture they already possess, and warns them not to be the first to reject it and not to exchange His signs for any worldly advantage, closing with the command to be mindful of Him alone.

*Wa aminu bima anzaltu musaddiqan lima ma'akum* — "and believe in what I have sent down, confirming what is with you." The operative word is *musaddiqan*: confirming, declaring true, bearing witness to the truth of. This is the Qur'an's own account of its relationship to the earlier scriptures, and it is stated repeatedly — the Qur'an is "confirming what was before it" (10:37, 12:111, 35:31, 46:30) and, in the fuller formulation of 5:48, it is both confirming and *muhaymin*, a guardian and criterion over them.

The argumentative strategy here should be noticed, because it is the Qur'an's constant method with the People of the Book. It does not open by demanding assent to something alien. It says: this confirms what you already accept. If the Torah is true — and you say it is — then the revelation that confirms it and comes from the same source is true by the same reasoning. The demand is built entirely on a premise they already hold, which is why the charge that follows is so damning: rejecting it is not caution, it is inconsistency.

*Wa la takunu awwala kafirin bihi* — "and do not be the first to disbelieve in it." Why "first"? The commentators gave readings that reinforce one another. The Arab Jews of Madinah were the first community of the People of the Book to whom this revelation came directly, so their rejection would set a precedent for Jewish communities elsewhere who would hear of it only through them. More generally, being first means being followed: the one who opens a door is followed through it, and bears a share of what follows. Both readings make the same point — the gravity of an action is multiplied by its position, and the one who goes first carries more than his own portion.

That principle is not limited to this verse. The Prophet ﷺ said, in a narration recorded by Muslim from Jarir ibn 'Abdullah: "Whoever introduces a good practice in Islam has its reward and the reward of those who act upon it after him, without their reward being diminished at all; and whoever introduces a bad practice in Islam bears its burden and the burden of those who act upon it after him, without their burden being diminished at all." The application is immediate and uncomfortable: the influencer who mocks religion publicly, the celebrity who announces a deconversion, the respected family that quietly abandons the prayer — each of them is "first" for the people watching them, and the arithmetic of the hadith applies.

*Wa la tashtaru bi-ayati thamanan qalilan* — "and do not exchange My signs for a small price." The verb is from a root used in the Qur'an for both buying and selling, and the sense is: do not take a price in exchange for My verses. The phrase *thamanan qalilan*, "a small price," is one of the Qur'an's fixed expressions, and it recurs wherever revelation or covenant is sold: 2:79, 2:174, 3:187, 5:44, 9:9 and 16:95. The adjective is the point. Whatever is gained is *qalil* — small, cheap, paltry — measured against what is given up.

What was the price in this case? The commentators described it as worldly advantage of the ordinary kinds: the status of the rabbis as the religious authorities of their communities, the offerings and gifts that came with that status, the tribal alliances that would be strained, and the fear of losing position if their followers accepted a new prophet. The details vary, but the shape is constant: religious authority traded for security, income or standing.

This is one of the Qur'an's most frequently repeated condemnations, and it is worth stating plainly why. The corruption of religious leadership is not one sin among many; it is the sin that corrupts everything downstream, because it distorts the transmission of the truth itself. The Sunnah is severe about it. A narration recorded by Abu Dawud and Ibn Majah states that whoever learns knowledge that ought to be sought for the sake of Allah, seeking by it nothing but a portion of this world, will not smell the fragrance of Paradise. And in a narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi from Ka'b ibn Malik, the Prophet ﷺ said that whoever seeks knowledge in order to argue with the ignorant, to show off to the scholars, or to turn people's faces towards him, Allah will admit him to the Fire.

*Wa iyyaya fa-ttaqun* — "and be mindful of Me alone." The object pronoun is fronted, *iyyaya*, which in Arabic produces exclusivity: Me, and no other. Note the parallel with the close of verse 40, which ended "and stand in awe of Me alone" using the same fronted construction. The two verses bracket this demand with the same note: the whole issue is where your ultimate reference lies.

Now bring this into the present, because the verse's three parts map onto three recognisable modern failures.

The first is refusing something because accepting it would cost you your position. The scholar who privately thinks a position is right and will not say so because his institution would not like it. The community leader who sees the truth and stays silent because his donors would object. The academic who cannot afford to be seen favouring religion. The believer who will not pray at work because it would complicate his career. In each case, the *thaman* is genuinely small and the thing sold is genuinely large, and the verse's adjective — *qalilan* — is a standing invitation to check the arithmetic honestly.

The second is the "first" principle, already mentioned: no one sins only for himself when others are watching.

And the third is the temptation the verse is really aimed at, which is the one most applicable to anyone with religious knowledge or influence: the slow conversion of religious authority into personal advantage. It rarely announces itself. It arrives as a reasonable accommodation, then a speaking fee, then an audience you begin to need, then a position you cannot afford to lose, until the price has been paid without anyone having made a decision to sell.

## Verse 42

**"Do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly."**

In simple terms, the verse forbids two distinct things: blending the truth together with falsehood so that people cannot tell them apart, and deliberately concealing the truth — and it specifies that both are done knowingly, which is what makes them so serious.

*Wa la talbisu al-haqqa bi-al-batil* — "and do not clothe the truth with falsehood." The verb *labbasa* comes from the root that gives Arabic its word for clothing, and it carries the sense of covering something over, or mixing two things so thoroughly that they can no longer be separated. That is more precise and more damning than simply "do not lie." A lie is a counterfeit presented as true. What this verse prohibits is subtler and often more effective: taking something true and weaving falsehood into it, or dressing falsehood in enough truth that the whole thing becomes indistinguishable.

The commentators applied it to the specific case at hand — presenting distorted descriptions of the Prophet ﷺ alongside the true ones, mixing genuine Torah with invented material, presenting innovations as though they were revelation. But the principle is universal, and it is one of the most contemporary verses in the Qur'an. What the verse describes is precisely the mechanism of disinformation as we now understand it: not the crude falsehood, which is checkable, but the blend. The quote that is real but shorn of its context. The statistic that is accurate but stripped of its denominator. The video that is genuine but clipped. The account that reports nine true things in order to make the tenth credible. The half-truth works precisely because it *is* half true, and the verse names the crime at the level of the mechanism rather than at the level of the content.

*Wa taktumu al-haqqa* — "and do not conceal the truth." This is a second and separate prohibition. Mixing corrupts the truth; concealing withholds it. Both are named because both destroy the same thing, and a person can be guilty of either without the other. The Qur'an returns to this charge against the People of the Book repeatedly: "Those to whom We gave the Scripture know him as they know their own sons, but indeed a party of them conceal the truth while they know" (2:146), and most explicitly in 3:187, where Allah describes the covenant taken from those given scripture — "you must make it clear to the people and not conceal it."

From verses like these the scholars derived a principle that runs through Islamic ethics: religious knowledge carries an obligation of *bayan*, making clear, and its deliberate concealment when it is needed is a grave sin. A narration recorded by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah states that whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled with a bridle of fire on the Day of Resurrection. The obligation is real, and it falls with weight proportional to what a person knows.

*Wa antum ta'lamun* — "while you know." The same phrase closed verse 22 ("do not set up rivals to Allah while you know"), and it carries the same function here: it identifies knowledge as the aggravating factor. Ignorance is one thing; a person who has the truth, recognises it as the truth, and then blends or buries it has committed something of a different order. The Qur'an grades sins by the state of the heart that commits them, and here the state is: you knew.

Now apply this, because both halves of the verse are in constant use today.

The mixing has an obvious public form — the doctored clip, the selectively quoted verse, the out-of-context hadith used to win an argument, the report shared without anyone checking it. But it also has a quieter and more universal form, which is self-deception. The account you give yourself of why you did what you did, which contains just enough truth to be plausible and just enough editing to make you the reasonable party. The religious opinion you hold, which happens to align perfectly with what you already wanted to do. The Qur'an's word for this is precisely *talbis*: clothing something in truth so that it cannot be recognised for what it is.

The concealing has an equally wide application. The scholar who knows the ruling and stays silent because stating it would cost him his position. The professional who sees the fraud and says nothing. The witness who does not come forward. The friend who knows the marriage is being destroyed and stays out of it. The person who has been given knowledge — of a fact, a danger, a truth — and withholds it because speaking would be inconvenient. In each case, silence is not neutrality; it is the second half of this verse.

And there is one application that belongs to everyone, because everyone now participates in the distribution of information. Forwarding something without knowing whether it is true is not a small thing in this framework; it is participating in the mixing. The Prophet ﷺ gave the governing rule in a narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: "Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent." And in the two Sahihs, from Ibn Mas'ud, he warned against lying in general, saying that lying leads to wickedness and wickedness leads to the Fire. The verse is not asking you to verify everything before you speak. It is asking you to refuse, deliberately and as a matter of principle, to be the person who makes the true and the false indistinguishable.

## Verse 43

**"Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and bow down with those who bow down."**

In simple terms, the Children of Israel are commanded to establish the prayer, to give the obligatory charity, and to bow — that is, to pray — together with the Muslim community.

*Aqimu al-salah* — "establish the prayer." As at 2:3, the verb is not merely "perform" but "establish": to set it up on its proper footing and keep it standing — with its conditions, its pillars, its times, and its inward presence. This is the second time the command appears in the surah, and it will recur; the prayer is the most frequently commanded act in the Qur'an.

*Wa atu al-zakah* — "and give the zakah." The word comes from a root meaning to grow and to purify, and Islamic usage holds both senses together: zakah purifies the wealth and the giver, and it is described in the tradition as growth rather than loss. This is not voluntary charity, though voluntary charity is also commanded elsewhere. Zakah is the obligatory due, one of the five pillars of Islam, about which the Prophet ﷺ said, in the narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Ibn 'Umar, that Islam is built on five: testifying that there is no god but Allah and that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah, establishing the prayer, giving the zakah, pilgrimage to the House, and fasting Ramadan.

A necessary caveat, because a single verse never contains a whole legal ruling. The obligation of zakah is established here; its details come from the Sunnah and from the consensus of the scholars working with the whole body of evidence. The summary ruling is that it is due from a free Muslim who possesses the minimum threshold (*nisab*) of zakah-eligible wealth for a complete lunar year, at rates that vary by category — a quarter of a tenth (2.5%) on gold, silver, currency and trade goods, a tenth or a twentieth on agricultural produce depending on whether irrigation is artificial or natural, and specified amounts for livestock. Its recipients are the eight categories named in 9:60: the poor, the needy, those employed to administer it, those whose hearts are to be reconciled, the freeing of captives, those burdened by debt, the cause of Allah, and the traveller. The structure is captured in the instruction the Prophet ﷺ gave Mu'adh when he sent him to Yemen, recorded in the two Sahihs: inform them that Allah has made obligatory upon them a charity taken from their rich and returned to their poor.

The severity of withholding it is stated in the Qur'an itself: "Those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in the way of Allah — give them tidings of a painful punishment, the Day when it will be heated in the fire of Hell and their foreheads, their sides and their backs will be branded with it" (9:34–35). And in a narration recorded by al-Bukhari from Abu Hurayrah, the Prophet ﷺ described the wealth of one who withholds zakah as appearing on the Day of Resurrection in the form of a venomous serpent that will be made to coil around him.

*Wa irka'u ma'a al-raki'in* — "and bow with those who bow." Why is bowing singled out? The commentators gave several answers that converge. The bowing position (*ruku'*) is used here as a synecdoche for the prayer as a whole — "bow" meaning "pray." Some noted that bowing was a distinguishing feature of the Muslim prayer, absent in that form from the prayer of the People of the Book, so "bow with those who bow" means specifically "join the prayer of this community." And the preposition matters most of all: *ma'a*, "**with**." The command is not merely to pray but to pray *with* the praying people.

That makes this verse something more than a ritual instruction. Addressed to the Children of Israel, it is an invitation into the community: become part of the people who pray. The prayer is presented as the visible badge of membership in the ummah. And the command to do it *with* others carries the religion's insistence that worship is not a private transaction. The Prophet ﷺ emphasised the congregation powerfully — in the two Sahihs from Ibn 'Umar, that the prayer in congregation is superior to the prayer of a man alone by twenty-seven degrees; in Sahih Muslim, his reply to the blind man who asked to be excused, "Do you hear the call? Then answer it"; and in the two Sahihs from Abu Hurayrah, his saying that he had considered ordering the houses of those who stayed behind from the congregation to be burned down around them.

On whether the congregation is legally obligatory, the schools differed and the difference should be reported fairly. The Hanbalis held that it is individually obligatory for men who are able. The Shafi'is generally held it to be a communal obligation, discharged when enough people attend. The Hanafis and Malikis held it to be a strongly emphasised sunnah, with a number of their scholars describing it as necessary (*wajib*). All of them agree that it is among the greatest of the emphasised acts of worship and that habitual abandonment of it without excuse is blameworthy. What is important for the reader is not the technical ruling but the direction of the evidence: this is not an optional extra.

The practical reach of this verse today is considerable. The pairing of prayer and zakah is the Qur'an's constant coupling of the vertical and the horizontal — the duty to God and the duty to His creation — and the religion will not accept one as a substitute for the other. The zakah, at 2.5% of saved wealth annually, is small enough that its real function is not economic redistribution alone but the repeated breaking of the illusion of ownership: every year, a Muslim acknowledges in action that the wealth was never really his. And the command to bow *with* those who bow is a direct counter to the privatisation of religion that characterises modern life — the tendency to treat faith as a personal, internal, app-mediated thing with no necessary connection to a physical community standing shoulder to shoulder. The verse says the opposite. Join the praying people.

## Verse 44

**"Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?"**

In simple terms, this verse rebukes those who command other people to do good while neglecting it themselves, and it points out that this is especially indefensible in people who recite and study the scripture that commands the very thing they are not doing.

The verse opens with an interrogative particle, but as elsewhere in the Qur'an, a question of this shape carries rebuke rather than enquiry: "Do you command people to righteousness and forget yourselves?" The word for what they command is *al-birr* — righteousness in its comprehensive sense, the word the Qur'an will define in full at 2:177, where *birr* turns out to be belief in Allah and the Last Day, the angels, the Books and the prophets, giving wealth to relatives and orphans and the poor and travellers and beggars and for freeing captives, establishing prayer, giving zakah, keeping promises, and patience in hardship. So they were calling people to something vast and then exempting themselves from it.

*Wa tansawna anfusakum* — "and you forget yourselves." The verb means to forget, and the commentators took it here in the sense of neglecting so thoroughly that it is as though the thing had been forgotten: they remembered to instruct others and "forgot" their own selves. The sharpness of the reproach lies in the comparison. The same person who is precise about someone else's duty is careless about his own, and the Qur'an treats that not as a minor inconsistency but as a deformity of the intellect.

*Wa antum tatluna al-kitab* — "while you recite the Scripture." This is the aggravating circumstance, and it is the third time this passage has used it: "while you know" (2:22, 2:42), and now "while you recite." They are not ignorant. They study the very book that commands what they are not doing, which means the recitation itself stands as a witness against them. The verse implies that reading scripture should have produced the obvious conclusion — that it applies to you first — and asks the question that follows.

*A-fa-la ta'qilun* — "will you not reason?" The verb is from the root meaning to bind, which is why the intellect is called *'aql*: it binds things together and reaches conclusions. The question identifies the failure as a failure of reasoning, not only of will. Reading the scripture and applying it to everyone but yourself is not a lapse in effort; it is a broken piece of thinking.

The hadith literature on this verse is severe and famous. In a narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Usamah ibn Zayd, the Prophet ﷺ said that a man will be brought on the Day of Resurrection and thrown into the Fire, and his entrails will spill out and he will circle around them as a donkey circles a millstone. The people of the Fire will gather around him and say: "O so-and-so, what is the matter with you? Did you not used to enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong?" And he will say: "I used to enjoin what is right but not do it, and forbid what is wrong and do it." Note carefully what he is punished for — not for enjoining, but for not doing.

The same rebuke is addressed to the believers themselves, which is what makes the verse unavoidable. "O you who believe, why do you say what you do not do? Greatly hateful is it in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do" (61:2–3). The Qur'an does not reserve this charge for another community; it levels it at its own followers in nearly the same words.

Now a clarification that is essential, because this verse is used to justify two opposite errors. It does **not** mean that a person who falls short must stop calling to good. The obligation to enjoin right and forbid wrong does not lapse because the one discharging it is himself deficient. He has two obligations — to do and to call — and failing one does not cancel the other. If it did, no one could ever call anyone to anything, since no one is free of shortcoming.

Conversely, the verse does not permit the person with a gap to be comfortable about it. The rebuke is real, the hadith is graphic, and the correct response to "I am not practising what I preach" is not to abandon the preaching but to add the practice. The two errors to avoid are the one that uses imperfection as an excuse for silence, and the one that uses the verse to dismiss a message by pointing at the messenger.

The contemporary forms of this gap are everywhere, and they are worth naming because recognition is the first step. The parent who disciplines a child for lying and lies in front of him. The imam who preaches humility and dominates his committee. The activist who demands accountability and refuses it. The academic who writes about justice and exploits his students. The person whose public religious persona is meticulous and whose private life is not. The organisation whose mission statement is beautiful and whose internal culture is cruel. And in its mildest and most universal form: the gap between what you say matters and what you actually spend your time on.

There is one more thing the verse teaches, and it is the positive counterpart to the rebuke. The scholars of this tradition have always insisted that the most persuasive call is the one that is lived. A single consistent life outweighs a library of exhortation. Which means that the fastest way to close the gap is not to speak less but to align more — and to begin, as the verse itself suggests, by reading the scripture as though it were addressed to you, because it is.

## Verse 45

**"And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble—"**

In simple terms, Allah instructs them to seek His help by means of two disciplines — patience and the prayer — and then adds, with striking honesty, that this is genuinely heavy for everyone except those whose hearts are humble before Him.

*Wa-sta'inu bi-al-sabri wa al-salati* — "and seek help through patience and the prayer." The verb is the derived form meaning to seek assistance, and the help sought is from Allah, as the whole context makes clear. What is worth noticing is *what* is prescribed. Not information, not argument, not a change of circumstance, but two disciplines: one of restraint and one of connection.

*Sabr*, patience, comes from a root meaning to bind and restrain, and the scholars defined it precisely rather than vaguely. It is restraining the self from panic and rebellion when afflicted, restraining the tongue from complaint, and restraining the limbs from wrongdoing. That yields the threefold division standard in the literature: patience in obeying Allah, patience in refraining from disobeying Him, and patience in bearing what He decrees. It is an active capacity, not a passive waiting.

The prayer is the second means, and the pairing is one of the Qur'an's most repeated prescriptions — it appears again at 2:153 and in 11:115 and elsewhere. Why the prayer helps is not mysterious: it is the point at which the servant stands before the One who controls the thing that is troubling him. The Sunnah makes this concrete. Abu Dawud records from Hudhayfah that when a matter distressed the Prophet ﷺ, he would turn to the prayer. And in Sahih Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ described the prayer as light and patience as illumination.

*Wa innaha la-kabiratun* — "and indeed it is burdensome." The feminine pronoun refers most naturally to the prayer and the patience just mentioned. The word *kabirat* means great, heavy, difficult. This is one of the most pastorally valuable sentences in the Qur'an, and it should be lingered over: Allah Himself states that this is hard. The Qur'an does not pretend that the prayer is effortless, or that patience comes naturally, or that anyone who struggles is somehow defective. The difficulty is conceded at the moment the duty is commanded.

*Illa 'ala al-khashi'in* — "except for the humble." *Khushu'* is the interior state of reverent awe before Allah — a quietness of heart in His presence that manifests in the prayer as stillness and attention. For those who have it, the prayer is not a burden but a relief, because a heart already inclined towards its Lord finds rest in turning to Him. For everyone else it is a labour, because the self resists what the self does not want.

That sets up a paradox that is the most practically useful thing in the verse. The prayer is heavy except for the reverent — and yet the prayer is precisely the means by which reverence is acquired. So the reverent are not people who found the prayer easy and therefore did it; they are people who did it and found reverence through it. The way into the loop is to perform it anyway, before it feels like anything. Anyone who waits until the prayer feels light in order to start praying properly will wait forever.

Now, this verse is directly addressed to a people who were being told to do something costly — to accept a prophet whose coming would overturn their position — and it is therefore a verse about how to carry a heavy obedience. The contemporary applications are immediate.

Notice that both prescribed means are *actions*, not feelings. Patience is something you do with your tongue and your limbs. The prayer is something you do with your body at fixed times. Neither requires motivation, and neither waits for it. That is precisely what makes them remedies: on the days when you have no resources left, a discipline can still be performed, where an emotion cannot be summoned. Modern clinical practice has arrived at something structurally similar in its own vocabulary — distress tolerance and grounding exercises, the deliberate regulation of response rather than the correction of feeling — and the correspondence is worth noting as illumination. It should not be overstated into a claim that the verse is a clinical protocol; but it is fair to say that the Qur'an prescribes, for the overwhelmed person, exactly the two things that do not depend on how he feels.

And there is a particular word here for anyone whose prayer has gone flat. The verse diagnoses heaviness as a symptom of absent reverence, and reverence as a fruit of the meeting with the Lord described in the next verse. So the remedy for a heavy prayer is not a new technique, an app, or a better schedule. It is the renewal of certainty about where you are going — which is what verse 46 supplies.

## Verse 46

**"those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return."**

In simple terms, this verse identifies who the humble are: they are the people who are absolutely certain that they are going to meet their Lord and that to Him they are returning.

There is a genuine linguistic crux at the very start of this verse, and it is worth explaining because a reader with a dictionary would be misled by it. The verb is *yazunnuna*, from *zanna*, which in ordinary Arabic usually means to think, suppose or conjecture — that is, to hold an opinion *without* certainty. But here it plainly means the opposite: firm, settled certainty. The commentators and grammarians addressed this directly, noting that *zann* is used in the Qur'an in two contrary senses — in some places for doubt and suspicion, and in others, as here, for knowledge that is settled and unshakeable. The evidence that certainty is meant is the context: the verse is describing the *khashi'in* of verse 45, and the content of their conviction is an article of faith, not a guess. The same usage appears at 2:249: "those who are certain that they will meet Allah."

Some commentators added that the word implies a certainty so complete that it is as though the thing were before the person's eyes. That is exactly what the Prophet ﷺ described in the famous narration of Jibril, recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, when he was asked about *ihsan* and replied: "It is to worship Allah as though you see Him, for if you do not see Him, He sees you." The station of "as though you see Him" is precisely the *zann* of this verse — a conviction with the force of sight.

*Annahum mulaqu rabbihim* — "that they will meet their Lord." The meeting with Allah is one of the great themes of the Qur'an: "Whoever hopes for the meeting with Allah — indeed, the term decreed by Allah is coming" (29:5); "O mankind, indeed you are labouring toward your Lord with [great] exertion, and you will meet Him" (84:6). This is not a metaphor for death alone; it is the personal encounter that follows it.

On the nature of that meeting there is a well-known theological difference that should be stated briefly and accurately. The mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah, relying on verses such as 75:22–23 — "faces that Day will be radiant, looking at their Lord" — and on hadiths transmitted by so many chains that they reach the level of mass transmission, affirm that the believers will truly see their Lord in the Hereafter, in a manner befitting Him and without asking how. The Jahmiyya and the Mu'tazila denied the seeing and interpreted those texts otherwise. The disagreement is real, and the mainstream position is the first.

*Wa annahum ilayhi raji'un* — "and that to Him they will return." The return is the second certainty, and it is the phrase Muslims are commanded to say when someone dies: "Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return" (2:156) — a verse that appears later in this very surah.

Now notice how the verse completes the argument of verse 45. Why is the prayer light for these people and heavy for everyone else? Because a person who is certain that he is going to stand before his Lord treats the prayer as a preparation for an appointment he knows he has. The heaviness of the prayer is not a liturgical problem; it is an eschatological one. A prayer that feels like dead weight is very often the symptom of a heart that does not really believe, at the level where it matters, that the meeting is coming. Which means the remedy for a lifeless prayer is not a new technique but a renewed certainty, and the way to renew it is the ordinary means: reflection on death, on the return, on the standing, on the fact that the people who died last year are already past the meeting and you are not.

The practical outworking of this is the phrase at the graveside. "We belong to Allah and to Him we return" is not merely something to say at a funeral; it is a statement about ownership and destination that, held steadily, reorganises a life. You do not own the things you are holding — not the wealth, not the health, not the years, not the people. And you are travelling towards a meeting, not towards an ending. A person who holds both halves of that sentence cannot be destroyed by loss or inflated by gain.

And there is a final, searching application. In a narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever loves to meet Allah, Allah loves to meet him; and whoever hates to meet Allah, Allah hates to meet him." The Companions found this hard, because the thought of death is naturally unwelcome, and the Prophet ﷺ explained: it is not about disliking death itself, but about what a person has prepared. At the approach of death, the believer is given glad tidings of Allah's pleasure, and nothing is dearer to him than what lies ahead, so he loves the meeting and Allah loves to meet him. That is a test worth applying to oneself, gently: if the meeting were announced for tomorrow, what would your first feeling be? The verse's answer to a heavy heart is not to work harder at feeling reverent. It is to become certain about the meeting, and reverence will follow.

## Verse 47

**"O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others."**

In simple terms, this verse repeats the call of verse 40 — remember the favours I gave you — and adds something new: remember that I preferred you over the peoples of your time.

*Ya bani Isra'ila udhkuru ni'mati allati an'amtu 'alaykum* — "O Children of Israel, remember My favour which I bestowed upon you." This is the third time this call appears in the surah, and it will appear again at verse 122. Repetition on this scale is never filler in the Qur'an; it is the rhetorical structure of the whole address. The surah is building a case: you were given, you were given, you were given — and now look at what you have done with it. Gratitude is the hinge on which the entire address turns, and forgetfulness of blessing is presented as the root of everything that follows.

*Wa anni faddaltukum 'ala al-'alamin* — "and that I preferred you over the worlds." What was this preference? The commentators were careful and specific: it was a preference in the favours bestowed, not in essence or race. It consisted in the number of prophets sent to them, the scripture given to them, the signs shown to them, and the honour of being, in their era, the people who carried the revealed law to the world. And *al-'alamin* here is understood by most commentators as the peoples of their time — the nations contemporary with them — rather than every human being who would ever live.

Two clarifications are necessary here, and both matter a great deal for how this verse is read today.

The first is that this preference was conditional and historical, not permanent or inherent. It was a favour tied to obedience, and it passed. The Qur'an states the transfer explicitly: "You are the best nation produced [as an example] for mankind, enjoining what is right and forbidding what is wrong and believing in Allah" (3:110) — and note that the verse immediately ties that status to the enjoining of good, which is to say, to conduct rather than to descent. Islamic theology is unambiguous that there is no favoured lineage. "Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" (49:13). And in a narration reported by Ahmad from the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Prophet ﷺ said: "O people, your Lord is one and your father is one. There is no superiority of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a white over a black, nor of a black over a white, except by taqwa."

The second is that being preferred is a test, not a privilege. This is the lesson the verse is actually built to deliver. The Children of Israel were given more than the nations around them, and the surah is about to spend the next dozen verses detailing how they responded — with the calf, with the demand to see Allah, with the refusal to enter the city, with the breaking of the Sabbath. Favour received and not acknowledged is the precise mechanism by which a community falls. Which is why the command is not "celebrate that you were preferred" but "remember the favour" — remembrance being the thing that produces gratitude rather than entitlement.

There is a direct and uncomfortable word here for the Muslim reader, and it is the reason this verse is in the surah. The community that receives the most is the community most accountable. The Children of Israel were not destroyed because they were abandoned; they were rebuked because they had been given. And the Muslim ummah has been given the final scripture and described in 3:110 as the best nation brought forth for humanity — on the same condition. The warning embedded in this verse is that the title can be forfeited exactly as it was forfeited before.

For an individual reader, the application is quieter but equally sharp. The blessings you did not earn — the family you were born into, the country, the health, the mind, the faith itself — are *ni'mah*, favour. The danger is not forgetting that they exist; everyone knows they exist. The danger is the slow conversion of favour into entitlement, in which the thing you were given becomes the thing you were owed. That conversion is the first step towards the arrogance that ruined Iblis in verse 34, and it is cured by exactly what this verse commands: remember. Remember it was given, remember by Whom, and remember that it can be taken back.

## Verse 48

**"Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given."**

In simple terms, the verse warns of a Day on which every avenue of escape is closed: no person will avail another in the slightest, no intercession will be accepted, no ransom or substitute will be taken, and no one will be given any help.

*Wa-ttaqu yawman* — "and guard yourselves against a Day." The object of the guarding is the Day itself, which means the action required is taken now: you prepare for that Day by what you do before it arrives. The verse is placed immediately after the catalogue of favours is announced, and that placement is part of the argument. Blessing is followed by accountability, because blessing always is.

*La tajzi nafsun 'an nafsin shay'an* — "no soul will avail another soul in anything." This is the principle of individual accountability, and the Qur'an states it in many places: "No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another" (6:164, 35:18, 53:38). It does not mean a person is not held responsible for his influence on others — leadership carries its own accounting, as the hadith of Jarir showed at verse 41. It means that no one else's merit can be substituted for yours on that Day. Your father's piety, your teacher's rank, your community's reputation — none of it can be transferred to your account.

*Wa la yuqbalu minha shafa'ah* — "nor will intercession be accepted from it." This raises an apparent difficulty that needs to be addressed directly, because the Qur'an and the mass-transmitted Sunnah both affirm intercession, and a careless reading of this verse could suggest otherwise. The reconciliation the scholars gave rests on two observations.

First, the context. The verse is describing the disbelievers, and the phrase is best read as "no intercession will be accepted **for it**" — for that soul. That is exactly what 74:48 says: "So the intercession of intercessors will not benefit them."

Second, intercession in the Hereafter is real but is entirely dependent on Allah's permission and His pleasure with the person interceded for. The Qur'an states this restriction in several places, most clearly at 2:255 — "Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?" — and at 20:109: "Intercession will not avail that Day except for those to whom the Most Merciful has granted permission and whose word He is pleased with." So what this verse denies is intercession for those who died in disbelief, and intercession conceived as an independent claim upon Allah — which is how the pagans understood their idols. It does not deny the intercession established by hadiths reaching the level of mass transmission: the Prophet ﷺ said, in a narration recorded in the two Sahihs, that every prophet has one supplication that is answered, and that he has stored his as intercession for his community on the Day of Resurrection. A narration recorded by Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi states that his intercession is for the people of major sins among his community; scholars differed on its chain, with a number grading it acceptable. What is certain from the mutawatir narrations is that intercession is real, that it requires Allah's permission, and that it benefits believers, not those who died rejecting Him.

*Wa la yu'khadhu minha 'adlun* — "nor will any ransom be taken from it." *'Adl* here means a substitute or equivalent offered in place of the person: a buyout. The Qur'an is emphatic elsewhere that no sum would suffice — "Indeed, those who disbelieve, if they had all that is on the earth and the like of it with it by which to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Resurrection, it would not be accepted from them" (5:36), and "if every soul that did wrong possessed everything on earth, it would offer it in ransom" (10:54).

*Wa la hum yunsarun* — "nor will they be helped." No rescue from any quarter.

Taken together, the four negations close every escape route a human being actually relies upon: another person's merit, another person's word on your behalf, your own wealth, and any external power that might deliver you. Those are precisely the four things people build their lives around — who you know, who speaks for you, what you own, and who can get you out of trouble. The verse declares all four null on that Day.

The positive counterpart is stated elsewhere in this surah: "Whatever good you send forward for yourselves, you will find it with Allah" (2:110, and again at 73:20). The only thing that travels is what you sent ahead.

And the Sunnah applies this with total directness. In a narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah, when this verse's teaching was made concrete, the Prophet ﷺ stood and called his own clans one by one: "O Banu Hashim, buy yourselves from Allah — I can avail you nothing with Him. O Banu 'Abd Manaf, I can avail you nothing with Him. O 'Abbas, son of 'Abd al-Muttalib, I can avail you nothing with Him. O Safiyyah, aunt of the Messenger of Allah, I can avail you nothing with Him. O Fatimah, daughter of Muhammad — ask me whatever you wish of my wealth, but I can avail you nothing with Him."

That is the verse lived. The most beloved man to Allah addressing the people closest to him, and telling them that the relationship will not function as a currency on that Day. If it does not work for the daughter of the Prophet ﷺ, it will not work for anyone. Which means the practical conclusion is not despair but a reallocation of effort: spend less on building the connections, the wealth and the reputation that will be worthless on that Day, and more on the deeds that will not be.

## Verse 49

**"˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord."**

In simple terms, this is the first specific favour named in the catalogue: Allah saved the Children of Israel from Pharaoh's people, who were subjecting them to the worst kind of punishment — killing their newborn sons and sparing their daughters for servitude — and in all of that there was a great trial from their Lord.

*Wa idh najjaynakum min ali Fir'awna* — "and when We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh." Note that the deliverance is from *al* Fir'awn, the people, household and apparatus of Pharaoh, not from Pharaoh alone. That is a precise and deliberately political phrasing. Tyranny is not one man; it is a system of officials, soldiers, scribes and enforcers who carry it out, and the Qur'an names it as such. The same phrasing recurs at 14:6, where the verse is nearly identical to this one.

*Yasumunakum su'a al-'adhab* — "who were afflicting you with the worst of torment." The verb *yasumunakum* comes from a root meaning to impose something upon someone continuously and repeatedly — so the emphasis is on sustained, systematic imposition rather than a single act of cruelty. And *su'a al-'adhab*, "the evil of punishment," denotes its worst kind: the Qur'an describes elsewhere what it consisted of — forced labour, humiliation, and the killing, as in 28:4 and 7:127ff.

*Yudhabbihuna abna'akum wa yastahyuna nisa'akum* — "slaughtering your sons and keeping your women alive." The first verb is in an intensive form indicating repeated mass slaughter. The second is the crueller word: *yastahyuna* means "they let live," "they spared." The sparing was not mercy. The girls were kept alive for servitude and degradation, and the commentators noted that the phrasing itself exposes the horror — a "mercy" that was worse than the killing it replaced.

Why the boys? The Qur'an's own account points to Pharaoh's fear: "And We wanted to confer favour upon those who were oppressed in the land and make them leaders and make them inheritors, and establish them in the land, and show Pharaoh and Haman and their soldiers through them that which they were fearing" (28:5–6). Beyond that, the familiar details — Pharaoh's dream, the soothsayers' prediction, the astrologers' warning — belong to the narrative material that entered the commentaries from earlier scriptural tradition. They are reported and they are well known, but they should be cited as reports rather than as established fact, and nothing in the lesson depends on them. What the Qur'an establishes is the fact of the slaughter, the fact of the deliverance, and the fact that Allah's plan was being worked out through the very thing Pharaoh was trying to prevent.

*Wa fi dhalikum bala'un min rabbikum 'azim* — "and in that was a great trial from your Lord." The commentators differed over what "that" refers to, and both readings are transmitted. Some said the trial was the deliverance itself: having been saved, would they be grateful, or would they corrupt themselves the moment the pressure lifted? Others said the trial was the affliction: would they be patient? The strongest reading gathers both — the whole episode, rescue and suffering alike, was a *bala'*, a testing that carried both a burden and a favour. The word itself holds both meanings in Arabic: a trial that afflicts, and a blessing that is being tested by how it is received.

The applications here are unusually direct.

The first is political and theological at once: liberation from oppression is presented by the Qur'an as a divine favour of the first order, one worth naming first in the catalogue of blessings. Freedom from tyranny is not a secular add-on to the religious life; it is among the things Allah counts as a gift for which gratitude is owed. And the verse names the slaughter of children as the paradigmatic atrocity — which is why the killing of children remains, in this tradition, among the gravest of crimes and among the things the Qur'an records as the signature of Pharaoh's rule.

The second is about the nature of the test. The deliverance was itself the test. This is the pattern of the whole surah's address to them: they were given, and the giving was the examination. It is also the hardest lesson for any community that has come through a hard season into an easier one — that prosperity tests differently from adversity and is, if anything, more dangerous. The Children of Israel passed through the sea and then, within weeks, worshipped a calf. The affliction did not ruin them; the rescue nearly did.

And the third is the hidden mercy in the verse's closing words. Calling the episode a *bala' min rabbikum* — a trial *from your Lord* — reframes it. What was being done to them was permitted by the same Lord who was going to save them, which means the suffering was not meaningless and not outside His knowledge. The One who decreed the deliverance also decreed the delay, and both were from Him.

## Verse 50

**"And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes."**

In simple terms, this is the second favour: Allah split the sea for them, saved them, and drowned Pharaoh and his army while they watched.

*Wa idh faraqna bikum al-bahra* — "and when We parted the sea for you." The verb means to split and separate, and the preposition indicates that the parting was done so that they might pass through it. The Qur'an gives the fuller picture elsewhere: "Then We inspired to Moses: 'Strike the sea with your staff,' and it parted, and each portion was like a great towering mountain" (26:63), and "And leave the sea in stillness; indeed, they are an army to be drowned" (44:24). The narrative is told at length in Surah al-Shu'ara and Surah Ta-Ha: Moses strikes, the sea opens, the Children of Israel cross, Pharaoh and his army pursue, and the sea closes over them. Transmitted details in the commentaries — such as the sea parting into twelve separate paths corresponding to the tribes — are reported by some early authorities but are not stated in the Qur'anic text, and should be cited as reports.

*Fa-anjaynakum* — "and We saved you." The same root of rescue used in verse 49, so the two favours are deliberately linked: rescued *from* Pharaoh, and rescued *through* the sea.

*Wa aghraqna ala Fir'awna* — "and We drowned the people of Pharaoh." Again the emphasis on the apparatus, not only the man, though Pharaoh himself drowned with them — and the Qur'an records his belated declaration at the moment of drowning and the reply: "Now? And you had disobeyed before and were of the corrupters? So today We will save you in body, that you may be to those who succeed you a sign" (10:91–92).

*Wa antum tanzurun* — "while you were looking on." This is the detail the verse closes on, and it carries real weight. They saw it. The deliverance and the destruction were not reported to them second-hand; they watched the army that had enslaved them disappear behind them while they stood safe on the far shore. The commentators noted the purpose: the favour was made visible so that there could be no doubt about its source and no excuse for forgetting it.

A caution belongs here about a modern claim that has become popular. Because 10:92 speaks of Pharaoh's body being preserved as a sign, some contemporary writers have identified a particular pharaoh of the Exodus and pointed to a particular preserved mummy as the fulfilment of that verse. It should be said plainly that these identifications are speculative and contested. The date of the Exodus, the identity of the pharaoh involved and the identification of any specific mummy are all disputed among specialists, and the majority of historians and Egyptologists reject the confident versions of the claim. The Qur'anic statement is that his body was preserved as a sign; which body that is, we cannot establish. Attaching the verse to a contested identification only puts the text at the mercy of a debate that is not settled.

Now the deeper lesson of this verse, which is the one the surah is building towards.

These people saw the sea part. They watched their oppressors drown. And then, within a matter of weeks, while Moses was on the mountain, they took a calf and worshipped it — as this very surah records at verse 51 and again at verse 92. That is the devastating point, and the Qur'an places it there deliberately. Miraculous evidence does not produce faith. These are the people who witnessed the most spectacular public miracle in their history and then melted their jewellery.

That should recalibrate how a modern reader thinks about signs. The objection "if God would just show me something, I would believe" is answered by this verse: a whole nation was shown something, and it did not hold them. The Qur'an states the principle explicitly: "And nothing prevented Us from sending signs except that the former peoples denied them" (17:59). Signs were given, and they were denied — which is why the central challenge of this surah, at verse 23, is not a demand to produce a miracle but an invitation to examine a text. Faith, in the Qur'an's account, is not the mechanical result of overwhelming evidence; it is a moral response to truth, and it can be refused in the presence of a parted sea.

The other great theme is the fate of the arrogant. Pharaoh is the Qur'an's archetype of pride, the human echo of Iblis from verse 34 — a being who said in substance "I am greater," and was brought down. "So Allah seized him with the exemplary punishment of the last and the first" (79:25). The deliverance of the oppressed and the destruction of the arrogant are presented here as a single divine act, seen from two sides.

And there is one more thing for the reader who is oppressed, or who has been waiting a long time. The verse says *wa antum tanzurun* — you will see it. Allah does not always remove a tyrant at the moment His servants would choose, but the pattern of this story is that the oppressed end up standing on the far shore, watching. The appropriate posture in the meantime is the one named in verses 45 and 46: patience, prayer, and certainty about the meeting with your Lord.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Verses 41 to 50 complete one movement of the address to the Children of Israel and begin another. The first six verses state what is demanded: believe in the revelation that confirms what you already hold; do not lead the rejection and do not sell the signs for a cheap price; do not blur truth into falsehood and do not suppress what you know; pray, give the due, and join the praying community; and stop commanding what you will not do — seeking help instead through the two disciplines of patience and prayer, which are heavy for everyone except those who are certain that they are going to meet their Lord and return to Him. The last four verses turn to why the demand is owed. Remember the favours, and remember the Day on which no connection, no intercessor, no ransom and no rescue will avail; and then remember the two great deliverances — from Pharaoh's slaughter, and through the sea that closed behind you while you watched.

Three threads hold it together. First, blessing creates obligation: the entire catalogue of favours exists to make the original demand unanswerable, and the pattern of the whole address is given-then-accountable. Second, the corruption of those who carry religious knowledge is the specific danger the passage is aimed at — mixing truth with falsehood, concealing it, selling it, and preaching it without practising it — and the Qur'an treats all four as variations on one disease. And third, the remedies the Qur'an prescribes are disciplines rather than feelings: patience, prayer, zakah, congregation, and above all the certainty of the meeting with your Lord, which is the one thing that makes every other act of worship light rather than heavy. It is worth noting where the argument has arrived: the people who saw the sea part later worshipped a calf. Evidence does not produce faith. That is why the surah's central challenge was not a miracle but a text, and why the address now proceeds to the favours that were met, again and again, with refusal.
