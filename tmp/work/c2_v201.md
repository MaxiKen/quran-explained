**A PRAYER IN FULL**

Read in its phrases, the prayer arrives with three requests already in its mouth: ***“Yet there are others”***, ***“who say, “Our Lord!”***, ***“Grant us the good of this world”***, ***“and the Hereafter, and protect us from the torment of the Fire.””***. and the final phrase points to the fire it asks to escape. The order of the requests is the order of a balanced life: the good of the standing world first, the good of the standing home second, and the shelter from the accounting third. The prayer is answered before it is finished because the same Book that teaches it promises the answers, and the walk keeps the requests in the sequence the liturgy keeps them. Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss on the two goods runs through knowledge and worship in the first and the gardens in the second.

**ASKING FOR BOTH**

The prayer taught in this verse is the correction of the half-request read out at the monuments in the verse before it. The speaker of the complete prayer asks for the good of this world and the good of the next in one breath, and the joining is the discipline: the two goods are requested together so that neither becomes an idol. Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss fills the first request with concrete goods — knowledge, worship, protection from sins, and the provisions of the road — and the second with the gardens, which turns the prayer into a syllabus rather than a wish. The reward promised to the people of this exact prayer is the double reward, granted in the currency each world pays in (3:148 — **“So Allah gave them the reward of this world and the excellent reward of the Hereafter. For Allah loves the good-doers”**). Because the requests are spoken together, the servant is trained to want both without splitting the loyalty behind the wanting.

**GUARDING THE THIRD ASK**

The third request is the one the first two need: protection from the torment of the Fire. The prayer that asks for goods without asking for shelter has not understood the accounting that the following verse describes, and the ordering of the requests teaches the understanding. The safeguard is asked in the same voice as the bounty because the same hand gives both. The petition of the believers who came after their leaders carries the same care for the exposed: they ask forgiveness for themselves and for those who preceded them in faith, and the prayer reaches backward as well as forward (59:10 — **“And those who come after them will pray, “Our Lord! Forgive us and our fellow believers who preceded us in faith,”**). Because the request is addressed to the One who hears the monument, the third ask is heard with the first two.

**ANSWERED BEFORE ASKED**

The verse that teaches this prayer sits beside the verse that judges the two prayers, so the teaching and the grading are placed in the same schoolroom. The application is to recite the full request at the memorials of the year and to notice which clause arrives in the mouth first. The person who finds himself asking only for the world has read the previous verse's account and should read this one again. Because the prayer is taught in the words of the Qur'an, the vocabulary of the servant's need is supplied by the revelation itself, and the supply is one of the gifts the prayer asks for. The complete prayer keeps both homes in view and the fire behind the eyes.

The three stages of the attachment are described in the early commentary with the honesty of a clinic: the terror at the chest, the teeth of the show, and the press upon the belly. Each stage marks a different grip of the world upon the heart, and the anatomy rewards patient study. The rider who has fled the field at the height of battle understands the first stage, while the shopper who cannot leave the market understands the last. Because the grip is described rather than denounced, the student of the self can locate the exact point where the bridle begins to slip. Catching the slip early is the whole craft of the discipline. The heart at the moment of the flight is the textbook of the greed described here, and the study of one's own flight is the commentary that never leaves the desk. Three stages, one animal, and a rider who must choose which stage he will answer.
