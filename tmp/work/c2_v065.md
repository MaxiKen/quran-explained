**A TOWN THAT SET TRAPS FOR ITS OWN LAW**

The address opens as a reminder of what every listener already possessed: ***“You are already aware of those of you”***. The knowledge named here is the knowledge of recognition, and the oath form that carries it marks the story as common property among the people addressed because it was their own history, not a tale imported to shame them. The crime is then named with the word for crossing a limit, which places the act at the level of defiance rather than slip. The phrase that locates the crime has also been read closely, since the transgression fell in the ordinance of the Sabbath rather than in the day as a piece of time. The name of the day itself carries the sense of rest and ceasing, and an early lexicography connects it with the cutting off of work, so the day was a weekly lesson in who governs labour. The setting the reports give is a town by the sea in the age of David, and the fuller account supplies every detail the short verse assumes: (7:163 — **“Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ about ˹the people of˺ the town which was by the sea, who broke the Sabbath. During the Sabbath,”**). Fishing was forbidden to them on that day, and the sea answered the law with its own sign. Fish surfaced in visible abundance on Saturdays and stayed hidden the rest of the week, so the test was made plain before the verdict arrived.

**A PROHIBITION DEFEATED BY ITS OWN FINE PRINT**

What the town did with that sign is the lesson of the whole passage. They did not at first fish on the Sabbath in the open. They set nets and ropes and channels before the day began, let the fish collect themselves against the devices while the law stood, and gathered the catch after the day had passed. The procedure kept the letter and gutted the intent, which is the exact anatomy of every evasion built on fine print. The accounts record that the practice began as pretence and ended as open hunting, and that a company among them warned their people and pressed them to stop. When the warnings were refused, those who forbade the wrong removed themselves to the other end of the town, and the Book gives their fate as the mirror of the other: (7:165 — **“When they ignored the warning they were given, We rescued those who used to warn against evil”**). The division of the town is therefore part of the teaching. A community that splits over a sin is not left to share one fate, because the warners were pulled out of the ruin they had refused to join. What follows for any age is the measure the devices expose. A prohibition answered by engineering proves that the heart behind it never submitted; the difference between obedience and evasion is not the difference between two techniques but between two states of the soul.

**WHEN A VERDICT TAKES THE SHAPE OF THE CRIME**

The sentence of the court is a single line: ***“who broke the Sabbath. We said to them, “Be disgraced apes!””***. The second word of the sentence carries the sense of the driven off, the creature sent away with contempt, and the lexicography of it is drawn from the driving of a dog from a door. The reports describe what followed the word. The young among them were changed into apes, and one account from Ibn ʿAbbās divides the fate by age, the young becoming apes and the old becoming swine. Qatādah describes the apes as howling creatures with tails who still recognised their families and approached them weeping, since the humanity they had debased was not yet out of memory, and the accounts agree that the transformed died within three days. The Prophet was asked about the apes and swine that people see, and Ibn Masʿūd reports his answer: *"when Allah sends down this particular kind of punishment on a people, the race comes to an end with this, and there is no further breeding"* (Muslim). The Book itself keeps the fate in view whenever their later history is counted: (5:60 — **“It is those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure—some being reduced to apes and”**). A verdict that reshapes the body answers a crime that treated the sacred as a technicality, and the symmetry of the two is part of the warning.

**A VERDICT OF BODIES, NOT ONLY OF HEARTS**

Two readings of the sentence have stood in the field, and the disagreement is early rather than modern. One reading takes the command as its words stand, so that the people were changed in form, and it rests on the reports just described: the kinship recognition, the weeping, the death within days, and the Prophet’s own ruling that such a people leave no progeny behind. The other reading, transmitted from Mujāhid, takes the change as one of the heart, comparing the case to the parable of the bearers of the Torah: (62:5 — **“The example of those who were entrusted with ˹observing˺ the Torah but failed to do so,”**). A third treatment makes the line rhetorical, like the address elsewhere that names what a stubborn party may as well become: (17:50 — **“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹Yes, even if˺ you become stones, or iron”**). The reading followed here is the first, because the transmitted material about the event is material about bodies and deaths and lineages, and a moral metaphor does not leave corpses or end a breeding line. What the other readings guard is not discarded: in other words, the example teaches with equal force that conduct shapes a person until the shape answers to it, and the Book’s comparison of the unfaithful bearers of a book to a beast of burden is the moral form of the same lesson. The command of the verdict also carries the word of creation itself. At a single utterance forms can change, which is why every ordinance stands for eternity in the custody of the One who says to a thing only that it is.
