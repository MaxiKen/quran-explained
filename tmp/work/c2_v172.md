**LAWFUL PROVISION AS A SIGN OF WORSHIP**

The sequence reaches lawful provision as a sign of worship through ***“O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided”***. The preceding verse addressed all humanity; this one addresses believers specifically. Faith changes the way a person receives provision. Food is not merely fuel or pleasure. It is a gift from Allah, obtained through a chain of created means, and it should be consumed within the limits He has set. The command combines eating good things with gratitude, showing that bodily nourishment and spiritual worship are not enemies. Believers may enjoy lawful food without treating enjoyment as shameful. The Qur’an rejects ascetic inventions that declare Allah’s lawful adornments forbidden. At the same time, enjoyment must remain disciplined by moderation, health, justice, and gratitude. *Shukr* begins in the heart by recognizing Allah as the giver. It appears on the tongue through praise and the mention of His name, and it appears in action by consuming responsibly and using strength in obedience. Saying praise belongs to God after a meal is beautiful, but gratitude is incomplete if a person wastes food, hoards it while neighbours are hungry, or uses the resulting energy for oppression. The believer also thanks the people through whom provision arrives. Islam commands gratitude to parents, workers, hosts, farmers, and all who provide lawful benefit, while remembering that their ability is itself from Allah. Appreciation of created means does not compete with divine oneness; it is part of good character. (2:168 — **“O humanity! Eat from what is lawful”**) Because lawful provision as a sign of worship governs 2:172, gratitude as proof of devotion arises from lawful provision as a sign of worship. It follows that food, justice, and community completes lawful provision as a sign of worship through gratitude as proof of devotion.

**GRATITUDE AS PROOF OF DEVOTION**

Gratitude as proof of devotion takes shape in ***“for you. And give thanks to Allah”***. The closing condition connects dietary practice to exclusive worship. If Allah alone is worshipped, then His servant accepts His guidance about what to consume rather than allowing appetite, fashion, or commercial pressure to define every limit. Worship is not confined to prayer in a mosque. Choosing lawful food, earning lawful income, and refusing exploitation can all become acts of worship when done for Allah. The phrase also prevents ritual from becoming disconnected from ethics. A person cannot claim that he worships Allah while deliberately consuming stolen wealth or supporting cruelty when lawful alternatives are available. What enters the body affects the heart, and the heart’s state affects worship. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah is good and accepts only what is good, then described a traveller whose supplication is hindered by unlawful food, drink, clothing, and nourishment, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith places a high value on lawful provision. It does not mean that every poor person’s prayer is rejected or that every affluent person’s prayer is accepted. It warns that deliberate unlawful consumption can become a barrier to spiritual receptivity. The Prophet also taught moderation. The stomach should not be filled without restraint, and the believer should leave room for food, drink, and breath, according to the well-known report in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī. Moderation protects health and helps a person remain energetic for worship and service. (7:31 — **“Dress properly whenever you are at worship. Eat”**) The reason food, justice, and community follows gratitude as proof of devotion lies in lawful provision as a sign of worship. Therefore, food, justice, and community returns practical force to gratitude as proof of devotion.

**FOOD, JUSTICE, AND COMMUNITY**

The wording for food, justice, and community is ***“if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺”***. Zakah, voluntary charity, fair wages, honest trade, and refusing food waste are expressions of gratitude. A society cannot praise *shukr* while allowing preventable hunger through greed or corruption. The Prophet ﷺ said that the one who is not concerned with the affairs of Muslims is not following the proper spirit of the community, and he encouraged feeding the hungry. The exact legal duties vary with circumstances, but the moral direction is clear: Allah’s gifts should circulate with mercy. The verse prepares for the specific prohibitions in the next āyah. Muslims do not invent dietary restrictions from superstition, caste, or religious competition. They follow what Allah has made lawful and unlawful, and they avoid both excess and negligence. A food is not spiritually powerful merely because a culture labels it so, nor is a lawful blessing made impure by baseless fear. A clean vessel can carry clean water, while a contaminated vessel spoils even what was pure when poured into it. Lawful food and thankful worship belong together in that way: what sustains the body enters the moral life rather than remaining a merely private purchase. Present-day supply chains can hide exploitation, fraud, or forbidden earnings behind attractive packaging, but concealment does not change the source. The verse asks the worshipper to care about the vessel of livelihood as seriously as the visible act of devotion. (16:114 — **“So eat from the good, lawful things”**) In other words, food, justice, and community answers lawful provision as a sign of worship.
