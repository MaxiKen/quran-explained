***“O children of Israel!”*** calls the descendants of Jacob by their father’s honoured name. Israel here is Jacob, the son of Isaac and grandson of Abraham. It is a name in the history of the prophets, not a claim that descent alone secures salvation. Ibn ʿAbbās explains the name through the sense of being God’s servant. The appeal therefore recalls an ancestor whose worth lay in serving God. His children are invited to act in keeping with that inheritance. A similar appeal recalls Noah, (17:3 — **“He was indeed a grateful servant.””**). The honour of a forefather should move later people toward his faithfulness, not excuse them from it. In the setting of this chapter, the address comes especially to the Jewish communities of Madinah and its surroundings. Their knowledge of earlier revelation makes the coming reminder close and recognizable. After the account of humanity’s first father, the Quran now addresses a family of nations with its own long record of divine gifts. This narrows the historical setting without changing the rule just stated: guidance must be followed when it comes from God.

**MEMORY THAT CALLS FOR GRATITUDE**

The first demand is ***“Remember My favours upon you.”***. Remembering here must do more than preserve stories about a distinguished past. The favours call for gratitude toward the One who gave them. Mujāhid includes the water brought from rock, the manna and quails, and rescue from Pharaoh’s rule. Abū al-ʿĀliyah draws attention to prophets raised among them and Books revealed for their guidance. Bodily safety and revealed teaching thus belong within the same remembrance. The later generations did not themselves pass through the sea, but their life as a community depended upon that rescue. Because a gift can reach children through their parents, God may remind the children of what He gave their fathers. Moses had made the same appeal: (5:20 — **“Remember Allah’s favours upon you when He raised prophets from among you, made you sovereign,”**). Such memory should humble rather than inflate them. It begins with what God did, not with a claim that their own worth forced His hand. The right answer is to use the inherited good in obedience to its Giver.

**A COMMITMENT WITH DUTIES AND A PROMISED RETURN**

The reminder becomes a demand for faithfulness: ***“Fulfil your covenant”***. The covenant is not an empty title of honour. It binds conduct. Qatādah and Mujāhid connect it with the covenant whose terms include (5:12 — **“If you establish prayer, pay alms-tax, believe in My messengers, support them, and lend to Allah a good loan,”**). Prayer, giving, belief, and support for the messengers form a single duty toward God. A people cannot preserve that duty by accepting earlier messengers while rejecting a later one whom God sends. This is why Ibn ʿAbbās applies the covenant here to belief in Muhammad and obedience to his message. The specific application does not replace the wider covenant; it brings that covenant to bear on the choice now before the hearers. Their own received scripture is not offered as a shelter from further truth. It is part of the evidence that should prepare them to recognize it. To fulfil a pledge is to carry it through when its demand becomes costly, not merely to honour it while it confirms an established position.

God’s reply is ***“and I will fulfil Mine,”***. The two sides are related, but they are not equal powers bargaining over an uncertain result. God sets the terms and freely promises the reward. Human obedience does not place Him under a debt imposed by anyone else. His own promise is the firm ground of hope. Al-Suddī explains the promised fulfilment as admission to Paradise for those who obey. The language of covenant therefore gives hope a clear direction: attend to what has been required and trust the One who has promised. Elsewhere the Quran asks, (9:111 — **“And whose promise is truer than Allah’s?”**). The uncertainty lies in human faithfulness, not in God’s truth. This duty also reaches those who now read the verse as Muslims; it is not a failing safely assigned to another community. A report carried by Muslim describes a flag raised beside each betrayer on the Day of Judgement, its size marking the extent of the betrayal. A broken trust that seemed hidden in worldly life is then made plain. A pledge must be kept; a good name among loyal people cannot replace the deed.

**AWE THAT OUTWEIGHS THE PRESSURE OF PEOPLE**

The closing command guards the covenant: ***“and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺”***. The wording gives God the sole place in the awe that governs obedience. It does not deny the natural fear of a danger or demand that a person stop taking wise precautions. It refuses to let fear of losing rank, followers, or income become a reason to betray known truth. In the address to the keepers of the Torah, the same demand is stated directly: (5:44 — **“So do not fear the people; fear Me!”**). The relation is close. People entrusted with a Book may know what it requires yet shrink from saying so when their audience resists. Since the covenant is with God, the audience cannot release them from it. The reminder of favours first awakens gratitude; the promise then gives hope; the final command supplies the fear that checks disloyalty. Neither fear alone nor a claim of inherited favour is allowed to govern the response. Jacob’s descendants are called back to the service by which their father was honoured: receiving God’s gifts with thanks and keeping His word above every competing claim.
