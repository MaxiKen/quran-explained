**THE ADDRESS TURNS TO A NEW AUDIENCE**

The story of Adam closes and the sūrah turns to a people who will occupy most of its remaining pages: ***“O children of Israel!”***, ***“Remember My favours upon you. Fulfil your covenant”***, ***“and I will fulfil Mine”***, ***“and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺”***. The change of address is abrupt and deliberate. After describing the first household and the promise made to everyone leaving the garden, the Book speaks directly to the community that had inherited a scripture, a history, and a set of obligations, and it speaks to them with the same word it has used throughout the chapter: remember. What follows in the sūrah is a long account of what that community did with its inheritance, and this verse stands at the door of the account with an invitation rather than an accusation. The favours named in the sentence are not itemised here, and the reader is expected to know what they were. The Book spells them out later in the chapter with a list that runs from the rescue at the sea to the shade in the desert and the manna from the sky, and each item is introduced with the same command to recall. The word for favour names a benefit freely given, which means the remembering is not about flattery. A person who has been carried for generations is being asked to notice by whom, and the notice is the first step of gratitude. Everything else in the passage follows from whether that step happens.

**HOW A COVENANT IS PRESENTED**

The middle of the verse sets out a transaction, and the Book does not disguise its shape. Fulfil your covenant and I will fulfil Mine: the two halves are placed side by side, and the verse leaves them side by side without turning the relationship into a bargain between two equals. Even the phrasing shows the asymmetry, since the command falls on the human party while the promise of fulfilment is spoken by the Lord who did not need the covenant in the first place. The Book gives the same covenant a detailed form later in the chapter: (2:63 — **“And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you”**), and it summarises the terms at the beginning of another passage: (5:12 — **“Allah made a covenant with the Children of Israel and appointed twelve leaders from among them and ˹then˺ said,”**). The pattern is consistent. The covenant is an arrangement of grace in which the initiative belongs to one side and the obligation to the other. The verse then ends with a command that fits the arrangement: stand in awe of Me alone. The word is the same one the sūrah opened with when it described the mindful, which means the reader who has followed the chapter from its first line has arrived back at the beginning with more information than he had. Awe of one is the practical form of loyalty to one, and it is offered here as the single alternative to the kind of divided attention the earlier verses described so carefully. The children of Israel are not accused yet. They are told what their position requires of them.

**AN INHERITANCE THAT CAN STILL BE HONOURED**

The practical use of the verse for a reader who is not a child of Israel is the question of inheritance, because every reader receives something from the generations before him. A tradition, a language, a set of habits, a community that carried him when he could not carry himself, and a revealed book that reached him through other people's labour: the list of favours in the sentence is longer in his own case than he assumes. The verse asks two things of whoever holds such an inheritance, remembrance and fulfilment, and both are within reach today. Remembering requires the naming of what was given, and fulfilment requires one concrete act of loyalty to the terms attached to it. Today the exercise is to write down three gifts received from the generations before him and, beside each, one obligation that comes with it. The Book is not asking for nostalgia; it is asking for the response that the gift has always required. A person who honours one such obligation has answered this verse in the way the children of Israel were being invited to answer it, with a deed rather than a sentiment, and the chapter's later verses will show what happens to a community that chooses the sentiment alone.
