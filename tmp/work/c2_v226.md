**THE OATH OF ĪLĀʾ**

***“Those who swear not to have intercourse with their”*** establishes the oath of īlāʾ. This verse addresses īlāʾ, an oath by which a husband commits himself to abstaining from marital intimacy with his wife. In pre-Islamic practice, such an oath could leave a woman suspended indefinitely: neither enjoying the rights of marriage nor being released through divorce. The Qur’an sets a limit of four months and then requires a decision. The rule protects the wife from being used as a prisoner of anger. A husband may need temporary distance after a dispute, but he cannot turn an oath into permanent emotional or physical abandonment. Marriage is a covenant of rights, and a person cannot use the name of Allah to destroy those rights without consequence. The four months provide time for reflection, reconciliation, and the cooling of anger. They are not an invitation to delay until the final day while offering no communication or support. During the period, the husband remains responsible for maintenance and respectful treatment, and the couple may seek mediation or counselling. Classical jurists discuss whether the period begins with the oath, how it applies to different forms of intimacy, and what happens when the wife is pregnant, ill, or otherwise affected. The exact legal procedure is handled by a qualified judge or scholar. The moral boundary is clear: the wife must not be left indefinitely in uncertainty. If they return—fa-in fāʾū—means that the husband resumes marital relations and ends the harmful abstention within the permitted time. (5:89 — **“Allah will not call you to account for your thoughtless oaths,”**) Because the oath of īlāʾ frames the four-month period, the four-month period returns attention to the oath of īlāʾ. Ibn ʿAbbās understands the four months as a real limit on an oath of abstention, after which return requires restored marital life rather than indefinite suspension.

**THE FOUR-MONTH PERIOD**

***“wives must wait for four months. If they change”*** deepens the four-month period. Allah’s forgiveness and mercy welcome the return. The husband should also repent for the harm caused, fulfil his wife’s rights, and not repeat the oath as a form of control. Depending on the juristic analysis, breaking the oath may require the expiation described in 5:89, while the procedure of īlāʾ has its own legal consequences. A person should not use a legal technicality to avoid the spirit of the ruling. Repentance includes restoring emotional safety and trust. The verse prepares for 2:227, which says that if the husband resolves upon divorce, Allah hears and knows. The husband therefore has two responsible paths after the period: return honourably or proceed toward divorce through lawful procedure. He may not keep a wife suspended simply because he refuses to decide. This principle appears elsewhere in the prohibition against retaining a divorced woman to harm her. The Qur’an repeatedly closes the door to using marriage status as a weapon. A spouse is not property to be held or released according to one person’s anger. Many oaths of abstention are made during anger. Islam recognizes anger but does not let a moment of emotion govern another person’s life indefinitely. The Prophet ﷺ taught that true strength is controlling oneself in anger, and he encouraged silence, changing position, ablution, and seeking refuge in Allah. A person who frequently threatens divorce or vows to abandon intimacy should seek help. (2:227 — **“But if they settle on divorce, then Allah is indeed All-Hearing, All-Knowing”**) Since returning to the marriage follows the oath of īlāʾ, the four-month period links purpose to returning to the marriage.

**RETURNING TO THE MARRIAGE**

***“their mind, then Allah is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”*** clarifies returning to the marriage. Repeated threats damage the household even when no legal divorce occurs. Taqwā requires choosing words carefully and not making Allah’s name a weapon. The waiting period can be used for sincere mediation. Trusted relatives, counsellors, or judges may hear both spouses, protect against abuse, and help determine whether return is safe and meaningful. Reconciliation must not be forced upon a person facing violence. Mercy includes protecting the vulnerable and ensuring that return does not simply renew harm. Where the relationship can be repaired, returning can be an act of forgiveness and growth. The husband who returns should not demand that the wife forget everything immediately. He takes responsibility, listens, and rebuilds trust through consistent conduct. The closing names of Allah show that legal limits are not merely punitive. They create a path to mercy. Allah forgives the person who abandons the harmful oath and returns to justice. The wife’s right is protected, the husband’s repentance is accepted, and the family has an opportunity to heal. The verse also warns that Allah’s mercy should not be manipulated. A person who repeatedly harms a spouse and then cites forgiveness without reform has not fulfilled the requirement of return. Think of the oath of īlāʾ through a man who quarrels with his brother and swears never to enter his shop again, and then stands in the street for a year watching the door. The shop is open every day, the brother is behind the counter, and the only thing that keeps the man outside is a sentence he said aloud and never meant. (2:231 — **“When you divorce women and they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period,”**) A boundary against suspension matters, which means returning to the marriage returns a boundary against suspension to the oath of īlāʾ.

**A BOUNDARY AGAINST SUSPENSION**

The oath of īlāʾ confronts anyone treating the four-month period as remote; under the oath of īlāʾ, such a person has stopped speaking to his wife and counts the days of the silence as if the silence were the stronger position. The verse gives him four months and a door, and the oath of īlāʾ asks what he is waiting for. Because the closing names describe a Lord who forgives the broken promise and is merciful to the one who returns, the four-month period makes the oath of īlāʾ show that the way back is open from the first day of the quarrel. Because the sentence ends on the two names, the oath of īlāʾ shows that the whole matter of the oath is left inside the mercy of the Lord. Since a term of four months was set for a quarrel inside a house, the four-month period makes the oath of īlāʾ show that the law cares for the side that cannot speak for itself. A family quarrel is settled by a term, a door and a name, and the verse closes with the gentler of the two names. The four-month period makes the oath of īlāʾ practical; returning to the marriage therefore matters. Today, a spouse must not use silence, absence, or an oath to suspend a marriage while avoiding either reconciliation or release.
