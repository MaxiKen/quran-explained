**THE SENTENCE THAT CLOSES THE DEBATE**

Almost the same words closed an earlier stretch of this argument, and the echo marks the end of a section: ***“That was a community”*** and ***“that had already gone before.”***. The Book's decision to state the same principle twice is not redundancy but punctuation, since the second occurrence falls where the argument has been carried to its furthest point. The intervening verses have taken the reader through the claim about the patriarchs, the witness that was withheld, and the question about who knows better, and this sentence closes the file. The principle stated here had already been stated once, and it is repeated now that the argument has run its course, which shows that the matter was settled on the same ground from the start and is settled there again. Ibn ʿAbbās read the two earnings as goodness credited to each generation separately and the final clause as freedom from answering on the Last Day for another people's conduct.

**THE ACCOUNT THAT CLOSES WITH THE DEAD**

The first clause puts the ancestors in the past tense, so that their page is shown to have been closed. The reason the wording matters is that those who argued about them had been treating them as living assets, and the wording leaves them where they are. Nothing here diminishes them; what is refused is a later generation's attempt to collect on their behalf. Another passage states the same principle in a sentence about the day of reckoning, and the unit of account there is the individual rather than the family: (6:164 — **“No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another”**). What the clause draws is a line where the arguing parties had been trying to cross it.

**TWO ACCOUNTS KEPT SEPARATE**

The middle of the sentence sets out the terms of the accounting in two identical halves, and the echo between the phrases is the argument: ***“For them is what they earned”*** and ***“and for you is what you have earned.”***. The Book sets the two records side by side and refuses to let either be transferred, which answers the whole claim about inherited standing. The word used for earning covers everything a person does, so that the clause leaves room neither for an assumed credit nor for an assumed debt. The point of saying it twice is that the two sides of the sentence are being measured on one scale, which is why no one may borrow against another's record. Another passage carries almost the same words about the same history, and there the repetition closes the discussion at its end: (2:134 — **“That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned”**). Anyone who has counted on the standing of others has the correction in the sentence.

**AN LEDGER THAT CANNOT BE INHERITED**

Think of a man who inherits a warehouse and believes the reputation attached to it will be entered into his own accounts at the day of settlement. The verse separates the two records and leaves each party with what he has done, and the identical phrasing is what makes the separation visible. The comparison explains the Book's repeated returns to the deeds of the ancestors, whose record is a warning and an example rather than a balance a descendant may draw on. Anyone who has seen a family live off a name it no longer deserves can see the situation the sentence describes. The plain arithmetic of a personal account is what the verse sets before him, and the warehouse is a reminder that a reputation is not a balance.

**THE INHERITANCE THAT DOES PASS ON**

The last clause of the formula removes the remainder of the descendants' expectations: ***“And you will not be accountable”*** and ***“for what they have done”***. The two records are left separated, the ancestors already judged and the living still writing, and the long argument about Abraham and his family is settled on that basis. What has been on offer all along is the tradition that can be inherited rather than the standing that cannot, and the account of the man's prayer and his answer has shown what that tradition consists of. Another passage names the inheritance that does transfer, and it describes a community raised up for the benefit of others through the same mercy that answered Abraham's prayer: (52:21 — **“We will elevate their descendants to their rank”**). The choice left is between resting on a closed account and attending to an open one. Today, family honour may open social doors, but it cannot transfer a completed moral record from an ancestor to a descendant.
