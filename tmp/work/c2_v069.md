**A COLOUR GIVEN AND A FIELD NARROWED AGAIN**

The second request is about appearance, and the answer this time is a description that can be seen from a distance: ***“They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify”***, ***“for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should be a bright yellow cow”***, ***“pleasant to see.’””*** The animal is now middle-aged and unmistakably coloured, and the questions have removed two large classes of cattle from the herd in two verses. What the verse adds to the description is the effect the colour has on the people who look at it, and that detail is not decoration; it tells the reader that the animal would be recognisable on sight, which makes the search a matter of looking rather than of guessing. An early report attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās describes the shade as the deepest yellow, the kind that draws the eye of anyone who sees it, and the Book's own phrasing leaves the beholder inside the sentence. A command that had been answered with one word now describes a specimen.

**WHO ASKED FOR THE DETAILS, AND WHAT THEY COST**

The people had asked for the colour, so the colour is supplied, and this is the second occasion on which their question shaped the obligation. There is a pattern here that readers of the chapter have noticed for centuries: the more precise the answer, the fewer the animals that qualify, and by the end of the exchange the descriptions add up to a creature so specific that its owner had to be sought out. The reports that came down about this search say that the animal was eventually found in the keeping of a young man, and that he named a price which the people paid. A reader may take that report or leave it, but the arithmetic of the command is in the Book itself and needs no report: three questions, three specifications, and a herd of thousands reduced to one. The questions were answered generously, which is exactly why they became costly.

**THE OLD CALF THAT THEY HAD MADE**

The colour in this verse is also the chapter's quietest reminder of a failure that came earlier in the same history. While Moses was on the mountain, the people had made an idol from their ornaments and called it their god, and the Book describes that image as a calf: (20:88 — **“Then he moulded for them an idol of a calf”**). A community that had once asked for a golden calf to worship is now being asked for a yellow cow to offer, and the parallel is difficult to miss. The similarity is in the eye rather than in the substance, since one was an object of worship and the other an animal of sacrifice, while the opposing purposes of the acts separate worship from obedient sacrifice. The Book places the two close enough for a reader to feel the correction: the wealth of Egypt taken as jewellery had once been melted into a god, and it is now spent on an offering to the God who had brought them out.

**NEGOTIATION PRICED AT ITS TRUE COST**

A person who is used to quick transactions will find the sequence in these verses familiar in a way that is not flattering. Think of a man who asks a builder for a quote, then asks for a second option, then a third, and finds at the end that the work costs more and the season is gone. The people of this story did the same with a command of God, and the last verse of the exchange reports what it did to them: they said the truth had come at last, and they killed the animal without conviction. The specification that made the search hard was their own work, and the Book lets the reader feel the weight of the search before it says so. What a reader should take from the colour is that the answer to a request is not always a gift; sometimes it is the request itself, returned to the asker with its price attached.

**A SPECIFICATION AND A SAINT'S RELUCTANCE**

The verse ends with an animal that can be identified and a duty that can be discharged, so its lesson is not the impossibility of obedience. The lesson is that the field of options narrows with every negotiation, and that the people who negotiate are usually the last to notice the cost of the narrowing. A reader who has a task in front of him and a habit of asking for more clarity should hear the third question coming, because it arrives in the next verse with a complaint about how much alike cows look. The colour given here was a mercy on its own terms, since a bright yellow animal is not hard to find; it became a burden only when the people treated the finding as a puzzle instead of a task. The instruction at the end of the previous verse is still waiting to be carried out, and no further information is going to make it easier. Today, an attractive detail can occupy so much attention that the duty for which the detail was supplied nearly disappears. The effect of the colour is therefore practical as well as visual, because each answer removes another excuse for postponement.
