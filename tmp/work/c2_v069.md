**A RESTRICTION ASKED FOR A SECOND TIME**

The pattern of the exchange is now unmistakable, and the commentary tradition names it plainly: ***“They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify”***. This is a second obstinacy laid on top of the first, because the people take upon themselves the very burden that had already been lifted from them. The opening command restricted them to no animal at all; their first question restricted them to a middle-aged one; and the second question moves to restrict themselves again by a colour, which means the penalty of the first has already taught them nothing. The sequence resolves the question that hangs over the whole episode — why the descriptions kept arriving at all — and the resolution standing in the glosses is that each further clause is a penalty attached to the asking itself. One glossed report puts the whole transaction in one frame: had they slaughtered the first cow brought to hand, that would have been enough for them. What follows is therefore not pedagogy but accounting, since every needless question is charged to the asker in the currency of the search. In the same register of accountability, the narrations about the two disputing parties of the town conclude with a rule that outlives the murder: the one who kills for inheritance does not inherit, so that the motive of the crime is cut off behind it. The narration of the town's kingship ends the same way, sealing the office that had produced the feud. The chain of questions thus has a measurable effect on the field of search itself, and the effect only grows.

**YELLOW TO ITS DEEPEST DEGREE**

The answer narrows the field by colour, and the wording of the answer is thicker than the question: ***“for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should be a bright yellow cow”***, ***“pleasant to see.’””***. The description here carries two transmissions from one authority, and both are worth keeping in view. Ibn ʿAbbās describes the actual animal as yellow in its hoofs and horns while its body was black, and al-Awfi transmits from him a second gloss in which the brightness is a deep yellowish white; the shared centre of the two reports is an intensity of colour that strikes the eye at a distance. Both transmissions also keep the second half of the description in his reading: the brightness is the light of the colour itself, and the gladness of the beholders is placed in the eye of whoever looks at her.

The lexicographers of the commentary explain why the wording doubles the description instead of naming the colour once. The plain term supplies the hue; the intensified form marks that hue at its most saturated, so that the sentence judges the animal yellow and then judges the colour itself extreme. This is the same construction the language uses in its most emphatic colour phrases — whitest bright, blackest deep, reddest blazing — where the second word exists to press the first to its limit. The designation for colour in this language also carries the sense of kind or type, so that a thing ever described as shifting its colour is a thing that never settles into one character. The description of the cow therefore answers their hunt in both dimensions at once. The reason the wording stacks the two judgements is emphasis: the cow was to be unmistakable at a glance.

That public checkability is confirmed by the closing words of the description, which turn the search over to the eye of every bystander. Al-Suddī reads the closing phrase as the animal pleasing those who look at it, and the same reading stands with Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Qatādah, and al-Rabīʿ b. Anas; the sense is its beauty pleasing those that look at it. The practical consequence for their trial is immediate, since a colour that gladdens the eye is a colour that can be verified without an expert, and the herd grows smaller in every village the search enters. Their next complaint shows the narrowing has begun to bite (2:70 — **“for all cows look the same to us. Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.””**), and the final specification completes the strangling of the field (2:71 — **“He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields;”**). For the reader today the sequence is a caution about bargaining with plain commands; in other words, the cost of every avoidable question is paid in the same coin as the question itself.
