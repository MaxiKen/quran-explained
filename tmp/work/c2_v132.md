**AN ANCESTOR'S ADVICE TO HIS CHILDREN**

The verse reports what the man said to his own household, and it names two generations in the same sentence: ***“This was the advice of Abraham”***, ***“as well as Jacob”***, ***“to his children, ˹saying˺, “Indeed, Allah has chosen”***. The advice is not recorded as a private conviction but as something handed on to children, which places the instruction inside the family at the moment the family was becoming a nation. Jacob is named beside Abraham, which extends the same counsel across another lifetime and shows that the tradition had a lineage rather than a single author. The religion of Abraham has been described in this sūrah by what he did and what he was promised, and this verse adds the act of transmission. The inheritance here is deliberately given rather than passively received. Ibn ʿAbbās understood the dying instruction of Abraham and Jacob as a charge to hold fast to divine oneness until death, not merely to inherit a communal name.

**WHAT WAS CHOSEN FOR THEM**

The advice opens with a statement about what God did rather than about what the family had: ***“for you this faith”*** and ***“so do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission.”***. The faith is described as chosen for the children, which places the initiative with God and the response with the people who receive it. The instruction that follows is about dying in a state rather than about performing a single act, and the wording makes the state the goal of a whole life. Another passage addresses the community of believers in the same terms and with the same conclusion, which shows that the counsel to the family became the counsel of the Book itself: (3:102 — **“and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺”**). The choice that God made is therefore presented as a gift whose value depends on how a person ends.

**AN INHERITANCE THAT HAS TO BE KEPT**

The verse treats the faith as an estate that is passed on and that can also be lost, and the warning about how a person dies is a warning about the whole interval between receiving it and leaving it. The account of the Children of Israel in this sūrah has been a study of exactly that risk, since a community can carry the name of a tradition and end somewhere else. The same message is given in another passage where the community is told to follow the way of the ancestor rather than the customs that grew up around his name: (16:123 — **“Follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists.”**). Someone who has watched a family keep its name and lose its practice has the warning in view without needing a commentary, and the sentence is written for him as much as for the children addressed.

**A FATHER LEAVING INSTRUCTIONS WITH AN ESTATE**

Think of a man who gathers his sons before he dies and tells them that the land matters less than what they do with the name attached to it. The counsel in this verse is given exactly at that point in the family's history, and the phrase about the manner of dying shows where the father's concern was fixed. The comparison explains why the advice is recorded rather than the bequest, since the inheritance that survives a death is the one that has been spoken into the next generation. Anyone who has been entrusted with something by a parent and has had to decide what to do with it will find the sentence more than a piece of ancient history. The responsibility that comes with receiving a tradition is what the sentence puts on him.

**A COUNSEL CARRIED ACROSS TWO LIFETIMES**

The verse names Abraham and Jacob together, and the pairing is important because the counsel was not a single man's preference but a practice that the family carried forward. Jacob had his own history in the Book, and he appears here as the one who repeated the instruction to his own children, so that the sentence spans two lifetimes of transmission. The pattern is the same in this sūrah's account of the covenant, whose terms were given once and then repeated. The picture is of a religion moving through a household, and that movement is the part of the story the arguing communities had left out. The next verses turn to the question of who the ancestors actually were, which the Book answers from the record rather than from the claims. In the present day, the most valuable inheritance a parent leaves is not a religious label but a lived charge to remain surrendered until death.
