**THE GIFTS THAT ANSWER THE COMMAND OF THE VERSE BEFORE**

The command to worship is followed by a list of what the Lord has already done, and the list opens with the One being named: ***“˹He is the One˺”***, ***“Who has made the earth a place”***, ***“of settlement”***, ***“for you and the sky a canopy”***. The first gift is a ground that carries the weight of a life, and the phrase describes a floor that was made fit for dwelling rather than left as a hazard. Ibn ʿAbbās and others explained the canopy as a roof over the earth, raised without pillars and guarded from falling, and the picture is one of a house rather than of a wilderness. The Book states the same pair of gifts in another sūrah with almost the same words, which shows that the reader is meant to hear the sentence as part of a settled description of God's dealing with the human being: (40:64 — **“It is Allah Who made the earth a place of settlement for you and the sky a canopy”**).

**THE PROVISION THAT COMES DOWN AND COMES OUT**

The list widens to the rain and to what it produces: ***“and sends down rain from the sky,”***, ***“causing fruits to grow”***, ***“as a provision for you.”***. The order of the sentence is the order of a person's dependence, since the water arrives before the fruit and the fruit arrives before the eater knows he will need it. The Book repeats the same chain elsewhere as a reminder of where the arrangement comes from: (14:32 — **“causing fruits to grow as a provision for you”**). The phrase about provision covers everything a person lives on, whether he earned it by work or received it without work, and the wording leaves the credit with the sender of the rain. That is why the sentence does not mention farmers or markets, because the arrangement is older than either of them and larger than both. What turns on the sentence for the reader is that a man may spend his life producing food and never once look past the market to the chain that brought it, and the verse makes the chain visible in a single line.

**THE WARNING AT THE END OF THE LIST**

The verse closes with a prohibition attached to all that came before it: ***“So do not knowingly set up equals”***, ***“to Allah ˹in worship˺”***. The word for knowledge in the middle of the sentence is doing real work, since it removes ignorance as an excuse and leaves the hearer with what he already knows. The Book uses the same charge in a question that puts the whole matter in one line: (41:9 — **“And how can you set up equals with Him?”**). The reason the prohibition is placed after the gifts rather than before them is that the gifts are the proof, and a person who has received a house, a roof and food from one hand cannot plead that he did not know whose the hand was. Ibn ʿAbbās read the closing as a warning to those who knew the truth about God's oneness and still gave a share of their worship to something else, and the sūrah keeps that reading in view in the verses that follow. What turns on the word for knowledge is that the excuse of a person who never heard does not survive it, since the reader of this verse has heard the argument and can no longer claim the benefit of the doubt.

**A CHILD WHO THANKS THE WELL FOR THE WATER**

Think of a child who is brought water from the well by his father and thanks the well for it. The gratitude is real and the direction is wrong, and the mistake costs nothing until the day someone asks the child who built the well. The verse makes the same correction with the whole of a human life, and it does it by naming the gifts before it names the error, which is the way a good teacher corrects a student. These days a person can live inside a system of provision so efficient that the sender disappears behind the machinery, and the passage asks him to look up from the machinery once. Nobody is asked to despise the means; the child is asked to know whose hands carried the water, and the knowledge is what the next verse puts to the test with a challenge that no rival of God has ever answered.
