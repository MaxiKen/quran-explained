**THE CLAIM THAT PARADISE BELONGS TO ONE COMMUNITY**

Two communities are reported here as holding a conviction about themselves, and the belief is stated plainly: ***“The Jews and Christians each claim that”*** ***“none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof”***. The Book joins the two communities in one sentence because the claim is formally the same on both sides, each excluding the other while using identical logic. The clause about desires comes before the invitation to bring proof, and its placement tells the reader that the Book is treating the belief as an expression of preference rather than as a conclusion drawn from evidence. The following verse supplies the answer, and this one prepares for it by asking the claimants to produce what they have. What the reader is watching is the Book's standard method with confident assertions: it asks for the document.

**A DESIRE NAMED AS A DESIRE**

The word used for the claim describes a wish rather than a judgement, and the choice of word does the work of an argument. A person who wants a conclusion to be true will often hold it with the confidence that belongs to a demonstrated fact, and the Book names the difference between the two. The claim is not answered by denying that its holders are sincere; it is answered by asking them to show that it rests on something outside their own wanting. Another sūrah states the same principle in the form of a direct correction, and its words are near enough to be the verse's own comment: (4:123 — **“neither by your wishes nor those of the People of the Book”**). A reader who has held a belief because it favoured his own community will find the sentence uncomfortably exact, and the exactness is the point of the verse.

**THE PROOF CALLED FOR AND WHAT IT WOULD BE**

The invitation to produce proof is phrased as a challenge rather than a request for a discussion, and its last clause qualifies the proof being asked for: ***“if what you say is true.”***. The qualification makes the demand reasonable, since a claim about the unseen can only be settled by a report from the One who knows the unseen. The Book does not ask the claimants to argue better; it asks them to demonstrate the authority behind a statement that no human being could otherwise make. The same standard is applied to the believers elsewhere in the sūrah, where their confidence is grounded in what has been sent down rather than in their own standing. The reading attached to Ibn ʿAbbās treats the proof being called for as the scripture already in the claimants' hands, which is why the correction that follows is not introduced as an argument. Anyone who has heard a person speak with certainty about who is saved and who is not will find the challenge useful, because it returns the matter to the only place where it can be settled.

**TWO FAMILIES DISPUTING AN INHERITANCE**

Think of two families who each insist that the estate belongs to them alone, neither of them able to produce the document that settles it. The two communities in this verse made the same claim about the inheritance of the world to come, and the Book asks both of them for the paper. The comparison is sharp because the claimants in it are not dishonest people; they are confident people, and confidence is easy to mistake for evidence. Someone who has seen a dispute run for years on nothing but the certainty of the parties can see how the Book wastes no time in choosing between them: it asks for the proof, and the absence of proof is the answer. What the verse leaves with the reader is a standard he can apply to his own convictions about who will be saved.

**WHAT REMAINS UNSETTLED HERE**

The verse does not name the answer to the claim, and that is deliberate, because the next verse supplies it in the Book's own words. What this sentence establishes is the common ground between the two claimants: both had a desire, both expressed it as doctrine, and neither could produce the authority they were asked for. The Book's order in the passage is worth noticing, since it lets the claimants state their case fully before it gives the correction that follows. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the sequence as a single argument in which the demand for proof and the answer that follows are two halves of one reply. Anyone who has followed the sūrah this far is now in a position to hear the correction as something more than a denial of two communities' hopes.
