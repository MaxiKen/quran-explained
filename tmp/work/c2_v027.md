**REBELLION IS NOW SHOWN IN ITS BROKEN BONDS**

The “rebellious” at the end of verse 26 are not left as a vague class. They are ***“those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained”***. The first image is of something made firm and then undone. To violate a covenant is not simply to overlook an unknown duty. It is to treat a known bond as though it no longer binds. Early readings apply the covenant at several levels. It includes the basic claim of the Creator upon His creatures, God’s commands given through revelation, the pledges placed upon earlier communities, and the duty to receive a true messenger. These senses share one root: God has made His right known, and the rebel answers a settled claim with breach. The Quran recalls the first human witness when God asked, (7:172 — **“and had them testify regarding themselves. ˹Allah asked,˺ “Am I not your Lord?” They replied, “Yes, You are!”**). It also records the covenant laid upon the prophets concerning a confirming messenger: (3:81 — **“if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him.” He added,”**). The point is not to blur every covenant into one event. It is to show that revolt takes shape wherever a person receives a binding truth from God and then works to unmake its hold.

**AFFIRMATION MAKES THE LATER BREACH MORE GRAVE**

The reference to prior affirmation adds moral weight. A rope that was never tied cannot be accused of being untied. Here the bond was made firm by witness, promise, revealed command, clear proof, or all of these in their proper settings. This is why the breach is more than weakness during an honest search. The violator has enough ground to be bound and still chooses release from the duty. Ibn ʿAbbās applies the verse to the pledge concerning the Prophet: people had confirmed what bound them, then resisted the messenger named by that duty. Other early explanations widen the covenant to God’s charge that people obey His commands and avoid what He forbids. The readings meet rather than cancel one another. A covenant with God cannot be kept while His true message is rejected, and a verbal pledge is hollow when life refuses His rule. The opposite character appears in those (13:20 — **“who honour Allah’s covenant, never breaking the pledge”**). Honour here requires more than preserving the words of a promise. It means allowing the promise to govern the moment when keeping it becomes costly. Many pledges feel firm while nothing presses them. Their truth appears when status, desire, tribe, wealth, or fear pulls the other way. The verse therefore asks a living reader not only what was once affirmed, but which present choice is quietly trying to loosen it.

**WHAT GOD JOINS REACHES FROM WORSHIP TO HUMAN DUTY**

The second mark is severing what should remain joined. Ibn ʿAbbās includes sworn bonds and family ties, as well as the bond of faith with the Messenger. Other explanations extend the words to every relation God has ordered people to maintain: the servant with the Lord, children with parents, relatives with one another, neighbours, friends, believers, and human beings bound by justice. This breadth is sound because the phrase itself says “whatever” God ordered, not one tie alone. The Quran gives the positive mirror in those (13:21 — **“and those who maintain whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment”**). Joining begins with loyalty to God, since His command defines and protects the other bonds. It then becomes visible in care, truth, aid, lawful loyalty, and refusal to abandon a duty when a person is no longer useful. Family ties deserve clear stress, but they do not permit support for wrong. Nor does devotion to God excuse cruelty toward people. The same Lord commands both worship and justice. Therefore, the joined life is not a web of blind attachment. It is an ordered set of bonds kept under God. Sin cuts across that order: it separates claim from conduct, knowledge from obedience, kin from kin, and the good of one person from concern for another.

**CORRUPTION ENDS IN THE LOSS OF THE SELF**

Broken bonds do not remain private. Such people ***“and spread corruption in the land. It is they”*** ***“who are the ˹true˺ losers”***. Corruption includes blocking faith, weakening trust, spreading disobedience, and harming the relations on which common life rests. This is why another verse places public ruin and severed kinship in one line: (47:22 — **“Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship”**). A person may imagine that breaking a pledge creates freedom or that cutting a demanding bond saves effort. The final account reverses that judgment. The rebel loses trust in this life, loses the good carried by faithful ties, and faces the loss of the Hereafter. Ibn ʿAbbās calls them cheated of both worlds. The strongest loss is not merely that some advantage slips away; the person has spent the self on a course that cannot deliver safety. Surah al-ʿAṣr begins with the rule (103:2 — **“Surely humanity is in ˹grave˺ loss”**) and then names the way out through faith, good work, truth, and patient counsel. Each of those repairs a bond this verse shows being cut. Thus the passage answers the taunt about a mosquito with a moral portrait. The parable did not force innocent people off the road. It exposed people already willing to break God’s claim, divide what He joined, and call the resulting damage gain.
