**THE PORTRAIT FINISHED: TIES BROKEN, LAND SPOILED**

The previous verse ended by naming the rebellious, and this one describes them: ***“those who violate Allah's covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained”***, and ***“and spread corruption in the land. It is they”***, ***“who are the ˹true˺ losers”***. The description has three parts and they run in a deliberate order, from the agreement to the relationships to the ground itself. Something was pledged, the pledge was broken, and the breaking damaged more than the person who did it. The last phrase then delivers the verdict in the vocabulary of business, the same vocabulary the sūrah used when it described the man who traded guidance for error. Think of a bridge built to carry a whole village across a river. The person who saws through one of its ropes may feel nothing at the time, and the collapse will still be his doing.

The Book repeats this sentence almost word for word in another sūrah, which tells the reader how central the charge is: (13:25 — **“And those who violate Allah's covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained,”**). In that passage the end of the line names a condemned home rather than a loss, and the two endings describe one thing from different sides. What makes the repetition so pointed is that the covenant in view is usually not a formal oath. For the audience of this sūrah it was the agreement their own scripture recorded and their own history carried, and every reader since has received something similar in the plainest form: the acknowledgement that there is a Maker and that he is owed.

**A BROKEN TIE AND THE GROUND UNDER EVERYONE**

The middle phrase is the one that turns a private failure into a public one. The ties the Book orders to be maintained are the relations between people: the parent and the child, the neighbour and the neighbour, the lender and the debtor, the community and its poor. A person who cuts those ties does not simply lose a virtue; he changes the ground everyone else is standing on, which is why the next clause mentions corruption in the land. The Book keeps the two ideas joined everywhere, because a society is not damaged by large crimes alone. It is unstitched thread by thread by people who have decided that the obligations they inherited are optional. The last phrase then names what the person has actually done to himself. He is not described as wicked but as a loser, which means he has spent something and received nothing. That is the same arithmetic the sūrah used for the hypocrite's trade, and the same conclusion follows: the loss is invisible at the time and complete at the end. A reader who wants to test the verse on himself does not need to search his memory for a dramatic betrayal. He can begin with the relationships he has quietly let go and ask who is carrying the cost of the letting go.

**THE HABIT OF KEEPING ONE TIE**

The practical instruction that follows from the verse is narrow and unglamorous. A person can pick one obligation he has been treating as optional, and maintain it for a month: the call to a relative, the payment owed and not mentioned, the promise made in a busy week and forgotten. The Book does not present these as heroic acts, and that is precisely their value, since a covenant is kept in ordinary moments or not at all. The rebellious in the previous verse were undone by small things, and this verse says that the same small things are where the ties are either held or cut. Because the verse ends with the losers rather than with a threat, it leaves the reader free to do the arithmetic himself. Today he can take the one relationship he has been avoiding and make one concrete move toward repairing it, before the cost of the avoidance has been fully paid. Nobody in this life is asked to be perfect at it, and the Book is patient with the person who stumbles and returns. What it will not accept is the settled decision that the tie does not matter, because that decision is the whole of the failure named in the verse.
