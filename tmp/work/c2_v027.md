**THE PEOPLE THE PARABLE WAS EXPLAINING**

The verse that follows the parable describes the people the parable was given for: ***“those who violate Allah's covenant after it”***, ***“has been affirmed”***. The word for violating carries the sense of breaking something that was sound, and the early teachers described the covenant as the binding agreement by which a person acknowledges his Lord. Ibn ʿAbbās read it as the covenant concerning the Prophet, taken from the communities through their books and their prophets, and another reading places it at the moment when every human being was asked to bear witness to his Lord. The Book states the same scene in a sūrah that records the answer of humanity at that moment, and the reader who keeps it in view understands why the verse treats the break as a violation rather than as a simple mistake. A person who has given his word has already been bound by it, and the sentence says so before it names any other offence. The point of naming the covenant first is that every other charge in the verse hangs on it, because the ties and the corruption are the visible form of the broken bond rather than separate crimes.

**THE TIES THAT WERE ORDERED TO BE MAINTAINED**

The verse widens the charge to the relationships a person holds: ***“break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah”***, ***“has ordered to be maintained”***. The early teachers read the phrase as covering every bond the religion commands a person to keep, from the bond with God to the bond with parents, relatives and neighbours, and some of them noted that the word used here is about joining rather than about kindness, which makes the duty a matter of maintaining a connection. The Book names the same offence in another sūrah with a description of the offenders that follows it to its end: (13:25 — **“And those who violate Allah's covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land”**). The reason the ties are named after the covenant is that a broken relationship with God shows itself first in the relationships a person can see, which is why the verse moves from the unseen bond to the visible ones in a single line. The order is the order of diagnosis, since a community cannot see what is in a man's heart and can see what he does to his neighbours.

**THE SPREADING OF CORRUPTION AND THE LOSS THAT FOLLOWS**

The charge closes with the same offence named earlier in the sūrah: ***“and spread corruption in the land. It”***, ***“is they”***, ***“who are the ˹true˺ losers”***. The sūrah has already described the pretenders as the corrupters of the community, and the verse here applies the description to the people who break their bonds, which shows that the two groups are the same in result if not in name. Ibn ʿAbbās explained the losing as the loss of this world and the next together, and the phrase carries the sense of a bargain in which a person's capital is gone. The Book states the same outcome for the men who traded their pledges for a small gain: (16:95 — **“And do not trade Allah's covenant for a fleeting gain”**). What turns on the verse for the reader is that the loss is measured by what was given away and not by what was gained, since a man who sells a foundation for a handful of coins is poorer by the whole house. That is why the sentence ends with a verdict about the men rather than about the crime, because the Book is describing a condition that has already been reached.

**A MAN WHO PULLS OUT THE FOUNDATION STONES**

Think of a man who takes the stones out of his own house's foundation to sell them, and who lives comfortably for a season on the price. The walls stand for a while because they were built well, and then the first storm shows what he did. The verse describes the same arrangement in the life of a person who breaks the bonds that hold his world together, and it calls the result a loss rather than a punishment in the first place. These days the ties in question are broken quietly, through absences, silences and paperwork, and the passage asks the reader to look at the foundation he is standing on. The next verses turn to the ground of the whole matter by asking the human being a question about his own beginning, and the question is one that nobody can answer except by admitting where he came from.
