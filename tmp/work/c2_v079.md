**A CURSE ON THOSE WHO WRITE AND LIE**

The next verse names the offence and pronounces on it twice: ***“So woe to those”***, ***“who distort the Scripture with their own hands”***, ***“then say, “This is from Allah””***, ***“seeking a fleeting gain!”***, ***“So woe to them”***, ***“for what their hands have written”***, ***“and woe to them”***, ***“for what they have earned”***. The charge is not that a text was copied badly; it is that hands wrote something and a mouth attributed it to God for a price. The Book's double woe separates the two halves of the crime, so that the writing and the gain are each answered on their own. What makes the verse heavy is the audience: the people doing this were not outsiders attacking a scripture; they were the ones entrusted with it, and the thing they sold was the authority of the One who had given it. The Book keeps a place for the same instinct in another charge about people who raise themselves above the truth they were given: (4:49 — **“Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who ˹falsely˺ elevate themselves?”**). Ibn ʿAbbās read the small gain as livelihood and property acquired through alteration, making the repeated woe answer both the forged writing and the unlawful earnings it produced.

**THE FEE THAT MADE THE FRAUD WORTH IT**

The verse describes the motive in two words, and it is not large: a small, passing profit. A person who rewrites a verse for a fee is not buying an empire; the Book's choice of words tells the reader how cheaply the traders valued what they had. The same subject comes up elsewhere in the sūrah where concealment of the Book is traded for a little gain, and the pattern the Book lays out is that the sellers are always convinced the sum is modest and reasonable. A reader can test the arithmetic in his own work, since the compromises that damage a community are almost never made for a fortune. They are made for a favour, a position, or the avoidance of an awkward conversation, and the Book lists the consequence beside the price so the reader can see the imbalance.

**THE HANDS AND THE MOUTH**

The verse locates the crime in two parts of the body, and the pairing is deliberate. The hands write what was not given, and the mouth supplies the attribution, which means the forgery requires the cooperation of both the maker and the speaker. This is why the Book does not describe the offence as a slip of the pen; a written change becomes a lie only when it is presented as revelation, and both steps were taken knowingly. The same image appears in a passage that warns those who read a fabricated text to an audience that cannot check it, and it appears again among the charges laid against this people in the chapters that follow. A reader who handles religious material in any form is being shown the two moments at which the offence is committed, and both moments are available to him.

**THE GAINS THAT ARE GONE BY MORNING**

Think of a clerk who copies a land record with one measurement altered, and whose fee for the alteration is modest, and who then lives with the result for the rest of his life. The verse's phrase about a fleeting gain is a reminder of the time horizon, since the Book regularly measures profit against what remains after it is spent. The people in this passage were paid in standing, influence, or income, and none of those currencies is recognised on the day the Book keeps describing. The double woe at the end covers both the document and the career built on it, so that no part of the transaction escapes the sentence. A reader who wants the verse to be about him rather than about a distant scribe can begin with the question of what he would alter in his testimony if the price were right.

**THE WRITING AND THE PROFIT JUDGED TOGETHER**

The structure of the verse gives the reader a rule about deception in religious matters: the falsification and the benefit always arrive together, and the Book names them together so that neither can be hidden behind the other. A community that tolerates edited scripture for the sake of a smooth relationship has bought the same arrangement at one remove, since the income from a lie is not always money. The passage ends here, and the next verse begins a new accusation about the claims this community makes about its own future, which shows how one corruption prepares the ground for the next. What a reader takes away is not only a warning about forgery but a test he can run on the material he receives: where a claim about God arrives with an advantage attached, the Book's verdict on the transaction is already written. Today, fabricated religious claims can still purchase influence, donations or access, making the small gain recognisable in modern forms.
