**A SENTENCE LAID DOWN TWICE**

The verse hands its verdict out in pairs because the crime itself arrived in pairs: ***“So woe to those”***, ***“who distort the Scripture with their own hands”***, ***“then say, “This is from Allah””***, ***“seeking a fleeting gain!”***. The sequence is a forensic file. The hand is named first, because the fabrication was manual — pages rewritten by their custodians — and the glosses on the distortion fix its two chief items: the portrait of the expected prophet in the Torah, redrawn so that it would describe someone else, and the penalty that had stood in the text and was taken out of it. The claim of divine authorship then seals the fraud, and the motive named at the end of the sentence is the smallest item in it: a fleeting gain, the price at which eternity was discounted. The narrations about the trade give the marketplace its floor — fabrications pawned on the unlettered for subsistence money and surplus property — and the whole transaction is what the revelation now bills at full rate. The description they rewrote is the description the revelation restores in public view: (7:157 — **““˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah”**). What follows from the pairing is the passage's arithmetic of guilt: a people were robbed twice over, in their religion and in their wealth, and the sentence counts both takings.

**THE EARNINGS ARRIVE FOR SENTENCE TOO**

The repetition of the woe then turns from the deed to its wage: ***“So woe to them”***, ***“for what their hands have written”***, ***“and woe to them”***, ***“for what they have earned”***. The first count is the fabrication; the second is the fee. The two-faced wrong stands analysed in the tradition with a clarity that needs no amplification: the men disguised the religion of their clients and then took the clients' money for the disguise, which makes the transaction worse than robbery and theft, since the robber takes wealth and leaves religion intact. The doubled woe is therefore the exact shape of the doubled charge, and the smallness of the price is itself part of the judgement. The world entire, from its first day to its last, is a small price; the falsehood these men manufactured became a trap by which they fished the purses of a whole population. The parallel exposure of the same trade in another assembly says the quiet part aloud — (3:78 — **“it is not what the Book says. They say, “It is from Allah”—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺”**) — and the sentence inside that passage marks the intent as knowing. For any age that has watched sacred text edited for market share, the verse sets the two ledgers side by side and audits both: the doctrine that was bent and the money that the bending collected.

**THE TERMS OF THE CURSE, OPENED**

The cry of woe itself drew the lexicographers into one of their richest debates, and the debate is worth recording for the weight it puts on the word. The form is a verbal noun with no verb of its own shape, and the definitions gathered around it run from intense evil to sorrow to sheer destruction. Al-Khalil took it as the severity of harm; another lexicographer took it as grief; the wider view holds it as ruin. Al-Asma'i marks the term as an utterance of lamentation, used at times even of pity in the idiom of the language — yet when revelation wields it, the pity is gone and the lament is for the one it lands on. The transmitted glosses from the early generations keep to the plain sense: al-Dahhak transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās the definition of the term here as simply *"the chastisement is upon them"*, and a second line of transmission from the same authority renders the woe as a valley of the Fire. A report traced to the first of the successors of the caliphal house has the Prophet define the term as a mountain of the Fire; another, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, pictures it as a valley between two heights into which the falling one tumbles for forty autumns before reaching its floor. The authenticity of that valley-report is itself disputed in the tradition, and some of the learned read the term instead as the very cry the punished will utter. The readings do not cancel one another, because the point is the same in all of them.

**WHERE THE POINTED WORD LANDS**

The proper-name readings and the common-word reading converge on one fact, and the fact is the register of the sentence. Whether the cry names a pit or announces one, it is the heaviest noun the language keeps for such a verdict, and the verse spends it three times. The structure of the passage then does with syntax what the term does with sound: the first woe hangs over the writers, the second over the writing, the third over the wages, and no actor in the chain is left outside the sentence. The judgment follows the paperwork exactly. In other words, the verse is not raining general wrath on a town; it is signing each ledger line with the same name. The effect on a later reader is the effect of reading a bill of particulars, since every clause names a party and every party receives the same currency. A congregation that has learned to treat tampering with scripture as a clerical dispute rather than a crime will find this passage restoring the older scale.

**THE CRY AND THE ACCOUNT IT FOLLOWS**

Read beside the exposure that opened the section, the woe closes a bracket that began with secret pricing. The men who kept two speeches and two inventories now meet a sentence with two counts, and the symmetry is deliberate rather than decorative. What turns on the symmetry is the rule of proportion in the accounting: the punishment fits the paperwork because the paperwork did the harm. The lesson pressed on every later custodian of revelation is therefore exact and practical today: the hand that edits a text for pay signs its own sentence in advance, and the signature is read aloud at the end of the passage in a voice that has never been negotiated with. The pairing of deed and wage is why the passage ends where it does, since a verdict that named only the error would leave the motive unjudged, and the motive is what repeats the crime in every generation.
