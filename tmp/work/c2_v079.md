**A SENTENCE LAID DOWN TWICE**

The verse hands its verdict out in pairs because the crime itself arrived in pairs: ***“So woe to those”***, ***“who distort the Scripture with their own hands”***, ***“then say, “This is from Allah””***, ***“seeking a fleeting gain!”***. The order is a forensic file. The hand is named first, because the faking was done by hand — pages redone by their keepers. The glosses on the distortion fix its two chief items: the portrait of the hoped-for prophet in the Torah, redrawn so that it would paint someone else, and the price that had stood in the text and was cut out of it. The claim that it is from Allah then seals the fraud, and the motive named at the end of the line is the smallest item in it: a passing gain, the rate at which the next world was marked down. The tales of the trade give the market its floor — fakes pawned on the unlettered for bread money and spare goods — and the whole deal is what the revelation now bills at full rate. The portrait they redrew is the portrait the revelation puts back in plain sight: (7:157 — **““˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah”**). What follows from the pairing is the passage's tally of guilt. A people were robbed twice over, in their faith and in their wealth, and the line counts both takings.

**THE EARNINGS ARRIVE FOR SENTENCE TOO**

The second woe then turns from the deed to its wage: ***“So woe to them”***, ***“for what their hands have written”***, ***“and woe to them”***, ***“for what they have earned”***. The first count is the fake; the second is the fee. The two-faced wrong stands laid out in the tradition with a clearness that needs no gilding. The men dressed up the faith of their clients and then took the clients' money for the dress-up. That makes the deal worse than plain theft, since the thief takes wealth and leaves faith whole. The doubled woe is thus the true shape of the doubled charge, and the smallness of the price is itself part of the court's finding. The world entire, from its first day to its last, is a small price. The lie these men made became a hook by which they fished the purses of a whole people. The same trade is exposed in another scene, and there the quiet part is spoken aloud: (3:78 — **“it is not what the Book says. They say, “It is from Allah”—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺”**). The line inside that passage marks the aim as knowing. For any age that has watched holy writ cut to fit the market, the verse sets the two ledgers side by side and audits both: the creed that was bent and the coins the bending pulled in.

**THE TERMS OF THE CURSE, OPENED**

The cry of woe itself pulled the word-men into one of their richest spats, and the spat is worth setting down for the weight it puts on the word. The form is a verbal noun with no verb of its own shape, and the glosses gathered round it run from fierce harm to grief to sheer ruin. Al-Khalil took it as the severity of harm. Another word-man took it as grief. The wider view holds it as ruin. Al-Asma'i marks the term as a cry of wailing, used at times even of pity in the speech of the Arabs. Yet when revelation wields it, the pity is gone and the wail is for the one it lands on. The glosses handed down from the first ages keep to the plain sense. Al-Dahhak hands down from Ibn ʿAbbās a flat form of the gloss in this place: *\"the chastisement is upon them\"*, and a second chain from the same voice renders the woe as a dell of the Fire. A tale traced to the first of the successors of the caliphal house has the Prophet name the term as a peak of the Fire. Another, from Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī, paints it as a dell between two heights into which the falling man tumbles for forty autumns before he lands. The truth of that dell-tale is itself spurned in the tradition, and some of the learned read the term instead as the very cry the punished will shout. The readings do not cancel one another, because the point is the same in all of them.

**WHERE THE POINTED WORD LANDS**

The name-readings and the plain-word reading meet on one fact, and the fact is the weight of the line. Whether the cry names a pit or warns of one, it is the heaviest noun the tongue keeps for such a court-finding, and the verse spends it three times. The build of the passage then does with word-order what the term does with sound. The first woe hangs over the writers, the second over the writing, the third over the wages, and no hand in the chain is left out of the line. The judgment follows the paperwork exactly. In other words, the verse is not raining broad wrath on a town. It is signing each ledger line with the same name. The effect on a later reader is the effect of reading a full list of charges, since each clause names a party and each party is paid in the same coin. A people that has learned to treat tampering with holy writ as a desk quarrel rather than a crime will find this passage putting the old scale back.

**THE CRY AND THE ACCOUNT IT FOLLOWS**

Set beside the exposure that opened the section, the woe shuts a bracket that began with secret pricing. The men who kept two speeches and two lists now meet a line with two counts, and the match is there on purpose. What turns on the match is the rule of fit in the tally: the price of the wrong is shaped by the paperwork, because the paperwork did the harm. The lesson pressed on each later keeper of revelation is thus exact and live today. The hand that edits a text for pay signs its own sentence in advance, and the sentence is read out at the end of the passage in a voice that has never been bargained with. The pairing of deed and wage is why the passage ends where it does. A verdict that named only the slip would leave the motive un-judged, and the motive is what repeats the crime in every age.
