**PUBLIC AND PRIVATE CHARITY**

***“To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the”*** establishes public and private charity. The verse gives a balanced judgement about the visibility of charity. Public giving is good when it encourages others, reports a public trust, or makes an important project possible. Concealing charity and giving it to the poor is better for the giver’s sincerity and for the recipient’s dignity. The verse does not declare every public donation corrupt or every private donation automatically sincere. The deciding question is purpose and effect. A public campaign may feed thousands and inspire a culture of generosity, while a private gift may be used to control or harm. A hidden gift may protect the heart from showing off, while a public zakāh payment may provide transparency. Allah knows the full circumstance. Hidden charity—tukhfūhā—can protect both sides. The giver is less likely to seek praise, and the recipient is less likely to feel exposed. A quiet transfer, anonymous payment, or discreet delivery can preserve a person’s dignity. The donor should not later reveal the secret to win an argument or demand loyalty. The Prophet ﷺ mentioned the person who gives charity so secretly that the left hand does not know what the right hand has given among those shaded by Allah’s mercy on the Day of Resurrection, in al-Bukhārī and Muslim. The image emphasizes sincerity rather than prescribing secrecy in every case. Public giving can be necessary. **CONCEALING CHARITY**

***“poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you”*** deepens concealing charity. An institution may need published accounts, donors may need to verify funds, and a visible project may encourage others to contribute. A person who gives publicly should protect the recipient from being made a spectacle and should renew his intention when praise arrives. Leaders should also avoid using charity to build a personality cult. A public donation that makes the poor chant the donor’s name or suppresses criticism is no longer simple generosity. Transparency should serve accountability, not domination. Allah promises that charity can erase some sins. This does not erase debts or rights owed to people without restitution, and it does not make deliberate injustice harmless. Repentance, restoration, and lawful settlement remain necessary. The promise refers to Allah’s forgiveness of shortcomings and the purification produced by sincere giving. The person who gives should not become confident that charity permits him to continue fraud. A gift from stolen wealth does not cleanse theft. The best charity is accompanied by lawful earning, repentance, and a change in conduct. The verse specifically mentions giving to the poor—al-fuqarāʾ. Charity should reach those with genuine need, and the giver should learn how to identify need without humiliating people. Not every poor person asks, and not every person who asks lacks resources. Trustworthy institutions and discreet inquiry can help. A gift should be appropriate. Food, rent, medical treatment, education, debt relief, and tools for work may serve different needs. (2:264 — **“O believers! Do not waste your charity with reminders ˹of your generosity˺ or hurtful words,”**)

**PUBLIC CHARITY WITH SINCERITY**

***“of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do”*** clarifies public charity with sincerity. The giver asks the recipient what would help rather than imposing an unwanted project for the sake of public appearance. Allah is fully aware—khabīr—of the gift, the source, the intention, the recipient’s condition, and the public or private consequences. A charity may be celebrated by people while Allah knows that it was accompanied by manipulation. Another may be unknown in the world while Allah knows every sacrifice. This awareness gives the poor giver comfort and the public donor accountability. No good deed is lost, and no spiritual corruption is hidden. The preference for hidden voluntary charity does not abolish organized zakāh. Zakāh often requires records, collection, and distribution. The administrators should preserve confidentiality as far as possible and report finances honestly. The recipients should not be shamed for receiving an obligatory right. The community can combine anonymous assistance with public education about poverty, fair wages, disability, housing, and debt. Good charity relieves immediate need while seeking to remove the causes of preventable deprivation. Think of public and private charity through a man who leaves a bag of rice at his neighbour's door before the dawn prayer and walks away without knocking. The neighbour never learns who left it, and the man finds that he sleeps better on the nights when nobody says his name. Public and private charity confronts concealing charity in a reader who gives in the open because he wants his neighbours to know that he is not a miser. (76:8 — **“and give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive”**)
