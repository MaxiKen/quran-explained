**THE METAPHOR OF A LOAN**

***“Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will”*** establishes the metaphor of a loan. Allah is free of all need, yet He calls charitable spending a good loan—qarḍ ḥasan. The metaphor honours the giver by promising that what is offered for Allah’s sake will be returned with multiplication. A loan normally expects repayment, but Allah does not need the servant’s wealth. The expression teaches that charity is secure with Him and that no sincere gift is lost. A qarḍ ḥasan is given from lawful wealth, with sincere intention, without humiliating the recipient, and without demanding an unfair return. It can include financial support, food, education, medical care, shelter, and resources for a just public cause. The goodness lies in the source, intention, method, and benefit. Allah multiplies the reward many times, as explained in 2:261: a seed produces seven ears, each with a hundred grains, and Allah multiplies for whom He wills. Multiplication may appear as blessing in the giver’s wealth, forgiveness, protection, guidance, benefit to descendants, or reward in the Hereafter. The servant does not dictate the form of repayment; he trusts Allah’s wisdom. The promise is not a guarantee that every donation produces immediate worldly profit. A person may give and remain poor, but his charity can still be accepted and multiplied beyond worldly measurement. (2:261 — **“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a”**) Because the metaphor of a loan frames multiplication without calculation, multiplication without calculation returns attention to the metaphor of a loan. Ibn ʿAbbās understands the good loan as sincere charity that seeks Allah alone, with increase belonging to the One who gives and withholds provision.

**MULTIPLICATION WITHOUT CALCULATION**

***“multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases”*** deepens multiplication without calculation. The verse protects generosity from the market’s narrow calculation. Allah withholds and gives abundantly—yaqbiḍu wa-yabsuṭu—reminds people that provision is in His hand. Wealth may expand or contract as a test. The person who gives does not lose control of his future because Allah can replace what he spends. The person who withholds should not assume that hoarding guarantees safety. This does not deny human economic responsibility. A person budgets, supports dependants, repays debts, and invests lawfully. The verse teaches that planning should not become an excuse for miserliness or the belief that wealth is independent of Allah. The immediate context includes preparation for a just struggle and support for the community. Spending may therefore include equipment, food, medical care, refuge, and assistance to families affected by conflict. It also includes the broad charitable purposes named throughout the Qur’an. The cause must be lawful; donations to terrorism, aggression, corruption, or the abuse of civilians are not a qarḍ ḥasan. Institutions receiving funds must be transparent. A charity cannot invoke Allah’s promise to hide fraud, excessive salaries, or political manipulation. Trustees are responsible for directing money to its stated purpose. Wealth can become a rival to Allah when a person loves it more than truth and refuses every claim upon it. (9:103 — **“Take from their wealth ˹O Prophet˺ charity to purify and bless them,”**) Since provision narrowed and enlarged follows the metaphor of a loan, multiplication without calculation links purpose to provision narrowed and enlarged.

**PROVISION NARROWED AND ENLARGED**

***“and increases ˹wealth˺. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned”*** clarifies provision narrowed and enlarged. Spending breaks the illusion of absolute ownership. The giver says through action that money is a trust, not a master. This is why charity is called purification in 9:103. The recipient also has dignity. A gift should not be accompanied by reminders, insults, public photography without consent, or conditions that create dependence. The Qur’an warns that injury can cancel the reward of charity. The best giver seeks Allah’s acceptance, not the recipient’s humiliation or the audience’s applause. To Him you will be returned gives the metaphor its final seriousness. The wealth left behind will not accompany a person, but its use will. Every coin spent, withheld, earned, or taken unjustly returns to the account before Allah. The wise investor sends a portion ahead through charity and righteous support. The return also relativizes temporary scarcity. A person may feel that generosity makes him vulnerable, but Allah controls the final balance. The believer gives within ability, asks Allah for blessing, and avoids both waste and fear-driven hoarding. Think of the metaphor of a loan through a merchant who leaves his goods in the hands of a partner while he travels, and who spends the journey worrying about the stock. The goods are sold at a price he never asked for, and the worry was the only part of the trip that was his. (57:11 — **“Who is it that will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply ˹many times over˺ for them,”**) Spending in allah’s cause matters, which means provision narrowed and enlarged returns spending in allah’s cause to the metaphor of a loan.

**SPENDING IN ALLAH’S CAUSE**

The metaphor of a loan reaches a reader today who counts every gift he has made and wonders when the return will appear in his account. The verse gives him a loan, a promise and a return, and it asks him where he thinks the increase is kept. Because the hands that decrease and increase are named before the gathering at the end, multiplication without calculation makes the metaphor of a loan show that the wealth he holds is already on its way somewhere. Since the question is addressed to a person rather than to a crowd, the reader sees that the invitation stands open in every generation. The same invitation is made with the same words in the chapter of the mutual disregard. And the figure of the multiplication is drawn out in the parable of the grain. Multiplication without calculation makes the metaphor of a loan practical; provision narrowed and enlarged therefore matters.
