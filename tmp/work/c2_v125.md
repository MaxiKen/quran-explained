**SANCTUARY, RETURN, AND SAFETY**

The verse continues the account of Abraham with a place rather than a promise, and it describes what was made of the sanctuary: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We made the Sacred House a centre”*** and ***“and a sanctuary for the people ˹saying˺, “˹You may˺ take the standing-place of Abraham”*** and ***“as a site of prayer.”***. The two words for the House describe a gathering point and a place of safety, and the Book treats both as gifts given to humanity rather than privileges held by one family. The instruction that follows tells the visitors what to do there, and the instruction is attached to the name of the man who built it. The sanctuary that emerges belongs to the people who come to it, with a command to use it rather than to guard it as property. Ibn ʿAbbās read the House as a place people repeatedly long to return to, its safety as protection for the entrant, and its purification as removal of idols for every form of worship named.

**THE STANDING-PLACE AND THE PRAYER SITE**

The clause about the standing-place of Abraham turns a spot associated with a man into a place of prayer for everyone who arrives, and the Book's wording makes the association a reason for the command rather than a relic to be admired. Another passage joins the same two elements when it describes the House and names the sign within it: (3:97 — **“In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham”**). The verse's instruction is addressed to the visitors rather than to the man, which is worth noticing, since the sanctuary's purpose is stated in terms of what people do when they reach it. Someone who has seen a place of worship turned into a monument to its own history will find the clause's direction of attention useful, because the command is about prayer and not about veneration.

**THE TRUST TO PURIFY THE HOUSE**

The sentence then turns to the two men who were given a task: ***“And We entrusted Abraham”***, ***“and Ishmael to purify My House”***, and the task is described as purification rather than construction. The Book's word for purity here covers the removal of what does not belong in a place of worship, which is why the following clauses are about the people who use it rather than about the building's fabric. Another passage reports the same commission in the same words, and it adds the prohibition of associating anything with God in that place: (22:26 — **“purify My House for those who circle”**). The verse therefore presents the sanctuary as a trust held by two men for the benefit of everyone who comes, and the readers who inherit the story are meant to understand the work as continuing.

**A CARETAKER GIVEN THE KEYS**

Think of a caretaker handed the keys to a hall that belongs to a whole city, with instructions to keep it clean for the people who use it and not to make it his own address. The verse gives Abraham and his son exactly that kind of commission, and it names the users of the building in the clauses that follow. The comparison is useful because the temptation in such a role is always to mistake custody for ownership, and the sūrah is about to describe communities that made that mistake with an inheritance. Anyone who has seen a public building turned into a private club will understand why the next clauses describe the worshippers rather than the walls. The purpose for which the House was entrusted is what the sentence puts in front of the reader.

**CIRCLING, MEDITATING, BOWING**

The verse closes with a description of the worshippers, and the description is a list of postures: ***“for those who circle it”***, ***“who meditate in it”***, and ***“and who bow and prostrate themselves ˹in prayer˺”***. The three are named together without a rank among them, so that the House belongs to every kind of worshipper who comes to it. The same house is named in another passage as the starting point of a journey made at night, which shows how far the Book lets its honour travel: (17:1 — **“from the Sacred Mosque”**). The list also shows that the purification was for the sake of the people doing these things, so that the caretakers were servants of the worshippers rather than the other way around. Someone given charge of something that others use will recognise the arrangement described here, and the arrangement is the standard the Book applies to the sūrah's discussion of who may be trusted with sacred things. What remains with him is the picture of a house kept clean for the sake of those who pray in it. Today, a busy place of worship still needs physical care and moral purification so that visitors, residents and worshippers can approach without obstruction.
