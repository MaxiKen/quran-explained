**VICTORY SOUGHT THROUGH THE ONE LATER DENIED**

The verse recalls a hope the community had carried for generations and the way it behaved when the hope was granted: ***“Although they used to pray”***, ***“for victory ˹by means of the Prophet˺ over the polytheists”***, ***“when there came to them a Book from Allah”***, ***“which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had ˹in their hands˺, they rejected it.”***, ***“So may Allah's condemnation be upon the disbelievers”***. They had asked for a victory that would come through a prophet, and they had described his coming as their own vindication; the prophet came, the Book came, and their answer was the reverse of their prayer. What the verse adds is that they recognized it, so the refusal was not the result of a misunderstanding about which message had arrived. The confirmation of their own scripture is named as evidence in the sentence, since the Book that came confirmed what they already held and gave them no ground for surprise. Ibn ʿAbbās connected the earlier prayer for victory with neighbouring tribes and read the recognition as knowledge of the messenger's description already present in their possession.

**RECOGNITION AND WHAT CAME OF IT**

The Book uses the vocabulary of recognition deliberately, and it uses it again in a passage where those who were given the scripture are described discarding the new revelation as though it were merchandise: (2:101 — **“Now, when a messenger from Allah has come to them—confirming”**). The same image of a people who can identify the truth and then refuse it runs through the sūrah's account, and the psychology is not unusual. A person can recognise a claim as true and still reject it because accepting it would rearrange his position, and the Book presents this community as the clearest case of that condition in its own history. Anyone who has seen a person accept a fact and then act against it has watched the same behaviour at a smaller scale. The verse records it at the level of a nation so that the pattern is unmistakable.

**ANSWER TURNED INTO A GRIEVANCE**

A prayer for victory had long been part of how this people understood itself, and its fulfilment through a prophet of another nation turned the hope into a grievance. The covenant to acknowledge the messenger is recalled in another sūrah, which shows how old the arrangement was: (3:187 — **“˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when Allah took the covenant of those”**). What the community wanted was the victory rather than the one through whom it would arrive, and the verse exposes the difference between the two desires. The same difference is available to any reader who has prayed for an outcome and then found the means of it inconvenient. Nothing here suggests the prayer was insincere; what the sentence establishes is that the answer did not arrive in the shape the askers had imagined.

**RECOGNITION WITHOUT ACCEPTANCE**

Think of a student who has spent a term predicting the question that will appear on the examination, and who sees it on the paper and cannot bring himself to answer it because the answer is not the one he had prepared. A scripture was already in these people's hands, describing the messenger's coming, and the messenger's arrival made the description an obligation rather than a comfort. The Book's phrase about recognition removes the possibility that the refusal was honest confusion, and it leaves the community with a choice they had made in full knowledge. A reader who has been waiting for a particular thing to happen can ask himself what he would do if it happened through a door he does not like. The answer is what the verse is designed to test.

**A PRONOUNCEMENT AT THE CLOSE**

The sentence that closes the verse is a pronouncement rather than a description, and the Book uses that form when the conduct has been established beyond doubt. The community's prayer is set beside its refusal, and the verdict follows from the two facts in the same sentence. The chapter's argument does not rest on the verdict alone, since everything before it has been laid out for the reader to examine, and the reasoning is laid out so that a reader can follow it rather than accept a conclusion. What makes the passage useful is that the same pattern can be reproduced in a person's own life in a single week: a request made in earnest, an answer that arrives with a condition, and a decision taken quietly against it. The Book records the pattern at the scale of a nation and leaves the reader with the smaller version of it.

**PRAYER AS A MEASURE OF WHAT A PERSON IS WILLING TO RECEIVE**

The verse teaches something about the difference between wanting a result and wanting the truth, and it does so without any commentary of its own. The community's long prayer had become a slogan that no longer described what its speakers wanted, since a prayer for victory through a prophet is a prayer to be led by him. A reader can apply the test to the requests he makes most often, and he will find that some of them include conditions he has never spoken. The Book's account here is one of its clearest illustrations of that gap, because the community had stated the condition in advance without noticing it. What this refusal produced is traced in the verses that follow, and the sequence shows a people defending a position they have stopped examining. Today, a person may pray for an answer and resent it when it arrives through someone he did not expect to honour.
