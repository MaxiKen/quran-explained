**FOLLOWING DIVINE PLEASURE**

***“Are those who seek Allah’s pleasure like”*** Are those who seek Allah's pleasure like those who deserve Allah's wrath. Converts the spoils-guard's audit into the passage's sorting-question. And the question's two columns are the community's whole interior census. Afamani ttaba'a ridwana llahi. Is then the one who pursues Allah's pleasure. The ridwan-word. The divine good-pleasure this Book prices above Paradise's own furnishings. The column of the ranks whose day. Its obedience and its pardoned slips alike. Was transacted toward the Pleasure. Ka-man ba'a bi-sakhatin mina llah. Like the one who returns with wrath from Allah. The ba'a-verb. The counter-column carrying home, as its campaign's whole takings, the sakhat. The displeasure. The skimmer of the surrounding passage's file (his spoils' units converting to the neck's fire), the whisperers of the aftermath, every rank whose day was transacted toward the world's payroll at the Pleasure's expense. The question's rhetorical form leaving the columns' inequality to answer itself. Wa ma'wahu jahannam. And his refuge is Hell. Wa bi'sa l-masir. And evil is the destination. The wrath-column's terminus posted in the question's own breath. (3:161 — **“It is not appropriate”**)

**RETURNING WITH DIVINE WRATH**

***“those who deserve Allah’s wrath? Hell”*** The question's placement is the final account-passage's studied pivot. The Uhud-file had mingled the columns in one army. The hill's fifty containing both desires, the night's one darkness covering the drowsy and the whispering, the spoils-murmurs touching the ranks' trust. And the sorting-question separates what the field mingled. The community taught that its body's one uniform clothes two pursuits, and that the Day's census proceeds by the pursuits, never the uniforms. Whence the next verse's grading-clause (they each have varying degrees in the sight of Allah) will complete the census at its full resolution. Not two blocks but a spectrum of ranks, each soul's degree filed at its pursuit is exact register. (3:163 — **“And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do”**) In modern life, returning with divine wrath matters because two people may perform similar outward acts while moving toward divine pleasure and wrath in opposite directions.

**A MISERABLE DESTINATION**

***“is their home. What an evil destination”*** The contrast between pleasure and wrath supplies a lasting audit of every campaign. Every season's takings weighed at the question's scale. What did this venture return with. The Pleasure pursued, or the displeasure earned. The ba'a-grammar's severity being its mercy. The returns are chosen cargo, loaded transaction by transaction, and the columns exchangeable while the campaign runs. The question standing, in its interrogative permanence, over every homecoming. With what does this day return. Imagine two roads beginning side by side before one reaches a garden and the other a fire. For following divine pleasure, Ibn ʿAbbās contrasts one who seeks Allah’s pleasure by avoiding deceit with one who earns wrath and ends in the Fire. The question denies any moral equality between two directions; which means seeking Allah’s pleasure orders a life toward obedience, while returning with wrath means arriving with the result of chosen rebellion. Similar outward acts can therefore have different weight when their aims and limits differ; therefore the contrast also answers the temptation surrounding spoils. A person may gain an object and lose divine pleasure, which is not a profitable exchange; in other words hell is called the home of the second path because repeated choices eventually reveal where that path belongs. In present life, success is often judged by whether an aim was achieved; hence the verse first requires an account of whose pleasure the method sought and what anger it risked. An efficient wrong does not become equal to a costly obedience; this is why final destination exposes the difference that immediate results may hide. The verb seek places direction before arrival. A person repeatedly chooses what approval to pursue until those choices form a return. This makes daily motives part of the final destination. Small acts done for divine pleasure belong to one road, while impressive acts that knowingly earn wrath belong to the other. The contrast should therefore be applied before an action, when intention and method can still be corrected, rather than only after its result appears. The destination gives present motives a seriousness that outward success alone cannot supply.
