**THE CORRECTION THAT OPENS WITH A REFUSAL**

The claim of the previous sentence is met here, and the reply refuses the whole framing rather than adjusting it: ***“But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah”***. The refusal is aimed at the idea that salvation belongs to a community by name, and the sentence replaces the criterion with a description of a person. Submission and good conduct are named as the two marks of the one who has a reward with his Lord, and neither of them is an inherited identity. The Book's method here is to widen the door at the same moment that it removes the labels from it, which leaves the reader with a test he can apply to himself. What the previous verse asked the claimants to prove, this one answers by stating the condition on which the reward actually rests.

**SUBMISSION AND GOOD CONDUCT NAMED TOGETHER**

The two qualities are placed together because the Book does not recognise a submission that leaves conduct untouched, and the pairing is what turns a slogan about belonging into a description of a life. The word for submission carries the sense of handing over the whole self, and the good conduct that follows is the visible form of that handing over rather than a separate achievement. Another sūrah describes the same pair as a firm hold that a person can grasp, using language that fits this verse closely: (31:22 — **“Whoever fully submits themselves to Allah and is a good-doer”**). The sentence therefore states a condition that any person can meet, whatever community he was born into, which is why the refusal at its opening is followed by an invitation rather than by an exclusion. A reader who has been told that his standing depends on the group he belongs to will find the sentence's terms refreshingly ordinary.

**THE REWARD THAT BELONGS TO THE PERSON**

The next clause places the reward with the Lord rather than with the community: ***“and does good will have their reward with their Lord.”***. The Book consistently describes the reward as held by God rather than distributed by institutions, and the phrasing here removes every claim of ownership a community might assert over it. The same promise is made in the account of the believers, the Jews, the Christians, and the Sabians earlier in the sūrah, and the repetition of the terms there and here shows that the standard is not new: (2:62 — **“will have their reward with their Lord”**). What this sentence has accomplished, in two clauses, is to strip the claim of its exclusivity and to restate the condition in a form that applies to every human being. A reader who wants to know where he stands has the sentence's own criterion in front of him.

**AN ENTRANCE WITH NO GUEST LIST**

Think of a hall where the organisers had been arguing over whose names were on the list, and where the announcement comes instead that the door is open to anyone who meets the stated condition. The verse functions as that announcement, and it does so without naming the communities whose claims it has just refused. The comparison makes plain why the Book's answer is not a compromise between the two claimants, since neither is given what it wanted and both are offered the same terms. Anyone who has seen an argument about belonging collapse when the actual requirement is read out loud has seen the movement of this verse, and the movement is the Book's way of settling a dispute about privilege. What remains after the sentence is the condition itself, which each reader may test in his own life.

**THE FEAR AND THE GRIEF THAT ARE REMOVED**

The verse closes with a promise that appears in the Book when the reward has just been named, and it is stated twice in one sentence: ***“And there will be no fear”*** and then ***“for them, nor will they grieve”***. The two clauses remove the anxiety that belongs to the future and the sorrow that belongs to the past, which together cover the whole span of a person's worry. The Book uses the pair together here because the promise is being given to people who had been made anxious about their standing by the claims of others. The Book's habit of using these two words together makes the sentence a recognisable signature, and its appearance here ties the promise to the condition stated in the earlier clauses. The result is a verse that answers the claim of the previous sentence completely: it denies the exclusive reading, states the real condition, names where the reward is kept, and then takes away the two fears that motivated the dispute in the first place. A reader who has finished the sentence knows what is being asked of him and what he is being spared.
