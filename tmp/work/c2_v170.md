**INHERITED PRACTICE AND REVEALED GUIDANCE**

Inherited practice and revealed guidance draws together ***“When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “No!”***. The verse describes people who answer a call to follow revelation with an appeal to ancestral custom. They do not say, We examined the evidence and found it unconvincing. They say, We will follow what we found our forefathers doing. Their argument is social rather than intellectual: the practice is old, familiar, and attached to respected names, so it must be preserved. Islam honours righteous ancestors, family ties, and beneficial tradition. The problem is not that a practice has been inherited. Much of religious knowledge is transmitted through trustworthy teachers and communities. The problem is treating ancestry as a proof that can overrule Allah’s revelation. A custom is evaluated by truth; truth is not evaluated merely by age. The rhetorical question exposes the weakness of blind imitation. The rebuke assumes that ancestors may have lacked understanding, investigation, and guidance. Reverence for parents cannot require following them into shirk or injustice. The Qur’an commands kindness to parents while stating that they must not be obeyed in associating partners with Allah. Good family relations and independent moral responsibility coexist. The verse is not an insult to every forefather. Some ancestors were knowledgeable and guided, and following their sound teaching can be praiseworthy. It is directed at people who use ancestry as a shield against clear evidence, even when the inherited practice is demonstrably false or harmful. (2:168 — **“O humanity! Eat from what is lawful”**) Because inherited practice and revealed guidance governs 2:170, blind imitation and responsible learning arises from inherited practice and revealed guidance. It follows that the courage to break a harmful pattern completes inherited practice and revealed guidance through blind imitation and responsible learning.

**BLIND IMITATION AND RESPONSIBLE LEARNING**

***“We ˹only˺ follow what we found our forefathers practicing.” ˹Would they still do so,˺ even”*** mark the turn toward taqlīd and responsible learning. The Arabic concept of *blind imitation* can mean following a qualified authority without personally deriving every ruling from its evidence. For a non-specialist, asking scholars is necessary and reasonable: Ask the people of knowledge if you do not know. The condemned imitation is not all reliance on scholarship; it is stubborn adherence to an inherited position after the truth has become clear, or following an unqualified person simply because he is famous or familiar. Responsible learning combines trust with discernment. The ordinary believer consults reliable scholars, compares claims with the Qur’an and authentic Sunnah, and avoids making every personal preference into an independent legal school. The scholar, in turn, does not demand loyalty to himself in place of loyalty to Allah. Every society has customs that appear natural because they are repeated. Some are harmless or beneficial; others preserve discrimination, exploitation, superstition, or family abuse. The verse asks Muslims to examine inherited practices through the principles of revelation. A tradition that strengthens honesty, hospitality, kinship, and justice may be retained. A tradition that violates divine oneness or human rights must be corrected, even if correction is unpopular. The same warning applies to modern customs. People sometimes reject revelation while claiming to follow *what everyone does.* Modernity is not automatically guidance, just as antiquity is not automatically truth. The Qur’an gives a criterion independent of fashion and ancestry. (5:104 — **“if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance”**) The reason the courage to break a harmful pattern follows blind imitation and responsible learning lies in inherited practice and revealed guidance. Therefore, the courage to break a harmful pattern returns practical force to blind imitation and responsible learning.

**THE COURAGE TO BREAK A HARMFUL PATTERN**

The verse develops the courage to break a harmful pattern with ***“if their forefathers had ˹absolutely˺ no understanding or guidance”***. Following revelation can require a person to disappoint relatives or leave a familiar environment. This must be done with wisdom, patience, and good character. The believer does not humiliate parents or insult a culture when rejecting a false practice. He explains the reason, preserves lawful ties, and refuses obedience only where obedience would violate Allah’s command. Prophets repeatedly stood against the inherited religion of their people. Ibrāhīm confronted the worship of idols while speaking with clarity and courage. His example shows that loyalty to truth is not disrespect for family; it is the highest form of concern when a family is travelling toward harm. An inherited route can be like a trail worn deep across a field: its depth proves that many feet used it, not that it reaches the right destination. A traveller still needs a map and must be willing to leave the track when it leads away from safety. Family custom and communal habit have similar force in the present day. The verse does not despise ancestors; it refuses to make age a substitute for understanding and guidance, preserving what is true while testing every inheritance by revelation. (31:14 — **“So be grateful to Me”**) In other words, the courage to break a harmful pattern answers inherited practice and revealed guidance.
