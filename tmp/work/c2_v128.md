**THE PRAYER CONTINUES**

The verse carries on the prayer that the two builders began, and it moves from their own work to the community that would come after them: ***“Our Lord! Make us both ˹fully˺ submit to You”*** and ***“and from our descendants a nation”*** and ***“that will submit to you. Show us our rituals”***. The first request is about the two men themselves, and it asks for a state of submission rather than for a reward, which fits the posture the Book has assigned to this prophet from the beginning of his story. The second request reaches forward to a people not yet born, and it asks for the same condition in them as in their ancestors. The founder in view was concerned not with completing a building but with continuing a religion.

**A NATION ASKED FOR FROM THE DESCENDANTS**

The clause about the descendants uses a word that describes a whole community rather than a family line, and the prayer asks for a nation that will submit as the two men had submitted. Another passage sums up the same history by naming the way of this prophet as a designation that God Himself gave, which shows how the request was answered: (22:78 — **“the way of your forefather Abraham”**). The hopes of the founders are placed in a people rather than in a place, and the account of the Children of Israel earlier in the sūrah has shown how often the two were confused. A community that inherits a sanctuary without inheriting the submission of its builder has inherited the smaller thing, and the prayer here asks for the greater.

**RITUALS SHOWN, NOT INVENTED**

The request about the rituals is worth a second look, since the two men ask to be shown rather than to be left to work the matter out. The Book's treatment of worship in this sūrah is consistently about what has been prescribed rather than what has been devised, and the prayer states the principle in three words. Another passage describes the community that resulted by listing what the messenger did among them, and the list begins with recitation and purification before it reaches the teaching of the Book: (2:151 — **“reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you”**). The order in the prayer is therefore the order the Book keeps elsewhere, with the showing and the following placed before everything else.

**TWO REQUESTS IN ONE BREATH**

The verse then asks for a turning in grace, and it closes with the two names that make the request reasonable: ***“and turn to us in grace. You are truly the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”***. The men who were building a house of worship asked to be received, and the Book records the request in the same breath as their work. The names used at the end are the ones a person needs when he has built and cannot assess what he has built: one who accepts the return, and one whose mercy is not exhausted. The reader has already heard the same man asking for his prayers to be accepted, and the repetition shows how the posture of the builder and the posture of the supplicant were one thing in this prophet. Another passage names the same attribute for a community in distress, which shows how the Book uses it when a person has nothing left but the request: (9:118 — **“And ˹Allah has also turned in mercy to˺ the three who had remained behind”**). What the sentence leaves with him is the practice of ending work with a request for pardon.

**A BUILDER WHO ASKS TO BE RECEIVED AS HE WORKS**

Think of a man who finishes a wall for a mosque and then asks the owner of the land to forgive him for the parts he did poorly. The verse does not separate the work from the request, and the two are placed together in the same prayer. The comparison matters because the Book's account of worship treats confidence and humility as partners rather than alternatives, and the men in the scene had both in full measure. Anyone who has completed something for God and then examined it honestly will know why the sentence asks for grace after asking for guidance. The acceptance of a community begins with the acknowledgment that it has nothing to present on its own account, which is why the request for grace stands beside the work.
