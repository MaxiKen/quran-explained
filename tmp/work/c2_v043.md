**KEEPING THE PRAYER IN ITS PROPER FORM**

Belief in the message is to take visible form in worship: ***“Establish prayer,”***. The people addressed must not remain observers who discuss scripture while standing outside its demands. Qatādah identifies prayer and the alms due beside it as two binding duties. Establishing prayer means keeping it faithfully, with its required acts and conditions, rather than merely performing a gesture from time to time. The command does not invent worship for a people who had never known it. Jesus speaks of the duty (19:31 — **“to establish prayer and give alms-tax as long as I live”**). Earlier messengers taught prayer and giving, even where the prescribed forms differed. Here those duties must be fulfilled in obedience to the Messenger whose revelation the preceding verses command them to believe. Because the call to worship follows that call to faith, the two cannot be separated into rival claims. Respect for earlier revelation is not a reason to refuse the worship now required. The response involves time, bodily action, and willing submission to God, not an opinion about the merits of someone else’s religion.

Regular prayer has an appointed place in the day: (4:103 — **“Indeed, performing prayers is a duty on the believers at the appointed times”**). Its timing prevents worship from being postponed whenever another wish seems more pressing. Its form also matters. In the account from Rifāʿah ibn Rāfiʿ carried by al-Nasāʾī, the Prophet sends a man back to repeat a prayer that he has not performed properly. He then teaches him to let the body settle in its positions, including bowing, standing upright, and prostrating. Since each act must be done fully, rushing through the movements does not fulfil the command. The heart’s humility does not excuse carelessness with the body, nor can correct movements excuse deliberate neglect of the heart. Prayer is meant to shape the person who performs it: (29:45 — **“Indeed, ˹genuine˺ prayer should deter ˹one˺ from indecency and wickedness.”**). If wrongdoing persists, the worshipper should therefore examine how prayer is being kept, not claim that God’s promise has failed. The command seeks a sustained act of obedience whose effects reach beyond the moments spent standing and bowing.

**WEALTH HAS A DUE THAT THE OWNER DOES NOT INVENT**

The next duty is ***“pay alms-tax,”***. This is zakāh, the prescribed due from wealth that meets its conditions, not simply a generous feeling or whatever donation the owner finds convenient. Giving it requires delivery to those entitled to receive it. The Quran identifies its recipients, beginning with (9:60 — **“Alms-tax is only for the poor and the needy,”**). Their right is not created by the donor’s wish to be praised. The act also cleanses the giver: (9:103 — **“charity to purify and bless them,”**). What leaves the hand can free the heart from a claim of absolute ownership. Its growth lies in blessing and reward, not in a promise that the sum remaining in a purse instantly increases. Prayer and this due thus make different claims upon the same believer. One requires the body and time; the other requires surrender of a share of wealth. Paying is an act of worship because God has assigned the obligation. It cannot be replaced by admiration for charity while the amount actually owed is kept back.

To work out the amount due, the giver needs the Prophet’s teaching. In al-Bukhārī, Ibn ʿUmar reports that the Prophet set a tenth on crops watered by rain or natural sources, and half that rate on crops watered by irrigation. The difference takes account of the work involved in supplying water. It does not leave the owner free to choose either rate. The same collection carries Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī’s report of minimum amounts below which no due is taken from specified kinds of wealth. A person must therefore learn the rule for his wealth, not use the broad command to choose a sum at will. Not every possession falls under one rate or one threshold. The practical issue is the right amount, from the right property, delivered in the right way. This also explains why voluntary giving and the prescribed due are not identical. Generosity may go beyond what is owed, but a larger display of kindness elsewhere does not by itself discharge the amount owed.

**ENTERING THE ROW WITHOUT CLAIMING A PLACE ABOVE IT**

The words ***“and bow down”*** name a bodily act that belongs to prayer. Bowing can express submission in ordinary speech, but the prayer setting gives it its specific ritual sense here. Muqātil understands the address as calling the People of the Book to pray with Muhammad and his Companions. Naming bowing can therefore stand for the whole prayer, because it is one of the prayer’s essential acts. It is not an invitation to offer an isolated bow as a substitute for the rest. The Quran elsewhere joins the movements: (22:77 — **“O believers! Bow down, prostrate yourselves, worship your Lord,”**). Nor is bodily bowing wholly unknown in the worship of earlier servants of God. Mary is told, (3:43 — **“and bow along with those who bow down.””**). The present command is thus best understood through the worship and company required, rather than a sweeping claim that no earlier prophet’s followers ever bowed. The person’s willingness to lower himself before God must take the form God teaches, not a form chosen to protect his own dignity.

The added demand is to do so ***“with those who bow down”***. Those addressed must join the people at prayer, not merely approve of them from a distance. The legal weight of congregational prayer also depends on the Prophet’s teaching. In Aḥmad’s ruling, each man able to attend must do so. In Muslim’s report from Abū Hurayrah, a blind man seeks permission to pray at home because he has no guide; after learning that he hears the call, the Prophet tells him to attend. The strongly urged practice reading instead stresses the report from Ibn ʿUmar in Muslim that prayer together exceeds prayer alone by twenty-seven degrees. That comparison recognizes a prayer performed alone, so the dispute also concerns whether attendance determines validity. This duty for able men without a valid excuse is adopted here, because the instruction to answer the call is direct. This need not make every prayer offered alone invalid. Being bound to attend is one issue; whether a prayer offered alone is valid is another. Neither view makes it a slight matter to keep away from the prayer in the mosque.

Joining the row also changes the place from which a learned person serves. He is no longer outside the practice, advising others to do what he regards as beneath himself. Knowledge is used to guide worship. In al-Bukhārī, Ibn ʿUmar relates that Sālim, the freedman of Abū Ḥudhayfah, led the early emigrants in prayer because he knew more of the Quran. His service shows that birth and rank do not decide who is fit to lead. The congregation needs knowledge of the prayer and faithful conduct, not a claim of social importance. Once an imam leads, those behind him must follow rather than race him. The warning in al-Bukhārī and Muslim against raising the head before the imam makes that discipline serious. The shared act is not a contest to finish first. Thus the closing command answers both pride and isolation. The one who accepts God’s message joins its people in an ordered act of worship, serving through what he knows and submitting alongside those he might once have looked down upon.
