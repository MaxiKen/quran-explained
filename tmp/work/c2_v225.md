**UNINTENTIONAL OATHS**

***“Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional”*** establishes unintentional oaths. This verse distinguishes careless or unintentional expressions from deliberate commitments. People often say By Allah as a habitual phrase without intending an oath, or they swear about something while honestly believing it to be true and later discover that they were mistaken. Such speech is not treated as though the heart consciously entered a binding covenant. The jurists call this category laghw al-yamīn, an idle or unintended oath. Its precise examples differ among the schools, but the principle is that Allah judges intention and awareness, not isolated sounds detached from meaning. This is mercy for ordinary speech and human error. Allah holds people accountable for what their hearts have earned—kasabat qulūbukum. This refers to deliberate intention, conscious commitment, and the falsehood or harm a person chooses. A person who knowingly swears a lie, uses an oath to consume another’s wealth, or makes a vow and then breaks it without proper reason has acted from the heart, not from a slip of the tongue. The heart in Qur’anic language is the centre of intention, understanding, and moral choice. Accountability is not limited to visible action, nor is it based on thoughts that a person rejects. A passing temptation is not the same as a settled decision. Allah knows the difference between involuntary mental noise and chosen intention. A deliberate oath that is broken requires the expiation detailed in 5:89: feeding ten needy people, clothing them, or freeing a slave; if unable, fasting three days. (5:89 — **“Allah will not call you to account for your thoughtless oaths,”**) Because unintentional oaths frames what the heart has earned, what the heart has earned returns attention to unintentional oaths.

**WHAT THE HEART HAS EARNED**

***“oaths, but for what you intended in”*** deepens what the heart has earned. A false oath concerning a past or present matter is a grave sin and requires sincere repentance and restoration of rights, not a mechanical payment that allows the lie to remain. If an oath prevents a better action, the person follows the better action and offers expiation, as taught by the Prophet ﷺ. This balances the seriousness of vows with the priority of righteousness. The believer does not make many oaths and then treat them as harmless, but he also does not believe that every habitual phrase creates a legal burden. Allah is Ghafūr, forgiving, and Ḥalīm, forbearing. His forbearance means that He does not rush to punish every error and gives servants time to correct themselves. This encourages the person who discovers that he spoke carelessly to repent and repair without falling into despair. Forbearance is not permission to continue false oaths. It is an opportunity for the heart to become truthful. A person who repeatedly invokes Allah’s name in deception should fear that the delay of punishment is a test, not approval. Oaths make speech weightier, so a believer uses them sparingly. He avoids swearing to pressure a spouse, persuade a customer, frighten a child, or win a dispute. If he is uncertain, he says he is uncertain. If he made a mistake, he corrects it plainly. Reverence for Allah’s name produces calm truthfulness rather than theatrical certainty. The Prophet ﷺ warned against false oaths that take a Muslim’s property, even when the oath is persuasive. (2:224 — **“Do not use Allah’s Name in your oaths as an excuse for not doing good,”**) Since oath, error, and expiation follows unintentional oaths, what the heart has earned links purpose to oath, error, and expiation.

**OATH, ERROR, AND EXPIATION**

***“your hearts. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing”*** clarifies oath, error, and expiation. He taught that a person who swears and then sees something better should do the better act and expiate. These teachings show that words are connected to rights and conduct. The verse does not make human beings responsible for every involuntary thought or emotional reaction. A person may feel anger and still choose not to act unjustly; he may experience doubt and still seek knowledge; he may have a passing desire and reject it. Allah judges what the heart intentionally adopts and what the person brings into speech and action. This distinction offers hope to people troubled by intrusive thoughts or unwanted impulses. They should not treat involuntary mental events as chosen disbelief. They seek help, ignore the whisper, and return to deliberate faith and action. Think of unintentional oaths through a man who keeps a book of the debts owed to him and the debts he owes, and who writes the second column in pencil because nobody reads it. The debts are collected from him one by one, and the pencil marks are honoured by the day he has to answer. Unintentional oaths reaches a reader today who says by God in every second sentence and then wonders why his promises have no weight in his own house. The verse gives him a distinction between a sound and a decision, and it asks him how many of his oaths his heart ever signed. Because the closing name is the Forbearing, what the heart has earned makes unintentional oaths show that the mercy of God is not the licence of the tongue. (3:154 — **“Then after distress, He sent down serenity in the form of drowsiness overcoming some of you,”**) Forgiveness beyond careless speech matters, which means oath, error, and expiation returns forgiveness beyond careless speech to unintentional oaths.

**FORGIVENESS BEYOND CARELESS SPEECH**

Since the account of an oath is kept by the One who knows the intention, unintentional oaths shows that the careless word and the sworn promise are read in the same book. Because the two names follow a ruling about speech, the reader is shown the temper of the law. Since the patience of the Lord is named in the same sentence as the forgiveness, what the heart has earned makes unintentional oaths show that the mercy of a ruling is part of its meaning. A person may speak a hundred oaths in a month and be asked about none of them, and the patience of the Lord is the ground under that mercy. The Book rules on the broken deliberate oath in the chapter of the table, and the sentence before this one forbids the use of the name of God as a wall against good. The reader of the two passages is shown that the Book treats the vow as a matter of purpose from its first line to its last. What the heart has earned makes unintentional oaths practical; oath, error, and expiation therefore matters.
