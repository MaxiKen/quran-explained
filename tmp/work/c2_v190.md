**PERMISSION WITH A BOUNDARY**

The permission's phrases stand in a deliberate order of cause, target, limit, disposition: ***“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you”***, ***“but do not exceed the limits. Allah does not like transgressors”***. Reading the permission in this order keeps the fence attached to the verb, since the limit arrives before the disposition that guards it. A permission memorized without its fence is a different permission entirely, and the walk keeps them together. Ibn ʿAbbās's wording on the condition is confirmed by the order of these phrases. The commander who holds the four elements in one memory holds the permission as it was granted and not as the campaign's momentum would prefer to remember it.

Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you grants a permission and fences it in the same breath. The cause is Allah's, the targets are defined by their own aggression, and the second clause — but do not exceed the limits — turns the permission into a legal category with edges. The verse was revealed in the setting recorded after the pact of Hudaybiyya, when the community was prevented from the House and then promised its return, and the permission to fight belongs to that refusal and the pressures that followed it. The permission is granted to the wronged rather than to the strong, which is the logic of the legislation on fighting everywhere it appears (22:39 — **“Permission ˹to fight back˺ is ˹hereby˺ granted to those being fought, for they have been wronged.”**). The cause and the target are both required; the one who fights the wrong enemy has left the permission's fence.

**AGAINST THE AGGRESSORS ONLY**

The defining phrase is doing the work of the entire law of war: the criterion is aggression, not identity. Ibn ʿAbbās's wording keeps the condition in view — the command is against those who initiate fighting against you, and the prohibition follows: begin not hostilities. Because the criterion is behavioral, it can be applied by anyone with eyes, and the soldier is spared the task of sorting peoples instead of sorting acts. The permission's fence is therefore practical: the one who has not waged war is outside the verse's target, whatever his other faults. The same logic appears in the prohibition against killing those who do not fight, and in the treaties the mission honored even with its enemies. The defining phrase functions as a standing court for every commander.

**DISLIKED AND NAMED**

The close of the verse gives the limit its theology: Allah does not like transgressors. The dislike is named in a sentence attached to the boundary, so the one who exceeds the limit does not merely break a rule — he acts against the divine taste. The phrase returns in the legislation on the lawful and the forbidden, where the same dislike guards the boundary from the other side: do not forbid what God has made lawful, and do not transgress (5:87 — **“Allah does not like transgressors”**). The two boundaries — the battlefield's and the table's — are therefore guarded by one disposition of God toward the transgressor. Because the dislike is stated in the middle of the permission's verse, the permission itself is to be exercised with fear. The soldier who reads the closing words fights differently from the one who stops at the middle.

**A LIMIT INSIDE THE ORDER**

The verse's arrangement — cause, target, limit, dislike — is a compressed manual, and each element corrects the one before it. The cause corrects the target, the target corrects the cause, and the limit corrects the strength. The application is to read the whole sentence before acting on the verb: the fight belongs to a cause that is not the fighter's ego, against an aggressor the fighter did not choose, within limits the fighter did not draw. The one who cannot find all three elements in his situation stands outside the permission. Because the permission is granted in the cause of Allah, the fighting is a duty of protection rather than a sport of conquest. The boundary is the meaning of the permission. The verse is worth reading against the war-songs of every age, because the permission is written for the wronged and the songs are usually sung by the strong. The one who quotes the first clause without the second has purchased a weapon the verse does not sell. Because the limit is stated by the same voice that grants the permission, the soldier's discipline is a matter of obedience and not of taste. The permission travels with its fence. The verse is short enough to be memorized in a minute and strict enough to govern a campaign, which is the standard proportion of the chapter's laws. The one who memorizes the verb and forgets the fence has memorized half a verse and armed himself with the dangerous half. The chapter's method is to give permissions their boundaries in the same breath, and this verse is the clearest instance of the method.
