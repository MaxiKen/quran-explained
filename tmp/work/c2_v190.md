**THE PERMISSION IS BOUNDED**

The wording for the permission is bounded is ***“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you”***. This verse addresses the Muslim community in a context of persecution, expulsion, and armed conflict. The permission is not a general command to attack every non-Muslim, nor is it a licence for private violence. It concerns a legitimate community defending itself under lawful authority and within moral limits. A conflict motivated by conquest, wealth, ethnic hatred, revenge, or personal power is not made righteous by a religious slogan. The cause, intention, authority, conduct, and treatment of non-combatants all matter. The immediate wording identifies fighting opponents who are actively waging war. Other Qur’anic passages confirm that Muslims are to act kindly and justly toward those who do not fight them or expel them because of religion, and that if an enemy inclines to peace, Muslims should incline to it. The command therefore cannot be separated from the surrounding legal and historical context. This does not mean that a community must wait passively until every attack reaches its homes. Defence can include protecting borders, preventing imminent aggression, and assisting people under attack, according to lawful authority and just cause. But claims of *pre-emptive defence* cannot become a cover for domination or aggression. The principle is protection of life and freedom from oppression, not the pursuit of empire. (5:8 — **“O believers! Stand firm”**) Because the permission is bounded governs 2:190, restraint within defence arises from the permission is bounded. It follows that legitimate authority and accountability completes the permission is bounded through restraint within defence.

**RESTRAINT WITHIN DEFENCE**

The movement toward restraint within defence rests on ***“but do not exceed the limits. Allah does not like transgressors”***. The prohibition of transgression—*do not exceed the limits*—is the controlling limit. It includes initiating unjust aggression, killing children, women, elderly people, religious recluses, and other non-combatants, mutilating bodies, destroying crops and homes without military necessity, torturing prisoners, betraying agreements, and exceeding proportionality. The Prophet ﷺ prohibited the killing of women and children and forbade treachery, as reported in Muslim’s collection and the Ṣaḥīḥ of al-Bukhārī. The legal details of combat are handled by qualified authorities and jurists, but the moral core is clear. The enemy’s wrongdoing does not make every act against him lawful. Muslims remain servants of Allah in war; they do not suspend prayer, truthfulness, mercy, or accountability when violence begins. The Qur’an normally treats life as sacred and killing as a grave matter. Fighting is permitted under exceptional circumstances because refusing to restrain aggression can expose more people to death and oppression. The permission must therefore be directed toward ending aggression and restoring safety, not feeding anger. The Prophet ﷺ showed this balance in his conduct. He prepared for defence, accepted treaties, forgave when victory made revenge possible, protected those who sought safety, and prohibited the abuse of captives. His military practice cannot be reduced either to pacifism or to unrestricted violence. (60:8 — **“Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly”**) The reason legitimate authority and accountability follows restraint within defence lies in the permission is bounded. Therefore, legitimate authority and accountability returns practical force to restraint within defence.

**LEGITIMATE AUTHORITY AND ACCOUNTABILITY**

Individual Muslims do not decide on their own that a neighbour, government, religious group, or stranger is an enemy who may be attacked. Declaring war and applying criminal penalties require legitimate authority, evidence, consultation, and accountability. The chaos produced by private militias and terrorist groups is itself a form of transgression. Leaders are not exempt from the verse. They must state the cause honestly, protect civilians, care for prisoners and wounded people, and stop hostilities when the lawful objective is achieved. Soldiers remain accountable for unlawful orders; *I was following orders* does not make deliberate cruelty permissible. The command tests the believer’s character precisely when anger is strongest. The Qur’an later says that hatred of a people must not cause believers to abandon justice. Even an enemy retains human rights, and the possibility of repentance, surrender, or peace must be respected. Justice is not a favour granted only to friends. This principle also applies outside war. A person who is insulted cannot answer with slander, assault, or harm to innocent relatives. A community that suffers injustice may defend itself, but its defence must remain proportionate and directed at the wrongdoer. Rules for fighting are like the banks built around a dangerous river. The force may be necessary to stop an aggressor, but without firm boundaries it floods the very people it was meant to protect. Cause, target, and restraint are therefore not decorations added after battle; they distinguish lawful defence from transgression. In present-day conflicts, anger, propaganda, and distance can hide civilians behind labels, so the command continues to judge conduct even when a side believes its cause is just. (8:61 — **“If the enemy is inclined towards peace, make peace with them.”**) In other words, legitimate authority and accountability answers the permission is bounded.
