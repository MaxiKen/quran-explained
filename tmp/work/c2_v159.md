**THE KEEPERS WHO WITHHELD WHAT THEY HELD**

The verse returns to the theme of concealment that has run through the sūrah since the change of direction, and it states it in its widest form: ***“Those who hide the clear proofs”*** and ***“and guidance that We have revealed”***. What is hidden is not an opinion or a private reading but the evidence itself together with the guidance that follows from it, which means the fault lies in withholding what people need in order to find the way. The wording places the responsibility on those who have received the knowledge, since a person cannot conceal what he does not possess, and the verse is therefore addressed to the class of people who were meant to be the teachers of others. The account of the covenant that the People of the Scripture had taken is kept in another sūrah for exactly this reason: (3:187 — **“who were given the Scripture to make it known to people and not hide it,”**). What the sentence describes is not ignorance but a decision made after knowledge has arrived.

**THE CLARITY THAT MAKES THE SILENCE GUILTY**

The verse interrupts its own description to name the point at which concealment became indefensible: ***“after We made it clear”*** and ***“for humanity in the Book”***. The word rendered as making clear carries the sense of putting something in the middle of the road where it can be seen, and the phrase "for humanity" removes the excuse that the knowledge belonged to one nation alone. The book in view is the scripture that the people addressed had in their hands, and the matter made clear in it includes the description of the messenger who was to come. A later verse of the same sūrah describes those who hide the revelations as trading them for a passing gain: (2:174 — **“Indeed, those who hide Allah’s revelations, trading them for a fleeting gain consume nothing”**). The guilt is measured by the clarity, and the clarity is measured by what the Book had already shown them.

**A CURSE THAT COMES FROM EVERY SIDE**

The sentence then pronounces the judgement: ***“will be condemned by Allah”*** and ***“and ˹all˺ those who condemn”***. The curse is not a sentence spoken once, since the wording describes the condition of being driven away from mercy and remaining under that exclusion. The cursers have been understood to include the angels, the believers, and even the creatures of the earth, since the harm of a concealed guidance is not confined to the one who hides it. The Book describes a scholar's knowledge as something for which the fish of the sea and the birds of the air ask forgiveness, and the antithesis of that picture is the man who buries what he was given. A later verse of the same sūrah names the company that joins in the curse, so that the exclusion is total: (2:161 — **“Surely those who disbelieve and die as disbelievers are condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity”**). What the verse shows, then, is a wrong whose consequences run through the whole creation.

**A WELL COVERED OVER IN A THIRSTY TOWN**

Think of a man in a thirsty town who finds a well and covers it over, not because the water is bad but because he wishes to keep for himself the advantage of knowing where it is. The travellers who die on that road are not killed by his hand, and yet the harm is his, and the town's memory of him will be shaped by the well he hid. The verse describes the same act committed with knowledge rather than water, and it treats the harm as both public and permanent. The traveller who dies of thirst is not the only loser, since the town loses the confidence that used to hold it together. Anyone who has watched a teacher withhold what a student needed will recognise the shape of the offence. The sentence's further point is that no concealment stays private.

**THE END OF THE MATTER AND WHAT FOLLOWS IT**

Read with the verse that comes next, the passage does not end in condemnation, since the door of repentance is opened at the very next sentence for those who mend what they broke. The order is deliberate: the offence is named in its full weight, and then the remedy is named in full, so that the warning serves the correction rather than replacing it. The Book repeats the theme of concealed revelation in several places, and it always attaches the warning to the covenant of knowledge rather than to a personal failing. The community is therefore taught that what it has learned carries an obligation towards those who have not learned it yet. The sentence that pronounces the curse is thus the last word on concealment and not the last word on the people who conceal.
