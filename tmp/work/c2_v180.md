**WEALTH AND THE APPROACHING END**

***“It is prescribed that”***, ***“when death approaches any of you”*** establish wealth and the approaching end. This āyah turns from criminal justice to the justice of family wealth. When death approaches and a person leaves property, Allah prescribes a *bequest*, a bequest, for parents and close relatives in a fair manner. Death exposes the temporary nature of ownership. What a person gathered must be transferred without greed, revenge, or neglect of those who depended upon him. The command to make a bequest also encourages preparation. A Muslim should not leave debts, trusts, family responsibilities, or charitable commitments hidden. The Prophet ﷺ taught that a person who has something to bequeath should not allow many nights to pass without having his bequest written, as recorded in al-Bukhārī’s Ṣaḥīḥ and Muslim’s corresponding collection. Writing protects memory and prevents disputes, especially when illness or sudden death makes speech difficult. Parents and relatives are mentioned because family ties create rights and responsibilities. Wealth should not be treated as an instrument for rewarding favourites or punishing relatives after death. It should account for genuine need, prior support, debts, and the rights established by revelation. The verse is not a permission to deprive a spouse, daughter, orphan, or weaker relative through clever documents. Justice in inheritance protects people who might otherwise be overridden by the strongest members of a family. A testator’s affection is respected, but it is governed by Allah’s limits. (4:7 — **“whether it is little”**) Because wealth and the approaching end governs 2:180, the relationship with fixed inheritance arises from wealth and the approaching end. It follows that fairness within a bequest completes wealth and the approaching end through the relationship with fixed inheritance.

**THE RELATIONSHIP WITH FIXED INHERITANCE**

For the relationship with fixed inheritance, the decisive wording is ***“if they leave something of value”***. The inheritance shares are detailed later in chapter al-Nisāʾ. Classical scholars discussed how those later verses relate to this early command. The accepted legal framework is that Allah’s fixed shares must be observed, and a bequest cannot be used to alter them. The well-known hadith states, Allah has given each person entitled to a share his share, so there is no bequest for an heir, recorded in Sunan Abū Dāwūd, Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, and other collections. Thus a person may bequeath up to one-third of the estate to non-heirs or charitable purposes, while heirs receive the shares Allah assigned. A bequest to an heir may be valid only with the consent of the other heirs after death, according to the details of the schools. These rules prevent the living from using final instructions as a way to override the divine distribution. Some exegetes described the early general command as qualified or superseded in its application to fixed heirs by the later inheritance verses. Others emphasized that the principle of making a fair bequest remains for relatives who do not inherit under the fixed shares. In either case, the Qur’an’s enduring aims are clear: protect family rights, prevent arbitrary exclusion, and preserve lawful charity. Before distributing an estate, debts and valid trusts must be settled. chapter al-Nisāʾ repeatedly mentions inheritance after a bequest or debt. A person should record loans, unpaid wages, entrusted property, mahr, alms obligations, and other liabilities. Concealing a debt so that heirs can enjoy the estate is a grave betrayal. The executor must act as an *trustworthy executor*, a trustworthy administrator. He may not take hidden fees, favour one heir, delay distribution to control relatives, or pressure vulnerable heirs into surrendering their rights. The Prophet ﷺ warned against consuming the wealth of orphans and taught that trusts must be returned to their owners. (4:176 — **“so you do not go astray.”**) The reason fairness within a bequest follows the relationship with fixed inheritance lies in wealth and the approaching end. Therefore, fairness within a bequest returns practical force to the relationship with fixed inheritance.

**FAIRNESS WITHIN A BEQUEST**

Read under fairness within a bequest, the relevant clauses are ***“a will should be made in favour of parents”***, ***“and immediate family with fairness. ˹This is˺ an obligation on those”***. The testator considers actual need and the wider obligations of the estate. The bequest should not become a tool for continuing family conflict after death. The Prophet ﷺ advised Saad ibn Abu Waqqas that leaving heirs financially secure is better than leaving them dependent upon people, while allowing a charitable bequest within the lawful limit. This hadith, recorded in al-Bukhārī’s Ṣaḥīḥ and Muslim’s corresponding collection, balances generosity with responsibility toward dependants. The final phrase calls the practice a duty upon the *muttaqūn*. Mindfulness is necessary because wills are often executed when the person who wrote them can no longer defend himself. The mindful person knows that Allah sees hidden arrangements, verbal promises, and the treatment of the weak. He writes honestly, seeks qualified legal and religious advice, and tells his family where the document can be found. The heirs also need mindfulness. They must not manipulate the dying, forge documents, conceal assets, or consume another heir’s share. A person who receives less in the worldly division but remains patient and lawful has not lost before Allah. A person who takes more through deception has not truly gained. (5:106 — **“otherwise, two non-Muslims if you are afflicted with death”**)

**MERCY AND FAMILY CONTINUITY**

Mercy and family continuity begins from ***“who are mindful ˹of Allah˺”***. Islamic inheritance law is not merely a system of arithmetic. It is designed to distribute wealth, protect kinship, support dependants, and prevent accumulation in one powerful hand. A bequest can address needs that fixed shares cannot cover: a relative in hardship, a non-heir who cared for the deceased, a charitable project, or a public benefit. The law also recognizes that grief can make families vulnerable. Clear writing, witnesses, transparent debts, and fair administration reduce conflict. The best legacy is not only property; it is a family taught to fear Allah and respect one another’s rights. Writing a fair bequest before death is like labelling entrusted parcels before the courier arrives. Once departure comes, intention can no longer correct confusion, hidden resentment, or competing claims. The labels must therefore be clear, lawful, and considerate while there is time to make them so. Families today often postpone such conversations because mortality feels uncomfortable, but avoidance merely transfers the burden to grieving relatives. The verse turns preparation into moral care and makes fairness at life's edge part of mindfulness. (17:26 — **“Give to close relatives their due”**) In other words, mercy and family continuity answers wealth and the approaching end.
