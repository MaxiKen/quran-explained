**THE WILL AT THE HOUR OF DEATH**

***“It is prescribed that”*** introduces a ruling in the same voice used for the law of retaliation, and its moment is given at once: ***“when death approaches any of you”***. The condition follows in the plainest terms, ***“if they leave something of value”***, and the obligation takes shape with ***“a will should be made in favour of parents”***. The verse then widens the circle and sets the standard in one line: ***“and immediate family with fairness. ˹This is˺ an obligation on those”***, and the qualifying character is named last: ***“who are mindful ˹of Allah˺”***. Ibn ʿAbbās gives the report that the kindness commanded reaches the parents first and most. The duty is thus placed at the head of the list where the heirs will later stand.

**FAIRNESS AT THE BEDSIDE**

The content of the fairness is given with practical precision: the bequest must be just, not exceeding the allotted third and not preferring the richer relative over the poorer one. The will is the last act of a fair life, and its fairness is measured in exactly the terms the wealthy find hardest. The third that cannot be exceeded and the richer relative who cannot be preferred are the two edges that make the bequest a deed of justice rather than of affection. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the tenderness of the direction — kindness to both, with the parents the more deserving of honour in the wording of the gift. The ruling attaches to deathbed property because that is the human moment in which wealth is most tempted to stray toward favourites; the approach of death concentrates both the property and the affection. The duty is a discipline of the heart as much as of the ledger: the dying person practices justice at the hour when justice costs the most. (63:10 — **“And donate from what We have provided for you before death comes to one of you, and you cry, “My Lord!”**)

**SCOPE AND CORE**

A hearer may ask how the bequest relates to the fixed shares of inheritance that appear later in the Book. The relation is historical and orderly, and the tradition reports the abrogation of this verse's legal scope by the inheritance law. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the same note: the verse was abrogated by that on inheritance. The report of the Prophet is transmitted in al-Tirmidhī in its own words: *"Do not make testament for one already inheriting."* The reason the verse remains recited is that its moral core outlives its legal scope: the mindfulness that makes a person fair at the bedside is the same mindfulness that makes him honest in the probate court. A rule's first form is its training; its settled form is its regulation, and the scripture keeps both visible. The ruling trains the character the inheritance law will later regulate.

**THE LIVING EQUIVALENT**

The settlement of bequests before distribution is preserved inside the inheritance law itself in (4:11 — **“then your mother will receive one-sixth—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. ˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children,”**). The report of Ibn ʿAbbās holds that the duty belongs to those who ascribe no partners to Allah, which is the same character the verse names at its close. The application is to keep the bedside standard in mind long before the bedside: to hold property fairly while the hands are steady, and to leave a record of kindness in whatever form the current law provides. The verse trains the disposition the inheritance law will later measure in shares and fractions. Because the ruling is placed at the hour of death, every fair deed performed earlier is the same ruling practised in advance. The mindful are the ones who need no new command at the end, because the fairness is already their habit. The mindful are the ones who practise the fairness before it is prescribed for them at the end. The verse places its obligation on that character rather than on the dying alone, so the ruling reaches backward through the whole of a life. Because the fairness is owed to parents and kin at the last hour, it is owed to them at every hour before it. The will is a duty at the hour of death, and the living equivalent of that duty is every fair dealing performed while the hour is still distant. Those who are mindful keep both.
