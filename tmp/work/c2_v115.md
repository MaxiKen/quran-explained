**BOTH DIRECTIONS BELONG TO HIM**

The dispute about places and directions is settled here by removing the ownership of them from human hands, and the answer comes in the opening words: ***“To Allah belong the east”*** and ***“and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing”***. The two directions are named as a pair because they cover the whole compass between them, and the Book's point is that the owner of one half is the owner of the other. The previous verse had described people who tried to control where God's name is mentioned, and this one states who owns the space in question. The verse therefore answers the whole dispute about sanctuaries and orientations without mentioning either party to it. What the reader is offered is a fact about ownership rather than a ruling about a building.

**WHEREVER A PERSON TURNS**

The sentence draws a conclusion from the ownership, and the conclusion is stated without conditions: wherever the turning happens, the One being faced is Allah. The Book's phrasing is generous in its breadth, and the clause has been read by the early authorities in more than one setting: some reports tie it to a change of prayer direction, others to a prayer offered in the wrong direction by mistake. Ibn ʿAbbās is said to have connected the verse with a question the community raised about the earlier direction, and the answer given is about the One faced rather than about the wall faced. The sentence's purpose in the sūrah is to prevent a place from becoming a rival to the One who is worshipped in it, since a direction belongs to its owner as surely as the place does. A reader who has made a sacred thing out of a location will find the verse's correction in the clause about turning.

**DIRECTION LOST DURING THE JOURNEY**

The verse sits in a passage where the community's change of orientation had been raised by its critics, and the sentence supplies the ground on which such a change is no difficulty. Another sūrah records the same principle in a positive command, and its wording fits the situation this verse addresses: (2:148 — **“Everyone turns to their own direction ˹of prayer˺. So compete with one another in doing good”**). The Book's interest is in the competition of deeds rather than in the geography of prayer, and the sentence here states the reason that competition is possible: no direction is distant from its owner. The argument establishes that the dispute about orientation was never a dispute about access, since access has never depended on a wall.

**A MERCHANT WHO FINDS THE SAME OWNER EVERYWHERE**

Think of a traveller who looks for a particular merchant and discovers that every shop he enters belongs to the same man, so that no mistake about the address can keep them apart. The verse's claim about east and west works the same way, and it explains why the Book can treat a change of orientation as a small matter. The comparison also answers the resentment of the people described in the previous verse, since a person who owns the whole of a street cannot be shut out of part of it by anyone. Anyone who has feared that he was praying in the wrong place will find the sentence reassuring for the plainest reason, which is that the One addressed is not confined to the place. What the verse leaves the reader with is the freedom of the person who knows whose the ground is.

**ENCOMPASSING AND KNOWING**

The verse closes with two names that answer two different fears: the first answers the worry about being out of reach, and the second answers the worry about being unnoticed. The name translated as All-Encompassing describes a presence that no boundary can exclude, and the name that follows describes a knowledge that no hidden act escapes. The pairing is deliberate, because a person who is convinced that God is everywhere might still wonder whether his act in a strange place counts, and the second name settles that question. The Book frequently closes a statement about God's ownership with an attribute that covers the reader's practical worry, and the two words here do exactly that. What the sentence establishes is that the whole dispute about places was settled the moment its true owner was named, and the names that follow make the settlement personal. Today, travel and displacement can remove familiar surroundings without placing a worshipper beyond the ownership, knowledge or nearness of God.
