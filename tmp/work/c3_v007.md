**CLEAR AND LESS EXPLICIT VERSES**

***“He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are”*** establishes clear and less explicit verses. This verse gives one of the Qur’an’s most important principles of interpretation. The Book contains muḥkamāt, verses that are precise, clear, and decisive, and mutashābihāt, verses whose wording or subject matter requires greater care because its full reality, exact application, or intended interpretation is not immediately apparent. The muḥkamāt are the foundation of the Book. They establish tawḥīd, worship, moral commands, lawful and unlawful conduct, accountability, and the essential truths of faith. Less explicit passages must be understood in harmony with these foundations, not used to overturn them. A sound interpretation lets the clear verses explain and control the less clear ones. The word mutashābih can refer to something resembling another thing or containing multiple possible readings. It may include descriptions of the unseen, matters whose full reality belongs to Allah, concise expressions requiring contextual explanation, or passages whose meaning becomes clearer when read with the rest of revelation. Not every mutashābih verse is meaningless, and not every difficult verse is inaccessible. The warning concerns the attitude with which a person approaches it. The Qur’an itself explains that Allah has sent down the best statement, a consistent Book. Apparent difficulty is therefore not a contradiction in revelation. It may be an invitation to learn, a test of humility, or a reminder that the unseen exceeds human experience. The problem described is not merely intellectual weakness. Those with a deviation in their hearts deliberately pursue the elusive verses to create doubt and discord. (39:23 — **“˹It is˺ Allah ˹Who˺ has sent down the best message—a Book of perfect consistency and repeated lessons—which”**)

**WHAT MUTASHĀBIH MEANS**

***“the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt”*** deepens what mutashābih means. The Arabic expression points to a heart that has already tilted from truth. Such a person does not approach a difficult passage in order to submit it to careful explanation; he selects it as a weapon against the clear body of revelation. This warning applies to anyone, including Muslims. A person can misuse verses about freedom to deny responsibility, verses about mercy to deny justice, or verses about divine decree to deny human responsibility. The cure is not to ignore the difficult passage but to return it to the Qur’an’s decisive principles, authentic Sunnah, Arabic context, and responsible scholarship. The word taʾwīl can mean interpretation or the final reality and outcome of a matter. The verse is commonly recited with a pause after none knows its full meaning except Allah, followed by those firmly grounded in knowledge say, ‘We believe in it.’ This reading emphasizes that the complete reality of certain matters belongs to Allah. Another grammatical reading connects those firmly grounded in knowledge with knowledge of interpretation, while still requiring humility and consistency with revelation. Both readings agree on the central lesson: no interpreter may claim mastery over the unseen or invent meanings that contradict the clear teachings of the Qur’an. Scholars may explain what can be explained, while saying Allah knows best concerning what remains beyond them. The rāsikhūn fī al-ʿilm do not allow a difficult passage to make them reject the Book. (4:82 — **“Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah,”**)

**THE DISEASE OF DEVIANT HEARTS**

***“through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We”*** clarifies the disease of deviant hearts. They say, We believe in it; all of it is from our Lord. Their knowledge produces faith, not vanity. They distinguish between what is clear and what requires restraint, between a legitimate interpretation and speculation, and between asking a question and spreading doubt. This is not blind faith. The grounded use evidence, language, reason, and scholarly inheritance. Their humility is intellectual honesty: they do not claim to know more than the text and the evidence allow. The Prophet ﷺ recited this verse and warned that when people follow the ambiguous portions seeking discord, they should be avoided. This report is recorded in al-Bukhārī and Muslim. The warning concerns a recognizable method: isolating a disputed phrase, ignoring the clear verses, and using the result to confuse people or justify a prior agenda. A believer should begin with clear verses, read passages in context, gather all relevant Qur’anic evidence, consult authentic hadith, respect Arabic usage, and refer to qualified scholars. He should not build a creed or legal ruling on a solitary speculative reading. Nor should he turn every difference among recognized scholars into a crisis of faith. The verse ends by saying that only people of reason will take heed. Here, reason is not the rival of revelation. It is the faculty that recognizes revelation’s authority, separates evidence from desire, and accepts that wisdom includes knowing when to stop. Clear verses are like a building’s foundation, while harder passages are upper rooms whose safe use depends on the structure below. (2:269 — **“Allah grants wisdom to whoever He wills.”**)

**THE MEANING OF TAʾWĪL**

***“believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason”*** closes with the meaning of taʾwīl. Today, difficult passages should be returned to clear revelation, sound language, and qualified learning rather than recruited for outrage. Because clear and less explicit verses opens into what mutashābih means, the disease of deviant hearts joins clear and less explicit verses to what mutashābih means. Since the disease of deviant hearts develops clear and less explicit verses, what mutashābih means carries the transition. The meaning of taʾwīl matters, which means the disease of deviant hearts answers what mutashābih means. What mutashābih means gives practical form to clear and less explicit verses; the meaning of taʾwīl sets its horizon. Clear and less explicit verses stays bound to the disease of deviant hearts because what mutashābih means supplies the human setting of clear and less explicit verses. The meaning of taʾwīl carries clear and less explicit verses into conduct shaped by the disease of deviant hearts. (17:36 — **“Do not follow what you have no ˹sure˺ knowledge of. Indeed,”**)
