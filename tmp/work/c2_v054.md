Moses’ call to repentance comes as a direct address to the people he serves: ***“And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people”***. His appeal, ***“O my people!”***, retains the bond between them while refusing to conceal their offence. The charge is specific: ***“Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf”***. They had harmed themselves by giving worship to what could not be their Lord. Their act did not diminish God; it placed them under a judgement they had brought upon themselves. Al-Ḥasan relates Moses’ rebuke to their recognition that they had gone astray and their plea, (7:149 — **“If our Lord does not have mercy on us and forgive us, we will certainly be losers”**). Since they had begun to recognize the wrong, the speech directs that recognition toward a real return. It does not leave them with shame and no way to respond. At the same time, affection for his people does not make Moses rename calf worship as harmless. He identifies both the deed and the people it has harmed. Their own lives are at stake, and the one addressing them seeks their restoration to the Creator they have forsaken.

**RETURN TO THE ONE WHO GAVE THEM LIFE**

The first demand is ***“so turn in repentance to your Creator”***. Repentance is a return from disobedience to the obedience God requires. For these people, it means abandoning the calf and submitting to the true Lord. Abū al-ʿĀliyah explains the divine title here as the One who created them. Its use is pointed: they had shaped an object for worship, but neither that object nor its maker had given them life. The Creator’s care and power stand against the helplessness of their manufactured god. Elijah raises the same contrast in his rebuke, (37:125 — **“Do you call upon ˹the idol of˺ Ba’l and abandon the Best of Creators”**). The title thus supplies a reason for the required return, not only a name for its destination. Because their being comes from God, devotion belongs to Him rather than to something within His creation. Their regret must become a changed allegiance. It cannot remain an uneasy feeling while the old worship continues. Moses is not asking them to invent a rite that feels severe enough to earn relief. He conveys the form of return required of them in this particular crisis.

**THE SEVERE COMMAND BELONGS TO THIS HISTORICAL JUDGEMENT**

That form includes ***“and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves”***. The reading followed here is that members of the community carried out the penalty upon those who had worshipped the calf. The reference to themselves describes people of one community acting upon others within it; it is not an instruction that each person take his own life. The account from Ibn ʿAbbās describes the guilty being put to death by those who had not joined their worship. Other early reports describe the event more broadly as members of the people killing one another. They differ over its circumstances, but they do not reduce the event to an inward struggle with desire. The inward need to abandon sinful wishes does not cancel the historical penalty described. Its scope must also be kept clear. This was a command delivered by Moses for that community’s repentance, not permission for later readers to use suicide or private violence as penance. The command to believers is, (4:29 — **“And do not kill ˹each other or˺ yourselves”**). In the law brought by Muhammad, repentance from such worship is a return to God and abandonment of the sin, not a rite of self-destruction.

Moses gives the reason as ***“That is best for you in the sight of your Creator”***. The judgement is measured by the Creator and the final outcome, not by the ease of the act at that moment. The second mention of the Creator directs them back to the authority under which the command was given. It does not make suffering desirable in itself or mean that any harsh demand spoken by a human being must be obeyed. The history concerns a known prophetic command. Ibn ʿAbbās’s account in al-Nasāʾī’s narration of Moses’ trials joins their admission of guilt and response to the command with forgiveness for those involved. Al-Zuhrī’s account also records Moses’ grief over the deaths and the assurance of mercy for the dead and pardon for the survivors. The grief is important: the cost is not treated as slight. The severity shows the gravity of the breach, while the ending shows that the purpose was not to leave the people beyond hope. Their return was accepted, and the killing was brought to an end.

**ACCEPTANCE FOLLOWS THE RETURN TO GOD**

The narrative then states, ***“Then He accepted your repentance”***. It moves from the command conveyed by Moses to the outcome granted by God. The actions between the command and acceptance are not all repeated in the short verse, but the accepted repentance presupposes their response. They did not merely hear the demand and continue to defend the calf. This matters because the mercy recalled earlier was not approval of their false worship. It was God’s acceptance of a return from it. The giver of life was also the giver of pardon. The people could acknowledge their guilt and obey, but they could not declare that God now owed them acquittal. Nor could Moses create forgiveness by his own authority. He conveyed the command and pleaded for his people; the verse attributes acceptance to God. Since that acceptance is plainly affirmed, the account is not an invitation to imagine a punishment continuing without end. It explains the great cost of their return and the mercy by which their relation to God was restored.

The conclusion identifies the Lord as ***“Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”***. The first name expresses His abundant acceptance of repentance, from many servants and through repeated returns. It does not describe God as repenting of an offence. The servant turns away from sin; God turns toward the servant with pardon and favour. His mercy also precedes and enables the return. Of another group the Quran states, (9:118 — **“Then He turned to them in mercy so that they might repent”**). The opportunity to repent is therefore not a resource a sinner possesses independently of God. His help opens the way, and His acceptance completes it. Joined to the name of the Most Merciful, the title keeps the severe episode within its stated outcome: the people were not denied every path back. The same Lord who judged their wrong accepted their repentance. This offers no excuse to make light of calf worship or of the deaths in its aftermath. It locates the lasting hope in God’s mercy rather than in the people’s ability to erase their own guilt. Repentance is a real change of course, and acceptance remains His gift.
