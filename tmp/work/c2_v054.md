**THE REMEDY MOSES PRESCRIBED**

The command that follows is one of the hardest sentences in the chapter, and the Book reports it without softening: ***“And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people!”***, ***“Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf”***, then ***“so turn in repentance to your Creator”***, ***“and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves.”***, ***“That is best for you in the sight of your Creator.”***, and then ***“Then He accepted your repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”***. The remedy is named as severe and then described as best, which is a combination a reader should sit with rather than skip. The Book records what the same offence brought elsewhere, where the sentence is a warning rather than a prescription: (7:152 — **“Those who worshipped the calf will certainly be afflicted with Allah's wrath”**). Nothing here pretends that certain repairs can be made without what looks like a loss. The sequence is worth following because it is the Book's standard account of a certain kind of wrong. First the wrong is named to the people who did it, and named as a wrong against themselves before it is called a wrong against God. Then a specific remedy is demanded, not a general sorrow. Then, once the remedy is described, the verse reports the acceptance of the repentance and names the mercy behind it. The Book does not leave a person to guess whether the remedy worked; it tells him, so that the passage about the calf ends where this verse ends. Ibn ʿAbbās transmitted that those who had not worshipped the calf were set against those who had, so the public rupture made by the idol was answered within the same community.

**A WRONG NAMED AS AN INJURY TO THE SELF**

The first clause in the speech of Moses identifies the injury in a way that a modern reader may not expect. The people are told that they have wronged themselves, which places the damage inside the community rather than in a distant ledger. Idolatry in the Book is never only a mistake about metaphysics; it is the handing over of a person's own dignity to something that cannot carry it, and the consequences fall on the worshipper first. The language is the same the sūrah used when it described the man who traded guidance for error and lost his capital in the transaction. Moses also calls the people his people in the middle of the accusation, and the Book keeps the possessive as a small but deliberate comfort. The rebuke comes from someone who is still standing with them, and the remedy he prescribes is presented as being for their benefit rather than as a punishment owed to an offended authority. The verse's final clause confirms the tone, since the one who accepted the repentance is named as Most Merciful. The severity of the route and the mercy at the end of it are reported by the same sentence.

**ACCEPTING THE REMEDY THAT FITS**

The practical lesson for a reader whose failures are of a different kind is about accepting the remedy that fits his case rather than a remedy that preserves his comfort. The children of Israel were told to do something that cost them their attachment to the thing they had made, and the Book calls that cost the best option available. A person today is usually asked for something smaller: the thing returned, the relationship repaired, the practice resumed, the admission made out loud. The Book's account of the calf establishes a principle that the rest of the sūrah keeps applying. The repair is judged by whether it removes the wrong rather than by whether it is agreeable, which is why the verse can call a hard measure the best option available. One thing a reader can do in response is to take an old mistake and look at the remedy he has been avoiding rather than the one he has already performed. The comfortable half of a repair is usually the visible half, and the missing piece is what the story of the calf would call the calf itself. The Book is not asking for self-punishment, and it does not want a person to invent a severity for himself. It is asking for the specific removal of the specific thing, which is what the verse records and what the mercy at its end responds to. The reason acceptance is announced after the severe remedy is to prevent severity from becoming the final name of God in the account.
