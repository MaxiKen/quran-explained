**SPENDING AS SURVIVAL**

The verse's three phrases move from the spending to the ruin to the love: ***“Spend in the cause of Allah”***, ***“and do not let your own hands throw you into destruction ˹by withholding˺.”***, ***“And do good, for Allah certainly loves the good-doers”***. The walk is a complete economy in three moves: the command to release, the warning against the hold, and the valuation of the character the release builds. Any household budget can be checked against these three moves in a single afternoon. The warning Ibn ʿAbbās unpacks is the sequence read aloud. The three phrases are worth reading before every pledge of money, because the pledge sits between the ruin and the love whichever way the hand decides. The hand decides in the pledge's very hour.

Spend in the cause of Allah is written in the imperative of survival rather than of charity. The clause that answers it — and do not let your own hands throw you into destruction ˹by withholding˺. — identifies the withholding as the danger, so the person who keeps everything is the person at risk. The destruction named is the ruin that arrives when the community's defense, its poor, and its worship have no funding. The report transmitted through Ibn ʿAbbās unpacks the warning in the setting of the mission's expenses: the one who withholds from the cause risks the ruin the verse names. Because the hands are named as the instruments of the destruction, the withholding is an act and not a state. The verse's accountancy is long-term: the treasure kept is the person endangered, and the treasure spent is the person saved.

**YOUR OWN HANDS**

The verse blames the hands the money sits in, which is the chapter's most economical description of how wealth destroys its owner. The hand that refuses to release the dinar is the hand that throws the person into the ruin, and the causation is stated as a single motion. The surrounding legislation of spending returns to the rhythm of the donation — day and night, secretly and openly — and the rhythm is the opposite of the hoarder's freeze (2:274 — **“Those who spend their wealth in charity day and night, secretly and openly—their reward is with their Lord, and”**). Because the danger is self-inflicted, the verse's warning can be heard before the crisis arrives. The application is to practice the release while the hand is steady, so the hand learns the motion before it is needed. The one who waits for the emergency to be generous has practiced his whole life for the wrong act.

**WITHHOLDING AS DESTRUCTION**

The definition of destruction here is social as well as personal: the community that cannot fund its obligations begins to lose them one by one, and the loss is the destruction moving through the body. The verse's order — spend first, warn second — puts the command at the head of the sentence so the warning is heard as its explanation. The close of the passage converts the warning into character: And do good, for Allah certainly loves the good-doers. The good-doer is the person whose hands have learned the motion of release, and the love attached to the character is the verse's highest valuation. Because the doing of good is placed after the spending, the spending is the first exercise of the character. The destruction and the love are separated by one habit.

**LOVE FOR THE GOOD-DOERS**

The closing word of the verse is a love, and its placement is the argument's last move. The one who spends in the cause is described as a good-doer and loved, and the one who withholds is described as standing near destruction. The pairing refuses the accountancy of the miser, where the money kept is the self kept; the revelation counts the kept money as the thrown self. The cooperation the wider legislation commands — in goodness and righteousness rather than in sin and aggression — makes the same judgment in social terms (5:2 — **“who once barred you from the Sacred Mosque provoke you to transgress. Cooperate with one another in goodness and righteousness,”**). Because the love is named at the end, the motive of the spender is allowed to include it: the servant may spend to be among the loved. The application is to set the budget of the cause before the budget of the appetite, and to let the hands practice the verse's verb every month. The destruction is avoided by the same hands that could have caused it. The symmetry of the verse is complete: the hand that threw the person toward the ruin is the hand that pulls him back. The verse's budget of the person is worth reciting when the accounts are drawn at the year's end: what was spent on the cause is listed beside what was spent on the appetite, and the two columns tell the truth. Because the hands are named, the budget is a moral document as well as a financial one. The verse's warning is addressed to the community's accounts as well as its souls, since a cause with empty hands is a cause at the edge of the ruin the verse names. The application is therefore organizational as well as personal.
