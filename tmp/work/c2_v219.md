**ACKNOWLEDGING BENEFIT WITHOUT APPROVING HARM**

***“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about intoxicants and gambling. Say, “There is great evil in”*** establishes acknowledging benefit without approving harm. The verse addresses khamr, intoxicants that cloud the mind, and maysir, gambling and games of chance in which wealth is gained through speculation and another person’s loss. It acknowledges that people may perceive benefits in them: pleasure, temporary escape, social excitement, commercial income, or a chance of profit. Acknowledging an apparent benefit does not make the practice lawful. Allah states that the sin and harm are greater. This was part of the Qur’an’s gradual education concerning intoxicants. The final prohibition in 5:90–91 makes the ruling explicit and commands believers to avoid them. The progression taught the community to understand the moral and social consequences before the final boundary was established. Intoxication clouds ʿaql, the faculty by which a person understands, restrains desire, and fulfils responsibility. It can lead to violence, neglect of prayer, family breakdown, accidents, health damage, debt, exploitation, and addiction. The temporary pleasure does not cancel these harms. A person who says that he can control an intoxicant should consider the wider effect on people around him and on those who become dependent. The prohibition extends to producing, selling, transporting, serving, and promoting intoxicants when these activities support their consumption. The Prophet ﷺ cursed multiple parties connected to wine in the hadith recorded by Sunan Abī Dāwūd and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī. The principle is to close the commercial path to a known harm. (5:90 — **“O believers! Intoxicants, gambling, idols, and drawing lots for decisions are all evil of Satan’s handiwork.”**) Because acknowledging benefit without approving harm frames the harm of intoxicants, the harm of intoxicants returns attention to acknowledging benefit without approving harm.

**THE HARM OF INTOXICANTS**

***“both, as well as some benefit for people—but the evil outweighs the benefit.” They ˹also˺”*** deepens the harm of intoxicants. Maysir transfers wealth through chance, rivalry, and the hope of gaining without productive work. It can include betting, lotteries, casinos, wagering on games, and modern forms of speculative play that exploit addiction. Even when a winner receives money, the system depends upon others losing and encourages a cycle of hope, debt, and resentment. The Qur’an later says that gambling creates enmity, hatred, and distraction from remembrance and prayer. Games can be harmless when no unlawful stake or deception is involved. The problem is making chance a mechanism for consuming wealth and cultivating greed. People often reason that if a forbidden thing has any benefit, it must be permissible. The verse teaches the opposite method: moral judgement considers the whole balance of benefit and harm, not the existence of a single advantage. Medicine may contain a substance with a harmful side effect and be governed by necessity and expert judgement, but recreational intoxication is not justified by the claim that it helps a person relax. The final ruling on khamr and maysir protects individuals and society from industries that profit from weakness. It also prevents the wealthy from shifting the harm onto families, workers, and communities while keeping the visible profit. The second question concerns charitable spending: What should we spend. The answer, al-ʿafw, has been explained as what is beyond one’s needs, what can be given with ease, or what remains after fulfilling legitimate obligations. (5:91 — **“Satan’s plan is to stir up hostility and hatred between you with intoxicants and gambling and to”**) Since the harm of gambling follows acknowledging benefit without approving harm, the harm of intoxicants links purpose to the harm of gambling.

**THE HARM OF GAMBLING**

***“ask you ˹O Prophet˺ what they should donate. Say, “Whatever you can spare.” This”*** clarifies the harm of gambling. This does not command a person to neglect dependants, debts, health, or necessary provision. It teaches generosity without irresponsibility. A person begins with obligatory support and rights, then gives from what Allah has enabled. The best charity may be regular and modest rather than dramatic and financially destructive. Regarding charity, the Prophet ﷺ taught that giving begins with those for whom a person is responsible and remains best when made despite felt need. The two questions are placed together because both concern how people treat resources and judgement. Intoxicants and gambling consume wealth without producing wholesome benefit, while charity directs wealth toward people and purposes that preserve life. One feeds addiction and rivalry; the other cultivates mercy and social stability. A believer examines not only what he spends but what his spending strengthens. Supporting a harmful industry, paying a bribe, or wasting food can contradict gratitude even when the amount is small. Allah asks for responsible stewardship. Allah makes the revelations clear so that people may reflect—yatafakkarūn. The Qur’an educates the heart through explanation, gradual legislation, consequences, and reminders. Reflection should lead to action: abandoning intoxicants, refusing gambling, seeking treatment for addiction, supporting affected families, and giving wealth with wisdom. A person who is already dependent should seek medical, psychological, spiritual, and community support. Repentance includes stopping the harm, repairing rights, and removing access where possible. (4:43 — **“O believers! Do not approach prayer while intoxicated until you are aware of what you say,”**) The benefit is not a licence matters, which means the harm of gambling returns the benefit is not a licence to acknowledging benefit without approving harm.

**THE BENEFIT IS NOT A LICENCE**

***“is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you ˹believers˺, so perhaps you may reflect”*** closes with the benefit is not a licence. Shaming an addict can deepen the problem; mercy includes firm help toward recovery. Think of acknowledging benefit without approving harm through a fisherman who counts the fish he catches with a net that also brings up the young of the year. He knows the price of the catch and the cost of the empty season to come, and the choice he makes in the boat decides whether there is a catch next year. Acknowledging benefit without approving harm reaches a reader today who can name the harm of a habit and keeps it because he can also name its pleasure. The verse gives him a scale with the harm written on the heavier side and leaves the weighing to him. Because the sentence closes on reflection, acknowledging benefit without approving harm teaches that a scale in the hand is worth more than a ruling he has not yet applied. Because the closing word is reflection rather than obedience, the reader is shown what kind of community the passage is building. The answer about wine had left a scale in their hands, and the Book expected them to weigh with it before the next ruling came. (7:31 — **“O Children of Adam! Dress properly whenever you are at worship. Eat and drink,”**) The harm of intoxicants makes acknowledging benefit without approving harm practical; the harm of gambling therefore matters.
