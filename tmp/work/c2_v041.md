**HONOURING EARLIER REVELATION BY RECEIVING THE NEW**

The covenant now makes a direct claim on its hearers: ***“Believe in My revelations”*** ***“which confirm your Scriptures.”***. The revelation being received is the Quran. It does not ask them to deny that God sent earlier Books. Rather, its message agrees with the truth He had already taught. Another passage states the relation plainly: (3:3 — **“the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel”**). Abū al-ʿĀliyah also connects the confirmation with the description of Muhammad known from the earlier scriptures. The Quran speaks of the Messenger (7:157 — **“whose description they find in their Torah and the Gospel.”**). Confirmation therefore works in two related ways: the same basic faith is upheld, and the promised messenger has come. A change in a particular law need not make the two messages false to one another. God may prescribe a rule for one time and replace it when He sends a later law. The unity lies in their divine source and truth, not in making every ruling timeless. Nor does this confirmation approve whatever people may add to a Book. Since the command concerns what God revealed, loyalty to that revelation should lead toward the Quran, not become a reason to refuse it.

**A FIRST REFUSAL CAN LEAD OTHERS**

The warning ***“Do not be the first to deny them”*** addresses people whose knowledge should have prepared them to believe. Ibn ʿAbbās stresses that they possessed knowledge others did not have. Their learning thus increased the duty to recognize the message. They were not the first people anywhere to reject Muhammad; opposition in Makkah had already preceded this address. The force is that they should not lead their own community into denial. Others looked to them as people trained in scripture, so their refusal could become an excuse for many further refusals. This is why being first makes the warning sharper. It does not make later denial lawful. The Quran describes the added burden of those who mislead: (16:25 — **“as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge.”**). That burden comes from their own act of leading others astray, not from cancelling the responsibility of the followers. Knowledge is therefore a trust with a public effect. It can open the way to faith or place an obstacle before people who assume that their teachers have examined the evidence honestly.

**PAYMENT FOR SERVICE IS NOT A PRICE FOR SILENCE**

The next prohibition is not to ***“or trade them for a fleeting gain.”***. Gain may take the form of money, office, influence, or the approval that keeps a leader in place. The wrong occurs when the truth is withheld or changed to protect that gain. An important teaching then becomes a tool for preserving the teacher’s position. Because worldly benefit ends, even a large return is small beside the good being surrendered. The Quran gives the right comparison: (16:95 — **“What is with Allah is certainly far better for you, if only you knew”**). The warning is not against a poor bargain that could be repaired by asking a higher price. No payment can make false witness about God lawful. It is the exchange itself that is corrupt. In this setting, concealing the Messenger’s description or refusing the Quran for the sake of influence places a passing interest above a known duty. The same wrong remains possible for a Muslim who bends a religious answer to please a patron. The verse’s first audience does not confine its rule to one people.

A teacher’s pay for the time spent teaching the Quran correctly is a separate question. Abū Ḥanīfah’s earlier ruling disallowed such wages because teaching was treated as a duty of worship. Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, and Aḥmad allowed teaching wages, since pay for sound teaching is not a payment to sell or hide the truth. Its evidence includes the report from Ibn ʿAbbās in al-Bukhārī, in which the Prophet approves payment for Quranic recitation used in healing. The report is about healing prayer, not a class fee. Yet it shows that payment for work with the Quran is not always unlawful. The ruling that allows pay is adopted here. A teacher paid to teach faithfully is not being paid to make revelation say something else. Later Hanafi jurists also allowed teaching wages when public support had failed and teachers needed a livelihood. This did not permit taking bribes or withholding an urgently owed explanation until payment arrived. The distinction protects both sides of the trust: the message must remain true, and those who devote their working time to transmitting it need not be deprived of lawful support.

Al-Dārimī records Abū Ḥāzim telling the ruler Sulaymān that just speech is truth spoken before someone whose power is feared or whose favour is sought. In that same encounter, he refuses to let offered wealth become a price for his counsel. The point is not that every gift to a teacher is forbidden; the preceding distinction would rule out that reading. It is that fear and hope must not decide whether the truth is spoken. A gift tied to a desired answer is no longer merely help for the work. It reaches into the judgement the hearer is entitled to receive. The covenant concerning scripture requires its holders (3:187 — **“to make it known to people and not hide it,”**). An audience may reward concealment more readily than candour. Since the knowledge was entrusted by God, the audience’s preference cannot transfer ownership of it to a ruler, donor, or teacher. Its duty remains disclosure in truth.

**MINDFULNESS THAT GOVERNS THE CHOICE**

The verse closes with ***“And be mindful of Me”***. Being mindful means guarding against God’s punishment by obeying His commands and shunning what He forbids. For Ṭalq ibn Ḥabīb, such obedience rests on knowledge from God and hope for His mercy. Avoiding sin is joined to that knowledge and fear of punishment. Knowledge, hope, and fear belong together in that account. A teacher does not honour God by replacing sound knowledge with alarm, or by invoking mercy to conceal a known command. The warning therefore asks for an inward loyalty that can withstand pressure on the outward answer. The previous verse called for awe; here that awe must govern the choices just named. It requires belief in the message, refusal to lead others into denial, and loyalty that does not trade truth for gain. These are connected duties because each can be defeated by the same desire to preserve one’s place. God’s judgement lasts after a salary, title, or following has passed away. Mindfulness weighs the choice by that lasting account rather than by the size of the immediate reward.
