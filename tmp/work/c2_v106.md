**WHAT ABROGATION MEANS IN THIS BOOK**

The verse takes up a question that had been raised against the revelation, and it answers it in the form of a law rather than a defence: ***“If We ever abrogate a verse”***. The Arabic term behind the translation carries the sense of lifting or transferring, and the Book uses it here for the withdrawal of a ruling by the One who gave it. The sentence is stated in the first person plural, which is the Book's usual manner when it describes acts it attributes to God, and the phrasing makes the whole matter an exercise of authority rather than an accident in the text. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās connects the verse to a specific objection from the People of the Book, who had asked why a ruling would be replaced if the earlier one had come from God. What the reader is offered is not an argument about the mechanics of revelation but a statement about who its author is.

**THE VERSE THAT IS REMEMBERED AND THE VERSE THAT IS REPLACED**

The sentence adds a second case to the first, and the second is the more striking of the two: ***“or cause it to be forgotten, We replace it with a better”***. The forgetting here is attributed to the same authority that sends, which places the removal of a revelation within the same will as its delivery. The Book treats both cases identically in the sentence, and the equality is part of the answer, since the objection had been to replacement as such. The two cases are placed side by side because the will behind the sending is the same will behind the removal, which is the point the objectors had missed. The objection is not that something was lost but that something was changed, and the verse meets it by naming the one who does both. A reader who has wondered why the Book would speak of a verse passing out of memory has the sentence's own account of it here, and the account is short.

**BETTER AND SIMILAR, NOT LESSER**

The replacement is described in two words that between them close off every way of complaining about the change: ***“or similar one. Do you not know”***. The substitute is either better than what it replaces or equal to it, and the Book's choice of the two words rules out the complaint that something valuable had been taken away. What turns on the pairing is that no replacement can be described as a loss, which is why the sentence moves straight from the two words to the question that follows. Another sūrah records the same exchange from the side of those who refused it, and it supplies the accusation that was made against the one who brought the change: (16:101 — **“When We replace a verse with another”**). The sentence's own words then address the reader directly with a question, which moves the matter from a dispute about texts to the plainest available fact. The one who has followed the argument this far is asked to test the objection against what he already knows about the one who gives and withdraws.

**THE JEWISH OBJECTION THE SENTENCE ANSWERS**

The objection behind the verse was not a request for explanation; it was a use of the change as evidence against the mission. The Book meets it by refusing the premise, and the refusal is made in a sentence that never mentions the objectors, because the argument rests on a fact about God rather than on anything the objectors accept. What the verse assumes is that the right to revise a ruling belongs to the one who issued it, and that assumption is what makes the question that follows so pointed. The same line of reasoning appears elsewhere in the sūrah where the community's objections are answered by an appeal to divine prerogative rather than to the objector's satisfaction. Ibn ʿAbbās connects the verse with a challenge about the direction of prayer and similar changes, which puts it squarely among the objections the section has been addressing. A reader sees here how the Book handles a dispute it does not consider to be about evidence.

**CAPABILITY AS THE GROUND OF THE ANSWER**

The verse ends with a question about knowledge that is also a statement about power: ***“that Allah is Most Capable of everything”***. The clause gives the ground on which the whole answer rests, since a change of ruling is only a difficulty for someone who doubts the competence of the one who makes it. Capability is named here because the entire objection presupposes that a ruling was settled beyond revision, and the question leaves that presupposition with nowhere to stand. The Book frequently closes an argument in this way, and the closing here is deliberately placed after the objector has been asked twice whether he knows. Another passage states the same authority in terms of what is written and what is erased, which places the verse's own claim in the plainest company: (13:39 — **“Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills”**). What the reader carries away is a question addressed to himself, since the verse asks whether he has understood the position of the one whose words he is reading.
