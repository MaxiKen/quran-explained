**A RELIGION LIKE A DYE**

The religion the community has been discussing is named here with an image from the dyeing trade: ***“This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah.”***. The translation offers two renderings of the term, and both are worth holding together, since one describes the religion as a way laid down and the other describes it as a colour that has soaked into the material. The term carries the sense of a mark that cannot be washed out because it has entered the cloth, and the claim here is that the religion in question is that kind of mark. The previous verses had described the creed of the believers and the guidance that follows from it, and this sentence names the whole matter in a single word. The religion, then, is not an ornament worn over the surface but a change in the fabric.

**THE DYE THAT TAKES AND THE DYE THAT SITS ON TOP**

The verse's second clause presses the image with a question: ***“And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way?”***. The question compares the colouring God provides with every other colouring a person might acquire, and it leaves the answer to the reader because there is no doubt about it anywhere. The comparison also rules out the notion that a person may keep several colourings at once, which is the assumption behind so much of the inherited religion the passage has been examining. The earlier communities in this sūrah have been a study in the difference between the two, since a people can carry the mark of a tradition while the tradition itself has not entered them. The word used here describes the standing colour of a thing rather than a coat applied for an occasion, which explains the severity of the Book's criticism of inherited religion. Anyone who has watched cloth dyed and then left in the rain can see what the sentence guards against.

**THE NATURAL WAY AND WHERE IT IS TRACED**

Another passage names the same reality and traces it to the constitution God gave human beings at their making, which is the sense the term's translation as natural Way is reaching for: (30:30 — **“the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in ˹all˺ people”**). This verse says the same thing by calling the religion a colouring that belongs to its owner rather than to its wearer's choice. The two together explain why the guidance of the earlier prophets is treated in the Book as one religion rather than as a series of separate dispensations, since a constitution given at a people's making is not replaced by a later fashion. The phrase is read by Ibn ʿAbbās as the religion of God without any addition, and that reading suits a sentence that closes an argument about inherited claims of every kind. The reader should see that the image is a correction of the assumption that religion is a garment a community may tailor.

**A DYER WHO OWNS THE VAT**

Think of a workshop where the colour in the vat is mixed by the owner and where the customer is invited to bring his cloth rather than his own dye. The verse's question works the same way, and it excludes every attempt to add a private colouring to the one provided. The comparison explains why the sentence is phrased as a comparison rather than a command, since the Book is asking the reader to look at the alternatives and find none. Anyone who has blended a private practice into a religion and then wondered why it feels provisional will recognise the situation, since a colour mixed by the wearer never holds as well as the one the dyer put there. The religion has one owner, and his colouring is the standard by which every other is measured.

**WORSHIP AS THE WHOLE RESPONSE**

The verse closes with the response the community makes to the colouring it has received, and that response is given as a statement about their practice: ***“And we worship ˹none but˺ Him”***. The Book's habit of ending a statement about God with a statement about the worshippers places the two halves of religion in one sentence, and it does so here to show what a dyed life looks like from the outside. The declaration is in the first person plural, which makes it the community's answer to the question embedded in the sentence, and the answer is a claim about conduct rather than about ancestry. Another passage makes the same claim in fuller form, where the speaker devotes his whole life and death to the Lord of all worlds: (6:162 — **“my prayer, my worship, my life, and my death are all for Allah—Lord of all worlds”**). What the reader carries from the verse is an image of a community marked by a colouring it did not choose and did not dilute. Present-day identity is often coloured by nation, market and fashion; worship asks which influence has penetrated deepest.
