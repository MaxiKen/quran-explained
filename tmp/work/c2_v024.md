**THE DOOR CLOSES ON THE CHALLENGE**

The next verse answers the challenge before it is taken up, and it does so with a certainty that leaves no room for ceremony: ***“But if you are unable to do”***, ***“so and you will never be able to do”***, ***“so then fear the Fire fuelled with people”***, ***“and stones, which is prepared”***, ***“for the disbelievers”***. The first clause allows for the attempt, and the second states the outcome in advance. The Book is not waiting to see whether anyone manages it; the inability is announced as a fact that will hold for every generation that tries. Then the reader is given something to do with the certainty, and it is not to keep arguing. It is to take the warning seriously.

The image of a fire that burns people and stones is a deliberately material picture, and the Book repeats it elsewhere when it warns a family to protect itself: (66:6 — **“O believers! Protect yourselves and your families from a Fire whose fuel is people and stones, overseen by formidable”**). Stones are what a person expects to survive a fire, so the fuel list is there to remove a false comfort. Nothing in the description is arranged to console the reader about his chances, and that plainness is the reason the verse is found in the opening pages of the Book rather than at the end. The Prophet ﷺ said, *"This fire of yours, which the son of Adam kindles, is one part of seventy parts of the Fire of Hell"* as al-Bukhārī carries it, which is why the verse treats the picture as a fact rather than as a figure of speech. The sūrah has already described three kinds of listener, and now it tells the honest one what is at stake if he joins the wrong group.

**PROOF FIRST, WARNING SECOND**

The order matters: the challenge is issued first, and the warning follows. A person is not asked to fear a punishment before he has been given a reason to take the message seriously, and the reasoning available to him is substantial. He has heard the claim, he has been offered the test, and he has been given every ally he could call for. Only then is the consequence named. This is how the sūrah has proceeded from its first line: guidance for the mindful, blindness for the closed, and now a plain statement of what the refusal costs, which means the reader has been given the reason before he has been given the warning. A reader who has followed the sequence can see that nothing is being imposed on him by surprise. The phrase prepared for the disbelievers also carries the sense of something kept ready rather than improvised. The Book uses the same word for the gifts promised to the believers, and the symmetry is not accidental: the two destinations are both prepared, and a person's life is a choosing between them whether or not he names it that way. The verse does not pretend that the choice is neutral, and it does not pretend that it is negotiable at the end. What it offers instead is the honest use of the present, which is the only part of the matter anyone actually controls.

**A WARNING THAT HAS ALREADY BEEN SETTLED**

A reader may ask what he is supposed to do with a passage that has already declared his position before he arrived. The answer lies in what the verse is directed at. The inability being described is the inability to produce a sūrah like the Book, not the inability to believe; the two are quite different, and the Book keeps them apart. A person can be entirely unable to meet the literary challenge and entirely able to turn toward his Lord in the next five minutes, and the verse is written so that the second possibility stays open even while the first is settled. The proof settles the question of the Book's origin; the warning addresses what a person does once that question is settled. Today the practical response is the one the sūrah has been building toward all along. A reader who is convinced by the challenge and frightened by the fire can act on both in the same hour, and the act is not complicated: turn to the one who sent the Book, ask for forgiveness for the years spent managing the question, and then begin the habits named earlier in the chapter. The door the verse opens is not closed by anything except delay, and the delay is the one thing the sūrah refuses to recommend.
