**A VERDICT PRONOUNCED BEFORE THE ATTEMPT**

The sūrah completes the test with a statement that turns it into a verdict: ***“But if you are unable to do so”***, ***“and you will never be able to do so”***. The construction announces the outcome before the challengers have tried, and the scholars explain the shape of it in the same way they explained the earlier verse of purpose: a condition spoken by God is not an expression of uncertainty but a statement of what will happen. The challenge was put to the pagans, the Jews and the Christians together, which means the test was offered to every community in the region that had a claim to a revelation and a reason to doubt this one. The wording is a prophecy before it is anything else, and the history of the text since that day has kept it standing. That is why the sūrah moves straight from the test to the consequence, because a challenge that cannot be met has finished its work. A doubter may still hold his doubt, and the sentence has told him in advance that the doubt is not something the text will lose sleep over.

**THE FIRE THAT WAS MADE READY**

The consequence is named without decoration: ***“then fear the Fire fuelled with people”***, ***“and stones, which is prepared”***, ***“for the disbelievers”***. The verse describes the punishment as something standing ready rather than as something to be arranged later, and the wording about its fuel is exact. The early teachers read the people as the disbelievers themselves, and the stones either as the idols that were worshipped or as a material that burns far hotter than wood, which is how they explained the intensity of the fire. The reading from Ibn ʿAbbās identifies the stones as the idols themselves, and the Book makes that reading explicit in another sūrah where the objects of worship are named as fuel beside the people who served them: (21:98 — **“you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell”**). The Book repeats the same phrase in a command to the believers a few sūrahs later, where they are told to shield their families from it: (66:6 — **“a Fire whose fuel is people and stones”**).

**CERTAINTY AFTER THE FAILED CHALLENGE**

The order of the two verses is part of the argument, and the reason is worth stating plainly. The sūrah first offers the doubters a way of testing the message with their own abilities, then tells them that the test will not be passed, and then sends them to the consequence with their eyes open. A person who has been shown that the text is beyond him has no ground left for treating the matter as an opinion he may sample at leisure, because the claim he is dismissing has proved itself in the only arena he asked for. The Book uses the same sequence elsewhere when it challenges the doubters to bring ten sūrahs and then states the failure they will meet: (11:13 — **“Produce ten fabricated sûrahs like it and seek help from whoever you can—other than Allah—if what you say is true!”**). What turns on the sequence is that the warning is aimed at a person who has already been given his chance rather than at a stranger who never heard.

**WRITTEN NOTICE BEFORE THE BLAZE**

Think of a traveller who reaches a bridge with a warning board signed by the engineer who built it, and who decides that the board is the opinion of an interested party. The bridge does not fail because he doubted it, and it does not wait for his book of reasons either. The verse treats the message in the same way, and it gives the reader the test first and the consequence afterwards, which is the fairest order the matter can have. These days a person may live inside a culture where every claim is treated as one option among many, and this verse asks him to notice that some claims have been tested in the way he would have chosen to test them. The next verse turns from the warning to the good news, and the sūrah shows that the same Book that leaves the doubter without an excuse gives the believer more than he asked for.
