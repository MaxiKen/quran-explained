**FORGIVENESS FOLLOWS A BREACH THAT STILL MATTERS**

The opening ***“Even then”*** holds the new gift beside the offence just described. The people had worshipped the calf after being delivered from Pharaoh and given clear signs. Abū al-ʿĀliyah relates the pardon to that very breach. God did not forgive because there had been no wrong, nor because the wrong was too slight to matter. Its gravity makes the mercy more striking. The same history is recalled in another passage: (4:153 — **“Still We forgave them for that ˹after their repentance˺”**). Since the crime and the pardon are both named, neither can be used to erase the meaning of the other. The calf did not become worthy of worship when its worshippers were forgiven. Rather, people who had placed themselves in danger were given a way back. This is why pardon belongs among the favours the Israelites are told to remember. Rescue from an enemy had preserved their lives; forgiveness now preserved them from the final ruin toward which their own act had led. Mercy did not leave the history at the point of their betrayal.

The words ***“We ˹still˺ forgave you”*** place the act of pardon with God. The sense includes wiping away an offence and passing over the punishment it has earned. It is not merely the fading of human memory about an event. Ibn ʿAbbās connects the favour with God’s not destroying the people altogether. Their continued life was not proof that the calf had been accepted. Nor is pardon here a promise that repentance can be left out. The account of their return says, (2:54 — **“Then He accepted your repentance”**). That link matters because a gift of forgiveness cannot be turned into permission to persist in the same rejection. The people were brought back under the authority they had defied. Pardon need not mean that nothing painful had occurred during that return; it means that God accepted it and did not leave them to the full outcome of their sin. The next description of their repentance gives its own details. Here the stress is on the mercy that followed a real offence, not on a denial that the offence ever deserved judgement.

**WHY MERCY IS FOLLOWED BY AN EXPECTATION**

The purpose is expressed in ***“so perhaps you would be grateful”***. Gratitude is the fitting response to mercy received, rather than a price already paid to secure it. The wording places a duty before those who have been spared. Its note of expectation does not mean that God is unsure what they will do. The Lord is described as having (2:29 — **“˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”**). Here the expression directs the people toward the response that pardon should produce. A person looking at such a gift would have reason to expect thanks from its recipient. Yet being given a reason to obey does not remove the person’s capacity to turn away again. This is why the last clause is an appeal, not a claim that every forgiven person has already become grateful. The relation between the clauses is plain: God has dealt with them in mercy so that their conduct may answer that mercy. Gratitude recognizes both the giver and the value of what was given. Forgetting the need for pardon would make the person less able to give that answer.

**THANKS TAKES THE FORM OF OBEDIENCE**

Gratitude reaches the heart, speech, and deeds. It acknowledges the gift, speaks rightly of its giver, and uses what has been received in a way pleasing to Him. Muslim preserves the Prophet’s teaching that the command given to believers agrees with that given to the messengers. The messengers are told, (23:51 — **“Eat from what is good and lawful, and act righteously”**), while believers are told, (2:172 — **“And give thanks to Allah if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺”**). The parallel explains why gratitude cannot be reduced to a polite phrase. Good action gives the acknowledgment a form in life. The family of David receives the direct instruction, (34:13 — **“Work gratefully, O family of David!”**). For the people forgiven after worshipping the calf, the first fitting act was to leave that false worship and remain faithful to God. Because the pardon restored a relation they had violated, thanks must include care for that relation. Praise offered while defending the old offence would contradict its own purpose. The gift is recognized by a changed response, not by claiming that mercy has made obedience unnecessary.

Al-Fuḍayl describes gratitude for a favour as not disobeying God after receiving it. That account does not require pretending to be beyond every future fault. It refuses the use of mercy as an excuse to stop resisting the wrong. Gratitude also makes a person fair toward the human means through which help has reached him. Abū Hurayrah reports in Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī that the Prophet said, *"He who does not thank people does not thank God."* Thanks to people does not make them rivals to God; it acknowledges the help He allowed them to give. In this history, the people owed attention to the prophetic guidance that called them back, not renewed attachment to the object that misled them. Even the ability to repent and give thanks is a gift, so gratitude is not a claim to have repaid God in full. It is a willing use of what He has given. The continued opportunity to obey becomes part of the mercy remembered, and the answer to that mercy is faithful service.
