**THE MOCKERY THAT COMES BACK**

The verse answers the man who called his double life a joke: ***“Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance”***. The clause is short and the image is a mirror. What they did to the believers is done to them, and the doing is not a punishment added later; it is the same act returning to its owner. The second half of the line says where the return leaves them: continuing, and blind, and self-assured. The punishment of the mocker in this reading is not a sentence handed down from a distance, but the life he has built for himself with the mocking still inside it. The word translated as throwing back carries the sense of an exchange in which the giver receives his own gift. That is why the clause reads less like a verdict and more like the account settled in the same currency. That is why the verse does not describe a retaliation so much as a settling of accounts in the same currency. A man who trains himself to see the believers as fools will eventually see the world that way, and the vision is not corrected by the events that follow because he explains them too. The Book treats mockery as a form of self-harm with a delay built in, and the delay is what makes it attractive at the moment of use. The verse removes the excuse by showing where the delay ends, and it ends in the same life the mocker is already living.

**THE PENALTY THAT IS INVISIBLE**

Notice that the verse does not finish with the mockery being exposed. Exposure would be a mild ending, and it would leave the mocker able to start again with better information. What the line describes is worse and more accurate: he goes on wandering, still confident, still convinced that he is the one person in the room who sees the situation clearly. The earlier verses had already used the same verb about people who think they are setting things right. Here the same condition is applied to the man who laughs, and the two descriptions agree: the loss of sight is the penalty, and it is self-inflicted in slow motion. The sūrah keeps the picture consistent with the rest of the Book. Those who mock the truth are described elsewhere as having traded away the very light that would have guided them: (2:16 — **“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided”**). The trade is the point. Nothing was taken from them by force; a purchase was made, and the goods they received were the ones they asked for. The man in this verse buys the pleasure of a joke about the believers and pays with the ability to evaluate his own position.

**THE SLOW RETURN OF A CHEAP JOKE**

A modern reader meets this verse at the end of a very long supply chain of mockery. Ridicule of religious people is one of the cheapest forms of entertainment available, and it is now delivered continuously to people who never meet the ones being laughed at. The verse is not offended by humour. It is describing what happens to a person who spends years training himself to see a group as contemptible, and who then finds that the training has shaped the way he reads everything else. The blindness is not imposed from outside; it is the end product of the exercise. The corrective is not to ban jokes but to restore the habit of looking at the strongest form of a claim before dismissing it. A person can begin by asking whether he has ever heard the case for the thing he laughs at stated by someone competent, and by noticing how little effort it took to laugh and how much it takes to examine. That asymmetry is exactly what the verse exposes. The mocker in this line is not condemned for one careless sentence. He is left with the consequences of a settled direction, and the reader is invited to change direction while the road is still visible.
