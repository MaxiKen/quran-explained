**A COMMUNITY THAT HAS GONE BEFORE**

The verse is a short reckoning with the generations this passage has been describing, and it opens by naming them in the past tense: ***“That was a community”*** and ***“that had already gone before.”***. The sentence places the whole history it has narrated at a distance from the reader who is hearing it, and it does so without diminishing the importance of what was described. The communities that argued about the patriarchs had made their ancestry into a claim, and the Book's reply is that the ancestors are finished with their own account. Another passage carries the same words about the same history, so that the sentence is repeated where the sūrah closes this discussion: (2:141 — **“That was a community that had already gone before”**). Lineage is thus treated as a record rather than a possession. Ibn ʿAbbās treated the earlier people as a closed account whose good belongs to them, while later people answer for what they themselves earn rather than for ancestral speech.

**WHAT EACH GENERATION EARNS**

The verse then states the terms of the accounting in two parallel clauses: ***“For them is what they earned”***, ***“and for you is what you have earned.”***. The two halves are identical in form and separated only by the people they refer to, and the construction states a general law in a single sentence. The word for earning places every generation under the same measure, so that the merits of the ancestors neither add to the descendants' credit nor carry their burdens. Another passage states the same law about individuals in a form that leaves no room for misunderstanding: (53:38 — **“no soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another”**). Anyone tempted to rest on ancestral standing finds the answer in this sentence.

**A CLOSED ACCOUNT AND AN OPEN ONE**

The two clauses describe one account that has been closed and another that is still being written, and the sūrah has just shown what the ancestors did with theirs. The Book's interest in Abraham and Jacob lies not in their standing as a credit balance for their descendants but in the pattern of submission they handed on to a living community. The same law is applied in another passage where the descendants of the righteous are raised to the rank of their parents, and the reward there is a mercy rather than a transfer: (52:21 — **“We will elevate their descendants to their rank”**). The reader should see that the Book's handling of inheritance is generous where God wills it and never automatic, which is precisely the distinction the arguing communities had missed in their treatment of the patriarchs.

**AN ESTATE SETTLED LONG BEFORE THE HEIRS ARRIVE**

Think of heirs who arrive at a property that was settled long ago and quarrel over a document their ancestors signed, without asking what the document asked of them. The verse turns the whole argument about ancestry into a statement about finished accounts, and it leaves the reader with the one that is still open. The comparison explains why the Book keeps returning to the deeds of the ancestors rather than to their titles, since a title settles nothing about the person holding it. Anyone who has watched an inheritance argued over while the work of the family goes undone can see the point being made, since the document being waved about settles nothing for the people waving it. The past is evidence rather than currency, which is what the sentence assumes.

**THE ACCOUNTABILITY THAT STOPS AT THE GRAVE**

The verse closes with the third clause, and it removes the last remaining ground for resting on another person's record: ***“And you will not be accountable”*** and ***“for what they have done”***. The same words stand in another passage where the Book applies the law to individuals rather than to peoples, and the repetition shows how firmly the principle is fixed: (6:164 — **“No one will reap except what they sow. No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another.”**). The sentence is symmetrical with the two before it, and the three together answer the claims of descent that have run through the sūrah. What stands in front of the reader is a community that has been judged, a community that is being judged, and a boundary between them that no claim of kinship can cross. The Book states this boundary without severity and without argument, which suggests that the fact was obvious to its first audience even as they disputed over ancestry. The plain question left is what his own page will say. Present-day pride in ancestors becomes an excuse when their achievements are treated as credit against duties that remain unpaid now. Therefore the repeated division of earnings is not a dismissal of history but the reason history can instruct without becoming inherited credit.
