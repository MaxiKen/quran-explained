**THE CREED THE BELIEVERS ARE TOLD TO DECLARE**

The verse gives the community the words it is to use, and the words begin with the articles of belief: ***“Say, ˹O believers,˺ “We believe in Allah”*** and ***“and what has been revealed to us”***. The sentence is addressed to the believers rather than to their opponents, which makes it a statement of identity before it becomes an answer to anyone. The Book places the revelation given to this community first, and then extends the same belief backward through the prophets, so that the creed is presented as one continuous giving. The reader should notice the order, since the sentence begins with God and then moves through history without a break. The faith described in the sentence is not a new departure but the acknowledgement of a single source.

**THE PROPHETS NAMED IN A SINGLE BREATH**

The list then reaches back to the family at the centre of the sūrah's argument and continues through the lawgivers: ***“and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob”***, ***“and his descendants”***, ***“and what was given to Moses, Jesus”*** and ***“and other prophets from their Lord.”***. The names are given without rank and without a distinction drawn between the earlier and the later, which is the point the following clause states directly. Another passage repeats the same creed in almost the same words, so that the reader meets it as a formula the community was taught rather than a sentence composed for one occasion: (3:84 — **“We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob”**). What the list does is to prevent any part of the prophetic history from becoming a separate religion.

**NO DISTINCTION DRAWN BETWEEN THEM**

The sentence then states the position the community is to take toward the whole company, and it states it as a refusal: ***“We make no distinction between any of them.”***. The refusal is aimed at the practice of accepting some messengers and rejecting others, which the Book treats as a rejection of the sending itself. Another passage describes that practice as a desire to split the matter in half, and it names the wish in the same words used here: (4:150 — **“wish to make a distinction between Allah and His messengers”**). The verse's creed therefore does not merely add prophets to a list; it makes the acceptance of all of them the test of accepting any. A reader who has been taught to honour one messenger while dismissing another will find the clause addressed to him.

**A SUBMISSION NAMED AT THE END**

The declaration closes with the word that has run through this stretch since the account of Abraham: ***“And to Allah we all submit.”***. The sentence therefore moves from belief to submission in one breath, which keeps the creed tied to the posture the Book has been demanding all along. The word used here has appeared in the command given to Abraham, in the answer he gave, and in the advice he left his children, and it appears now in the mouth of the whole community. The Book's practice is to close a statement of doctrine with a statement of the response it requires, and the verse follows that practice exactly. What the reader is left with is a creed whose last word is a handover rather than an assertion.

**A LETTER OF RECOMMENDATION READ ALOUD**

Think of a family that is asked to declare its loyalties and that answers by listing every ancestor it honours, including the ones the questioners had claimed for themselves. The believers are given a declaration of that kind here, and its form is brief enough to be repeated in a single sitting. The comparison explains why the list of names matters as much as the statement about God, since the argument of the sūrah is about who owns the prophetic inheritance. Anyone who has heard a community claim an ancestor while rejecting the message that ancestor followed can see what the creed is doing here. What remains after the sentence is the unity of the giving and the unity of the response, and the community's identity rests on both.
