**BELIEF WORSHIP AND OPEN-HANDED TRUST**

The verses that follow describe the people the Book will guide, and the description begins with what they hold to be true: ***“who believe in the unseen, establish prayer”***. The unseen is what is hidden from the senses, and the early authorities named the things it covers: the resurrection, the reckoning, the scale, the bridge, Paradise and the Fire. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with that list and with a wider reading in which the unseen includes whatever God has kept from human sight, including God Himself, who is not seen in this life. The second mark in the phrase is the prayer, and the wording here is not the word for praying once but for standing the prayer up and keeping it standing. Ibn ʿAbbās explained the phrase as performing the prescribed prayers in full, with their ablution, their bowing, their prostration and their appointed times, which makes the mark a matter of practice rather than of feeling.

The order of the marks is worth noticing, because the sūrah puts belief first and worship second. A person can pray without believing what he is told about God, and the prayer then becomes a habit that carries him nowhere; a person can believe and never pray, and the belief sits in him untested and slowly cools. The verse puts belief first and worship second, in the order of root and fruit. The Book repeats the pair elsewhere in a single sentence about the same people, and the reason for noticing it is that the description here is the standard and not a local arrangement: (8:3 — **“those who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them”**). What the reader is given at the head of the sūrah is therefore a portrait with its features in order, and the order tells him where to begin when his own practice has slipped.

**THE THIRD MARK AND WHY IT COMES LAST**

The third mark closes the verse: ***“and donate from what We have provided for them”***. The wording does not say from what they own, and the difference is deliberate. What a person holds is a provision given to him, which makes the gift an act of handling someone else's property in the way the owner intended. The early authorities read the phrase widely, some taking it as the obligatory poor-due and others as every act of giving in obedience to God, and the second reading does not cancel the first. The Book joins giving to prayer again in the same wording used here for the same people: (2:45 — **“seek help through patience and prayer”**) sits in a passage that sends the reader to the two instruments of patience and worship, and the giving in this verse belongs to the same family of habits. A community that prays and never gives can hold its worship inside itself, and the sūrah closes the door on that possibility on its first page. The reasoning behind the order is that a closed hand and a closed heart usually travel together, since the gift is what proves that the giver has stopped treating his provision as his own.

**TRUST BEFORE SIGHT**

A reader may wonder why the verse begins with what cannot be seen when so much of the Book deals with what can. The answer lies in what belief does to a person: it forms him around something he cannot check, and from that formation the actions in the rest of the verse draw their weight. Ibn Masʿūd said that belief is trust, and al-Zuhrī is credited with describing it as the deeds themselves, which shows how closely the earliest teachers tied the inner thing to the outer. A man who obeys only what he can see is obeying the visible authority in the room; the man who obeys the unseen has accepted a Lord over the whole of his life, including the parts no observer will ever see. The three marks in the verse are therefore one movement: a heart that trusts what it cannot see, a body that stands in prayer at the appointed time, and a hand that opens from what it has been given. That is why the verse reads as a single portrait rather than as three separate requirements, and the point is that a person who has grown in one of them is being pulled towards the other two.

**A SERVER WHO KEEPS A LEDGER IN TWO COLUMNS**

Think of a servant who is entrusted with a shop and keeps two books, one for the owner and one for himself. He has learned to put everything into the first book, because the second is a fiction that the owner's arrival would expose in a single hour. The verse trains the reader in that habit. It asks him to hold what he cannot see as certain, to stand where he has been told to stand, and to spend as a man spending another's property. Anyone who has watched a person give up a good deal for a promise that had not yet arrived will recognise the shape of this verse, and anyone who has watched a person hold on to everything until his last day will recognise what the verse is saving him from. The instruction is plain today, in a time when the visible is louder than it has ever been.
