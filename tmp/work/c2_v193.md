**THE OBJECTIVE IS THE END OF PERSECUTION**

The movement toward the objective is the end of persecution rests on ***“Fight against them ˹if they persecute you˺”***, ***“until there is no more persecution”***. This verse continues the rules of the conflict described in 2:190–192. The objective is that *fitnah*—persecution, coercion, and violent obstruction of faith—should end, and that religion should be for Allah. It is not a command to force every person to become Muslim. The Qur’an explicitly says there is no compulsion in religion, and it commands protection for a non-Muslim who seeks safety. The immediate conflict was with those who prevented the believers from worshipping and expelled them. The verse protects freedom of sincere worship by removing the armed oppression that denied it. The distinction between ending persecution and forcing belief is crucial. Faith is an act of the heart and cannot be produced by a sword. Islam may require a state to defend its people and stop an armed force from destroying religious freedom, but it does not make conversion the condition for every person’s safety. The Prophet ﷺ entered treaties with non-Muslim communities and recognized their rights under lawful agreements. The Qur’an’s use of *until* does not mean that fighting continues forever against anyone who holds another creed. It identifies the condition under which the conflict ends: when aggression and persecution end. Once that condition is met, the next sentence removes hostility except toward those who continue to oppress. (2:190 — **“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you”**) Because the objective is the end of persecution governs 2:193, if they cease, hostility ends arises from the objective is the end of persecution. It follows that the limits of the exception completes the objective is the end of persecution through if they cease, hostility ends.

**IF THEY CEASE, HOSTILITY ENDS**

The sequence reaches if they cease, hostility ends through ***“and ˹your˺ devotion will be to Allah ˹alone˺.”***. The repetition of *if they cease* makes the rule unmistakable. When the enemy stops fighting and persecution, Muslims may not continue aggression as punishment for a former identity. The remaining exception is directed at *oppressors*, those who persist in wrongdoing. Even then, hostility must be lawful, proportionate, and administered by legitimate authority; it does not authorize private revenge. A ceasefire may require monitoring, exchange of prisoners, return of property, and guarantees of safety. These practical measures serve the moral end of protecting life. The Prophet ﷺ honoured treaties and condemned treachery. A community cannot invoke divine authority while breaking agreements whenever it becomes advantageous. The believer should not make wealth, tribe, nation, leader, or desire the object of ultimate worship. A society can be free from external religious persecution while individuals remain enslaved to created powers. Divine oneness liberates the heart from these rivals. Publicly, the phrase requires that no human authority claim the right to determine worship by force. Privately, it requires sincerity. Muslims defend the freedom to worship Allah while not treating their own power as divine or using religion to justify oppression. (2:256 — **“Let there be no compulsion in religion”**) The reason the limits of the exception follows if they cease, hostility ends lies in the objective is the end of persecution. Therefore, the limits of the exception returns practical force to if they cease, hostility ends.

**THE LIMITS OF THE EXCEPTION**

The limits of the exception takes shape in ***“If they stop ˹persecuting you˺, let there be no hostility except against the aggressors”***. The Qur’an distinguishes theological disagreement, political opposition, crime, and armed aggression. This precision is essential today. A group cannot label a civilian population *oppressors* and attack them indiscriminately. A leader cannot call critics enemies of Allah to silence them. The verse’s own limits—those who fight, the prohibition of transgression, and cessation when aggression stops—must remain in view. If persecution ends through Muslim victory, the victors remain responsible for justice. They must protect non-combatants, secure places of worship, return rights, and prevent revenge. Victory is not proof that every act of the victorious side was righteous. Allah judges means as well as ends. The Prophet ﷺ showed mercy at moments when his enemies were powerless. He protected those who sought refuge and did not require every defeated person to adopt the faith. This is a practical interpretation of the verse: end aggression, establish safety, and leave hearts to Allah. Persecution is like a barrier placed across the only road by which conscience can reach worship. Removing the barrier is the aim; owning the road, humiliating travellers, or forcing inward faith is not. Once the obstruction ends, hostility has no lawful object except renewed aggression. This distinction matters acutely today, when political projects can borrow sacred language for domination. The verse directs force against coercive persecution so that devotion belongs to God, then commands the force to stop when the wrong stops. (8:61 — **“If the enemy is inclined towards peace, make peace with them.”**) In other words, the limits of the exception answers the objective is the end of persecution.
