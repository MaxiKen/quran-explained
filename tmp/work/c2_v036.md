**A PROMISE OF SAFETY USED TO BRING ABOUT LOSS**

Satan attacks the trust on which life in the Garden rests: ***“But Satan deceived them”***. The one who refused to honour Adam now seeks to turn him from God’s word. Ibn ʿAbbās explains the act as leading the pair astray. The wording carries the sense of a slip, while another recitation brings out their removal from the Garden. These are linked events: the lapse becomes the means of losing their place. The fuller account shows how the trap worked. Satan offered lasting life and rule: (20:120 — **“Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that does not fade away?””**). He then hid his hostile purpose under an oath: (7:21 — **“And he swore to them, “I am truly your sincere advisor.””**). The lure was not presented as ruin. It was presented as a way to keep and enlarge the good they already had. That is why the known command mattered more than the voice claiming to help. A pledge of goodwill could not change what God had forbidden. The warning reaches any claim that a clear wrong will secure a person’s future. The promised gain may be the very means by which present good is lost.

The result was ***“leading to their fall from the ˹blissful˺ state they were in”***. What they lost included ease, shelter, and the food freely given to them. The other account adds the shame that followed eating: (7:22 — **“their nakedness was exposed to them, prompting them to cover themselves with leaves from Paradise.”**). The verse attributes the fall to Satan because his deceit brought about their lapse. It does not grant him power to overrule God or to drive people from Paradise on his own. The command to leave comes from God in the next clause. Cause and authority must therefore be kept apart: Satan tempted, the pair ate, and their Lord judged. Nor does naming the tempter remove their own part in what happened. Satan’s later address to those who follow him states the limit of his power: (14:22 — **“I only called you, and you responded to me. So do not blame me; blame yourselves.”**). The parallel concerns his method, not an equal judgement upon Adam and those later followers. Adam will repent. His enemy’s role explains the danger; it does not turn an act done through deception into an act for which no one must seek pardon.

**THE CONFLICT CONTINUES AFTER THE CHANGE OF DWELLING**

The divine order is ***“and We said, “Descend from the heavens ˹to the earth˺”***, followed by ***“as enemies to each other.”***. The address is plural. Its reach is wider than a private quarrel between husband and wife. Mujāhid explains the enmity as that between Adam and his offspring, and Iblīs and his offspring. The warning thus follows the human family into its new life. Another reading extends the address through Adam and his wife to the strife that will arise among their children. These senses can meet: the enemy who seeks human ruin also encourages people to wrong one another. But the sentence is not an order to hate a spouse or a neighbour. It describes a danger to be faced, not a licence to produce more harm. The Quran gives the proper response to the chief enemy: (35:6 — **“Surely Satan is an enemy to you, so take him as an enemy.”**). Since Satan has already offered ruin in the dress of sincere advice, treating him as an enemy means refusing his counsel rather than merely speaking against him. The setting changes from Paradise to earth; the need to hold to God’s word does not.

**SUSTENANCE IS GRANTED WITH A TERM ATTACHED**

Even the order to leave contains a gift: ***“You will find in the earth a residence”*** ***“and provision”***. Earth is not described as a place where God will give them nothing. It has room for life, food, clothing, and the means of meeting human needs. Abū al-ʿĀliyah connects the residence with the earth made ready as a resting place. Another transmitted reading, from al-Suddī, includes the graves in which people will lie. The broader meaning can hold both stages. Earth receives human beings in life and in death, but neither state is their final end. The parallel account joins the stages: (7:25 — **““There you will live, there you will die, and from there you will be resurrected.””**). Provision concerns what can be used during that earthly course. It is real good, not an illusion to be despised because it passes away. Yet it is not the ease they have left. Food must be sought, bodies must be sheltered, and life must be lived amid trial. God has prepared the means without promising that the path through them will be free of toil or conflict.

The limit is ***“for your appointed stay.””***. Al-Suddī takes the end of enjoyment as death; Mujāhid relates the term to the Resurrection, when the world’s course ends. One concerns each person’s span, the other the span of earthly life as a whole. Neither makes the earth a lasting home. The fall also does not place an inherited sin upon every child of Adam. The rule of personal responsibility remains: (6:164 — **“No soul burdened with sin will bear the burden of another.”**). Later people share the setting of toil and temptation, not a guilt charged to them for an act they did not commit. Adam himself is not left beyond mercy. The Quran says, (20:122 — **“Then his Lord chose him ˹for His grace˺, accepted his repentance, and guided him ˹rightly˺”**). His accepted return to God does not cancel the earthly task announced before the lapse. This is why earthly life cannot simply be called proof that its people have been cast off. It is the place of their appointed trial, with provision for the journey and guidance still to come. The finite stay gives urgency to that guidance; it does not make the stay meaningless.
