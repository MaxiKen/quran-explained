**RIVALS IN LOVE AND WORSHIP**

Rivals in love and worship begins from ***“Still there are some”***, ***“who take others as Allah’s equal”***, ***“they love them as they should love Allah”***. After displaying the signs of Allah’s oneness, the Qur’an describes people who still take rivals or equals, besides Him. A rival may be a physical idol, a deified person, a spirit, an ideology, wealth, power, or any created thing given the devotion and obedience that belong exclusively to Allah. The central issue is not simply affection. It is a form of love that includes worship, ultimate submission, fear, hope, and the belief that the rival possesses independent power. Human beings naturally love parents, spouses, children, friends, homeland, beauty, and knowledge. Islam does not demand that these loves disappear. It orders them beneath love of Allah. When a created love agrees with obedience to Allah, it becomes a blessing. When it makes a person reject Allah, violate rights, or sacrifice truth, it has become a rival. The believers’ love for God is stronger and undivided. Their love is not a momentary emotion. It is rooted in recognizing Allah’s perfection, mercy, lordship, and countless gifts. It appears in obedience when desire pulls in another direction, in gratitude during ease, patience during hardship, repentance after sin, and preference for truth over applause. Love of Allah produces both intimacy and awe. The believer hopes in mercy, fears displeasing Him, longs to meet Him, and trusts His wisdom. The Qur’an describes love as a mark of faith and says that those who believe are more intense in love for Allah because no rival divides the centre of their devotion. (2:163 — **“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**) Because rivals in love and worship governs 2:165, the test of competing loves arises from rivals in love and worship. It follows that love and justice completes rivals in love and worship through the test of competing loves.

**THE TEST OF COMPETING LOVES**

***“but the ˹true˺ believers love Allah even more.”***, ***“If only the wrongdoers could see the ˹horrible˺ punishment ˹awaiting them˺, they would certainly realize”***, ***“that all power belongs to Allah”*** establish the test of competing loves. A person can examine his hierarchy of love through choices. Conflicts between truth and reputation disclose what a person protects; conflicts between lawful income and profit disclose what he obeys; and choices about clothing, speech, friendship, and public conduct disclose whose approval governs him. The answers reveal whether Allah is loved above all or merely included among other priorities. The Prophet ﷺ said that none truly believes until he loves the Messenger more than his parent, child, and all people, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim; another authentic hadith describes the sweetness of faith when Allah and His Messenger are more beloved than everything else. This does not make the Prophet an object of worship. It means loving him as Allah’s messenger and following him because of Allah. The verse suddenly turns toward the Day of Punishment: If only the wrongdoers could see. Their false hierarchy will then be exposed. They will see that all power—all power—belongs to Allah, while the rivals they obeyed possess no independent control. The phrase is a picture of regret: the truth becomes undeniable only after the opportunity to act upon it has ended. In this world, power may appear to belong to rulers, armies, money, or public opinion. These are created means, and Allah may allow them influence for a time. But no means operates outside His knowledge and permission. On the Day of Resurrection, the illusion of independent power disappears, and every false allegiance is seen in its true weakness. (9:24 — **“˹if all these˺ are more beloved to you than Allah”**) The reason love and justice follows the test of competing loves lies in rivals in love and worship. Therefore, love and justice returns practical force to the test of competing loves.

**LOVE AND JUSTICE**

For love and justice, the decisive wording is ***“and that Allah is”***, ***“indeed severe in punishment”***. The verse links shirk with wrongdoing—*al-ẓulm*. Giving worship to other than Allah is the greatest injustice because it places a created thing in the position of the Creator. It also creates secondary injustices: people may obey idols of tribe, race, nation, or leader and then oppress others to protect them. Pure love of Allah, by contrast, disciplines power. The servant knows that he will answer for how he treats people and creation. He can love his family deeply without owning them, love his homeland without worshipping nationalism, and respect a leader without obeying him in sin. Love grows through knowing Allah’s names, reciting and reflecting upon the Qur’an, remembering blessings, performing obligatory worship, engaging in voluntary deeds, and observing the effects of obedience on the heart. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah’s servant draws near through obligations and then through voluntary works until Allah loves him, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. Love also grows when a believer notices Allah’s mercy in forgiveness and guidance. It is not built only through fear. The names *al-Raḥmān* and *the Most Merciful* in the preceding verse show that the One who deserves ultimate love is the One whose mercy surrounds His servants. Picture a passenger who thanks the dashboard for a safe journey while ignoring the driver, because the glowing instruments are what he can see and touch. The instruments may be useful, but they neither chose the road nor preserved the traveller. Rivals set beside God occupy the dashboard's place: created means are treated as sources, and dependence is mistaken for worship. In a present-day culture of brands, leaders, and systems, love must pass through created means without stopping at them. (31:13 — **“while advising him, “O my dear son!”**) In other words, love and justice answers rivals in love and worship.
