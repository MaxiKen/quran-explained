**THE SHORT LIST AND ITS CAP**

***“He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine”*** gives the list its compact shape, and the fourth item completes it: ***“and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah.”*** The opening word is load-bearing, because it caps the list — whatever is not named here or elsewhere in revelation remains inside the permission. The cap is the verse's answer to the compilers of taboos: the forbidden is a short list held by revelation, not a lengthening one held by custom. Because the list is short enough to memorise, nobody can plead ignorance of its boundaries. The law keeps its edges where the eye can see them. Then the verse turns from law to life with ***“But if someone is compelled by necessity”***, qualified in two directions by ***“neither driven by desire”*** and ***“nor exceeding immediate need”***. The compelled person is answered by the close: ***“they will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. Law and emergency clause stand in one breath.

**INSIDE THE FOUR ITEMS**

Carrion is what has not been slaughtered under the prescribed rules, with fish and locusts given a special status, and the poured-forth blood is named alongside the flesh of swine. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the fourth item as what is immolated purposefully in other than the Name of Allah — that is, to idols — so the prohibition targets worship's direction as much as diet. The four items share one logic: each removes a form of sacred confusion from the table. The confusion runs in two directions — food made sacred to idols, and food made dangerous by superstition — and the list clears both. Ibn ʿAbbās reports further that the compelled one eats without filling himself and without carrying anything away, and those who rebel or transgress fall outside the dispensation. The two qualifiers of the clause measure both motive and amount, because necessity is a quantity and not an atmosphere. The clause is a bridge for the desperate and a closed door for the greedy. (5:103 — **“Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah,”**)

**OPTIONAL UNDER PRESSURE**

A hearer may ask whether the necessity clause makes the prohibitions optional under pressure. The clause does not make the prohibited permitted; it makes the sin forgivable for the compelled, which is a different category entirely. The one who eats carrion out of necessity does not declare it good; he keeps the law's verdict in his heart while his body survives on the exception. Breaking a window to escape a fire is necessity; breaking it to take the goods is the rebellion the clause excludes. Because the eater neither desires the prohibited nor exceeds the need, the dispensation measures both motive and amount. The law stands; the emergency is a mercy built into its doorframe. The clause also reveals the character of the lawgiver, because a system that writes the exception into the statute serves the people it governs. The mercy is named immediately after the prohibition, so the reader is never left alone with the list.

**LAW AND DOORFRAME**

The parallel legislation is given in (16:116 — **“Do not falsely declare with your tongues, “This is lawful, and that is unlawful,” ˹only˺ fabricating lies against Allah. Indeed,”**). The invented animals of the forbidden-ritual are rejected by name in the passage quoted above, and Ibn ʿAbbās's account carries the finding that the revelation of the list was itself the refutation of the fabricated taboos. The Sunna adds to carrion what is severed from a living creature, and al-Shāfiʿī holds that the traveller in disobedience is not covered by the dispensation. The boundary of the exception is drawn by the same care as the list itself. The application is balanced by design: hold the short list firmly and the narrow door open, because the same mercy that forbids the four items feeds the person who cannot reach anything else. The list and the clause are the two hands of one law, and the reader who honours only one of them has stopped halfway through the verse. Because the exception is written into the statute, the desperate are governed and not abandoned. Forgiveness and mercy are written into the verse that states the prohibition.
