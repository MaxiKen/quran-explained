**CLEAR PROHIBITIONS**

***“He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine”***, ***“and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah.”*** mark the turn toward clear prohibitions. This verse lists four primary categories of forbidden food: *maytah*, an animal that dies without lawful slaughter; flowing blood; swine; and an animal dedicated to other than Allah. The list establishes that dietary law is determined by revelation, not by superstition, ethnic pride, or personal taste. The phrase *He has only forbidden* also corrects people who invent unnecessary restrictions and then attribute them to Allah. The Qur’an gives related lists in 5:3, 6:145, and 16:115. The passages complement one another, while the Sunnah and juristic reasoning explain details such as lawful slaughter, hunting, impurities, and circumstances of necessity. A Muslim should learn from reliable scholarship rather than making confident rulings from a single translation. *Carrion* is an animal that dies without the prescribed process, though the jurists discuss exceptions such as certain sea animals and locusts based on authentic hadith. Flowing blood—flowing blood in 6:145—is prohibited, while blood that remains in the veins after proper slaughter is not treated in the same way. These distinctions show why Arabic wording, prophetic explanation, and legal method matter. The prohibition protects health, cultivates respect for life, and separates Muslim worship from pre-Islamic sacrificial customs. Slaughter is not a licence for cruelty. The Prophet ﷺ commanded excellence in slaughter, sharpening the blade, and sparing the animal unnecessary suffering, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. (2:172 — **“O believers! Eat from the good things We have provided”**) Because clear prohibitions governs 2:173, swine and sacrificial dedication arises from clear prohibitions. It follows that neither desiring nor transgressing completes clear prohibitions through swine and sacrificial dedication.

**SWINE AND SACRIFICIAL DEDICATION**

The verse develops swine and sacrificial dedication with ***“But if someone is compelled by necessity”***, ***“neither driven by desire”***. The flesh of swine is explicitly prohibited. Muslims accept the command as an act of obedience even where they do not know every wisdom. They may reflect upon hygiene, disease, and the moral symbolism of the prohibition, but they do not claim that an unproven explanation is the whole reason. Revelation is authoritative because Allah knows what His servants do not know. What has been dedicated to other than Allah concerns food offered as a sacrifice in the name of an idol, person, spirit, or other created object. The issue is theological as well as dietary. An act of sacrifice is worship, and worship belongs to Allah alone. Eating such a sacrifice would participate in or approve a rival allegiance, which contradicts *divine oneness*. Allah then gives an important concession: whoever is compelled by necessity may eat the forbidden food without sin, provided he neither desires it nor exceeds the need. *Necessity* occurs when a person faces a real danger to life or serious health and cannot find lawful food. Mere convenience, curiosity, social pressure, or a preference for forbidden taste is not necessity. The concession demonstrates that the divine law protects life. A person who would die or suffer severe harm without food may take enough to survive. He does not continue eating for pleasure once the danger is removed, and he does not turn the exception into a routine. The maxim *necessities permit the prohibited* is tied to genuine necessity and measured by its extent. (5:3 — **“what is killed by strangling, beating, a fall”**) The reason neither desiring nor transgressing follows swine and sacrificial dedication lies in clear prohibitions. Therefore, neither desiring nor transgressing returns practical force to swine and sacrificial dedication.

**NEITHER DESIRING NOR TRANSGRESSING**

At neither desiring nor transgressing, attention falls on ***“nor exceeding immediate need”***, ***“they will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. Neither desiring it nor exceeding the need is often explained as not seeking the forbidden item while lawful alternatives exist and not eating beyond what necessity requires. The person’s heart remains unwilling to violate the rule, and his action is restricted to preservation of life. If someone can obtain lawful food through asking, purchasing, travelling, or accepting help, he should do so. The concession also applies in a way that avoids harm to others. A person may not use emergency conditions as a licence to steal from someone more vulnerable, endanger another life, or profit from scarcity. Necessity removes the sin of the narrowly defined consumption; it does not suspend all other obligations. The verse ends with Allah’s forgiveness and mercy. The Lawgiver knows human weakness, accidents, travel, famine, coercion, and illness. Legal commands are not designed to trap a servant who is genuinely unable. The concession is not a loophole against obedience; it is part of obedience because Allah Himself revealed it. This principle extends across worship and social law. A sick person may pray sitting, a traveller may shorten prayer, and a person facing compulsion is judged according to what he could control. Mercy is embedded in the law while the normal rule remains clear. Necessity operates like an emergency exit: it is built to preserve life when the ordinary route is blocked, not to become the building's everyday entrance. The listed prohibitions remain real, yet the trapped person is not commanded to die in order to display obedience. That balance matters in present-day crises involving hunger, medicine, or isolation. Need must be genuine, the use no more than required, and the heart free of rebellion; within those limits, taking the exit is obedience to the merciful Lawgiver rather than escape from His law. (6:118 — **“So eat only of what is slaughtered in Allah’s Name”**) In other words, neither desiring nor transgressing answers clear prohibitions.
