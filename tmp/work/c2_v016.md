**A TRADE THAT LOSES MONEY**

The sūrah closes the account of the double game with a commercial image: ***“They are the ones”***, ***“who trade guidance for misguidance.”*** The word is not a metaphor for a mistake but for a transaction, and the early readers took it that way. Mujāhid is reported to have said that the man in this line believed and then disbelieved, while Qatādah said simply that they preferred deviation to guidance. Both readings describe the same exchange: something of real value is handed over, something worthless is received, and the man making the trade believes he has improved his position, since the goods he receives are the ones he wanted, and no one questions a purchase while it is still being enjoyed. The verse identifies such people by what they did rather than by what they felt about it.

The second half of the sentence delivers the outcome: ***“But this trade is profitless”***, and then ***“and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided”***. Nothing about the transaction is described as unsuccessful in the ordinary sense; the profit is simply absent, and the capital is gone. What makes the image so effective is that a merchant reads a loss more clearly than he reads a moral failure, and the Book uses his own ledger to describe the person who has given away his religion for a season of ease. The reader is shown the receipt rather than the motive, and the receipt is empty. Qatādah put the same exchange in a single phrase when he said that they preferred deviation to guidance, and that preference is what a bad trade looks like from inside. The report from Ibn ʿAbbās describes them choosing disbelief in place of faith and exchanging guidance for error, which is the same transaction described from the other side of the counter.

**A LOSS THAT HIDES FOR A SEASON**

Anyone who has watched a bad investment knows how the loss hides for a while. The money is gone and the story of the money is intact, so the investor feels exactly as wealthy as he felt the day before. The same is true of the trade described here. A man who bends his faith to keep a position, a friendship, or a share of influence loses the thing that made the position worth having, and he keeps his standing, so nothing in his week tells him that anything has changed. The loss becomes visible only when he needs the thing he sold. That is why the Book describes the transaction in advance, so the reader can recognise the moment while the decision is still his own.

The Qur'an reports the same exchange in another sūrah with almost the same phrase, which shows that this is a settled pattern rather than one incident: (2:175 — **“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance”**). The repetition is deliberate. A person reading the Book for the first time will meet the sentence in several chapters, and each time it stands next to a description of the loss that follows. The covenant is another thing that gets traded in the same way: (3:77 — **“Indeed, those who trade Allah's covenant”**), and the remainder of that verse names the punishment attached to the trade. The pattern is old enough to have its own name in the Book, and the reader is meant to recognise it in his own habits rather than only in the conduct of strangers.

**WHAT THE TRADE LOOKS LIKE IN A LIFE**

Concretely, the trades in this verse are small and unromantic. A young man lets an argument pass because of a friend's opinion; a woman keeps quiet about a practice she follows because it is easier at work; a family decides that the difficult part of the religion can wait until the children are grown. None of these moments look like the abandonment of a faith, and that is exactly the point. Each one is a purchase, and what is bought is a little more comfort or a little less friction. The Qur'an calls the goods misguidance because of what they do to the person's sense of direction rather than because of what they cost on the day. The trade only runs one way, and the verse says the lost guidance is not recovered by regret alone. The word for being guided is denied at the end of the line, which means the failure reaches the future as well as the past. This is the part a reader should take seriously: the man in the verse is not described as punished for a single bad deal, but as having ended up without the thing he sold and without the arrival he was walking toward. Naming the trades still on the table, and refusing them while the price is clear, is what this line is for.
