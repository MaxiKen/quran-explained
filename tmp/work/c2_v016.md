**THE TRADE THAT IS NAMED IN THE BOOK'S OWN TERMS**

The next verse gathers the whole passage into one image and describes the pretenders as traders: ***“They are the ones”***, ***“who trade guidance for misguidance.”***. The image is exact, and it means that the men had something of value in their hands and exchanged it for something worthless. Ibn ʿAbbās read the guidance as the clear road the message had brought them and the misguidance as the error they preferred, and al-Suddī and Qatādah are credited with the same reading of an exchange made knowingly. The Book uses the same figure for the people who were given a share of the Scripture and sold it: (4:44 — **“trade it for misguidance”**), and it uses it again for those who gave belief for disbelief in a later sūrah. What turns on the image is that the men are not described as ignorant of the value of what they held, since a trader knows what he is carrying, and their fault is the price they accepted.

**THE PROFIT THAT NEVER ARRIVED**

The sentence follows the trade to its ledger: ***“But this trade is profitless”***. The word describes a bargain in which nothing is gained, and the verse states the outcome as a fact rather than as a threat. The Book names the same transaction in another place and adds what the traders kept in place of what they sold: (2:175 — **“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance and forgiveness for punishment”**). The men in the passage did not lose something theoretical, since what they gave up was a road, a community and a destination, and the verse states plainly that the exchange produced nothing at all. That is why the sūrah does not present the pretenders as enviable, whatever the appearance of their position, because a trade with no profit leaves the trader where he started and poorer for the effort.

**THE GUIDANCE THEY NO LONGER HAVE**

The verse closes with the second half of the loss: ***“and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided”***. The sentence does not say that they will be guided later; it places them outside the description for the present, and the Book uses the same verdict in a passage about people who gave up belief for its opposite: (3:177 — **“Those who trade belief for disbelief will never harm Allah in the least”**). The wording also settles a question that a reader might carry about the men's sincerity, since a person who has made the exchange and knows it is not a person who is still looking. The early teachers explained the phrase as the loss of the direction that the Book gives to anyone who comes to it honestly, which means the guidance was available and refused. A man who has walked a road and then chosen another is not in the position of a man who has never seen the first, and the verse treats the difference as the whole of the matter. What follows in the sūrah is a pair of pictures that show how such a person ends up walking, and the reader meets the first of them in the next verse.

**A TRADER WHO SWAPS A PLOUGH FOR A BROKEN LAMP**

Think of a farmer who trades his plough for a lamp with no oil, and who defends the bargain in front of the neighbours because he has already made it. The verse describes a decision of that shape and follows it to the field left unploughed and the dark room where the lamp was meant to serve. These days the same bargain is made whenever a person sets aside a practice or a conviction for something that cannot carry him through a hard season, and the sūrah's plain account of profit and loss saves him from the salesmanship of the moment. The reader is asked to look at what he is holding and to ask what he traded for it, and the answer to that question is the beginning of the road back. The sūrah does not leave him with the picture alone, and what follows is a second picture that shows the same man in a storm, which is where every trade of this kind is finally tested.
