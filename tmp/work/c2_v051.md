**FORTY NIGHTS AND A GOLDEN CALF**

The history continues with a failure that happened while the leader was still away: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights”***, and ***“for Moses, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully”***. The Book places the two halves of the sentence side by side without any commentary at all, and the placement itself does the work of a whole passage. While the appointed meeting was being kept on the mountain, the people who had crossed the sea on dry ground were melting their jewellery into a shape of a cow. Nothing in the verse explains how the two things could be true of the same community, because the reader is meant to feel the weight of the gap rather than to explain it away.

The details of the appointment are given in another sūrah, where the period is described in stages: (7:142 — **“We appointed for Moses thirty nights then added another ten—completing”**), with the rest of the verse naming the total of forty. The length is part of the story. This was not a hasty absence of an afternoon, and the people had time to think about what they were doing. The Book gives a fuller account of the manufacturing of the calf elsewhere, and the worship scene there is described plainly: (20:88 — **“Then he moulded for them an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound. They said,”**). The next words in that verse record them identifying the calf as their god and as the god of Moses, which is the sentence a person reads twice.

**AN ABSENCE THAT BECAME AN EXCUSE**

The phrase about Moses being away is the hinge of the failure, and the Book uses it as a description rather than a defence. An absent leader is not an absent Lord, and the community had been given evidence that did not depend on Moses being visible, since the sea had opened in front of them whether or not he was standing on the shore. What they wanted was something they could see and touch, and the gap in their attention was filled with the nearest available object. The Book tells the same story about every generation that has ever substituted a manageable god for the one who is not available for inspection, and it uses this episode as the pattern. The Book also reports what the maker of the calf said and what the people answered, which shows the mechanism in its full detail. A visible object with a sound coming out of it was enough to satisfy a crowd that had been carried through a sea a few weeks earlier. The reader should notice that the people did not deny the exodus; they simply arranged their worship around something new, and the two things coexisted in their minds without difficulty. That is the condition the sūrah has been describing since its opening pages under other names.

**THE SHORT MEMORY OF A RESCUED PEOPLE**

There is nothing in this episode that a reader can safely file away as an ancient error. Every person who has been carried through a difficulty has a golden calf of his own within reach, and the object is usually something he made himself: a plan, a reputation, a habit of comfort, or an image of God that never asks anything difficult. The verse names the failure in the plainest terms, acting wrongfully, which means the conduct is described as a wrong rather than a lapse of judgment, and the Book elsewhere states what happens to the ones who settle there: (7:152 — **“Those who worshipped the calf will certainly be afflicted with Allah's wrath”**). The warning is placed beside the history so that a later generation can read its own risk in an older story. The practical move is to name the thing that is currently functioning as a substitute: what a person consults when he wants reassurance, and what he rearranges his life around when he needs to feel safe. The Book is not squeamish about the answer, and it does not require a person to despise the good things in his life. It requires him to notice when a good thing has been given the place of the one who gave it, and to take it down from that place while he is still able to see what he did.
