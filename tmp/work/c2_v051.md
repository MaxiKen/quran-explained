**AN APPOINTED TERM FOR GUIDANCE, NOT ABANDONMENT**

Freedom from Pharaoh left the people in need of a revealed rule for their life together. The next reminder concerns its preparation: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights for Moses”***. Moses went to the appointed place on the mountain, while his people remained under Aaron’s care. The stated term belongs to God’s arrangement, not to a promise the people could shorten when waiting became hard. Its full length is explained elsewhere: (7:142 — **“We appointed for Moses thirty nights then added another ten—completing his Lord’s term of forty nights”**). Abū al-ʿĀliyah relates it to the month of Dhū al-Qaʿdah and the first ten nights of Dhū al-Ḥijjah. Counting by nights includes the days belonging to them; it does not describe forty brief meetings with a return home between each one. The term was set apart for what Moses would receive from his Lord. The transmitted reading of Abū ʿAmr presents God’s promise, while the reading behind the appointment wording also brings out Moses’ willing response. Neither puts God and His servant on the same level. God fixes the call; Moses accepts and obeys. Because the absence served their need for guidance, it should have led the people to wait faithfully. The promise was another gift, not the withdrawal of the care that had brought them through the sea.

That setting gives weight to the next turn: ***“then you worshipped the calf”***. The wrong was not simply the making of an object. The calf was taken as a god, and worship was directed to it. The Quran identifies the material used: (7:148 — **“his people made from their ˹golden˺ jewellery an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound”**). Al-Suddī’s account connects the gathered ornaments with the work of the Sāmirī, who shaped the calf and urged the people to treat it as their deity. The lowing drew their attention, but it could not prove divinity because a created creature can also make a sound. Mujāhid describes a hollow form through which wind passed; al-Ḥasan’s account understands a living calf. The rebuke does not depend on choosing an account of how the sound arose. Even a living creature is not its own creator. The question the Quran presses is whether the object could answer or govern their affairs: (20:89 — **“it did not respond to them, nor could it protect or benefit them”**). Neither its rich material nor its strange appearance supplied the knowledge, power, and guidance that belong to God. The people had received a promise of revelation, yet turned to something made from possessions they had brought with them. They treated an object within their reach as though it were the source of the rescue they had already received.

**A TEMPORARY ABSENCE DOES NOT REMOVE A KNOWN DUTY**

The words ***“in his absence”*** refer to Moses’ departure, not his death. They had reason to expect his return, and another prophet was present to warn them. Aaron told them, (20:90 — **“You are only being tested by this, for indeed your ˹one true˺ Lord is the Most Compassionate”**). He did not authorize the calf’s worship or regard it as another path to the same Lord. Their reply was a refusal to stop: (20:91 — **“We will not cease to worship it until Moses returns to us”**). Since the warning had already been given, waiting for Moses was not an innocent way of suspending judgement. It prolonged a practice they had been told to leave. The contrast also explains why Aaron’s part in the story matters. The people were not deprived of every voice of guidance when Moses went away. Aaron appealed to them, although he feared that a split would tear the people apart. His difficulty in ending their conduct does not turn his warning into approval of it. Because the duty came from God, its force did not depend on Moses remaining visibly present. Moses’ absence changed who was there to address them; it did not change who alone deserved their worship.

The final description is ***“acting wrongfully”***. It judges the act they were committing. Worship belongs to God, so transferring it to the calf placed devotion where it could not rightly belong. The same gravity appears in Luqmân’s warning, (31:13 — **“for associating ˹others with Him˺ is truly the worst of all wrongs”**). This was more than impatience with a timetable or a mistake about when Moses would return. The people had replaced the worship taught by the messenger with worship of a creature. They wronged themselves by that choice, despite the gifts that should have helped them recognize their Lord. The collective address cannot mean every person worshipped the calf, because Aaron’s opposition is explicit. Nor does it give later readers leave to turn a named wrong in this history into inherited guilt for every descendant. The Book distinguishes the acts of successive generations: (2:134 — **“For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned”**). The hearers are made to face their community’s past so that they do not repeat its refusal of guidance. The episode shows why witnessing rescue does not by itself ensure lasting obedience. A gift still calls for a faithful response, and a leader’s absence cannot make a clear wrong right.
