**THE ADDRESS TURNED ON THE BELIEVERS**

The verse turns from the objections of the others to a question put to the believers themselves, and the question is about an appetite: ***“Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger”***. The address is startling because the community had just been defended against those who wanted to turn it back, and now it is asked whether it shares the very habit that made the others refuse. Nothing in the sentence claims that the believers had already asked anything; what is asked about is intention, and a question about intention is a warning rather than an accusation. The verse is attached by Ibn ʿAbbās to the Muslims in Medina, after some of them had begun to press the Prophet for the kind of signs their predecessors had demanded. The point of the question is to catch the desire at its root, because a community that copies the demands of an earlier people will eventually copy their conclusion as well.

**WHAT MOSES' PEOPLE HAD ASKED BEFORE**

The question is made concrete by a comparison with an earlier community: ***“as Moses was asked before?”***. The reference is to the demands recorded of the people who followed Moses, who asked to see God and asked for revelations in writing, and the Book treats the pattern as a familiar one rather than as an isolated incident. Another sūrah records the same demand being made to the Messenger in his own time, which shows how direct the likeness was: (4:153 — **“bring down for them a revelation in writing from heaven”**). The question in this verse therefore does not need to describe the earlier demands in detail, because the audience knew what had become of the community that made them. What the reader is asked to see is that the appetite for proof is not new, and that the Book's answer to it has been consistent from the beginning.

**THE INSTRUCTION ABOUT ASKING**

The comparison carries a warning that the verse states separately in another place, where the believers are told to leave certain questions alone: (5:101 — **“Do not ask about any matter which, if made clear to you, may disturb you”**). The Book's concern is not with curiosity as such but with the demand that God answer on the questioner's terms, which is what turned an earlier generation's request into a refusal. The verse assumes that a question can be an act of faith or an act of bargaining, and the difference lies in whether the questioner is prepared to accept the answer. A community that puts its confidence in the fact that the proof is available will not need to keep asking for more of it, because the point of asking has more to do with the asker than with the evidence.

**A CUSTOMER WHO KEEPS ASKING FOR SAMPLES**

Think of a buyer who asks for a sample of every item in a shop and never carries one home, and who eventually explains that he had no intention of buying. The people of Moses asked for signs and kept asking, and the Book records that the request did not improve their position; the verse asks the believers whether they intend to walk the same road. The comparison is uncomfortable because the motive in the shop is at least honest, whereas the demand for proof often dresses itself as a search for certainty. Someone who has watched a person demand more and more evidence before committing to anything has seen how the appetite works, and the verse's question is aimed at exactly that appetite. What the sentence offers as an alternative is the acceptance of a messenger, which is the thing the demands were designed to postpone.

**THE TRADE THAT ENDS IN STRAYING**

The verse closes by naming the outcome of the posture it has described, and it uses the language of commerce again: ***“But whoever trades belief”*** and ***“for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way”***. The metaphor of trade has run through this part of the sūrah, and the verb used here describes an exchange in which the better article is handed over for the worse. The clause about straying is the natural conclusion of the metaphor, since a person who has left the road is not one who is still examining the route. The verse therefore treats the persistent demand for proof as a transaction with a known price rather than as a neutral enquiry, and the reader is warned about the trade before he makes it. What remains with the reader is the question the verse opened with, now sharpened: what exactly does he intend when he asks for more than he has been given?
