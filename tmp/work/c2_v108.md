**ASKING FOR THE OLD SPECTACLE**

The passage now files the demands themselves as evidence: ***“Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger as Moses was asked before? ”***. The received record names the demands twice over. In the city the askers wanted the size of the place enlarged and the hill of Ṣafā made of gold. From the scripture party came the demand for the whole revelation at once, the way the Torah had been said to arrive. The transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās gives the older version its full face. They asked their prophet to show them the Lord outright and to let them hear his speech. The demand of the present hour is therefore not new merchandise. It is the same request wearing the current city's clothes. The second gloss names the Meccan version without ceremony. The ask was spectacle. The answer at hand does not produce the spectacle. It prices the asking.

**THE PRECEDENT OF THE MOUNTAIN**

The comparison to Moses does its work by recalling how that asking ended. The children of Israel had asked their prophet the same kind of question, and the answer arrived as a mountain raised over the covenant-party: (7:171 — **“and they thought it would fall on them. ˹We said,˺ “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you”**). The precedent is instructive because of the shape of the exchange. The askers wanted God on their terms. The answer put the covenant back in their hands. The sign for the sincere and the spectacle for the demanding serve opposite ends. The sign settles the truth for the one ready to submit. The spectacle feeds the appetite that will ask again tomorrow. The received tradition of this passage notes that the demands of this period repeated the pattern exactly. The promise of the revelation would be held hostage to the theater.

**THE TRADE NAMED AT LAST**

The verse then drops the pricing hammer: ***“ But whoever trades belief for disbelief has truly strayed from the Right Way”***. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the straying in plain terms: the trader has relinquished the path of guidance. The verb of trading is the surah's own accounting word, the one used at the market stalls of the wicked. The trade of guidance for misguidance has already been itemized in this passage's ledger, and the naming there was clinical. The demand for spectacle is therefore not a neutral question at the counter. It is the offer of belief in exchange for a demonstration on the buyer's terms. The verse marks the offer as the trade it is. The revelation can be removed at will, and the power behind the removal was noted in this surah's own defense: (2:20 — **“and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything”**). The terms of the supply are set by the supplier. The demand that forgets this fact has already left the Right Way by the door marked disbelief.

**THE SELF-ANSWERING QUESTION**

The unit works because the demand is self-incriminating. The askers stand inside a tradition that records the mountain, the tablets, and the punishment of the askers, and then they ask again in the same shape. The reason the verse references the older episode is that the pattern needs no new refutation. The demand for proof on demand is the refusal of the proof already sent. The passage therefore files the question under the trade rather than under the inquiry, and the filing is precise. An inquiry opens the book. The demand opens the account. The reader who wants the doctrine in one line has it here. Revelation arrives by the schedule of the one who owns the demonstrating, and the schedule is set at the level of the ownership. Everything else is the marketplace of the spectacle. The trader leaves that market with the book under one arm and the wish under the other, and the wish is the heavier load by the verse's own accounting. The whole unit is therefore a price list with one entry. The spectacle costs the belief. The belief costs the spectacle. The exchange rate is published in the verse itself, and no third booth opens along the road. The list never changes its prices.
