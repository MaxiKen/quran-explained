**RESCUE FROM A RULE THAT MADE FAMILY LIFE UNSAFE**

The gifts just recalled now take the form of a particular rescue: ***“˹Remember˺ how We delivered you”*** ***“from the people of Pharaoh,”***. Pharaoh is the title of the Egyptian ruler in the story of Moses. His people here include the followers and agents who upheld his rule, not merely his close family. God brought the Israelites out from the power of that ruler and his servants. The later hearers are addressed through their people’s history: the rescue of their forefathers made their own continued life possible. But the main stress is now on the hand that saved them. The Lord who will judge them is also the Lord who broke a power they could not escape by themselves. His purpose is stated in the fuller account: (28:5 — **“But it was Our Will to favour those who were oppressed in the land,”**). Their enemies’ strength was real, yet it did not give those enemies the last word. To remember this rescue is therefore to remember whose gift freedom was. The following words explain how great the need for that rescue had been.

They were delivered from people ***“who afflicted you with dreadful torment,”***. The description covers sustained ill-treatment, not a single harsh encounter. Al-Suddī includes the degrading labour imposed on the Israelites, together with the killing of their sons. The regime made use of their bodies and skills while denying them safety in their homes. The killing that follows in the verse explains the dreadfulness of the torment; it is not a minor detail added to a vague complaint. The people carrying it out are also named as its agents. Pharaoh’s command does not make their hands disappear from the deed. Because the violence is attributed to those who perform it, service to a ruler is not a way to rename cruelty as innocence. This does not supply every rule by which a court must judge a case of coercion. It fixes the moral fact in the story: a ruler’s policy was carried into the lives of families by people who served him. The rescue ended subjection to that whole power, not merely dislike of one man’s title.

The horror includes ***“slaughtering your sons”***. An account from Ibn ʿAbbās relates that Pharaoh was warned of a boy to be born among the Israelites whose rise would end his rule. The killing was ordered in fear for the throne. That account explains the fear behind the policy; the verse itself lays the deed before the hearer. The sons are understood here as the male children, especially the newborn, rather than only grown men. The danger faced by Moses’ mother supports that reading. She is told, (28:7 — **““Nurse him, but when you fear for him, put him then into the river,”**). Because the threatened child was still nursing, the policy cannot be confined to the killing of adult men. God taught his mother how to preserve him. The effort to secure the throne thus struck at those least able to resist. Yet the child whose life Pharaoh sought to prevent was not beyond God’s protection. This makes the rescue more than escape from forced work. It preserved a people’s future against a policy that reached into the birth of their children.

The paired act is ***“and keeping your women.”***. Keeping them means leaving them alive, and the expression includes the girls who would grow into women as well as the women already living. It does not mean that they were left free or treated kindly. Ibn Jurayj connects their retention with enslavement. That describes the condition imposed upon them, while being kept alive is the immediate sense of the wording. The two sides of the policy served the same power: sons were slain and women were kept under the ruler’s control. When Moses later confronts Pharaoh, the threat is renewed with the stated aim, (7:127 — **““We will kill their sons and keep their women. We will completely dominate them.””**). The two explanations can stand together because both concern Pharaoh’s grip on power. The women’s survival was not a favour that could cancel the killing of the sons. A life preserved for service under terror remains a life in need of rescue. The verse holds the losses and the enforced survival together within the torment God ended.

**AFFLICTION AND RELEASE BOTH TEST THE RESPONSE**

The closing judgement begins, ***“That was a severe test”***. A test can come through pain, but it can also come through a gift. Ibn ʿAbbās explains the trial here as a blessing, directing attention to the rescue with which the verse begins. The nearer account of slaughter also makes the sense of a grievous ordeal plain. Since the verse recalls both their suffering and their rescue, the whole change of condition is treated here as a test. Patient endurance was not the only response required; freedom would also test their willingness to give thanks. The Quran states the principle, (21:35 — **“And We test you ˹O humanity˺ with good and evil as a trial,”**). Pain calls for steadfastness; good calls for thanks. Neither condition places the person outside duty to God. Relief is not simply the end of every test, since it brings a new question about the use of the gift. The story is recalled to people now possessing the fruit of rescue. Their answer must include faithfulness to the Lord who gave it, not only grief for what their forefathers suffered.

The trial is ***“from your Lord”***. God’s rule over the event does not make Pharaoh’s deed good. The fuller narrative gives the ruler a clear verdict: (28:4 — **“He was truly one of the corruptors”**). God can let a tyrant act for a time without granting him the right to act, and He can bring rescue from a danger that the tyrant meant to make final. These are different kinds of agency. The men who killed are responsible for killing; the Lord who tested and delivered His servants is not praised for their cruelty. Nor does the suffering described here prove that the sufferers were worthless before Him. The verse names their trial as severe and their rescue as His gift. The title of Lord thus holds together power and care. Their life was never beyond His reach, even while it lay under Pharaoh’s rule. Remembering the event should turn them toward the One who saved them and away from the habits of pride and domination from which they were saved. The next verse recalls the act that made the rescue complete.
