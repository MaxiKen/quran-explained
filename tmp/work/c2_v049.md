**THE YEARS UNDER A TYRANT, REMEMBERED BY THE ONE WHO ENDED THEM**

The chapter now walks the children of Israel through their own history, and it begins at the worst point: ***“˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh”***, ***“who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons”***, ***“and keeping your women.”***, and ***“That was a severe test from your Lord”***. The command to remember is placed on the rescue rather than on the suffering, which is the shape of the whole passage. The Book could have opened with the slavery, and instead it opens with the one who broke it. Even the final clause, which names the ordeal as a test, treats the test as something administered by the same Lord who is doing the delivering. The details of the oppression are reported without softening, because the Book wants the reader to know what the deliverance cost and what the alternative was. A regime that kills the sons of a people and keeps its daughters alive is managing a population rather than fighting a war, and the verse names the practice in a single clause. The same events are recounted in other sūrahs with more detail, and the pattern there is identical: the cruelty is stated, and the rescue is credited to God in the same breath. The reader is not being invited to admire a victim or to hate a nation; he is being taught to attribute his own escapes correctly.

**THE SEVERITY THAT CUT BOTH WAYS**

The phrase about a severe test is easy to misread as a comment on the suffering of the enslaved, as though the Egyptians were an instrument of divine discipline. What the sentence actually names is the whole situation, and its severity cuts both ways. For the people being oppressed, the test was whether they would keep their faith in a God whose help was invisible for generations. For their oppressors, the test was whether they would recognise the authority that had raised them and used them. The Book reports both outcomes, and the second one is the reason the next verse is about a sea. The word for the test in this line is the same word the sūrah used when it spoke about the challenge of the Book and about the parable of the mosquito. In every case the test is a situation in which a person's direction is revealed, not a random misfortune. That consistency matters to a reader who is carrying his own trial: the Book treats hardship as a place where something is being decided, and it treats the outcome of the deciding as the part that lasts. The severe test in Egypt is named after the rescue rather than before it, which tells the reader where the sentence about hardship is going.

**READING A DELIVERANCE THAT HAPPENED LONG AGO**

For a reader who is not a member of that community, the verse asks him to look at his own escapes and to get the attribution right. Every life contains a few moments where the wrong thing was expected and the right thing happened: the illness that turned, the door that opened, the bullet that missed. The Book's habit is to name those moments as the favours of God, and the command to remember is aimed at exactly that. The point is not to replay the past but to keep a person from crediting his own cleverness for the ground he is standing on. A reader can put the verse to work by writing down one rescue from his own history and naming who arranged it. That is the same act the children of Israel were asked to perform with the exodus, and it is available to anyone with a memory. The Book wants the entry recorded correctly rather than a mood replayed, because a person who has forgotten who brought him out of Egypt will not recognise the hand that is carrying him now.
