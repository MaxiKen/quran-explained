This is the longest sūrah of the Book, and the first of the sūrahs revealed in Madinah in the order of the written text. It came down over a long stretch of the Prophet's life there, verse by verse and passage by passage, as the community in that city took shape and needed instruction, correction and law. The name it carries comes from a story told inside it, where Moses orders his people to slaughter a cow and they argue over which one was meant; the story became the sūrah's title because it captures so much of what the sūrah does. A people who had the truth in front of them kept asking for more detail until the simple command turned heavy, and the sūrah is full of that pattern. It opens with three letters, then divides all of humanity into those who believe, those who deny, and those who pretend, and it settles the foundation on which the rest of the Book stands.

The sūrah is not a single discourse but a set of them that belong to one purpose. It lays out who God is, what revelation is, and what the human being owes, and then it walks through the history of a community that was given the same trust before and lost its way. The story of the children of Israel runs through a great part of it, told as a warning and as a lesson for the new community, and the sūrah pauses to record the building of the House by Abraham and Ishmael, the prayer they made for a messenger to come from their descendants, and the answering of that prayer in the Prophet who recites these verses. From there the sūrah turns to law: fasting, prayer, alms, trade, debt, marriage, divorce, war and peace, so that the reader meets in one place both what to believe and how to live, and the two are held together as one subject rather than two.

Several of the Book's most particular treasures sit inside this sūrah. The greatest verse of the Book is here, the verse of the Throne, and so is the longest verse, which sets out the writing of a debt so that the rights of people are not lost. The change of direction in prayer is recorded here, and the fast of the month is commanded here, and the closing verses are a prayer of a community that God Himself taught to speak. The Prophet is remembered as saying that the two brightest sūrahs are al-Baqarah and Āl ʿImrān, and a report carried by Muslim has Abū Hurayrah report that he warned the community not to turn their homes into graves, because the house in which al-Baqarah is recited is not entered by the deceiver. Another report, in the collection of al-Nasāʾī, has Ibn Masʿūd report that the deceiver leaves a house in which al-Baqarah is heard. Those reports place the sūrah in the middle of ordinary life, in a house and in a family, and not only in the study of a scholar.

The opening of the sūrah teaches the reader how to read it. Three letters come first, unexplained, so that the student learns from the very beginning that some of what God has sent down is his to measure and some of it is his to receive. Then the sūrah names the people it is writing for and the people it is warning, and it does not flatter either group. The believers are described by what they do and not by what they say; the deniers are told plainly that no argument will reach them; the pretenders are given the longest description of the three, because they are the hardest to see and the most dangerous to the community. What follows is a sūrah that a reader can spend a life inside, and the commentary on this chapter takes it verse by verse, beginning with the letters that stand at its head.
