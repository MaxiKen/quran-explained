Al-Baqarah is the longest sūrah in the Qur'an and the first great body of Madinan instruction in the written Book. Its passages came across different moments of the new community's life, yet they form one sustained education. The sūrah moves from faith to worship, from memory to law, and from private conscience to public obligation. It addresses people who believe, people who reject faith, and people who hide rejection behind religious speech. It then turns at length to the Children of Israel, not to make their history a distant spectacle, but to show the new community how a people may receive revelation and still weaken its force through evasion, selective obedience, or argument. Its title comes from the command to slaughter a cow in verses 67–73. That episode gives the chapter a fitting name: a direct command becomes difficult when submission is replaced by delay, needless questions, and reluctance to act.

The sūrah answers the prayer that closes al-Fātiḥah. The worshipper has asked to be guided along the straight path; al-Baqarah opens by identifying the Book as guidance and then describes the people who can receive it. That connection governs the chapter. Guidance is not presented as information alone. It appears as belief in the unseen, prayer, generosity, patience, fasting, pilgrimage, just trade, careful debt, family responsibility, restraint in conflict, and repentance after failure. Abraham and Ishmael raise the foundations of the Sacred House and ask for a messenger among their descendants. The direction of prayer changes toward that House, making bodily worship declare a community's covenant. The history of earlier revelation and the law of the present community therefore belong to one argument: God guides through truth received inwardly and obeyed in visible life.

Its legal range is unusually broad because the community being formed needed more than private counsel. The chapter treats food, retaliation, wills, fasting, pilgrimage, marriage, divorce, nursing, spending, warfare, oaths, usury, contracts, witnesses, and secured loans. Yet law never becomes detached administration. Commands are repeatedly tied to mindfulness of God, the final return, mercy, and the danger of concealing truth. The longest verse in the Qur'an records a debt so that memory and power do not erase another person's right. The Verse of the Throne places every practical duty beneath God's life, knowledge, and rule. The final verses return the whole chapter to faith and prayer. In this way, the sūrah carries a society from its creed to its contracts and then brings it back before its Lord.

The Prophet gave this long chapter a place in ordinary homes. Abū Hurayrah reports his instruction, *"Do not turn your houses into graves"*, followed by the reason that the deceiver does not enter a house where Sūrah al-Baqarah is recited (Muslim). Abū Umāmah reports another instruction to recite it because taking hold of it is a blessing and abandoning it is a cause of regret (Muslim). These reports do not turn recitation into sound without obedience. They place revelation where people sleep, trade, disagree, raise children, and make decisions. The protection promised to a house belongs with a chapter that reforms what happens inside houses and beyond them. Its opening letters first teach restraint before hidden meaning; its next verses teach certainty, reverence, and action. The commentary therefore begins where the sūrah begins, with three recited letters that are fully preserved even though their final meaning has not been disclosed.
