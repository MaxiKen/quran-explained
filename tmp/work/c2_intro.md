This sūrah is the longest in the Book, and it is the first that was revealed after the community had become a community, with a city, a treasury, a law, and neighbours. Its two hundred and eighty-six verses are mostly addressed to the children of Israel, whose history occupies the earlier half, and to the new believers, who are given the same lessons in the second. The name al-Baqarah, the Cow, comes from an exchange in the middle of it that is examined in its place. What holds the whole chapter together is a single question, and the question is stated in its first sentence: who reads this Book properly, and what happens to the person who does not. Everything else in the chapter, from the descriptions of the three kinds of reader to the long legal passages at its centre, is an answer to that question delivered in stages.

The chapter opens where the previous sūrah left off. In al-Fātiḥah, the reader asks for guidance; here, the answer to the prayer is placed in his hands, and the rest of the sūrah shows what a person usually does with it. Three kinds of reader are described in the first twenty verses, and the description is followed by an invitation to everyone, a challenge to anyone who doubts, and then a long account of the first human household and its failings. By the time the book reaches its laws on fasting, marriage, debt, and war, the reader has been told who he is and what the Book expects to find in him, and those laws are easier to understand once the groundwork is in place.

Al-Baqarah is also the sūrah that most clearly carries the community's own history. The exodus from Egypt, the crossing of the sea, the forty nights on the mountain, the calf, the water from the rock, the shade and the manna: each of these is recounted as a favour received and as a failure that followed it. Then comes Abraham, who built the House with his son and asked for a community to come from his descendants, and the account of the qiblah, which turns the community toward the place Abraham had built. The chapter closes with a prayer placed in the mouth of the believers, so that a sūrah which began with a description of guidance ends with the guided asking to be kept on the road.

The chapter proceeds by argument rather than by announcement, and its method is worth noticing before a reader begins. It states a claim, places the evidence for it in front of the reader, and then describes what people do with the evidence. The parables of the mosquito and the fire are used for that purpose, and so are the challenges to produce a sūrah like this one and the accounts of past communities. A reader who expects a list of rules will find the rules interspersed with long passages of reasoning, and the reasoning is not decoration; it is the Book teaching a community how to think about the claims that will be made on it, and about the claims the community will make on itself.

The sūrah also repeats itself, and the repetition is deliberate. The address to the children of Israel begins twice, the description of the believers is echoed later, and the warning about the Day when no soul can help another is given more than once. Each repetition arrives after new material has been placed beside it, so the same sentence lands differently the second time it is heard. A reader who notices the pattern will stop treating the repetitions as redundancy and start using them as signposts, since the Book returns to a statement when it is about to build on it, and each return carries more weight than the last.

A sūrah of this length is best read in stretches, and the stretches are provided by the text itself. The opening twenty verses form one unit, the account of the covenant and the exodus forms another, the legal material in the middle is grouped by subject, and the closing passages return to the themes of faith and prayer. A reader who takes a passage at a sitting and finishes it will find that the chapter's arguments are delivered in complete units rather than scattered through it, since the Book was recited to a community over years and the units it was recited in are still visible. What the reader should carry into the chapter is the disposition the Book has already asked for: the humility of a creature who has been taught, patience with the difficulty that arrives without warning, the certainty that the meeting is coming, and the willingness to be corrected by what he reads.
