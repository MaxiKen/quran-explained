**A CONVERSATION ANNOUNCED BEFORE THE HUMAN WAS MADE**

The sūrah now opens a scene in the unseen: ***“˹Remember˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.” They asked ˹Allah˺, “Will You place in it someone”***, ***“who will spread corruption there”***, ***“and shed blood while we glorify Your praises”***, ***“and proclaim Your holiness?””***, and ***“Allah responded, “I know what you do not know.””***. The instruction to remember tells the reader that he is being shown something he could not have observed, since it happened among the angels before there was a human being to discuss the matter. The Book gives him this glimpse at the start of a long section about the children of Israel and about the human capacity for both justice and ruin, and everything in those later verses plays out inside the answer given here.

The angels' question deserves attention because it is not a protest. They name the two crimes that will fill the earth, corruption and bloodshed, and they compare them with their own continuous praise, and then they leave the matter where it belongs. The Book reports the question without criticising it, which is part of the scene's power. A reader who has ever asked why a good God allows damage will find his question already placed in the mouths of the angels and already answered with a sentence that does not explain the plan but claims the authority to keep it. The answer is not information; it is a relationship, and the Book leaves it in exactly that form. The authority later given to David is described in the same terms elsewhere: (38:26 — **“˹We instructed him:˺ “O David! We have surely made you an authority in the land,”**), and the words that follow bind the authority to truth rather than to appetite.

**AN ANSWER THAT SETTLES THE POSITION**

The five words at the end of the verse settle the position rather than the puzzle. What the angels are told is that the speaker knows something they do not, and the verse makes no attempt to list it. That reticence teaches the reader how to hold everything that follows: the story of Adam, the testing of the Israelites, the laws given to an entire community, and the failure and repentance that run through the chapter. None of it is arbitrary, and none of it is fully visible from inside a single life. The Book is not asking a person to understand the whole design before he takes part in it, and it never supplies that understanding to anyone. It is asking him to accept that the design is held by the one who made it. The Book uses the same scene in several places, and the wording varies with the point being made. In one account the angels are told about a creation from sounding clay: (15:28 — **“˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when your Lord said to the angels,”**), and in another the attention moves to the command to prostrate and the refusal of Iblīs: (7:11 — **“Surely We created you, then shaped you, then said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but”**). Read together with this verse, the scenes fill in one another: the appointment of a representative, the making of his body, and the fall of the one who would not bow.

**WHAT IT MEANS TO BE AN AUTHORITY ON EARTH**

The word for the position given to the human being is the word for a successor, someone who comes after and takes charge of a place. That is the frame the sūrah gives for every reader: he is not the owner of what he stands on, and he is not a visitor with no business there either. He is a caretaker with a mandate, and the mandate is what makes the story of the next verses so serious, since the same fact that gives him the earth also gives him the ability to ruin part of it. The angels' two words describe what happens when a caretaker forgets his brief, and the answer's last words describe who is still watching the estate. A reader can act on the verse this week by treating one piece of the earth in his care the way a steward would: a plot of land, a room in a house, a job that affects other people, a body that belongs to its maker. The Book does not ask anyone to resolve the question of why corruption is permitted. It places the reader inside the answer instead, with a mandate to make one small part of the earth better than he found it on the day he was handed it.
