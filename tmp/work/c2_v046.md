**CONVICTION BEFORE THE APPOINTED DAY ARRIVES**

The humble people of the preceding verse are now described as ***“those who are certain”***. Their prayer is sustained by a conviction about where life leads. In Arabic, the expression used here can carry either firm conviction or supposition; the setting decides the sense. Al-Suddī explains it as certainty. Ibn Zayd adds that the event has not yet been seen, but that this does not make their belief a doubtful guess. They trust the Lord’s account of what is coming. The same use appears in the words of the person who receives his record with joy: (69:20 — **“I surely knew I would face my reckoning.””**). He had lived in expectation of the reckoning before it became visible. This is why the belief changes the weight of present duties. Prayer is not wasted time if the one performing it knows that he will stand before its Lord. Nor is avoiding a forbidden gain a loss with no further account. The future is not yet present to the senses, but it is already part of the believer’s judgement about what matters. Certainty concerns God’s promise, not a boast that the believer has already secured a favourable verdict for himself.

What they know is ***“that they will meet their Lord”***. The meeting includes standing before God to answer for life and deeds. It is not confined to the humble, although they are the people who prepare for it. The Quran addresses humanity as a whole: (84:6 — **“O humanity! Indeed, you are labouring restlessly towards your Lord, and will ˹eventually˺ meet the consequences”**). That common end makes the present contrast serious. A person may deny the reckoning, but denial does not remove him from it. In al-Bukhārī and Muslim, ʿAdiyy ibn Ḥātim reports the Prophet’s teaching that each person will be addressed by his Lord without an interpreter between them. Looking around, the person finds the deeds he has sent ahead; the warning then urges protection from the Fire even through giving half a date. The small gift matters because the future account reaches the acts that may seem too slight for notice now. Neither a spokesman nor public rank can stand in for the person’s own response. The humble therefore learn to place even ordinary choices within that account. They do not wait for the appointed day before treating it as real, and they do not suppose that a respected place among people guarantees a safe place before God.

**HOPE DOES NOT REQUIRE FORGETTING THE ACCOUNT**

Meeting the Lord is not presented only as a terror for the believer. The Quran joins its warning with encouragement: (2:223 — **“Be mindful of Allah, and know that you will meet Him. And give good news to the believers”**). The divine title recalls the One who has nourished and guided His servants, not a judge unaware of their need. In the report from Ibn ʿUmar carried by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, the Prophet describes God shielding a believer while bringing his sins before him. After the believer admits them, God declares that He concealed them in worldly life and now forgives them. That pardon is mercy, not proof that the sins were never wrong. Some early readings also connect the meeting with the honour of seeing God, for the blessed faces are described as (75:23 — **“looking at their Lord”**). That special gift must not be confused with the bare fact that all people return for judgement. The verse’s immediate point is the certainty that shapes worship; the other texts show why hope belongs beside fear. Since the Lord can forgive what no creature can erase, the servant has reason to approach Him rather than avoid His command. Trust does not presume upon pardon, and fear does not make sincere obedience pointless.

The closing assurance is ***“and to Him they will return”***. Abū al-ʿĀliyah understands this as their return on the Day of Resurrection. Death begins the journey out of earthly life, but the return here includes being raised and brought to judgement. The earlier account makes that order plain: (2:28 — **“then He will cause you to die and again bring you to life, and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned”**). The second raising cannot be reduced to the first death. All affairs reach God’s judgement, and no power claimed in this world can make that end belong to someone else. The return also explains why the two clauses are joined. They will meet the Lord, and their final disposal is His; the appointment is not one encounter after which they pass beyond His authority. Because that is their settled expectation, worship gains a lasting purpose even when its present cost is felt. A tiring day, a hidden sacrifice, or an unanswered desire does not become the whole story. The humble can endure without claiming to control the outcome. Their Lord knows what they have done and will judge it justly. Thus belief in the return gives prayer both seriousness and hope: the act is offered now to the One before whom the servant will finally stand.
