**CHARITY MUST REMAIN A GIFT**

***“Those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah and”*** establishes charity must remain a gift. This verse gives the conditions that preserve the reward of charity. The giver spends for Allah and does not follow the gift with mann, reminding the recipient of what was given, or adhā, words and actions that cause hurt. A donation can be physically helpful yet spiritually corrupted if the giver later turns it into a chain around the recipient’s neck. Did you not remember what I did for you. Without me you would have nothing. Everyone should know that I supported you. Such words can destroy dignity and create dependence. Giving should leave the recipient grateful to Allah rather than permanently humiliated before the donor. The reward is with their Lord. People may forget the gift, reject the giver, or criticize the way it was offered. Allah does not lose the sincere act. The phrase also teaches the giver not to demand repayment from the recipient. He seeks his account with Allah, whose reward is safer and more generous than human gratitude. This does not mean that a charity may be hidden when public reporting is needed or that a donor cannot keep lawful records. Transparency protects trust. The danger is publicity or reminder used to establish superiority and control rather than accountability. The promise of no fear and no grief belongs to the Hereafter and can also bring tranquillity in this life. **THE REWARD IS WITH ALLAH**

***“do not follow their charity with reminders of their generosity or”*** deepens the reward is with allah. The giver who trusts Allah is less fearful of poverty and less grieved when worldly recognition does not come. He knows that the real transaction has not been lost. The promise does not guarantee that a generous person will never face anxiety or sadness. Believers can experience both while remaining sincere. It promises that Allah will protect them from the final fear and grief of abandonment when they meet Him. The recipient has dignity before he receives the gift. He is not a project for the donor’s image or a prop in a publicity campaign. A Muslim asks what form of assistance preserves privacy and independence. A cash transfer may be better than a public food distribution; a loan may be appropriate in one case and a grant in another; training may be more beneficial than a one-time payment. The Qur’an’s guidance is adaptable, but the moral rule is firm: do not make a person pay emotionally for receiving help. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the upper hand is better than the lower hand while also protecting the recipient from humiliation. Sincerity—ikhlāṣ—is not a feeling that needs to be announced. A giver can examine whether he would still give if no one knew, whether he is angry when the recipient cannot praise him, and whether he uses charity to gain political or personal influence. (2:215 — **“They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say,”**)

**NO FEAR AND NO GRIEF**

***“hurtful words—they will get their reward from their Lord, and”*** clarifies no fear and no grief. If the intention becomes mixed, he renews it rather than abandoning all good. Public charity can be good when it encourages others, funds a visible project, or fulfils a public duty. It should be accompanied by humility and accurate reporting, not by boasting or pressure. The verse also covers what happens months or years after a gift. A donor may later use past charity to demand obedience, win a family dispute, or silence criticism. Such conduct reveals that the gift was treated as a purchase. The recipient remains free to make lawful choices and offer advice; charity does not make him the donor’s property. If hurtful words have already been spoken, repentance includes apologizing, stopping the reminders, and restoring the recipient’s dignity. Allah’s mercy remains open. Think of charity must remain a gift through a man who pays the school fees of his brother's children and then tells the story at every wedding in the family for ten years. The children finish their schooling, the story is worn thin by the telling, and the brother's table is the one place where the man is never seated at ease. Charity must remain a gift confronts the reward is with allah in a reader who has helped a relative through a hard year and cannot stop mentioning the help at every gathering. The verse gives him a gift, a silence after it and a reward kept by his Lord; no fear and no grief asks, through charity must remain a gift, which of the two payments he would rather have. (76:8 — **“and give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive”**)
