**GOOD NEWS ANSWERS THE WARNING WITHOUT WEAKENING IT**

The Fire in the last verse does not fill the whole view. God then commands, ***“Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those”*** ***“who believe and do good”***. Fear shows a road to ruin, while good news gives the heart a home to seek. The Prophet must announce that home before his hearers can see it. Faith receives the promise as true. Good deeds show that faith has begun to guide the body, wealth, speech, and daily choices. This is why the two belong together rather than competing for the same place. Deeds do not buy Paradise at a price equal to it, for lasting bliss is a gift far beyond a person’s work. Yet inward belief is not an excuse for a barren life. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the good works as acts of obedience that bind servants to their Lord. Such acts make the outer life agree with the heart. This is the opposite of the split just exposed in the hypocrites. Their words and aims pulled apart; here trust and action meet. The same pair appears in the promise, (18:107 — **“Indeed, those who believe and do good will have the Gardens of Paradise as an accommodation”**). This matters because Quranic hope is not a wish that leaves conduct untouched. It is news believed so firmly that a person starts walking toward its truth now.

**THE GARDEN IS A HOME OF LIFE, NOT A THIN IDEA**

The news is ***“that they will have Gardens under”*** ***“which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,””***. The plural presents plenty rather than one small plot. Running water adds life, ease, beauty, and a sure supply. “Under” need not mean that the water is hidden beneath the soil. Early readings place the rivers below the trees and homes, or flowing through the Gardens. This detail would speak with special force to people who knew dry land and rare surface water. The point is that the waters belong to the Garden’s visible life and ease, not that they must be buried from view. Ibn ʿAbbās names rivers of water, milk, honey, and wine. A fuller description mentions (47:15 — **“that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink,”**). The picture should not be emptied into a mere symbol, as if bodily joy were too low for God to give. Yet earthly life does not set the limit of what Paradise can be. Familiar words give a true promise that people can grasp, while its final form is greater than present thought. A flowing river in dry country can mean relief, safety, food, and rest at once. The Garden holds all these goods without dirt, fear, unfair control, or loss. God does not promise bare life after judgment. He promises a home where each sign of life comes from a Giver whose care never runs out.

**RESEMBLANCE MAKES EACH GIFT BOTH WELCOME AND NEW**

The residents remember an earlier gift, ***“for they will be served fruit”*** ***“that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses”***. Early readers gave two main senses to “before.” It may recall fruit known in this world, or an earlier serving within Paradise. Both readings join recognition to fresh wonder. The difference between the readings is the remembered setting, not the promise that each new gift surpasses what was known. The fruit looks known in colour, shape, or kind, yet its taste is new. Ibn ʿAbbās explains the likeness through the same appearance with a different taste. The person can therefore welcome what is offered without already knowing all that it will give. Paradise avoids two limits of earthly pleasure. It is neither strange in a way that keeps joy at a distance nor repeated in a way that wears joy out. The gift can be known and still surprise. Its fruit is also (56:33 — **“never out of season nor forbidden—”**). No one waits in fear for the crop to fail. No owner locks the best part away. No last piece brings the good season to an end. This promise can train gratitude now. Common mercies often become hard to see because their names repeat: water, bread, shelter, a friend, another morning. Yet a repeated kind is not the same gift. It comes in a new hour, meets a present need, and gives one more chance to know and thank the Provider.

**PURITY AND PERMANENCE REMOVE THE SHADOW FROM JOY**

The promised companionship is pure. The words about spouses remove bodily filth, hurt, spite, betrayal, and each flaw that makes closeness unsafe. Early explanations mention freedom from menstruation and other impurity, but the broad promise is not confined to one bodily point. It presents a bond free from whatever spoils love or peace. The Quran also speaks of people entering (13:23 — **“the Gardens of Eternity, which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses,”**). Paradise is not a lonely feast or selfish desire cut loose from faithful ties. Its close bonds are healed under God’s pleasure. Then the last phrase makes every other gift secure: ***“and they will be there forever”***. A fine home can be darkened by the thought that it will decay. Love can ache under the fear of parting. Food ends, health fails, and every earthly stay must close. None of those shadows crosses this home. This is why forever changes the quality of every gift named before it, not just the length of the stay. Ibn ʿAbbās explains that its people neither die nor face exile. Another verse says, (15:48 — **“No fatigue will touch them there, nor will they ever be asked to leave”**). Life there does not grow stale. Weariness belongs to frail hearts and goods that lose their power to please. The Garden joins renewed gifts, clean hearts, and God’s endless grace. A brief good deed is not a poor exchange when the promised home never fades and never sends its people away.
