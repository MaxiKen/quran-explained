**THE GOOD NEWS THAT ANSWERS THE WARNING**

Good news stands directly across from the warning about the Fire: ***“Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those who believe and do good”***. The two marks are named together, faith and the work that follows it, which is the same pairing the sūrah used at its head. Ibn ʿAbbās read the believing as faith in the messenger and the Book, and the good work as the obedience that binds a person to his Lord, while others read the second phrase as covering every righteous act a person performs. The Book places the promise here immediately after the threat of the fire, so the two ends can be weighed as the two prices of the same choice. The news is commanded before it is described, which means the messenger is told to carry it to the people who earned it, and the sūrah treats the announcement itself as part of the mercy.

**THE GARDENS AND THE RIVERS BENEATH THEM**

The promise is then given its shape: ***“that they will have Gardens under which rivers flow.”***. The image is a garden with water running beneath the trees, and the early teachers named the rivers as water, milk, honey and wine, which the Book lists in a later sūrah where the whole garden is described: (47:15 — **“in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste”**). The reason the promise is given in the language of a garden is that the first hearers lived in a country where water made the difference between life and death, and the Book speaks to them in the terms of the thing they most desired. The wording about the rivers describes them as flowing rather than as standing, which means the garden is not a single gift received once but a provision that keeps arriving. A reader who has seen a stream run through a dry valley knows why the picture was chosen for the reward.

**THE FRUIT THAT LOOKS FAMILIAR AND TASTES NEW**

The verse then describes a conversation inside the garden: ***“Whenever provided with fruit, they will say,”***, ***“This is what we were given before,”***. The sentence records the surprise of people who recognise the shape of what they are handed and find the taste beyond anything they knew. The verse explains the recognition in the words that follow: ***“for they will be served fruit”***, ***“that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺.”*** Ibn ʿAbbās explained the resemblance as a likeness in appearance and colour and not in taste, and another reading applies it to the fruits of this world which the people of the garden remember as similar in form while the new ones exceed them. The Book states the same abundance in a description that closes the picture with a promise that nothing there runs out: (13:35 — **“eternal is its fruit as well as its shade”**). The reason the sentence is placed here is that a reward a person cannot imagine is a reward he cannot want, so the Book grants the familiar form and keeps the newness in the taste. A reader who has tasted two kinds of the same fruit will understand the arrangement, since the eye is satisfied by the look and the tongue is left with the surprise.

**THE SPOUSES AND THE FOREVER**

The verse finishes with two more gifts: ***“They will have pure spouses, and they will be there forever”***. The purity is explained by the early teachers as a freedom from everything that spoils company in this life, and the permanence is stated as plainly as the rest of the promise. The Book uses the same word for the lasting stay in several places, and the sūrah makes it the last note of the good news so that the reader ends the sentence with the thought that nothing he receives there will be lost. Think of a guest who is told at the door that the meal is ready and that the room he is given is his for good; the second half of that sentence is what makes the first half safe to enjoy. These days a person holds every good thing in his life with the knowledge that it will end, which is the reason the promise ends on the word forever, and the reader is invited to want a gift that no loss can touch.
