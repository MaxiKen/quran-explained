**GOOD NEWS ANSWERING THE FIRE**

Having named the fire, the sūrah immediately names the alternative, and it does so as an announcement: ***“Give good news ˹O Prophet˺ to those”***, ***“who believe and do good”***, ***“that they will have Gardens under”***, ***“which rivers flow. Whenever provided with fruit, they will say, “This is what we were given before,””***, ***“for they will be served fruit”***, ***“that looks similar ˹but tastes different˺. They will have pure spouses”***, and ***“and they will be there forever”***. The two qualifications are stated at the front, belief and good conduct, and everything that follows is described in the language of a place a person can picture. Gardens, rivers, fruit, and company: the reward is presented in the terms of the life the audience already knew, raised to a level that keeps surprising them.

The strangest detail in the passage is the one about the fruit. The people of the Garden are served something they recognise, and they say that this is what they were given before, and then they are given more that looks the same but tastes different. The wording keeps the pleasure of recognition and adds the pleasure of discovery at the same time. A reader who has tasted a familiar fruit in a new country will have felt exactly this. The Book elsewhere tells the same story in a longer description of the rivers of Paradise: (47:15 — **“The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water,”**), and the pattern repeats there too, with each river named and each delight specified.

**THE TWO CONDITIONS AT THE DOOR**

The verse attaches the promise to two things, and both are ordinary enough to be tested this week. Belief is the settled confidence the sūrah has already described in its opening verses, and doing good is the conduct that follows from it in public and in private. Neither condition is a performance standard, and neither is measured by how impressive a person appears to his neighbours. What the pair does is rule out the two easiest mistakes: a conviction that never reaches the hands, and a busy charity that never touches the heart. The chapter will return to this pairing many times, and each time it will place the two together, because the Book treats them as one thing seen from two sides.

The Book gives the same promise in a shorter form elsewhere, and the additional phrase there fills in what this verse leaves to the imagination: (4:57 — **“As for those who believe and do good, We will admit them into Gardens under which rivers flow,”**), with the promise of permanence and pure companions following in the next clause. Read side by side, the two verses describe one destination from two angles, and the repetition across the sūrahs means the reader will meet it often. The Book does not treat the reward as a footnote to the warnings. It places the good news on the same page as the fire, in the next sentence, so that the two are heard together.

**WHERE THE ANNOUNCEMENT IS MEANT TO GO**

The word used at the start of the verse is the word for announcing news, and news is meant to be passed on. A person who has received this announcement is being told something worth carrying to somebody who has not heard it, and the sūrah has already established who is doing the carrying: the community that believes and acts. That is why the description is given in such concrete terms. It is easier to invite a person to a garden with rivers and fruit than to an abstraction, and the Book intends the invitation to be made by ordinary people in ordinary conversation. The reader is not being handed a private comfort; he is being given the content of a message. For anyone who wants the promise to become more than a nice idea, the two conditions make an examination possible today. A reader can ask what he actually believes when the cost arrives, and what good he has done with his hands in the last week, and the answers will be specific. Both can be worked on in the same afternoon, since belief is strengthened by the habits the sūrah named earlier and good conduct begins with the nearest person who needs something. The announcement in this verse is addressed to people who are doing that work, and the permanence it promises is the reason the work is worth continuing when the results are not visible yet.
