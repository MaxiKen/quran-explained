**THE DISPUTE PUT IN ITS PLACE**

The verse takes the argument running through the passage and reshapes it. ***“Say, “Would you dispute with us about Allah”*** ***“while He is our Lord”*** ***“and your Lord?”***. The question is not who has the better claim. It is whose Lord is in question. The answer collapses the two sides into one sovereignty. The parallel invitation states the same ground in its constructive form (3:64 — **“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Let us come to common terms: that we will worship none but Allah,”**). Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Mujāhid keep the dispute inside the same frame as the colouring-verse. The way is His. The ordaining is His. The disputing is therefore conducted on His ground. So the verse does not forbid the dispute. It relocates it. Everything said must be said before the one Lord both parties already name. The reason the possessive is doubled is that the common ground is the whole court. Our Lord and your Lord. The verse is a rule of procedure as much as a creed.

**THE LEDGER OF THE DEEDS SPLIT**

The verse then settles the accounting exactly as the passed-community verse did. ***“We are accountable for our deeds”*** ***“and you for yours.”***. The splitting of the deeds is the same boundary drawn a second time. This time in the middle of a live dispute. The reading of Ibn ʿAbbās leaves the clause as the standing rule of argument. The deeds stay with their makers. The disputing cannot move them. The effect is that the argument loses its favourite weapon. The trading of ancestries. The charging of one generation's file to another. The difference between a dispute about truth and a dispute about records is what the verse marks. The truth is argued before the Lord. The records are closed per person. What follows is that the disputers learn where their energy will actually be spent. Not on moving the ledger. On filling it. The reason the verse splits the deeds in the middle of the dispute is that the split is the mercy. Everyone gets to work with what is still open.

**THE DEVOTION THAT CLOSES THE CASE**

The verse ends not on a verdict but on a posture. ***“And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺”***. The devotion closes the case the way the submission closed the roll of names. The parallel's summons runs to the same ending (3:64 — **“that we have submitted ˹to Allah alone˺.””**). Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Mujāhid keep the colouring and the devotion in one image. The one who wears the religion's colour devotes the worship to the one who ordained the colour. The point is that the last word is not about winning the dispute. It is about the devotion that outlives it. For the modern reader the ending is the instruction this age most needs. Arguments end. The posture continues. The posture is what the ledger records. The effect of the close is that the disputers stand with their own deeds, their own Lord, and their own devotion. The three together are enough to live on. The reason the verse stops on the devotion rather than the argument is that the argument was only ever the doorway. The case closes in the posture. It does not close in the last word. The relocation of the dispute changes its temperature. The argument is no longer between enemies. It is conducted before a shared Lord. The shared Lord hears both sides in one ear. The deeds then split the case into two files. Each file has one name on it. The name is the maker's. The dispute cannot move the entries between them. The devotion at the end closes the case without a verdict. The posture outlives the argument. The argument is a doorway. The posture is the room. A modern reader arguing across a divide can follow the verse's procedure. Keep the shared Lord visible. Keep the deeds separate. Close in a posture. The procedure is old and it is undefeated. The defeated thing is the appetite for prosecution. The appetite wants the other file. The verse returns every maker to their own. The return is merciful. The devotion named last is the only part of the exchange that survives it. The survival is the point. The case is closed in worship.
