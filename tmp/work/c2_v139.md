**A DISPUTE THE MESSENGER IS TOLD TO ANSWER**

A question placed in the Messenger's mouth turns the argument back on those who raised it: ***“Say, “Would you dispute with us about Allah”***. The dispute in view was about the choice of a messenger, and the reply begins by asking on what ground the objection could stand. The account that carries Ibn ʿAbbās's name traces the objection to the two communities and to those who thought a prophet should not be raised from among the Arabs, which is the ground the question removes. The sūrah has been answering claims made by the two communities about their own standing, and this sentence changes the subject from inheritance to the relation between God and both parties. The question is asked for the reader's benefit as much as for the disputants', since the reader is invited to see how little there is to argue about once the relation is stated. A reader who has been drawn into a quarrel about who is favoured will find the sentence moving the ground under the quarrel.

**THE LORD WHO BELONGS TO BOTH PARTIES**

The question then supplies the ground of its own answer: ***“while He is our Lord”*** and ***“and your Lord?”***. The clause places the two disputing sides under a single lordship, which removes the premise that one of them could own the deity or the messenger. The Book's argument here is not that the believers have a better claim to God but that the claim to exclusive possession is incoherent, since a lord who is the lord of both sides cannot be a party's private patron. The two names are placed in the same sentence without a rank between them, and the even-handedness is the whole point of the clause. Nothing in the sentence claims a superior position for the believers, which is what makes the reply to the dispute so difficult to answer. What the reader should notice is that the argument is settled before the next clause arrives.

**DEEDS THAT BELONG TO THEIR OWNERS**

The sentence continues with a statement about accountability, and it distributes the responsibility in two halves: ***“We are accountable for our deeds”*** and ***“and you for yours.”***. Throughout this sūrah a claim about standing is answered with a statement about deeds, and the sentence here does it in six words. The measure is applied to both sides equally, which is what makes it an answer rather than an assertion of superiority. Another passage records the same reply being given to the Prophet as an instruction for disputes he could not settle, and its wording keeps the two parties separate in the same way: (10:41 — **“My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours”**). Anyone who has tried to settle a religious argument by appealing to his own history has the correction in the sentence.

**TWO LEDGERS AND ONE LANDLORD**

Think of two tenants who argue about which of them the landlord prefers, and who are told that he owns the whole building and keeps a separate account for each. The verse's reply has that shape, and it leaves the tenants with nothing to argue about except their own records. The comparison clarifies why the Book calls the parties back to their deeds, since a claim about favour is a claim about another person's business. A dispute about standing that runs for years while both parties ignore their own ledgers is the situation addressed here. The reply does not settle which party the landlord prefers, and the silence is the answer. What he is left holding is a dispute dissolved rather than decided.

**DEVOTION AS THE ONLY DIFFERENCE ASKED FOR**

The verse closes with the one point on which the two sides could actually differ, and it states it as a fact about the believers: ***“And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺”***. The word describes a sincerity that keeps nothing back, and the Book uses it to describe a loyalty that is undivided rather than one that is merely professed. The distinction is the one the whole passage has been drawing, since the communities in view had a profession without the devotion that goes with it. The claim is made in another passage where the community is told to state it in the face of argument, and it is placed beside submission to God rather than beside victory in debate: (3:20 — **“I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers”**). The sentence therefore ends without a verdict on the opponents, which is deliberate, since the verse's purpose is to define the community's own position rather than to pass sentence on anyone else's. The plainness of the difference that actually matters is what the verse leaves. Today, argument about who owns God collapses when every party is returned to its own work and the sincerity with which it was done.
