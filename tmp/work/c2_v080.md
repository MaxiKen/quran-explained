**A CLAIM ABOUT THE FIRE AND THREE QUESTIONS**

The verse reports a doctrine the community had settled on for itself, and then answers it with questions: ***“˹Some of˺ the Jews claim, “The Fire will not touch us except”***, ***“for a number of days.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Have you taken a pledge from Allah”***, ***“for Allah never breaks His word”***, ***“or are you ˹just˺ saying about Allah what you do not know?””*** The claim is that the punishment will be brief, and the reply does not dispute the length; it asks where the length came from. A person may hold a doctrine about the Day of Judgement only if he has been given a promise about it, and the verse's first question establishes that a promise from God would have been honoured. The second question then separates the two possibilities left: either the community holds a contract it cannot produce, or it is speaking about God without knowledge, and the reader is free to choose which description fits. Ibn ʿAbbās transmitted the claim as forty days in the Fire, matched to the days of calf worship, and read the reply as a demand for a covenant that their own Scripture did not contain.

**THE DOCTRINE AS IT IS MET ELSEWHERE**

The Book reports this confident arithmetic about the Fire in more than one place, which tells the reader that it was a settled feature of the community's self-understanding rather than a slip of the tongue. In a later sūrah the claim is repeated with the number left vague and the consequence spelled out, since the Book treats the wish as the source of the deception: (3:24 — **“This is because they say, “The Fire will not touch us except”**). Another passage shows the claim being tested by an invitation, which is the Book's usual method with people who are certain about their own standing: (62:6 — **“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Jews!”**). The repetition matters for a reader because it shows the claim doing work in the community: it was not a private hope but a public doctrine, and it shaped how people behaved toward their own misconduct.

**A HOPE DRESSED AS A GUARANTEE**

The reply in this verse is a piece of reasoning that a reader can reuse, and it turns on the difference between a hope and a guarantee. If God had promised a short punishment for this community, the promise would be kept, since the Book says plainly that His word is never broken. What the verse then exposes is a claim made without such a promise, and the Book's word for that is speech about God without knowledge, which it treats as a serious matter in its own right. The doctrine was comforting, and comfort is not evidence; the question in the verse is designed to make the claimants notice the difference before they are asked to rely on it. A reader who holds any opinion about his own standing on the Day can run the same two questions over it, and he will find that most such opinions are hopes wearing the clothes of knowledge.

**A DOCTRINE THAT MAKES PEOPLE COMFORTABLE**

Think of a firm whose managers have convinced themselves that the inspection will pass because they have always passed, and whose confidence grows with each year in which nothing was examined. The same kind of assurance had been built here on a claim that nobody could trace to a source, and the Book's answer removes the assurance without removing the hope: a person may hope for mercy, and he may not announce terms for it. The difference between the two is the difference between humility and presumption, and the Book treats the second as a form of lying about God. A reader who has heard this claim discussed as though it were a settled arrangement can see how the verse dismantles it, and he can also see how easily the same structure rebuilds itself inside any religious community.

**THE UNSUPPORTED GUARANTEE EXPOSED**

The verse ends with the phrase about speaking without knowledge, and the phrase is the reason this passage sits where it does in the sūrah. The previous verses described people who altered the scripture and people who speculated about it, and this verse shows the fruit of both: a doctrine about the Last Day held with confidence and no evidence. The Book's remedy is not silence but knowledge, since it asks the community to test its claims against what has actually been given. A reader who takes the instruction seriously will find that the question is portable, and that it applies to the doctrines he inherited from his family as much as to the ones he chose himself. What the verse refuses to allow is the sentence that sounds like certainty without a source behind it. Present-day spiritual confidence is equally unsafe when it rests on an imagined exception rather than a promise God actually made.
