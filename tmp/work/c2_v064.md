**A PEOPLE SPARED BY MERCY THEY DID NOT EARN**

The covenant had just been taken, and the verse opens by naming what came next: ***“Yet you turned away afterwards. Had it not been”***, ***“for Allah’s grace and mercy upon you, you would have certainly been of the losers”***. Two sentences, and the second explains why the first did not end the story. The turning away is stated without softening, and the reader is left with a community that broke its word under a lifted mountain and remained on its feet anyway. The reason given is not their repentance, their merit, or their numbers; it is the favour and compassion of the One who took the pledge. The Book returns to this pattern at the end of the very same passage, and the same sentence appears a second time in this sūrah, which tells the reader that the account is being told for the sake of that clause rather than for the sake of the failure. Ibn ʿAbbās read the grace as the deferral of punishment and the mercy as a renewed summons to guidance, which explains why survival after turning away was itself a gift.

**WHAT LOSING WOULD HAVE MEANT**

The word chosen for the outcome is worth stopping over, because the Book uses it for a specific kind of ruin. A person who is lost in this vocabulary is not merely punished; he has thrown away his own share, which is the sense the term carries elsewhere when covenant-breakers are described in the opening of the sūrah. Earlier in this chapter the same word is used of those who violate the pledge after affirming it: (2:27 — **“those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed,”**). The community in the verse was on the road to exactly that description, and the sentence that saves them is a sentence about God rather than about them. A reader who notices this will see that the verse is not describing a light offence. It is describing a fall that was prevented by a hand that was not the people's own, and the prevention is the mercy named.

**MERCY THAT DOES NOT WAIT FOR REPENTANCE**

The reports and the rest of the Book agree on one point here that is easy to miss: the favour is described as already upon them, not as conditional on anything they had done. Another sūrah makes the principle general and puts it in the mouth of the revelation addressed to a people whose record matches this one: (7:153 — **“But those who commit evil, then repent and become ˹true˺ believers,”**). The door described there is open after the evil, which is the same order of events as this verse: failure first, mercy second. A third passage states the invitation in the widest terms available in the Book: (39:53 — **“Say, ˹O Prophet, that Allah says,˺ “O My servants”**). Read together, the three passages give the reader a settled picture: the Book never treats a broken pledge as the end of a person's case, and it never treats mercy as a licence to break the next one.

**SPARED DOES NOT YET MEAN SAFE**

A second point follows from the wording, and it belongs to the reader's own life rather than to the history. Being spared is not the same as being safe, since the people in this verse were kept alive and kept in the story while the pattern of their conduct was already visible. The Book tells the reader that the covenant was broken and the mercy came, and then it goes on in the next verses to describe what a later generation of the same people did with the Sabbath. A reader who takes the mercy as a verdict on the future will be surprised by the next page, and the one who takes it as an opening will use it, which is the difference the clause is meant to make. This is the chapter's constant method: it shows the kindness of God beside the conduct of man, and it refuses to let either of the two cancel the other.

**TURNING BACK AFTER TURNING AWAY**

The verse has an application that is worth stating plainly, and it concerns the shape of repentance rather than its sincerity. A person who has broken a promise and is still walking around has already received a favour he did not arrange, and the appropriate response is not a fresh vow spoken in fear but a change of course performed in calm. The same sūrah closes with a prayer the community is taught to make about exactly this: being spared the burden of what one has already done and helped toward what comes next. A reader with a broken covenant behind him can take two things from the verse: that his story is still running, and that the mercy which kept him standing through the failure is the same mercy whose standard will measure what he does with the time it bought, so the leniency is also a summons. Present-day reprieve is often mistaken for approval, although delayed consequences may instead be space in which return is still possible. It follows that grace is not merely an inward comfort here; it is the only barrier between a turned back and an irreversible loss.
