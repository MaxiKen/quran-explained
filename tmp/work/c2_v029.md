**EVERYTHING ON THE EARTH WAS MADE FOR THE READER**

The chapter returns to the theme of provision with a broader claim: ***“He is the One”***, ***“Who created everything in the earth”***, ***“for you. Then He turned towards the heaven, forming it into seven heavens.”***, and ***“And He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”***. The phrase for you is the hinge of the verse. The claim is not that the earth contains things a person may happen to use; the whole of it was made with him in view, which is a statement about intention rather than about permission. The second half then lifts the reader's attention to the sky and to the ordering of the heavens, and the verse closes by naming knowledge rather than power, as though to answer the question the reader is about to ask.

The order of the sentence follows the order of a person's looking. He notices the ground under his feet, the water and soil and animals he lives from, and only then does he raise his eyes. The Book uses this as the shape of its argument in the great creation verses, and the same movement appears later in the chapter when the signs in the sky and the earth are catalogued for people of understanding: (3:190 — **“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the day”**). The reader is meant to see a single hand in both halves, because a person who divides them will end up thanking a system instead of a Lord.

**THE SEVEN HEAVENS AND THE LIMITS OF PICTURING**

The verse says the heaven was formed into seven, and the Book repeats the number as a fact of the created order without asking the reader to draw a diagram. What the number communicates is arrangement: layers, order, and a structure that is neither accidental nor improvised. A reader who tries to fix the number to an astronomical model will find that he has taken on a job the verse does not require of him, and he will miss the point it does make. The point is the relation between the two halves: the same will that made the earth serviceable for a person also arranged the sky above him, and the two are not two projects but one plan with one author.

The closing clause about knowledge then arrives exactly where a reader needs it. Everything has been described as created for a human being, which could be mistaken for flattery, as though the universe existed to satisfy his appetites. The verse answers that misreading before it forms, because the one who arranged all of it already knows all things, including what the reader will do with the gift. The knowledge is not an afterthought; it is the reason the claim is credible, since a maker who knows the whole of what he made cannot be surprised by the use it is put to. A maker who knows everything about what he made is also the only one competent to say what the made thing is for.

**NOTICE WHAT HAS BEEN PUT IN YOUR HANDS**

The practical reading of the verse begins with an inventory a person can actually make. Water, food, shelter, a body that works, a day with hours in it: these are the resources named by "everything in the earth", and every reader has some of them. What is wanted is not an impressive use of the resources, only a use that acknowledges where they came from. That acknowledgement can be as small as naming it before a meal or as large as a career aimed at something other than accumulation, and the Book accepts both ends of the scale. What it will not accept is the assumption that the arrangement is a coincidence. Today a reader can test the claim in a single afternoon by listing three things he used of the earth's provisions and naming who they came from. The list will be longer than he expects, and the naming is the whole exercise. A person who has done it will understand why the sūrah puts knowledge at the end of the verse: the one who gave the earth to be used already knows whether it is being used with thanks or with the forgetfulness the Book keeps warning about.
