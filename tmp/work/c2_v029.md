**THE EARTH THAT WAS FURNISHED BEFORE THE TENANT ARRIVED**

The argument of the previous verses continues with a statement about the world the reader lives in: ***“He is the One”***, ***“Who created everything in the earth”***, ***“for you.”***. The words put the whole of the ground under the reader's feet at his service, and the early teachers read the phrase as covering the animals, the plants and the materials a human life depends on. Ibn ʿAbbās described the sentence as a blessing stated in the form of a fact, and he read the creation of these things as an act of subservience granted before the tenant had done anything to deserve it. The Book names the same arrangement in a later passage where the gift is stated in the same form: (45:13 — **“He ˹also˺ subjected for you whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth”**). That is why the verse is placed after the question about denying God, because a person who is surrounded by provision is being asked what he has done with it.

**THE TURNING TOWARDS THE HEAVEN AND ITS SEVEN STOREYS**

The verse moves from the ground to what stands above it: ***“Then He turned towards the”***, ***“heaven, forming it into seven heavens.”***. The early teachers discussed the turning with care, and the reading that carried the day among them is the one that gives the verb the sense of directing attention and purpose towards the heaven and shaping it as a builder shapes a roof. Ibn ʿAbbās read the forming as the arranging of the heavens in their layers, one above another, which is how the Book describes them in a sūrah where they are named as a structure with no fault in it: (67:3 — **“˹He is the One˺ Who created seven heavens, one above the other”**). The reason the number is stated rather than left to the imagination is that the reader is being told that the sky is not a void but an order, and an order is evidence of the One who arranged it. The verse does not describe what lies inside those heavens, since its business here is the fact of the making rather than the furniture of the made.

**THE KNOWLEDGE THAT CLOSES THE SENTENCE**

The verse ends with a statement that covers everything the reader has just been told: ***“And He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge”***, ***“of all things”***. The early readers noticed that the sentence is placed after the account of the earth and the heavens rather than before it, and they explained the placing as a way of joining the two facts: the One who made all of it is the One who knows all of it. The Book states the same pair in a passage about the making of the heavens, where the shaping and the knowledge are named in a single verse: (41:12 — **“So He formed the heaven into seven heavens in two Days”**). The reason the sentence matters for the reader is that a maker who does not know his work is a maker who has lost interest in it, and the verse denies the reader that unhappy picture before he can form it. What turns on the words is the difference between a world that is running down without supervision and a world whose every part is held in the knowledge of its maker.

**A HOUSE PREPARED FOR A GUEST WHO HAS NOT KEPT IN TOUCH**

Think of a traveller who arrives at a house to find the table laid, the lamps lit and a bed made up in a room he has never seen, all arranged by a host he has not written to for years. The verse describes the reader as the guest in that house, and it puts the whole of the arrangement into one sentence. These days the same furnishings are hidden behind the labels of science and industry, which describe how the table was laid and rarely ask who laid it, and the passage asks the reader to notice the arrangement as a whole. The next verse goes further and shows a conversation about this very planet, which is where the sūrah begins the long account of the human being and what he was made for.
