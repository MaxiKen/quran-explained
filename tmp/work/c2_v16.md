**A TRADE WITH NO PROFIT**

The verse names the transaction in which these men are engaged: ***“They are the ones”*** ***“who trade guidance for misguidance.”***. The Book uses the language of the market deliberately, because the bargain is one that a trader would reject on the floor of any bazaar. Two things change hands, and they are not equal. On one side sits guidance, which is the road and the light and the company that walks it, and on the other sits misguidance, which is the same road refused and the same light traded for a lamp of one's own making. The sentence does not say that the men were robbed; it says they did the trading, which is why the loss belongs to them rather than to the market. A shopkeeper who sells the family's only cart for a sack of sweets has not been cheated by the buyer. He has done something to himself, and the whole village watches the sweets disappear.

**COUNTING THE LOSS IN ADVANCE**

The middle phrase states the outcome before the reader has asked for it: ***“But this trade is profitless”***. The verdict is delivered in the language of accounting, and the point of it is that the loss is total rather than partial. A man who trades his share in a caravan for a horse that dies on the first night is not left with half a share; he is left standing at the gate with the saddle. The sentence gives the reader that accounting early, which is a mercy, because a warning delivered before the purchase can still save the buyer. Another verse in the same sūrah describes the same traders with the ledger opened further, since the exchange in it also includes forgiveness given away for punishment (2:175), and a third shows that the loss never touched God at all, (3:177 — **“will never harm Allah in the least”**). The bargain costs the buyer everything and the seller nothing.

**TWO CURRENCIES ON ONE SCALE**

The last phrase closes the account by naming what the traders do not have: ***“and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided”***. The Book places the two currencies side by side without pretending that they are equal in weight, because a scale is only useful when the difference between the pans is admitted. Guidance is worth what it costs a person to keep, and misguidance is worth what it saves him for a season. A man who stays away from a hard duty for one summer buys a summer, and the price he pays is the habit of staying away, which will outlast the weather. This is why the verse ends on a state rather than on an event: the traders are not described as having lost a round; they are described as not being guided, which is the condition they chose and the condition they keep. The sentence is therefore a diagnosis of a life rather than a report of one deal, and the Book wants the reader to hear the difference.

**THE SWAPS A READER MAKES TODAY**

The trade this verse describes is still open in every city, and the counters are not labelled, which is what makes it dangerous. A person trades the quiet of a household for a friendship that flatters him; a student trades an hour of study for a night that he will not remember; a worker trades a small compromise for a promotion that costs him his name. None of those transactions arrive with a written price, and that is why the Book states the rule once in the language of a market where the reader can see both pans. A man who keeps a ledger of even a single week will find that he has made one or two of these trades already, and the health of his religion depends on how he reads the entries. The verse is not asking him to be clever; it is asking him to look at what he has been buying, which is why the accounting comes before the reproach.
