**POWER CAN PRODUCE ARROGANCE**

***“Are you ˹O Prophet˺ not aware of the one who argued with Abraham about his Lord because Allah had granted him kingship?”*** establishes power can produce arrogance. The verse presents a ruler who argued with Ibrāhīm about Allah because Allah had given him kingship. The Qur’an does not name the ruler; an early report identifies him as Nimrod, but the moral lesson does not depend upon the name. Authority gave him the illusion that he could define reality and challenge the Creator. Power can make a person forget that it is borrowed. A ruler, employer, scholar, or wealthy individual may begin to think that because people obey him, the universe must obey him too. The verse exposes this confusion: human authority is limited, while Allah gives life, causes death, and controls every created power. Ibrāhīm begins with a clear proof: My Lord is the One who gives life and causes death. The ruler replies that he can give life and death. One early explanation states that he released one condemned person and killed another, pretending that this was equivalent to creation and death. His answer was a rhetorical trick, not a genuine response to Ibrāhīm’s argument. A ruler can decide whether a person lives under a legal sentence, but he cannot create a living being from nothing, control the soul, or reverse death by his own power. Human medicine can preserve life and postpone death through means, but it does not make the physician the Creator. The distinction between cause and ultimate source is central to tawḥīd. Ibrāhīm then gives a proof the ruler cannot redefine: Allah brings the sun from the east, so bring it from the west. (6:102 — **“There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. ˹He is˺ the”**)

**GIVING LIFE AND CAUSING DEATH**

***“˹Remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord is the One Who has power to give life and cause death.” He argued, “I”*** deepens giving life and causing death. The challenge moves from a manipulated social example to a universal order beyond the king’s control. The sun’s regular movement is a sign of a Creator whose authority cannot be imitated by political theatre. The ruler is confounded—fa-buhita alladhī kafara. He has no coherent answer. Silence after a clear proof is not the same as humble acceptance; a person may be intellectually defeated yet remain morally stubborn. The Qur’an records the collapse of the argument, while Allah knows whether the heart later repented. Ibrāhīm’s exchange demonstrates that reasoned debate can be useful when it clarifies truth. He begins with a principle, exposes the false analogy, and presents a proof the opponent cannot manipulate. He does not insult the ruler’s body, family, or ethnicity. His confidence comes from evidence and trust in Allah. The Qur’an also warns against fruitless argument. A debate is beneficial when participants seek truth, understand the evidence, and accept correction. It becomes harmful when the aim is humiliation, performance, or protecting power. Muslims should not imitate the ruler’s arrogance while criticizing it. The closing statement says that Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people—al-qawm al-ẓālimīn. This does not mean that Allah prevents every wrongdoer from ever receiving information or repentance. It means that the person who persists in injustice and arrogance is deprived of the special guidance that leads to humble acceptance. His wrongdoing becomes a barrier he chooses and reinforces. The Qur’an summons everyone to guidance, and a living wrongdoer can still repent. (3:189 — **“To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth.”**)

**THE DECISIVE COSMIC SIGN**

***“too have the power to give life and cause death.” Abraham challenged ˹him˺, “Allah causes the sun to rise from the”*** clarifies the decisive cosmic sign. A ruler can turn from oppression, a scholar from pride, and a wealthy person from exploitation. But guidance requires abandoning the posture that treats power as proof of truth. Authority should be measured by justice and humility. A government that provides security does not own its citizens’ souls. A leader cannot declare himself the source of life, law, or prosperity. He is a servant who will answer for blood, property, and speech. Ibrāhīm’s example also encourages truthful counsel to power. The adviser uses evidence, avoids flattery, and does not assume that a ruler’s confidence makes his argument true. Where direct confrontation creates danger, wisdom may require a different method, but truth must not be sold for safety or favour. The sun’s rising from the east reminds people that the world follows an ordered pattern. Regularity does not make creation divine; it makes the Creator’s wisdom visible. The ruler can neither command the sun nor guarantee tomorrow’s sunrise. Every created power is contingent and dependent. The believer uses this awareness in daily life. He respects worldly causes but does not fear them as independent gods. He seeks lawful help while keeping the heart attached to Allah. Imagine two men disputing the height of a courtyard wall, and one of them asks the other to hold back the tide with his hand. The tide comes in every evening, the wall is measured in the morning, and the man who spoke of the sea never spoke of the wall again. (67:1 — **“Blessed is the One in Whose Hands rests all authority. And He is Most Capable of everything”**)

**DEBATE AND ITS LIMITS**

***“east. So make it rise from the west.” And so the disbeliever was dumbstruck. And Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people”*** closes with debate and its limits. Power can produce arrogance confronts giving life and causing death in one present-day reader; the decisive cosmic sign meets, under the demand of power can produce arrogance, a tradesman whose new authority has persuaded him that rank settles every argument. The verse gives him a king with a throne and a friend of God with a sunrise; the decisive cosmic sign asks, through power can produce arrogance, which of the two would stand if the sky were asked to move. Because the close names the wrongdoers as the ones left without guidance, showing through power can produce arrogance that the greatest loss of a powerful man is the hearing he no longer has. The argument from the rising of the sun is kept beside another proof presented in Sūrah al-Anʿām. And the same mercy that keeps a believer's road lit is asked for at the close of this chapter. Because power can produce arrogance opens into giving life and causing death, the decisive cosmic sign joins power can produce arrogance to giving life and causing death. Since the decisive cosmic sign develops power can produce arrogance, giving life and causing death carries the transition. Debate and its limits matters, which means the decisive cosmic sign answers giving life and causing death. Giving life and causing death gives practical form to power can produce arrogance; debate and its limits sets its horizon. Today, power can produce arrogance shapes giving life and causing death in present decisions. (16:78 — **“And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing,”**)
