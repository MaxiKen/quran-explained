**A FLASH CAN DAZZLE WITHOUT BECOMING A STEADY GUIDE**

The storm reaches the eye: ***“It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight.”*** A near strike floods the dark with such force that the traveller is dazzled by the very thing that lets him see. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the flash as the Qur’an’s clear disclosure, which comes close to tearing away their error. The image holds both clarity and danger because their hearts do not receive clarity in a sound way. Proof breaks through, yet pride and doubt make its arrival feel like an attack. The words say that sight is almost taken, not that it has already gone. They still have moments in which a road appears and response remains possible. This makes their next act more revealing. They are not people to whom no light ever came; they are people whose use of light changes with the ease it offers. After describing rain and hail, another verse says, (24:43 — **“The flash of the clouds’ lightning nearly takes away eyesight”**). Here that created fact gives the parable its force while clear revelation exposes what their divided will does with a moment of sight.

**THEY MOVE ONLY WHILE FAITH SERVES THE NEAR MOMENT**

Their unstable course appears in ***“Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light”***. The flash is enough for a few steps, but they do not commit themselves to the road or to the One who shows it. Early readers connect this movement to the truths, gains, and ease in faith that suit them. When public belief gives safety or the believers gain strength, the hypocrites move among them. A later description catches the same wait for advantage: (4:141 — **“˹The hypocrites are˺ those who wait to see what happens to you. So if Allah grants you victory, they say ˹to you˺,”**). Their allegiance follows the visible turn rather than guiding them through it. This is not a rebuke of every pause in confusion. A sincere believer may stop to learn, seek proof, or avoid a rash act. The difference is that sincere caution wants enough light to obey, while hypocrisy wants only enough light to gain. Its standard shifts with comfort. The Qur’an depicts another person who worships at the edge: (22:11 — **“if they are afflicted with a trial, they relapse ˹into disbelief˺, losing this world and the Hereafter.”**). Faith rooted in God can walk through a hard command because truth remains true when the weather changes. Faith used as a bargain moves only while the next patch of ground looks useful.

**DARKNESS EXPOSES THE LACK OF AN INNER COURSE**

The other half follows: ***“but when darkness covers them, they stand still. Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing”***. The sudden stop is more than sensible shelter from a storm. Within the parable, doubt and disliked duties leave them with no settled aim of their own. They cannot move by trust when a quick worldly benefit is no longer visible. This is why their stillness belongs with the motion before it. Both are ruled by the same demand for immediate gain. The effect is a life of reaction: advance when success shines, freeze when cost appears, and never choose the road for its true end. The mention of hearing returns to the fingers pressed into ears in verse 19. They act as though hearing belongs to them to block at will, but God can remove the gift itself. The warning also joins moral loss to bodily power without confusing the two. Their inward hearing has failed through refusal; their outward hearing remains under the Lord who gave it. God asks elsewhere, (6:46 — **“Imagine if Allah were to take away your hearing or sight, or seal your hearts—who”**) other than Him could restore it. A faculty is not secure merely because it works today. Its right response is gratitude and use in the service of truth.

**SIGHT, SOUND, AND EVERY OUTCOME REST IN DIVINE POWER**

The close completes the threat: ***“and sight. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything”***. Hearing and sight are named because the storm has tested both. Thunder met the ear, lightning met the eye, and the hypocrites misused each meeting. The final statement widens the view beyond the storm. God can preserve, remove, judge, guide, and bring every promised end. No part of the parable lies outside His rule, and no clever shift between light and dark limits what He can do. This power should awaken thanks as well as fear. Humanity is reminded, (67:23 — **“He is the One Who brought you into being and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect.”**). Thanks is not only spoken praise. It is to let the ear receive what is true and the eye read signs as signs. Today, a person may obey while faith brings social ease, then stop when it costs status, time, or desire. The changing cost does not change the Lord or the road. Steady faith stores the direction given in light and follows it when the next stretch is hard. The storm parable warns against making convenience the lamp, because convenience flashes and fades. God’s guidance, received with a whole heart, supplies a course beyond the next bright moment.
