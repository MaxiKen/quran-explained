**THE LIGHT THAT COMES AND GOES**

The verse continues the storm and turns to what the lightning does to the people caught in it: ***“It is as if the lightning were about to snatch away their sight”***. The wording describes a flash so strong that the men expect to lose their eyes with it, and the earliest authorities kept the comparison for the terror the revelation produced in the hearts of people who had set themselves against it. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with the reading that the flash stands for the moments when the truth is shown plainly to the pretenders and the thunder for the warnings that follow, and the description of the eye as the thing nearly taken away belongs to the same picture. The Book uses the same image for the lightning in another sūrah where its effect is described in a single phrase: (24:43 — **“The flash of the clouds' lightning nearly takes away eyesight”**). The picture therefore joins the storm of the previous verse and carries it further, since the danger has moved from the ears to the eyes.

**THE TWO MOVEMENTS OF THE SAME PEOPLE**

The verse then records the two things the men do: ***“Whenever lightning strikes, they walk in its light, but when darkness covers them, they stand still”***. The sentence states the rhythm of a person who moves only when he is carried by an emotion and stops the moment it has gone, and the early teachers read the walking as the small amount of good such people do while the flash lasts. The Book draws the line between the people who walk in steady light and those who move in fits in another sūrah where the two groups are shown at the end of the road: (57:12 — **“with their light shining ahead of them and on their right”**). The contrast is exact, because the light of the believers is described as their own for the journey and shining before them, while the light of this passage comes in flashes that reveal and then vanish. What turns on the difference is the state of the lamp inside the man rather than the brightness of the weather around him.

**THE SENTENCE THAT NAMES WHAT COULD HAVE HAPPENED**

The verse closes with a statement about power that is aimed at the men in the picture: ***“Had Allah willed, He could have taken away their hearing and sight”***. The sentence names the two instruments and connects them to the same will that was behind the loss of the light, and it states the matter as a possibility that was not carried out. The Book uses the closing formula of the verse in another place where a great matter is described, and the wording there is worth placing beside it: (16:77 — **“Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything”**). The earliest teachers explained the verse as a reminder that the respite is itself a mercy, since the men had deserved the loss of the instruments in this life and were given time instead. That is the reason the sentence follows the account of their hiding, because the Book is telling them that the storm they are dodging could have finished the matter long before. The words that end it, ***“Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything”***, are the same words the Book uses when it describes the Hour arriving in the blink of an eye.

**A SAILOR WHO SAILS BY THE FLASHES**

Think of a sailor who steers only when a flash of lightning shows him the rocks, and who drops his hands from the wheel the moment the sky goes dark. He will hit the rocks in one of the intervals, and the reason is not the dark or the flash but his own refusal to hold a course. The verse describes this kind of sailor with the respect that comes from truthfulness, since it does not pretend that the flashes were useless: the men did walk, and the walking is recorded. These days a reader can find the same pattern in a life built around the high moments of a season and the flat weeks between them, and the sūrah offers no remedy here beyond the portrait itself. The long account of the pretenders ends with this verse, and the sūrah then turns to address the readers as a community of worshippers, which is the subject of the verses that follow.
