**THE OTHER SIDE OF THE SENTENCE**

The previous verse described a condition and its end, and this one takes the same shape and turns it toward the opposite kind of life: ***“And those who believe”***, ***“and do good will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever”***. Faith and good conduct are named in the order the Book keeps using, since the deed grows out of the belief rather than replacing it. What is permanent on this side of the sentence is a residence rather than a visit, and the pairing of the two verses is the chapter's way of showing that the same scale measures both outcomes. The reader who has just been told what happens to a life swallowed by wrongdoing is now told what happens to a life spent doing the good that faith produced, and both statements are addressed to people who can still choose. The Book makes the same invitation to hurry toward that residence in another sūrah: (3:133 — **“And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast”**).

**TWO DESTINATIONS WRITTEN SIDE BY SIDE**

A reader might wonder why the Book does not separate the two destinations, and the answer lies in the way it teaches. Placing them side by side prevents the reader from holding one without the other, so that the warning and the promise arrive in the same reading and the imagination has to work on both. The pair also protects the reader from the mistake of the previous passage, since a community that had decided on a short punishment for itself could have used a verse about mercy as a licence. The Book's habit is to state the door and the pit together, and it does so in a later passage where the residents of the Garden are described in similar terms and the description comes directly after the fate of their opposites: (98:7 — **“Indeed, those who believe and do good—they”**). A reader who only collects the promises has misunderstood the pattern the Book is using.

**WHAT RESIDENCE MEANS AS A PICTURE**

The word for residency in this pair of verses is not the same as the word for a long stay; it carries the sense of belonging to a place and remaining in it. Ownership of a home changes a person's relation to it, and the Book uses the image because it is the clearest way to describe a permanence that is also a privilege. In the present world the same term is used for the settlement of a community in a land, and the reader is being told that the life to come is not a campsite. The picture is completed later in the sūrah with descriptions of gardens, rivers, and companions, and those images come from the same desire to give an idea a shape the reader can hold. What matters here is that the promise is stated in terms of a settled condition rather than a moment of reward.

**TWO FAMILIES IN THE SAME CORRIDOR**

Think of two families waiting on the same morning for the outcome of a case that will decide where they live, and of how differently the same corridor feels to each of them. The Book's pairing of these two verses creates that corridor for the reader, and it does so without describing the argument or the judge, because the two sentences are enough. The reader's task is not to decide who occupies which room but to notice that the question of his own life is being asked in the same hearing. A person who can hold both sentences in his mind at once has acquired something valuable, since most self-deception works by keeping one of them out of view. The Book closes the door on that trick by writing them one after the other.

**THE GOOD THAT FOLLOWS BELIEF**

The second clause of the verse is a description of a life and not of a single act, and the Book's word for it covers everything a person does in accordance with what he believes. The verse's general wording is deliberate, since no list of prayers, charities, or fasts is given here, although the sūrah names them in other places; the general word is used to keep the promise open to every good deed a reader performs. The pairing with belief also rules out the notion that good conduct can stand on its own as a substitute for faith, since the deed is named as the fruit of a root. A reader who wants to test his own position can ask what his conduct has grown out of, because the Book's interest is in the tree rather than in the fruit alone. The verse ends where the chapter's other promises end, with a permanence given to those who believe and act on their belief.
