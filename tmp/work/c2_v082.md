**THE VERSE THAT MUST STAND BESIDE IT**

The promise that answers the threat is written in the same shape, and the pairing is the pedagogy: ***“And those who believe”***, ***“and do good will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever”***. The early analysts of the sequence note the wisdom in the alternation — the promise is sweetened with the threat and the threat with the promise, so that the warning grows by mention of what its people forfeit of reward, and the promise grows by mention of what its people escape of punishment. Ibn ʿAbbās glosses the first clause as belief in the Messenger and the revelation, and the second as acts of obedience between the servant and his Lord; the closing words he reads as perpetuity without death and without exit, the exact mirror of the Fire's own perpetuity. The symmetry is the point. Two destinies are stated in two sentences of identical architecture, so that the reader cannot extract the amnesty-half of the passage and file it alone. The composition itself disciplines the reader's eye: the two verdicts share a verb tense and a duration, and only their populations differ. Where the previous verse closed the door on a demographic exemption, this one opens the door on an individual covenant, and the covenant has two named terms and no third.

**TWO HINGES AND THE DOOR**

The grammar of the pair drew the language specialists more than once, because the second clause is coordinated onto the first rather than absorbed into it. A part is not coordinated onto a whole; if works were already inside the name of belief, the coordination would be tautology. The arrangement therefore marks two things in order: the assent of the heart and the fruit the assent bears. The glosses make the same point functionally — belief in God and His Messenger, then the deeds that conform to the revealed law — and the tradition of the doctrine adds the reason the unrepentant cannot claim the first term without the second. Faith is itself the greatest good in an account, so a believer's ledger can never be empty of goodness however thin the entries; and minor wrongs are absorbed in forgiveness unless obstinacy promotes them, which is why the surrounding-sentence of the last verse cannot land on this people. The reason the two terms travel together is now visible: the first without the second is assent without its fruit, and the second without the first is labor without its ground. The twin verdict in the other assembly of the same theme says it flatly — (98:7 — **“Indeed, those who believe and do good—they are the best of ˹all˺ beings”**) — and the reward-language of the covenant-curse keeps the individual ledger in view. What turns on the pairing is the whole shape of hope: entry is by faith and good work together, and the two terms are not rival claimants but one road with two feet.

**THE RULE BENEATH THE WHOLE CLAIM**

The closing of this argument is worth reading in its full severity, because it withdraws the assumption beneath the entire claim of counted days. Grace is not parceled by wishes or by ancestry; the reckoning runs on deeds and their doers: (4:123 — **“˹Divine grace is˺ neither by your wishes nor those of the People of the Book! Whoever commits evil will be rewarded accordingly,”**). The passage therefore settles what the whole exchange had circled: the judgment follows the individual's works, not the family's papers, and no community carries a standing exemption written into the terms. The early doctrine states the effect plainly — the denial of the later prophets put the claimants outside the covenant their argument needed, and the promise of release stands beside the works, not beside the bloodline. That is why the claim of the earlier verses had to be met with a question about a pledge rather than a counter-number. The difference the verse presses on every later audience today is the difference between inherited religion and practiced religion: the first assumes the covenant, the second keeps it. In other words, the door to the Garden is hung on two hinges, faith and work, and the passage that began with an invented amnesty closes by showing the only lease that holds.
