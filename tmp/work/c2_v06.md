**A DOOR THAT WAS SHUT BEFORE THE KNOCK**
The sūrah turns here from the company that follows the Book to the company that will not, and it turns without raising its voice. The line opens on a settled condition rather than a passing mood: ***“As for those who persist in disbelief, it is the same whether you warn them”***. A man who covers a well does not do it by accident, and the sense of the root is covering something over. Among the earliest readers, Ibn ʿAbbās placed the verse among people who accepted what had come to earlier messengers while refusing what came down to this one, and that reading tells the reader where the line is drawn. This is why the sentence sounds so calm, and the calm is the point. It marks the difference between a man still arguing and a man who has shut his door, and everything the sūrah says after this rests on that difference. A closed door does not need to be shouted at, which is why the verse describes it rather than arguing with it.
**TWO SOUNDS, ONE OUTCOME**
The second half of the line completes the first without raising its temperature: ***“or not they will never believe”***. The warning is not offered as the cause of faith, and its absence is not offered as the cause of refusal, because the outcome is placed beyond the argument altogether. Picture a nurse who still carries the dose to a patient whose chart already says he will push it away. Her feet are doing the duty, which means the outcome was never hers to sign for. Another sūrah opens with the same sentence word for word, (36:10 — **“It is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe”**), which shows a law being stated rather than a remark about one street in Madinah. Guidance belongs to God, and the Book says so without apology, (28:56 — **“but it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills”**). A caller is measured by faithfulness rather than by results, and that measure is a mercy to anyone who has watched a relative walk the other way.
Those who carry the message have always needed this sentence, since the work of inviting people is the slowest work there is. The way a teacher explains a sum to a boy who has decided that arithmetic is beyond him shows where the difficulty lies: the explanation lands nowhere until something moves inside the boy that the teacher cannot reach. The verse states that situation plainly rather than softening it, since a caller who expects results is a caller who will soon quit. The duty of the caller does not shrink into a formality, however, because the Book keeps commanding the reminder while it keeps the result in God's hand, (88:21 — **“So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind”**), and it removes the last excuse for pressure, (88:22 — **“You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺”**). A parent reading this today can put down a guilt that was never his to carry: he is asked to speak well and to keep speaking, not to open a heart.
**WHAT THE WARNING STILL DOES**
The warning keeps its work even when the answer is already known, and the earliest readers said so: it leaves the person addressed with no argument left when he is judged, and it clears the one who spoke. The report from Ibn ʿAbbās describes people who had the message in their hands and turned it away, and the sentence reports their turning rather than causing it. A man told to warn a village that has bolted its gates is not being mocked; he is being spared the illusion that the gates were his to open. Anyone who has tried to keep a brother inside the faith knows the weight of that, because the grief of a caller is real, and the verse never pretends otherwise, (18:6 — **“perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial”**). The verse stands as a boundary stone rather than a wall. It tells the caller where his reach ends and where God's begins, and it lets him sleep.
