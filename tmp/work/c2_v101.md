**THE RECOGNITION THEY COULD NOT DENY**

The passage opens on the arrival the whole earlier trial has been waiting for: ***“Now, when a messenger from Allah has come to them—”***, ***“confirming their own Scriptures—”***. The confirming is the heart of the charge. In Ibn ʿAbbās's own reading, the terms are the plainest available: the messenger came conforming to what their own book carried in traits and in description. The man matched the page. Their Torah had named him, and he stood in front of them named. The received account adds that the good news of his coming had been in the Torah all along, so that the arrival was not a new claim but a kept appointment. The point the verse builds toward is therefore sharp. The refusal at hand is not the refusal of strangers. It is the refusal of readers facing their own text and declining it.

**CAST BEHIND THE BACK**

The reason the gesture matters is that a document placed out of reach is a decision, not an accident. The deed is then drawn as posture, not argument: ***“some of the People of the Book cast the Book of Allah behind their backs ”***. The image is of a man putting a document where his hands cannot reach it. The glosses fix the gesture as refusal in action. They did not act on what the book said about belief in the messenger. The transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās presses the wording further: the flinging was a disbelieving in what the volume contained of the awaited one's traits and description. A second reading heard in the same tradition turns the gesture toward the market: the book was cast behind the back the way goods are disposed of — sold and spent. The book's own rule for that exact trade is short and old: (2:42 — **“Do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly”**). Either way the anatomy is the same. The document was not lost. It was placed out of sight on purpose.

**THE MASK OF THE NOT-KNOWING**

The closing mask is the verse's coldest stroke: ***“ as if they did not know”***. The phrase does not accuse the party of lacking the knowledge. It describes the party wearing the not-knowing as a costume. The gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās puts it in the voice of the scene: they behaved as though they were ignorant people who knew nothing, while the recognition was sitting in their own shelves. The standard of recognition had already been set in this surah in the family image: (2:146 — **“Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺ as they recognize their own children.”**). The description they feigned ignorance of is named in the final book without softness: (7:157 — **““˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah”**). Doubt and theater part ways here. Doubt stands outside and asks. This stands inside the text and pretends the text is empty. The reason the verse files the posture as the crime is that the knowledge was never the missing piece. The knowing was in the room, and the costume of ignorance was what the knowing wore to work.

**THE HABIT WITH A LATEST INSTANCE**

The placement of this verse right after the covenant-casting refrain is deliberate. The refrain said the casting was a habit. This is the habit's newest face. The same people who signed the papers now stand over the papers and act as if the papers say nothing. The doctrinal weight of the scene is heavy because the alternative was light. Accepting a confirmation of one's own book costs the pride of the shelf. Rejecting it costs the book. The passage makes the trade visible and lets the reader price it. For any reader today who keeps a cherished text at arm's length while quoting its approval of something refused, the mask in this verse has a familiar fit. The habit has a wide reach in households and in study circles both. A verse is quoted with reverence in the morning and shelved by the evening if it recommends an inconvenient step. That is the same back with the same book behind it. The verse answers the costume with one word of daylight. They knew. So do the people who quote and then look away. The page remembers either way.
