**THE MEANING OF BIRR**

The meaning of birr begins from ***“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east”***, ***“or the west. Rather, the righteous are those”***, ***“who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books”***, ***“and the prophets”***. This āyah gives one of the Qur’an’s most comprehensive definitions of *birr*, righteousness and expansive goodness. It comes in the middle of the qiblah discussion, where people had argued about direction. Allah does not dismiss the qiblah; facing the commanded direction is part of obedience. He corrects the reduction of religion to an outward symbol while faith, charity, justice, and endurance are neglected. The verse begins with negation—righteousness is not *that you turn your faces*—then gives the complete positive definition. *Birr* includes sound belief, worship, social generosity, moral reliability, and steadfastness under pressure. A Muslim cannot choose one dimension and abandon the others. Ritual without mercy is hollow, and good intentions without worship are incomplete. The righteous person believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets. These are foundations of *īmān*. Belief in Allah gives life its purpose; belief in the Last Day gives actions consequence; belief in angels affirms an unseen order and accountability; belief in the revealed Books establishes guidance; and belief in all the prophets protects the unity of the prophetic mission. The list joins the final revelation to the whole history of revelation. Muslims do not believe in Muhammad ﷺ while rejecting Mūsā, ʿĪsā, Ibrāhīm, or the other messengers. They affirm the original messages given to them and accept the Qur’an as the final criterion. This faith is not a vague spirituality. It shapes how a person sees time, responsibility, suffering, and the rights of others. (2:215 — **“for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor”**) Because the meaning of birr governs 2:177, giving wealth despite love of it arises from the meaning of birr. It follows that fulfilling promises completes the meaning of birr through giving wealth despite love of it.

**GIVING WEALTH DESPITE LOVE OF IT**

***“who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars”***, ***“and for freeing captives”***, ***“who establish prayer, pay alms-tax”*** establish giving wealth despite love of it. The righteous give wealth—giving cherished wealth—despite loving it. The phrase recognizes that wealth is naturally attractive. Islam does not demand that people pretend money has no utility or that they have no affection for what they earned. It asks them to overcome possessiveness when another person has a legitimate need. The recipients are listed broadly. Relatives have a claim because generosity should begin with family without becoming tribal favoritism. Orphans need protection because they have lost parental care. The needy may lack sufficient provision. The traveller may be stranded far from resources. Those who ask should be treated with dignity, even when a person cannot give. Freeing captives points to liberation from unjust bondage and the restoration of human dignity. This spending includes obligatory alms and voluntary charity, though the verse’s wording is broader than alms alone. It can include food, medical assistance, debt relief, education, shelter, and advocacy for the oppressed. The aim is not only to transfer money but to remove vulnerability and restore people’s ability to live with dignity. The verse joins *prayer* and *alms*, as the Qur’an often does. Prayer repairs the servant’s relationship with Allah, while alms purifies wealth and repairs social relations. Giving alms means calculating it accurately, paying it to eligible recipients, and refusing to treat wealth as absolute ownership. The pairing prevents two distortions. A person cannot claim spirituality while neglecting the poor, and he cannot claim social activism while refusing the worship that gives justice its ultimate foundation. The body turns toward the qiblah, and wealth turns toward those whom Allah has made deserving. (59:7 — **“As for gains granted by Allah to His Messenger from the people of ˹other˺ lands, they are”**) The reason fulfilling promises follows giving wealth despite love of it lies in the meaning of birr. Therefore, fulfilling promises returns practical force to giving wealth despite love of it.

**FULFILLING PROMISES**

For fulfilling promises, the decisive wording is ***“and keep the pledges they make”***, ***“and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity”***, ***“and in ˹the heat of˺ battle. It is they”***. *Those who fulfil their promises*—those who keep their pledges—are righteous. Trustworthiness includes personal promises, contracts, debts, covenants, employment agreements, marriage responsibilities, and public commitments. A believer does not use religious language to escape a written obligation or exploit a technical loophole against the purpose of an agreement. The Qur’an commands fulfilment of covenants and describes breaking promises as a sign of hypocrisy. The Prophet ﷺ identified breaking a promise as one of the signs of a hypocrite, while the hadith does not mean that every broken promise under every circumstance makes a person a hypocrite. It warns against making commitments carelessly or breaking them deliberately. The verse ends by naming patience in poverty, hardship, and battle. Poverty tests basic provision; hardship includes illness, fear, grief, and oppressive circumstances; battle tests courage, discipline, and moral restraint. The list shows that righteousness is proven when values are costly. Patience—*ṣabr*—does not mean accepting preventable oppression without action. The poor should be supported, the sick treated, and the oppressed defended. The patient person seeks lawful relief without abandoning faith, justice, or self-control. In war, patience includes refusing aggression, protecting non-combatants, honouring agreements, and stopping when justice requires it. Courage without restraint is not Qur’anic righteousness. (4:136 — **“Indeed, whoever denies Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers”**)

**TRUTHFULNESS AND TAQWĀ**

Read under truthfulness and taqwā, the relevant clauses are ***“who are true ˹in faith˺”***, ***“and it is they”***, ***“who are mindful ˹of Allah˺”***. It describes a direction of life in which belief, worship, generosity, reliability, and patience support one another. A believer can fail and repent; he cannot make hypocrisy his settled method while claiming that external symbols alone prove righteousness. *They are the mindful*—*al-muttaqūn*. Mindfulness is awareness and protective obedience to Allah. It guards the believer from showing off in charity, abandoning prayer in private, breaking promises when no one can enforce them, or using hardship as an excuse for injustice. The final measure is not public appearance but sincerity before Allah. The verse is a charter of integrated faith. It includes creed without abstraction, ritual without formalism, charity without vanity, social duty without secular emptiness, and patience without passivity. The qiblah matters because Allah commands it, but it becomes spiritually meaningful when the person who faces it also faces poverty, injustice, temptation, and responsibility with truth. The Prophet ﷺ embodied this integration. He worshipped at night, fulfilled covenants, fed the poor, protected orphans, forgave when justice allowed, and stood firm under persecution. His Sunnah shows how the list becomes a life rather than a slogan. Righteousness is like a tree more than a painted sign. Faith is its root, worship gives it a trunk, generosity and fidelity spread as branches, and patience is the strength that keeps it standing in bad weather. A sign can point east or west while remaining lifeless; a tree proves itself by fruit and endurance. The verse gives present-day believers the same test: public symbols matter within obedience, but a religious appearance that never feeds the needy, keeps a promise, or withstands hardship has not reached the life described here. (2:285 — **“The Messenger ˹firmly˺ believes in what has been revealed to him from his Lord”**) In other words, truthfulness and taqwā answers the meaning of birr.
