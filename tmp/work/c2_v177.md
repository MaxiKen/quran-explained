**MEASURING RIGHTEOUSNESS**

***“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east”*** strikes the first blow at a piety of directions: the prayer's turning is necessary, but it is not the whole of the account. The verse continues, ***“or the west. Rather, the righteous are those”***, and with that pivot it moves from geography to character. I take the opening to answer the claim that turning to a direction summed up righteousness — the verse rebuilds the word itself. What follows is the most complete portrait of the good life in the Qur'an, and each element of the portrait is a load-bearing wall, and the verse builds the character out of pieces that can be practised one by one. The definition therefore functions as a schedule as well as a portrait.

**CREED UNDER THE CHARACTER**

The structure begins with belief before behaviour, because behaviour without its ground is display. The verse lists the creed in one sweep: ***“who believe in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Books”*** and then ***“and the prophets”***. The order is deliberate: faith in God leads to accountability on the Day, and the Day is administered by the angels, recorded in the Books, and taught by the prophets — one chain of authority from the throne to the street. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the reading that righteousness here is not faith alone but faith's completion: the creed is named first, and the duties follow on its ground. The reason the creed is enumerated item by item is that each item anchors a different virtue; belief in the Last Day anchors honesty when nobody is watching, and belief in the Books anchors the standard by which every claim of piety is checked. The verse asks for the specific convictions that produce the character it goes on to describe.

**MONEY, WORSHIP, AND PLEDGE**

The second movement spends what the heart loves: ***“who give charity out of their cherished wealth to relatives, orphans, the poor, ˹needy˺ travellers, beggars”*** and then ***“and for freeing captives”***. The adjective "cherished" is the whole test, because the gift is real only when it costs affection. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the two edges of the recipient list: the needy are those who shy away from asking, and the traveller is the passer-by far from home, the guest who has run out of road. The freeing of captives stands beside the feeding because both restore a person to their own life. Then the verse turns to the discipline of worship with ***“who establish prayer, pay alms-tax”*** and, continuing into loyalty, ***“and keep the pledges they make”*** — the covenant kept whether it was made with God or with another person. Placing the pledge inside righteousness gives ordinary business honesty the status of devotion. (3:92 — **“You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish.”**)

**NOT A SCOREBOARD**

A hearer may ask whether such a list is not impossible to complete, and whether ordinary people are therefore excluded. The objection mistakes the verse for a constitution rather than a scoreboard, because the elements are not weighed equally and no perfection is claimed by their enumeration. The prayer is established — performed steadily rather than spectacularly — and the charity is given from cherished wealth in whatever measure the giver can carry. The reason the list is long is that righteousness is a life rather than a moment; no single act exhausts it, and no ordinary day lacks the chance to perform some part of it. Each element remains permanently available — the pledge kept, the alms paid, the patient endurance of the hard morning. Read slowly, the verse places the ceiling beyond reach only for those who read it in one sitting; read across a life, it is a path with steps.

**EARNED, NOT CLAIMED**

The feeding of the needy is drawn as the mark of the faithful in (76:8 — **“and give food—despite their desire for it—to the poor, the orphan, and the captive”**), and Ibn ʿAbbās gives it as his report that the obligations come in their order — faith, then the duties, then the legal prescriptions. The patience named at the verse's close is an accusative of praise, and its occasions are abject poverty, illness, and peril at the height of battle, so the endurance is social and physical both. The verse closes by naming its people twice, and both names are earned rather than claimed: ***“and who are patient in times of suffering, adversity”***, ***“and in ˹the heat of˺ battle. It is they”***, ***“who are true ˹in faith˺”***, ***“and it is they”***, ***“who are mindful ˹of Allah˺”***. The application is to read one's own days against the list: creed held, wealth loosened, prayer established, pledges kept, and pressure endured without the loss of kindness. The list is not a single day's programme but a year's map, and each column recurs on its own schedule. Because the verse defines rather than commands in this place, the definition itself becomes a mirror: the reader can see which column is standing and which has been left at the door. The direction of the prayer is the beginning; the character behind the hands is the completion. The verse keeps both in view so that neither can be substituted for the other. Because the list runs from creed to endurance, the map of righteousness covers belief, wealth, worship, loyalty, and pressure — the five territories of a whole life.
