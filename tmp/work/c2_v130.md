**THE FOOL IN THE QUESTION**

The people who might turn away from what Abraham had are addressed next, and the question asked about them leaves no room for a respectable reply: ***“And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool!”***. The Book's word for the fool describes a person whose judgement has failed him rather than one who lacks information, and the exclamation marks the verdict as settled. The sūrah has just recorded the prayer of the founder, and the sentence now asks what kind of person would turn away from the way the founder prayed for. The question is addressed to the reader as much as to the communities around him, since the objection it anticipates could belong to anyone. The rejection of this way is a failure of understanding rather than a difference of taste, which is what the question assumes. Ibn ʿAbbās read rejection of Abraham's way as self-ruin and folly, while the choice in this world included prophethood, submission and a sound line of descendants.

**A MAN SELECTED IN THIS LIFE**

The sentence then states the position of the man whose faith is under discussion, and the word chosen for it recurs in the sūrah's treatment of the prophets: ***“We certainly chose him in this life”***. The choice belongs explicitly to this world, which places the honour before any mention of the reward that follows it. The Book uses the language of selection for prophets and for communities, and the same verb appears in the verses about the choice of the believers which the sūrah has already discussed. Another passage describes the man as one who followed the guidance of his Lord and was described as upright, which is the Book's fuller account of the choosing: (6:161 — **“the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists”**). The reader is being shown what the choosing looked like in a life rather than in a title.

**SELF-RUIN THROUGH REJECTING ABRAHAM**

The verse also has a practical function in the argument of the sūrah, since it turns the question of who belongs to Abraham into a question about what Abraham did. The communities that claimed him had made him one of their own, and the sentence identifies the content of his way as something separate from their claims: he was chosen, and his choosing is the standard against which the claims are measured. Another passage puts the matter in the form of a comparison and asks who could be better in faith than those who follow this way, which is the positive form of the question asked here: (4:125 — **“And who is better in faith than those who ˹fully˺ submit themselves to Allah”**). The argument narrows here to a single test, which the sūrah's treatment of inheritance has been preparing.

**A SHOP THAT TURNS AWAY ITS BEST CUSTOMERS**

Think of a shop that refuses to serve the customers its own founder taught it to expect, and that then complains that its shelves are full. The verse asks what to call a person who turns away from a way that was laid down by the man he claims as a father, and the word chosen for him is not a polite one. The comparison shows how the Book treats the rejection of guidance when the guidance has a clear history: the failure is one of judgement rather than of evidence, and the sentence says so in a question. Anyone who has watched a community inherit a tradition and keep only its name can see what the question is pointing at. The plain sense left behind is that a great inheritance can be refused by a person who thinks himself wise.

**WHERE THE CHOSEN MAN ENDED UP**

The outcome of that choosing in the next life is named at the close: ***“and in the Hereafter he will surely be among the righteous”***. The sentence connects the honor of this world to a standing in the next, which is the Book's pattern when it describes the people it approves. The word for the righteous is the same word used of the conduct that the sūrah has been demanding from the communities that received scriptures, and it places this prophet among those whose record is sound. The choosing stated earlier in the verse therefore ends where the Book says such choices end, which is why the sentence gives the man a standing in both worlds. The Book's argument about inheritance therefore ends with a statement of where the ancestor himself ended up, which leaves the claims of his descendants to be judged against it. The picture left is of a man whose faith, choice and destination are described in three clauses. Present-day appeals to Abraham are tested by whether they preserve his submission or use his name to defend the very pride he abandoned. The effect of calling rejection self-folly is to move the loss inside the rejecter, since Abraham's chosen standing cannot be diminished by another person's refusal.
