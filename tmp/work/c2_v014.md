**ONE CLAIM IS MADE IN THE OPEN MEETING**

The passage now lets us hear both sides of the double life. ***“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.””*** Ibn ʿAbbās places Abū Bakr and the Companions among those met in the first setting. The hypocrites do not merely keep quiet or speak with common care. They claim the same deep and true faith as the people before them. Their words seek safety, trust, and a share in any good that comes to the Muslim group. This scene adds detail to the broad claim of verse 8. There the tongue spoke; here the audience and aim come into view. The claim changes with the company because it was made to manage the company. A later passage shows the same split: (3:119 — **“When they meet you they say, “We believe.” But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage.”**). The meeting did not make them faithful, and the time apart did not make them cease to be so. The two scenes uncover one fixed heart using two forms of speech. Like a coin stamped with a new face each time it changes hands, their words take the mark most likely to pass in the group before them. The gain may be near, but each use makes true trust harder to restore.

**THEIR EVIL ASSOCIATES ARE HUMAN GUIDES INTO WRONG**

The scene turns with ***“But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you”***. The source wording calls these associates their devils. Early readers such as Ibn ʿAbbās understood the first setting to include chiefs, soothsayers, and leaders in evil. The translation brings out their role rather than limiting the line to unseen beings. The Qur’an itself speaks of (6:112 — **“enemies—devilish humans and jinn—whispering to one another with elegant words of deception.”**). A devil in this human sense is not a person one merely dislikes. It is one hardened in revolt who adorns falsehood, calls others to it, and uses rank to keep them near. This matters because their lead is moral and chosen, not a spell that erases the follower’s will.

The act of going back to these guides also shows where the hypocrites feel bound to give a true report. With believers they offer a claim. With their chiefs they offer assurance of firm loyalty. The private room strips the public words of their worth. This does not make every difference between public and private speech a sign of hypocrisy. Good sense may choose a mild word, keep a trust, or wait for the right time to speak. The fault here is a clash in allegiance: faith is claimed to one group while aid and approval are pledged to those who oppose it. Another passage shows (5:52 — **“those with sickness in their hearts racing for their guardianship”**) because they fear a turn of fate, then facing regret for what they hid. Fear of losing access can make a person serve both rooms, but it cannot make the two loyalties true at once.

**MOCKERY IS THE EXCUSE THAT EXPOSES THE PLOT**

They close their report with ***“we were only mocking.””*** Ibn Isḥāq transmits Ibn ʿAbbās’s explanation that their claim of faith was meant to fool and deride the believers. Mockery here is not a harmless laugh shared by friends. It treats the believers’ faith as a prop and their trust as proof that they can be played. It also lets the speakers boast before their chiefs. By calling the public claim a joke, they assure the private group that no real bond was formed outside it. Their humour is thus an act of rank: the mockers place themselves above people whose trust they use. The laugh also binds the hidden group, since shared scorn can serve as a harsh pledge that none of them will cross sides.

The Qur’an closes the easy shelter of such an excuse elsewhere. When people hid behind the same excuse, they were asked, (9:65 — **““We were only talking idly and joking around.” Say, “Was it Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger that you ridiculed?””**). The next answer is stark: (9:66 — **“Make no excuses! You have lost faith after your belief. If We pardon a group of you, We will punish others”**). Not every failed joke has this weight. The object, aim, and state of the speaker matter. In this verse, the mockery is joined to hidden denial and a planned false claim. It is the final word in a scheme, not a stray word followed by regret. What they call play therefore reveals the grave truth of what they chose.

**A DIVIDED TONGUE FORMS A DIVIDED LIFE**

The warning reaches beyond one meeting in Madinah. Today, people move through home, work, places of worship, private chats, and public screens. Some change of tone is normal and wise. A child, a close friend, and a large group need not be addressed in one style. The danger begins when the core claim itself changes for gain: loyalty before one side, contempt before the other, and no speech ruled by truth. Each audience then owns a piece of the speaker. Fear grows because one room may hear what was said in the next. Faith seeks a sounder form of life. Private words need not reveal every secret, but they must not betray the truth claimed in public. A believer may fail, fear people, or speak poorly without becoming one of the hypocrites named by God. The verse gives no right to read hidden creed from one uneven act. It does give a clear test for the self: one should note whether, when no faithful friend is present, one mocks what one praised in their company. The cure is to choose one Lord and one moral bond across all rooms. That wholeness may cost a false ally, a passing gain, or the thrill of a cruel joke. It frees the tongue from serving each new crowd and keeps the heart from being split by the masks it has to defend.
