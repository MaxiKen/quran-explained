**TWO ROOMS AND TWO VOICES**

The verse shows the same people in two settings, and the difference between the settings is the whole point: ***“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.””***, and then, in private, ***“But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you”***, ***“we were only mocking.””***. Ibn ʿAbbās identified the believers in the first scene as Abū Bakr and his companions, and the earlier reports describe the people gathered in the second as the leaders the hypocrites answered to. What the verse records is a single man saying opposite things on the same day, without embarrassment, because the two audiences never compare notes. The structure of the line is worth noticing. It does not say that they lied to the believers and then told the truth to their friends; it puts both statements side by side and leaves the reader to decide which one carried the man's weight. The second speech is the one with the detail in it, the one spoken where the speaker had nothing to gain by pretending. The verse does not need to add a comment. The reader who has ever watched a person change register when the door closes has already reached the conclusion, and the Book trusts him to reach it, which is why the two rooms are laid out side by side.

**WHAT THE TWO STATEMENTS REVEAL**

The first statement costs nothing in a city where the believers are gaining ground, and the second costs nothing among friends who share the same arrangement. That is what makes the double game sustainable for a season. The man is not torn between two loyalties in any painful way, because neither statement came from conviction. He is managing his options, and the verse makes the management visible by placing the two rooms side by side. Anyone who reads it carefully stops asking which statement was true and starts asking what sort of person can produce both without noticing the contradiction. There is a hard edge in the second phrase, the mocking, which the verse keeps even while it quotes the man's own defence. He explains the whole performance as a joke told to people he does not respect, and the explanation is meant to buy him credit with the group he is with. The Book repeats the same scene in another sūrah with the roles of the audiences reversed, and the wording there is just as flat: (2:76 — **“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.””**). In both places the account ends with a question that the speaker cannot answer: do you not understand?

**THE REGISTER TEST**

A practical test follows from the verse and costs the reader nothing to apply. Where does his own voice change? Most people can locate the shift in a moment: the sentence they would say in the mosque and the sentence they would say in the office, and how far apart the two are. The gap is not always hypocrisy. Prudence, courtesy, and the difference between two audiences are real things, and the Book does not ask a believer to be tactless. What it forbids is the deeper split in which the private voice holds the truth in contempt, and that is a different matter altogether. The remedy the sūrah offers is not to make both voices identical but to make the private one honest. If a person cannot say the same thing in both rooms, he should at least refuse to mock in either. A man who keeps his reservations to himself and speaks fairly in both places is not in the condition described here. The difference between him and the man in this verse is invisible from outside and decisive inside, and it is the difference between holding a position carefully and holding none at all. Today the reader can find out which one he is by noticing what he says when the meeting ends.
