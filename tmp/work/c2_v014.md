**ONE TONGUE BEFORE TWO COMPANIES**

The verse shows the pretender in the two settings that decide his character: ***“When they meet the believers they say,”***, ***“We believe.”***, ***“But when alone with their evil associates they say,”***, ***“We are definitely with you”***, ***“we were only mocking.”***. The two speeches are given in full and without comment, and the contrast between them is the whole of the lesson. Ibn ʿAbbās named the emigrants and the helpers as the people they greeted with the first sentence, and the leaders of the hostility as the company they spoke to in the second. The verse is a portrait, and the manner of the portrait is worth noticing: the sūrah has not accused the men of a crime, and it lets them convict themselves out of their own mouths. That is why the passage has kept its power in every generation, since the words fit anyone whose company changes the shape of his speech.

**PURPOSE BEHIND THE PUBLIC PROFESSION**

The word they used among the believers is a claim, and the Book reports it as a claim and not as a fact: ***“We believe.”***. The purpose behind the sentence is named in the second half of the verse, where the men describe their own activity in the company of their leaders. The Book states the same behaviour about the same class of people in a later sūrah where their oaths are the subject: (63:2 — **“They have made their ˹false˺ oaths as a shield”**). The wording shows the reason for the shield, which was to keep the advantages of living inside a community while working against it, and the Book leaves the resulting position to the reader's judgment. What turns on the picture is that the pretender's faith is a tool rather than a conviction, and the difference shows itself the moment the two companies are compared.

**THE COMPANY THAT HEARD THE TRUTH**

The second speech is not even a denial of the first, and the men admit their motive inside it: ***“we were only mocking.”***. The Book records the same confession from another group and answers it with a question that closes the door they were trying to slip through: (9:65 — **“Was it Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger that you ridiculed?”**). The reason this verse is placed after the exchanges about belief and corruption is that the reader has now seen the whole machine at work, and the machine ends in mockery of the very thing the men were pretending to accept. The early teachers described the pretenders of this passage as those whose inside is the opposite of their outside, and the two sentences in this verse are the evidence for that description. A community that hears this verse can protect its business from such men, and an individual who hears it can watch his own tongue for the same shift, since flattery in one room and contempt in another is not a complication of faith but its replacement.

**A GUEST WHO PRAISES THE MEAL AND MOCKS THE HOST**

Think of a guest who praises every dish at the table while the host is standing at the door, and who describes the house as a joke in the car on the way home. His praise was not false in the sense of being overheard, and none of it was connected to anything he believed. The verse gives the reader both sides of the same evening, and the comparison is uncomfortable because most people can recognise a moment when their speech bent to fit the company they were in. These days the same man can be found in any workplace or family, agreeing with everyone and belonging to no one, and the sūrah has already given the cure in its first verses by naming the marks of the people whose speech does not change. The verse ends without a threat in it, which is the Book's way of letting a reader draw his own conclusion while the sentence sits inside him.

**BOTH SPEECHES IN ONE VERSE**

The remainder of the passage answers the two speeches with the mockery the men had intended for others, and the answer is delivered in the next verse. The Book holds both sentences in one place for a reason, since the pretender's problem is not one lie but a divided life, and a divided life cannot be described in one place any more than it can be lived in one place. The single verse that carries both conversations makes the division visible in a way that two separate verses would not, and the reader who sees it can no longer pretend that the two speeches belong to two different men. That is why the two sentences are quoted one after the other without a word between them, because the Book wants the reader to hear the same voice in both and to draw the conclusion for himself.
