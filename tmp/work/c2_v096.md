**A GRIP ON LIFE TIGHTER THAN ANYONE'S**

The Book keeps a separate sentence for those who reject the revelation outright, and this verse describes the appetite that holds them here: (64:10 — **“As for those who disbelieve and reject Our revelations,”**). What follows turns the reluctance of the previous sentence into a picture of desire: ***“You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years.”***, ***“But even if they were to live”***, ***“that long, it would not save them from the punishment.”***, ***“And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do”***. The comparison is striking, since the community is placed ahead of every other people in its attachment to this world, and even ahead of those who worshipped other gods. The thousand years names the shape of the wish rather than a number anyone expected to reach, and the Book then removes the comfort the wish was meant to secure. What the passage describes is a desire so strong that it becomes visible to an observer, which is why the verse opens with a command to look rather than with a report about belief.

**THE COMPARISON WITH THE POLYTHEISTS AND WHAT IT MEANS**

The Book had every opportunity to make this an attack on the community's religion, and it makes its comparison in the language of appetite instead. A polytheist believes in no reckoning to come, so his attachment to the present is entirely rational within his own view; a people who claimed an exclusive share of the next world should have been the least attached to this one. The Book's observation is that the actual order is reversed, and it states the reversal plainly. Another passage makes the same contrast, reminding this community of the favours it received and of how little the recipients gave in return. Anyone who has seen a group profess a future hope while clinging to present arrangements will recognise the portrait, which is offered without a single theological argument attached.

**THE THOUSAND YEARS AND THE LIMIT OF THE WISH**

The wish for a thousand years is the Book's way of describing a desire that wants more of the same rather than something better, and the phrasing quietly exposes the imagination behind it. A person who expected to be taken to a better place would not ask for a longer stay in the transit camp, and the wish in this verse asks for exactly that. The Book then removes the benefit the wish was intended to buy, since a longer life does not postpone the meeting and does not diminish the account. The same logic runs through the sūrah's treatment of spending and loss elsewhere, where what is kept back turns out not to be kept at all. What the verse teaches is that the length of a life is not the measure of what the life is worth, and the wish for length says something about the wisher rather than about his prospects.

**THE GUEST WHO WILL NOT LEAVE THE WAITING ROOM**

Think of a traveller who has been told that a room is prepared for him, and who spends the night in the airport lounge asking for the flight to be delayed. The Book's picture of this community is the same figure written at the scale of a nation, and the wish for a thousand years is the request for one more day in the lounge. The verse does not say that the community disbelieved in the promised room; it says the preference for the lounge was visible to anyone who looked. A reader who has been offered something better than his present arrangement and has quietly asked for more time will recognise the posture, and the Book's command to look at it is addressed to him as much as to the people of the verse.

**AN APPETITE THAT CANNOT BE HIDDEN**

The verse's opening verb is about observation, and the Book uses it because this condition cannot be hidden for long. A person's attachment to the present leaks into his decisions, his spending, and the risks he will not take, and a community's attachment shows in the arrangements it defends. The closing clause states that God sees what such people do, which places the observation made by any bystander on a firmer footing than a bystander could claim. What the reader should take from the verse is a way of reading conduct rather than a verdict on a people, since the appetite it describes is available to anyone who has been given a claim about the future. The next verse turns to the Angel who brought the revelation, and the community's objection to him, which is the same reluctance in another form.
