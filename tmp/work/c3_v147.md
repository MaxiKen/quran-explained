**FORGIVENESS BEFORE FIRMNESS AND VICTORY**

***“And all they said was, “Our”*** And all they said was, 'Our Lord. Forgive our sins and excesses, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the disbelieving people' opens the historical company's mouth. And the opening's restrictive grammar is the verse's first teaching. Wa ma kana qawlahum illa an qalu. Their speech was nothing but that they said. The illa-clause. Under the blows. Prophets wounded, ranks thinned. The whole recorded output of history's steadfast companies was one sentence, and the sentence is a prayer. No recriminations, no despair-speeches, no bravado. The unfaltering identified, at their hardest hours, by their speech's economy. The tongue's whole traffic routed upward. (3:146 — **“˹Imagine˺ how many devotees fought along with their prophets”**) Prayer, rather than panic, becomes their first response.

**FEET MADE FIRM**

***“Lord! Forgive our sins and excesses,”*** The prayer's own anatomy is the passage's studied inheritance. For its text is Talut's field-liturgy expanded by one clause, and the expansion is the depth. Rabbana ghfir lana dhunubana wa israfana fi amrina. Our Lord, forgive us our sins and our excesses in our affair. The forgiveness-petition set first. Before the firmness, before the victory. History's steadfast, at their causes' bleeding edge, begging pardon as the battle-need's first item. The israf-word (the excesses. The overreaches and derelictions in the cause's own conduct. The archers' hill being Uhud's exact instance) confessed by companies whose record the census just certified unfaltering. ***“make our steps firm, and grant”*** This clause places moral correction before the request for victory. Defeats trace to the ranks' sins before the enemy's strengths. Whence the steadfast attack the true variable first. The account cleared, then the feet, then the field. Wa thabbit aqdamana. And make our steps firm. The firmness begged, not self-credited. Wansurna alal-qawmi l-kafirin. And grant us victory over the disbelieving people. The nasr asked plainly, at its one source. (3:148 — **“and the excellent reward of the Hereafter. For Allah loves the good-doers”**) In modern life, feet made firm matters because crisis prayers should begin with moral self-correction before they ask for stability and victory over opponents.

**VICTORY SOUGHT FROM ALLAH**

***“us victory over the disbelieving people.””*** The verse's placement completes the census's conversion into instruction. The preceding passage certified the company's endurance and sealed it with love for the steadfast. This verse publishes the endurance's engine. The prayer. The three-petition liturgy being, by the passage's own arrangement, what the loved perseverance runs on. Pardon, firmness, victory. In that order, at every era's Uhud. A reader shaped by forgiveness before firmness and victory and the pair carries the liturgy as issued equipment, with the restrictive clause as its discipline. At the blows, the speech audited. All they said was. The tongue's traffic under pressure being the census's own entry-test. While the payment-verse now follows to close the company's file at both worlds. So Allah gave them the reward of this world and the excellent reward of the Hereafter. Imagine a team repairing its own footing before asking for the opposing wall to fall. For forgiveness before firmness and victory, Ibn ʿAbbās reads the prayer as seeking forgiveness for sins and grave excess, firmness in battle, and victory over rejection. The prayer begins with fault because a faithful army does not treat itself as morally innocent merely because its cause is just; which means sins and excess can weaken a community from within, so forgiveness is sought before firmness and victory. This order protects the request from arrogance; therefore firm feet are then asked from Allah, which means courage is received as help rather than claimed as a natural possession. Victory comes last, after the inner account and the need for steadiness have been admitted; in other words the pattern offers a sound response to collective failure today. A group should not blame opponents while refusing to examine its own breaches of trust, waste, rivalry, or excess; hence self-examination is not surrender to the opponent. It clears the ground for disciplined action; this is why prayer and reform belong together: the tongue seeks pardon, the ranks correct their conduct, and the outcome is entrusted to Allah. Excess includes crossing a limit, which can happen even while pursuing a worthy aim. The prayer therefore asks pardon for both ordinary sins and failures committed through undisciplined zeal. Firmness after forgiveness is not stubborn attachment to the old method. It is steadiness on a corrected path, ready to receive victory without repeating the breach.
