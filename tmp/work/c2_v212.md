**GLITTER ON LOAN**

The glitter, the mockery, and the reversal are drawn in phrases that cross the whole day: ***“The life of this world has been made appealing to the disbelievers”***, ***“and they mock the believers. Those”***, ***“who are mindful ˹of Allah˺ will rank above them on the Day of Judgment.”***, ***“And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit”***. and the final phrase removes the ceiling from the provision. The sequence describes one world and one judgment, and the judgment reverses every rank the world has published. The mockery is dated by the poverty of the mocked and the glitter by the loans of the mockers, and both dates are printed in the same passage. Ibn ʿAbbās names the glitter's owners and the mocked by their actual names, and the naming keeps the verse in its first audience.

**MOCKING THE POOR**

The mockery described in the verse is dated by its target: the believers who are poor in the world's accounting are laughed at by the people whose glitter is on loan. The reports on the occasion name the mockers and the mocked in the same breath, and the naming keeps the satire anchored to real streets. The earlier portrait of the flatterer has already shown where the glitter leads; this verse shows what the glitter laughs at. The legislation of the worldly life states the value of the whole display in the plainest terms the scripture owns (47:36 — **“This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But if you are faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺,”**). Because the mockery is aimed at the poor, the verse turns the economics around: the mocked hold the rank that the mockers are borrowing against. The comparison is the point of the whole portrait: the decorations belong to the market of the hour, while the hunger belongs to the settlement of the season.

**RANKS REVERSED**

The reversal promised for the Day is stated without decoration: the mindful will rank above them, and the ranking answers the laughter of the market. The adornment of the worldly life is drawn in the same legislation as a borrowed ornament whose repayment date is built into the loan (18:46 — **“Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life”**). Because the reversal is dated by the Day, the mockery's laughter is a short-term instrument. The application is to hear the laughter of the glittering as a market signal that is already being reversed. Its arrival is scheduled with the precision of a court calendar, and the scheduling is what turns the promise into a notice rather than a passing mood.

**PROVISION WITHOUT LIMIT**

The close of the verse is addressed to anyone who has ever been laughed at for his faithfulness: Allah provides without limit to whoever He wills. The provision is not the world's glitter but the world's provision, and the difference between the two is the verse's whole economics. Because the provision is unlimited and the provisioner is named, the poor believer is placed inside an economy the mockers cannot see. The verse ends the scene by handing the mocked their account book and their rank. The glitter is returned to its owners and the provision to its Lord. The address to the hearer keeps the promise personal to the one who reads it aloud at the quiet desk of the family.

The adornment is described by the classical commentators with the inventory of the jeweller: the plants of the earth, the silver of the bowl, the fruit of the orchard, and the animal of the caravan. Each item of the inventory is a piece of the merchandise of the world, and the merchandise is offered at the price of the heart. Because the inventory is drawn with pleasure rather than contempt, the reader can recognize his own favourite item on the shelf. The beauty of the wish is the polish the merchant puts on the merchandise at the opening of the market. The one who buys the merchandise of the adornment at the price of the covenant is the one who has paid in the wrong currency. The hunger and the fear that visit the believer are the visits that return the merchandise to its true price. Reviewing the inventory of one's own desires is the homework the class leaves with every reader. The adornment is not the enemy; the pricing is the whole lesson. The shelf of the desires is restocked every morning, and the pricing of the shelf is the exercise of every evening. The morning restocks the shelf, and the evening keeps the ledger of the prices. The evening keeps the ledger beside the shelf and the two are read together at the closing.
