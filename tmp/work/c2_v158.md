**TWO HILLS NAMED AS SIGNS OF THE RELIGION**

Attention turns here to a matter of pilgrimage, and the verse begins by naming two hills beside the House: ***“Indeed, ˹the hills of˺ Ṣafa”*** and ***“and Marwah are among the symbols of Allah.”***. The word rendered as symbols carries the sense of visible marks by which a religion is recognised, so the sentence is not praising two pieces of rock but declaring that the practice performed there belongs to the religion that God Himself appointed. That declaration is placed in the sūrah at the point where the rites of the House are being established, after the account of Abraham and his prayer for the people of that place. The verse therefore ties the smallest details of the pilgrimage to the authority of the One who made the House a centre of worship in the first place.

**A PRACTICE THAT THE FIRST MUSLIMS HESITATED OVER**

The permission that follows is phrased as the removal of a scruple: ***“So whoever performs the pilgrimage”*** and ***“or minor pilgrimage, let them walk between ˹the two hills˺.”***. The hesitancy it addresses was real, since the walk between the hills had been performed in the age before Islam, and some of the believers feared that they would be preserving a custom of the idolaters. The memory of the community preserves the answer given to that fear, and the practice was confirmed when those who had walked it were told to take their rites from the one who walked them first, a report carried by Muslim. The hesitation was not a small matter, since a community newly freed from the customs of the age of ignorance is careful about what it keeps from them. The Book reclaims what was good in the practice of the ancestors by attaching it to the worship of God alone, which is exactly what the sentence does here. What the believer is told is that the walk is not a concession and not an embarrassment but a part of what the House was built for.

**THE WALK THAT REMEMBERS A MOTHER'S SEARCH**

The walk between the two hills is connected in the tradition of the community with the search of Hagar for water for her son in that valley, when she ran from one height to the other looking for help. The connection is worth naming because it explains why the movement has the shape it has, being partway between walking and running, and why it belongs to a place where a woman was answered rather than to a place where anything is worshipped. The ritual has therefore been read as a remembrance of the mercy that met a desperate mother at the very spot, which the House was later built to honour. Nothing in the sentence asks the pilgrim to reproduce her fear, but the motion of the body carries the memory of it. What is walked between the hills is not a distance but an answer.

**A TRAVELLER WHO RETRACES AN OLD ROAD**

Think of a man who arrives in a town where his grandfather had once been rescued from hunger, and who walks the same road the old man had walked in fear. The road itself proves nothing, and the walking of it is not an act of worship of the ground, yet the man who retraces it does something the residents cannot do, because he carries the memory of a rescue into the streets that saw it. The verse gives the pilgrim exactly that position, with one difference: the rescue remembered here was granted in answer to a prayer, and the walk is performed as a rite because God has named the hills among His marks. Anyone who has visited a place where his family was saved will understand why the motion is kept. The sentence honours both the place and the mercy.

**THE GOOD DONE FREELY, AND ITS ANSWER**

The verse closes by naming a wider principle than the rite itself: ***“And whoever does good willingly, Allah is truly Appreciative, All-Knowing”***. The word rendered as willingly marks the deed that is not owed, and the sentence promises that such a deed is neither wasted nor unnoticed, so the pilgrim who adds more than the rite requires is met with a recognition that exceeds the act. The two names at the end of the sentence match the promise, since the Appreciative is the One who rewards the smallest offering, and the All-Knowing is the One who sees the intention behind it. Another passage places the honouring of the symbols of God among the proofs of a heart that fears Him: (22:32 — **“And whoever honours the symbols of Allah, it is certainly out of the piety of the heart”**). The passage ends where the pilgrimage itself ends, with a deed freely offered and fully known.
