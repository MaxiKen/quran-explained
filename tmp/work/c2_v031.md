**A LESSON GIVEN BEFORE ANY QUESTION WAS ASKED**

The answer to the angels is still being explained, and now the reader is shown how it was demonstrated: ***“He taught Adam the names of all things”***, ***“then He presented them to the angels”***, ***“and said, “Tell Me the names of these”***, ***“if what you say is true?””***. The scene is a test, and the test is fair because the material was placed in front of both parties. The angels had spoken about what the earth would produce, and here is a question about what the earth contains, put to them by the one who made it. Nothing depends on strength or on seniority. The whole matter turns on what a creature has been given, and the giving is the point of the sentence rather than the answering.

What Adam was taught is described as the names of all things, and the teachers of the earliest generation understood that broadly. Some read it as the names of everything that exists, and some extended it to the names of the descendants to come. Either way, the phrase is about the capacity to identify, distinguish, and speak about the world, which is the ability every reader is using at this moment. The Book elsewhere presents the same gift as what lifts a person above the animals, and the verse in this sūrah presents it as what settles a dispute in the highest assembly. Knowledge is being honoured here, not as a possession to be boasted about, but as something received from a teacher. The Book names the same gift at the head of the sūrah of the Most Compassionate: (55:4 — **“˹and˺ taught them speech”**), and it repeats it where the first revelation is described: (96:5 — **“taught humanity what they knew not”**).

**AN EXAMINATION IN THE HIGHEST ASSEMBLY**

The command to name the things is framed as a challenge, and the closing clause makes the challenge explicit. The angels had spoken with confidence about the human being, and the verse answers confidence with an examination. A reader should notice what the examination does not do. It does not shame the angels, and it does not accuse them of malice. It simply brings out the difference between a creature with an opinion and the one who knows, and the conditional clause at the end opens the door for that difference to appear. The rest of the scene answers the question for them, and it answers it in the most direct way available: by letting the facts speak through the creature they were about.

The Book reports the same exchange in other places with different details, and the differences are instructive. In one account the angels are told that a spirit will be breathed into the clay figure: (15:29 — **“So when I have fashioned him and had a spirit of My Own ˹creation˺ breathed into him,”**), and in another the same words are followed by the command to prostrate: (38:72 — **“fall down in prostration to him.””**). The scene in this sūrah stands before those commands and supplies the reason for them: what is being honoured in Adam is not his clay but what he was taught. What is being honoured in Adam is not his clay but what he was taught, and the teaching came first.

**WHY KNOWLEDGE IS THE FIRST DIGNITY**

The verse establishes something that the rest of the chapter will keep assuming: a human being is dignified by what he has learned, not by what he owns or by the material he was made from. The angels looked at the material when they asked their question, and the answer moved their attention to the knowledge. For the reader, the lesson is both encouraging and sobering, because knowledge is not a status a person is born with. It is given, it can be sought, and it can be neglected. The Book returns to this theme whenever it names the people of knowledge as the ones who truly fear their Lord, and it never treats the two things as separate achievements.

The practical use is plain. A reader can take one thing he has been content to know vaguely, in his religion or in his work, and learn it properly this month, because the capacity he is using was given to him for that purpose. Think of an apprentice handed the keys to a workshop: the tools were not made for him, and the trade he learns with them becomes his own. The Book does not ask every person to be a scholar; it asks him not to be careless about what he carries. The names Adam was given were the raw material he then had to use, and the same is true of everything a reader learns. The scene in this verse is not a story about a distant ancestor so much as a warning about what a person does with the capacity he has been handed, and the warning applies today as much as it did at the first lesson.
