**THE LESSON THAT SETTLED THE ARGUMENT**

The answer to the angels' question arrives in the form of a lesson: ***“He taught Adam the names”***, ***“of all things”***. The early teachers differed over what the names covered, and their readings are all worth knowing. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with the reading that they were the names people use in ordinary speech, down to the pot and the plate, while Mujāhid read them as the names of everything that exists, and a third reading takes them as the names of the angels themselves. The difference between the readings does not touch the point of the verse, which is that knowledge was given to the human being that the angels did not have. The Book states the same gift of teaching in its earliest revelation, where the Lord is described as the one who taught by the pen: (96:4 — **“Who taught by the pen—”**). The gift recorded here is therefore not an episode in a single story but the pattern by which this creature was made to live, since he is the only one in the scene who is given a lesson. A reader who wonders what made this creature fit for the earth is given the answer in the first word of the sentence.

**THE PRESENTATION AND THE CHALLENGE THAT WENT WITH IT**

The verse then stages the test: ***“then He presented them to the angels”***, ***“and said”***, ***“Tell Me the names of these”***, ***“if what you say is true?”***. The last clause refers back to the claim the angels had made about their own worthiness, and the early teachers read it as the reproach of a claim that had been made in good faith and without the full picture. The Book continues the same scene in the verse that follows this one, where the command is given to Adam to inform the angels of the names: (2:33 — **“O Adam! Inform them of their names.”**). The reason the test is public rather than private is that the angels had spoken in public, and the honour of the human being had to be settled in the same assembly. What turns on the scene is that knowledge is the qualification for the appointment, and the verse lets the reader see how the qualification was granted rather than assuming it.

**THE VOICE THAT SPEAKS AGAIN IN THE NEXT VERSE**

The scene is left hanging at the end of this verse, since the angels have been asked for the names and the answer comes in the verse that follows. The pause is deliberate, and the reader who has followed the sūrah has already been told what the angels will say, because the sūrah recorded their earlier words about the worship of God. The Book uses the same technique of a suspended answer in the passage where the fire and its keepers are described, and the effect there is the same as here, which is to make the reader wait for a reply he cannot supply himself. The reason the question is left unanswered is that the angels' answer is worth hearing in full, since it becomes a model of how a creature should respond to a limit it cannot cross. The Book teaches by scenes like this one, and the reader who watches the angels handle a question they cannot answer is being taught something the command alone could not deliver. The reader who has his own limits in front of him is being shown the right posture before he is shown the correction.

**A STUDENT ASKED TO NAME THE TOOLS OF A TRADE**

Think of a workshop where the master asks his apprentices to name the tools on the bench, and where the newest boy is the only one who knows every one of them. The master does not shame the older apprentices; he shows them what the owner of the workshop had seen in the boy. The verse stages the same demonstration with the whole creation as the bench and the angels as the onlookers, and it does it to settle a question about worth. These days a person can measure himself by the noise of his claim and forget that the appointment in this world was given with a lesson attached, and the passage asks him what he has actually been taught in order to carry it. The sūrah goes on to record the answer of the angels, which is the answer a humble creature gives when the question is beyond him.
