**THE OBJECT OF THE SENTENCE, UNPACKED**

A single pronoun in the verse carries the weight of the whole event: ***“So We made their fate an example to present”***. What the pronoun refers to has been settled two ways by the earliest glosses, and both routes run through Ibn ʿAbbās. One takes the pronoun as the punishment itself, the metamorphosis pronounced and performed; the other takes it as the community that was transformed. Further candidates appear later in the lists — the town, and less convincingly the fish that had been the instrument of the test — and the reason the first reading is stronger is that the previous verse ends on the verdict, so that what stands nearest to hand is what a pronoun takes. The word for example then supplies the teaching function of the thing. Its root family belongs to the fetter and the restraint, and the verbal form means to do to one party what holds others back from the same act. A lesson in this vocabulary is therefore not a story told for its own sake. It is a restraint fixed in public view. The making in the sentence is thus a making of the event into a permanent deterrent, and the difference between the event and the example is the difference between a punishment that ended in three days and a restraint that has not ended.

**HELD BEFORE AND BEHIND EVERY LATER AGE**

The reach of the example is drawn in an idiom of place: ***“and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing”***. The phrase for present and future generations is literally what stands in front and what stands behind, and the early glosses give the picture its full width. One reading takes it as the people of that time and everyone who came after them; another takes it as the towns near the scene and the towns far from it; the spatial phrase has been applied to both dimensions and both are true of the example’s reach. The Book itself performs the same work when it repeats the verdict of this town in its own ledger of punishments: (7:166 — **“But when they stubbornly persisted in violation, We said to them, “Be disgraced apes!””**). Repetition inside revelation is itself the mechanism of the deterrence. In other words, a story held before and behind cannot be mislaid by a later generation, because every age stands somewhere in front of it or somewhere behind it. The purpose clause that governs the whole arrangement is therefore complete: the event is kept in view so that the fear of it works in advance, restraining a person before he reaches the device that would make a sacred law into a technicality. History in this Book is never decoration. It is instrumentation, set where the living will pass.

**SIGNS THAT BENEFIT ONLY THE GOD-FEARING**

The closing designation narrows the audience of the counsel to those who stand in awe of their Lord. The word it renders has two capacities in the language, counsel and reprimand, and both senses stand here, since the example comforts the obedient and frightens the defiant in one and the same telling. The God-fearing are singled out because they are the party that actually benefits. The point is that a sign meets different hearts differently. In one it settles the fear that keeps a duty intact; in another it passes as news of someone else’s misfortune. Ibn ʿAbbās gives the closing phrase a face rather than a category: the God-fearing named here are the Prophet and his Companions, the generation standing at the example’s far edge and reading it as instruction. The Book states the same division of hearing in its own account of why stories are told at all: (11:120 — **“And We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ the stories of the messengers to reassure your heart.”**). The same narration that reassures one audience warns another. What the verse therefore settles is the moral economy of history. The example stands in public view for everyone, and it works only where a heart has already chosen to fear its Lord; that is why the verse leaves the counsel with the God-fearing rather than distributing it equally to all who hear.
