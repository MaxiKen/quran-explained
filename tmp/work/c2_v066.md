**A PUNISHMENT MADE INTO A WARNING FOR OTHERS**

The fate of the Sabbath-breakers is turned outward in this verse, and that is the whole of its work: ***“So We made their fate an example to present”***, ***“and future generations, and a lesson to the God-fearing”***. The word behind the translation "example" is not the ordinary word for a sign; it carries the sense of a punishment that is displayed so that others will be deterred by it and go another way. Two audiences are named, and the two are not the same kind of people. The generations are those who will hear about it later, and the God-fearing are those who will take the warning seriously, which the Book names because a warning does nothing for a reader who has already decided that it is not addressed to him. The verse therefore says something that many readers would rather not hear: a punishment can serve a purpose beyond the offender, and the Book is telling the reader plainly that this one does.

**AN ACCOUNT THAT ASKS FOR A CERTAIN READER**

The closing clause identifies the people who profit from the incident, and the identification is worth pausing over. A person who already fears God hears the story and finds in it a reason to keep to the command, and the same account leaves a careless reader with nothing but an item of history. This is the difference the Book keeps drawing between the two ways of reading a warning: the one who obeys before he is caught and the one who learns only from consequences. The same sūrah uses the same logic when it warns its readers of a fate that would be worse than the one described here, in the way their faces could be turned backwards and the people behind the warning stand as a precedent: (4:47 — **“O you who were given the Book! Believe in what We have revealed—confirming”**). The verse records a punishment that has already been paid by others precisely so that a later reader does not have to pay it himself.

**THE EXAMPLE AS PUBLIC HISTORY**

The Book's habit of turning events into public history deserves its own note here, because it explains why the story was placed in the middle of this chapter rather than in a chronicle. A community remembers what it is told to remember, and the Book tells it to remember this, which is why the verse assumes the reader has heard the account before it is repeated; the same assumption appears in another passage where the audience is told to ask about the town by the sea, which is the same story twice removed. The effect of retelling is that the offence is stripped of its particulars, since the fish and the day are left behind and what remains is a settled rule about what happens to people who arrange their lives around breaking a command. The reader receives a case with a verdict in it, and the verdict is meant to be carried rather than stored.

**KNOWING AN ACCOUNT AND PROFITING FROM IT**

There is a real gap between hearing an account and taking a lesson from it, and the verse names that gap when it says the lesson belongs to the God-fearing. The reader can test the difference in his own house, since the same story about a family's money that one son treats as a warning another treats as a joke, and the story is not the variable. What the Book calls fear of God is not a mood; it is the settled condition of a person who expects to be held to what he knows, and it is the soil a warning needs in order to produce anything. That is why the verse puts the account of the coast behind it and then refuses to promise that everyone who hears will improve, since the promise is made to a quality of reader rather than to a quantity of information.

**TURNING AN ACCOUNT INTO A DECISION**

A warning that is never applied becomes a story, and the Book's closing clause tells the reader which of the two he should aim for. The practical effect of the verse is that the punishment is no longer only about the people of the town; it is described as standing in front of later generations, which includes anyone reading the page. The reader who takes the account seriously will look at the rules he has learned to satisfy in form, since that was the offence, and will treat a small deliberate breach as the beginning of a longer road rather than as an exception. The verse ends on the God-fearing, and in doing so it quietly asks the reader which of the two companies he is standing in.
