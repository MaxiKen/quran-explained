**THE REPLY THAT NEEDED NO PAUSE**

This verse is the shortest exchange in the whole account, and it records a command and its acceptance in a single sentence: ***“When his Lord ordered him, “Submit ˹to My Will˺,” he responded, “I submit to the Lord of all worlds.””***. The Book reports no deliberation between the two halves, and the absence of a pause is the point of the sentence. The command is given in the same form the prayer of the previous verses used, and the answer returns the word to its giver in a longer and more specific phrase. The verse therefore shows the submission that Abraham had asked for on behalf of himself and his descendants being performed in his own case first. Anyone who has asked God for a quality and then been tested in it will recognise the sequence.

**NO PAUSE BETWEEN THE TWO HALVES**

The Book's prophets are shown receiving instructions in different ways, and this one is given as a direct address with no accompanying explanation. The sentence does not record what the man understood the submission to involve, and it does not record him asking either. Another passage places the same prophet in a scene where he is asked to explain his faith, and his answer there is a statement of what he was guided to rather than a defence: (6:161 — **“the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists”**). The brevity of this verse is a statement about the man as much as about the command, since the Book's account of him turns on the speed of his compliance.

**WHO THE ANSWER NAMES**

The response is worth reading closely because it names the One obeyed rather than the command received: he submits to the Lord of all worlds. The phrase is a declaration about the reach of the authority being accepted, and it answers the command by stating the scope of what is being handed over. The Book has already used the same title in the sūrah's opening verses, where the Lord of all worlds is named as the one who sent down the Book, so the reader meets a phrase here that the chapter has taught him from its start. A submission made to a local or tribal deity would have been a smaller thing; the phrase chosen by the man makes his acceptance universal, and the Book records the choice of words without comment. The argument about the religion of Abraham reaches its plainest statement in the man's own mouth.

**A SERVANT WHO ANSWERS THE CALL BY NAME**

Think of a craftsman who is asked to hand over his workshop and who replies by naming the owner of the city rather than the man who spoke to him. The answer in the verse has that quality, since it bypasses the command and addresses the commander, and the acceptance is complete because the person is. The comparison also shows why the Book treats this exchange as a definition of the faith it is discussing: submission is not the performance of one instruction but the handing over of oneself to the one who gives it. Anyone who has tried to obey in a piecemeal way will find the sentence's brevity instructive, since the answer given here is about a Lord and not about a task. The shape of a whole acceptance is what the sentence offers.

**THE PLACEMENT OF THE EXCHANGE**

The sūrah records the exchange between the prayer for a community and the advice the man later gave his children, and its position is part of its meaning. The verse shows the ancestor doing what he had asked God to make him and his descendants do, which closes the circle opened by the earlier request. The Book follows the same method in other places, where a prophet's prayer is answered by a scene in which the prophet himself acts out what he asked for. The prayer for a submitting nation and the founder's own submission in one sentence together give the whole account: what is at stake is a handover rather than a heritage. What remains when the sentence ends is the example it set for the family that came after.
