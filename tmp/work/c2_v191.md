**RETURNING TO THE HOMES**

The command's phrases trace the map from the first return to the last reward: ***“Kill them wherever you come upon them”***, ***“and drive them out of the places from”***, ***“which they have driven you out.”***, ***“For persecution is far worse than killing.”***, ***“And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque”***, ***“unless they attack you there.”***, ***“If they do so”***, ***“then fight them”***, ***“that is the reward of the disbelievers”***. The walk runs from the homes to the sanctuary and ends at the payment owed to the one who refuses the peace, which is the whole map in miniature. The orientation between two cities and one sanctuary is the discipline the command requires of its reader. The condition Ibn ʿAbbās records is visible in the order of the phrases.

Kill them wherever you come upon them and drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out. reads as an order of restoration: the verb of the second half answers the verb of the first, and the city's map is being put back. The expulsion of the believers from their homes is the wound the verse addresses, and the return is the closing of that wound. The report of Ibn ʿAbbās keeps the condition in view — the slaying is for those who start the fighting — and the expulsion is measured against the one already suffered. Because the verse names the places of the driving-out, the return is not conquest but restitution: the evicted come back to what was theirs. The command's symmetry is its justice, and the symmetry is the reason the verse can be read as the end of an exile rather than the beginning of a war.

**EXPELLED AND REPLACED**

The two expulsions — theirs of the believers, the believers' of them — are stated in one verse so that no memory can keep only one. The verse that follows names the deeper conflict beneath the physical one, since the persecution the believers endured was designed to separate them from their worship. The scripture's complaint against the persecutors is therefore theological before it is territorial: the crime of the expulsion is that it was aimed at the religion. Because the believers were driven out for their worship, the return restores the worship along with the homes. The community that re-enters the city carries the mission that was being suppressed, and the restoration is complete only when the mission stands where it stood. The geography of the verse is measured in the direction of the worship.

**PERSECUTION WEIGHS MORE**

The verse states its own hierarchy in the middle of the command: For persecution is far worse than killing. The comparison is deliberate — the killing of a body ends, while the persecution of a religion continues inside every life it touches. The claim returns in the later legislation on the sacred months, where the same weights are used to answer the question of fighting during them (2:217 — **“For persecution is far worse than killing. And they will not stop fighting you until they turn you away from your faith—if”**). Because the hierarchy is stated twice in the chapter, it is meant to govern the reader's judgment in every case where the two goods are weighed. The one who forbids resistance to persecution has inverted the scales the revelation has calibrated twice. The verse does not glorify killing; it locates it on the lesser pan.

**A SHIELD AROUND THE SANCTUARY**

The command carries its own sanctuary law: And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they attack you there. The mosque is granted a peace that overrides the general permission, and the exception is only the attack that breaks the peace first. The arrangement shows the mission's own priorities — the sanctuary is sacred even to those who denied its sanctity — and the restraint is the verse's proof of the point. Because the permission is limited by the sanctuary, the army of the verse is an army with a conscience and a map. The application is to read the permission together with its exceptions before quoting its verbs, since the exceptions carry the mercy the verbs lack. The one who fights inside a sanctuary after reading this verse has read half of it. The careful reader keeps the shield in place. The shield's existence is the proof that the permission was designed by a mind that also designs sanctuaries. The verse's restoration logic also answers the accusation that the mission was a war for territory: the territory named is the territory lost, and the verb that governs the map is the verb of return. Because the exile is the wound, the return is the healing, and the healing is the verse's purpose. The one who re-reads the passage from the first expulsion will find the whole command standing on one injury. The injury is named in the verse itself, and the naming is what keeps the permission from becoming a mood.
