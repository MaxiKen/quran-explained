**READ THE VERSE WITH ITS CONTEXT**

At read the verse with its context, attention falls on ***“Kill them wherever you come upon them”***, ***“and drive them out of the places from”***, ***“which they have driven you out.”***. This āyah must be read together with 2:190 and the verses that follow it. The pronouns refer to the hostile forces who fought the Muslims, persecuted them, and expelled them from Makkah—not to every non-Muslim person in every place and time. Verse 2:190 begins with *those who fight you* and prohibits transgression; verse 2:192 commands cessation if they stop. Removing those limits turns a passage about an armed conflict into a meaning the surrounding text explicitly rejects. The command belongs to a community in war, under lawful authority, against those responsible for aggression and expulsion. It is not permission for individuals to attack civilians, neighbours, visitors, or people of another faith. The Prophet ﷺ protected non-combatants, honoured treaties, granted safety, and forbade killing women and children. The believers had been driven from their homes because they worshipped Allah. Their property was seized, families were divided, and the persecution continued. The command to expel the aggressors from the places from which they expelled the Muslims describes a military response to an established campaign of expulsion. It is not a general doctrine of ethnic replacement or forced removal. The Qur’an’s language of justice matters: the response is tied to the crime. Muslims do not gain a right to harm a population merely because another generation once committed an offence. Contemporary conflicts must be judged by their present facts, lawful authority, and the protection of civilians, not by a verse detached from its occasion. (2:190 — **“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you”**) Because read the verse with its context governs 2:191, fitnah as persecution and religious coercion arises from read the verse with its context. **FITNAH AS PERSECUTION AND RELIGIOUS COERCION**

Fitnah as persecution and religious coercion draws together ***“For persecution is far worse than killing.”***, ***“And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque”***. Persecution here denotes persecution, coercion, and the attempt to force people away from their faith through torture and violence. It can also include the disorder created when people are prevented from worshipping Allah and are driven from their homes. The statement that fitnah is worse than killing does not make killing trivial. It describes the grave, sustained harm of a system that attacks people’s lives, faith, safety, and families. The Qur’an uses persecution in more than one sense, including trial, temptation, and civil strife. Its meaning here is determined by the context of persecution and armed aggression. Muslims must not use the word as a vague label for anyone who disagrees with them. The verse gives a special restriction: do not fight at al-Masjid al-Ḥarām unless the aggressors fight there. The sanctuary is a place of safety and worship, and violence within it is not the normal rule. The exception addresses an enemy who violates that sanctity and attacks first; even then, the command is framed as a response to combat, not a licence to initiate bloodshed in the sanctuary. The broader Qur’anic principle is that the Sacred House and the sacred months are protected. Muslims must respect sacred spaces and avoid turning them into military arenas. If an armed force attacks from such a place, the matter falls under the rules of defence and legitimate authority; individual people may not manufacture a crisis to justify violence there. **CESSATION REMAINS THE GOAL**

***“unless they attack you there.”***, ***“If they do so”*** mark the turn toward cessation remains the goal. The next verse says that if the opponents cease, Allah is forgiving and merciful, and 2:193 says that if they cease, there is no hostility except against oppressors. Thus the command is not a demand to continue killing after the aggression ends. The lawful objective is to stop persecution and restore security, not to destroy people because they once fought. The Prophet ﷺ accepted surrender and peace when enemies stopped fighting. He also granted safety to those who sought it, as required by 9:6. These practices show that the command is bounded by conduct and can end when the cause of fighting ends. The closing statement describes the recompense of those who rejected truth and committed aggression. It is not a formula that makes disbelief alone a capital crime. The immediate subject is the hostile disbelieving force engaged in persecution and war. The Qur’an repeatedly distinguishes peaceful non-Muslims from those who fight and expel believers. The distinction is essential for Islamic ethics. Muslims may disagree with a creed while still protecting its adherents from unlawful harm. Religious difference is addressed through invitation and evidence; armed aggression is addressed through lawful defence and justice. (60:8 — **“Allah does not forbid you from dealing kindly”**)

**THE MORAL DISCIPLINE OF WAR**

The verse develops the moral discipline of war with ***“then fight them”***, ***“that is the reward of the disbelievers”***. Even in the harshest conflict, the believer remains answerable to Allah. He must verify targets, avoid revenge against relatives or civilians, respect safe conduct, release or exchange prisoners according to lawful arrangements, and stop when peace becomes possible. The grief of persecution does not permit transgression. A person who quotes this verse to justify terrorism, murder in public places, or attacks on houses of worship is not applying the Qur’an; he is violating 2:190, 2:192, 5:8, and the Prophet’s explicit teachings. The text’s first guardrail is context, and its second is justice. Expelling an aggressor is like a family removing an armed intruder from a family home: resistance is directed at ending the invasion, not at acquiring a licence to harm every person outside the door. The Sacred Mosque adds an especially guarded boundary, breached only when the enemy begins fighting there. Present-day readers must keep that setting attached to the severe wording. Detached slogans erase expulsion, persecution, and prior attack; faithful reading retains the restrictions that the passage repeats. (8:61 — **“If the enemy is inclined towards peace, make peace with them.”**) In other words, the moral discipline of war answers read the verse with its context.
