**A SENTENCE WITH ITS VERB LEFT UNSAID**

The sūrah opens with the words ***“In the Name of Allah”***, and the sentence leaves its own verb unspoken. Nothing in it states what is being begun, whether a recitation, a meal, a journey, or a contract. The verb is understood rather than written, and it takes its colour from the act that follows. That openness is deliberate. If the verb were written, the sentence would belong to one act and would have to be reworded for every other one, and a person would need a different formula for each part of his day. Because the wording carries no single act, the sentence can stand at the head of every act. A phrase reading *I begin my prayer* would belong to the prayer alone. A phrase reading *I begin my trade* would belong to the market. The bare name belongs to both, and to the ordinary hours between them. The Book places the same words at the head of a written message from one king to another: (27:30 — **“It is from Solomon, and it reads: ‘In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”**). It places them again at the head of the first revelation given to the Prophet, joined to the command to read: (96:1 — **“in the name of your Lord”**). The practice of the community followed the wording. A sentence first heard at the head of a scripture became the ordinary furniture of speech among the believers. They say it over meals and journeys, and they learned to say it before they learned to write.

**THE TWO NAMES AND THE MERCY THEY CARRY**

The sentence is completed by two titles taken from one root: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***. The first of the two is a name the Arabs reserved for God alone. They would speak of the mercy of the Most Compassionate covering this world and the next, and they did not give that word to a creature in that absolute sense. The second is formed on a pattern that describes the one who shows mercy, and it may be used of a person. The Book therefore uses it of the Prophet in a sentence about his treatment of the believers: (9:128 — **“gracious and merciful to the believers”**). The two titles announce a mercy that is already present and a mercy that keeps arriving. The first name describes what mercy is in itself; the second describes mercy in its work toward the one who receives it. Both are needed here, because the sentence is being spoken by a servant and not by a student of grammar. The Book states the reach of the first in a sentence addressed to Moses: (7:156 — **“My mercy encompasses everything”**). Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with the reading that the first name covers every creature in this life, since every living thing is fed and sheltered whether or not it acknowledges the giver. The second belongs especially to the believers, and it carries them to the end. The pairing also answers a fear that rises in a person who has just named God as the Lord of everything. The titles stand between the ownership and the servant like a hand held out before the first demand is made. One sūrah opens with the single name on its own, as though the reader were meant to wait for the rest: (55:1 — **“The Most Compassionate”**).

**HOW THE FIRST COMMUNITY SAID IT**

The practice of the earliest Muslims around this sentence was watched closely and reported, which shows that it was never treated as a small matter of preference. Anas ibn Mālik, who prayed behind the Prophet and behind the caliphs after him, reported that they began the recitation with the praise of God and did not mention the name aloud, and the report is carried by Muslim. Abū Hurayrah recited the name aloud when he led the prayer in Madinah, and he said afterwards that his prayer was the closest of them all to the prayer of the Messenger of God; that report too is carried by Muslim. A further report, carried by Abū Dāwūd from Ibn ʿUmar, says that the Prophet did not know a sūrah had ended until the name was revealed, which is how the community learned where one sūrah finished and the next began. On that basis the schools of law took their two positions. One counts the sentence as the first verse of this sūrah. The other counts it as a verse revealed to separate the sūrahs rather than as part of them. Neither position questions the practice of beginning with the name. The difference between them turns on how the seven verses are counted, not on whether the words are said. The reader is therefore not asked to take a side in order to obey the verse. He is asked to say the name, and the saying is what the verse settles.

**THE MARK A PERSON SETS ON THE DAY'S WORK**

Think of a craftsman who stamps the mark of his workshop into every piece he finishes, whether the piece is a door for a palace or a stool for a kitchen. The mark does not make the work better, and a buyer may never look at it. Yet it decides whose the piece is and who answers for it. The sentence in this verse gives the servant a mark of the same kind, and it costs him one breath. A person who begins his work with the name of God has already answered the question of whom he works for. The answer steadies him when the work grows difficult or when the gain grows tempting. A task done under a name is done under an eye. Anyone who has watched a colleague do work he did not intend will recognise how much the first sentence of a day decides, and how little it costs. These days the hours are crowded with beginnings, from messages and purchases to journeys and meetings. The verse teaches the servant to put the name at the head of those beginnings rather than at the edges of his life, so that the whole of the day stands inside one short sentence.
