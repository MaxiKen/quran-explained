**PERMITTED IN STAGES**

Read in sequence, the passage's phrases release the night and fence the day: ***“It has been made permissible”***, ***“for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast. Your spouses are a garment”***, ***“for you as you are”***, ***“for them. Allah knows”***, ***“that you were deceiving yourselves.”***, ***“So He has accepted your repentance”***, ***“and pardoned you. So now you may be intimate with them”***, ***“and seek what Allah has prescribed”***, ***“for you. ˹You may˺ eat”***, ***“and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night”***, ***“then complete the fast”***, ***“until nightfall. Do not be intimate with your spouses”***, ***“while you are meditating in the mosques. These are the limits set by Allah”***, ***“so do not exceed them. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to people”***, ***“so they may become mindful ˹of Him˺”***. The sequence releases first and fences second, which is the order mercy keeps: the gift of the household arrives before the boundary of the retreat. The permissions and the fences alternate exactly as the day and the night alternate in the month governed here. The report on Ibn ʿAbbās's side keeps the same order of permissions and fences.

It has been made permissible for you to be intimate with your wives during the nights preceding the fast. reads like a simple permission, but its shape is a history. The ruling arrived after an earlier practice in which the fasting person ate, drank, and slept only at night and left the intimacy of the night as well, and the permission is recorded as a release from that harder discipline. The movement of the law is from strictness to ease, and the ease is dated to the nights that surround the fast rather than to the fast itself. The night is given back to the household, and the day is given to the discipline. The verse keeps both halves visible so that neither can swallow the other. Because the permission is placed at the head of the passage, the month of fasting is established as a month of households and not of retreat alone. The lawful boundaries of the household's days are drawn with the same care as the fences of the retreat (2:222 — **“and do not have intercourse with your wives during their monthly cycles until they are purified. When they purify themselves,”**)

**GARMENT FOR BOTH SIDES**

The verse's most quoted line — Your spouses are a garment for you as you are for them — describes the marriage with the image of the closest covering a person owns. The garment hides the body's faults from the world, keeps warmth inside, and is worn rather than displayed, which is three qualities of a good marriage in one noun. Ibn ʿAbbās's wording on the image is brief: the spouses are a repose for one another, which is the interior of the garment — the comfort no one outside sees. The symmetry of the phrasing refuses the household where one side is clothed and the other bare; each covers the other or neither is dressed. Because the image is placed in the fasting month's legislation, the discipline of the day is answered by the mercy of the night. The garment image also settles the question of priority inside the household, and the mercy named beside the comfort keeps the priority gentle (30:21 — **“so that you may find comfort in them. And He has placed between you compassion and mercy. Surely in this are signs”**): the covering runs in both directions or the household is undressed. A marriage where one spouse is the garment and the other is merely worn is not the marriage the verse describes, and the month of fasting will expose it quickly. The one who spends the days hungry and the nights cold has misread the permission that opens the passage. The verse hands the night back to the household with both hands.

**SECRETS THAT WERE KNOWN**

Allah knows that you were deceiving yourselves. So He has accepted your repentance and pardoned you. places the permission in the language of forgiveness, which is where its history is kept. The believers had been living under the stricter practice and testing its edges, and the verse announces that the testing was known and is now pardoned. The pardon is not a verdict on desire but on the self-deception that hid behind the stricter rule — the habit of pretending to be stronger than the law's mercy required. Because the acceptance is announced before the new permission, the permission arrives as pardon rather than as concession. The sequence teaches the reader how revelation works with the conscience: the weakness is named, the pardon is given, and the ruling is changed. The household enters the month forgiven and released.

**THREADS AT DAWN**

The timing of the fast is drawn as a visible boundary: and drink until you see the light of dawn breaking the darkness of night and then then complete the fast until nightfall. The dawn is described in the image of its threads — the white thread of light against the black thread of night — and the report preserved in al-Bukhārī puts the metaphor in the Prophet's own practical terms: each person has a white thread and a dark thread before the eyes, and the fast begins when they can be told apart. The rule is written for the eye rather than for the clock, so the boundary is public and the month keeps one time across a whole city. Because the nightfall closes the fast rather than the sunset's afterglow, the discipline ends in the darkness where the permission began.

**RETREAT BEHIND A LIMIT**

The retreat in the mosques is drawn with its own boundary: Do not be intimate with your spouses while you are meditating in the mosques. The retreat is a state of devotion with edges, and the edges are the doors of the mosque. The one who enters the retreat steps out of the permissions of the household for the days of the devotion and steps back in when the retreat ends. The verse does not abolish the household but sequences it, and the sequence is the meaning of the limit. Because the retreat and the intimacy are both lawful in their seasons, the unlawful act is only the overlap. The practice of the retreat, preserved in the reports of the month's final days, keeps the boundaries exactly where this verse places them.

**LIMITS THAT TEACH**

The close of the passage names the whole system: These are the limits set by Allah so do not exceed them. The permissions and prohibitions of the month are presented as one architecture — the night given, the day kept, the dawn measured, the retreat fenced — and the architecture is a revelation like the verses read at night. The application is to keep the month in its pieces: to eat at the lawful hour, to return to the household at the lawful hour, and to leave the retreat at the door the verse draws. The one who exceeds the limits is not more pious than the law; he is outside the mercy that made the law's shape. Because the limits are made clear to people, the failure to keep them is never an accident of geography. The month is a set of doors, and the doors are instructions. There is one more lesson in the passage's architecture worth naming: every permission arrives with its hour, which trains the believer to think of rulings as schedules rather than moods. The person who treats the law as a schedule keeps it when the mood fails. Because the schedule is published, the household can plan its worship and its tenderness together, and the planning is itself an act of devotion. The month ends with its limits intact and its members closer to the One who drew them. The passage as a whole is one of the clearest demonstrations in the chapter of how the law works: a permission, a purpose, a boundary, and a reminder, all in one breath. The four elements belong together, and the household that keeps them together keeps the month as it was designed. The household that keeps them discovers the same trust the legislation assumes: a law drawn in this detail is a law meant to be lived in. The passage also answers the person who complains that the month's rules are complicated: the rules are few, and each one protects a good the reader already values — the body, the marriage, the worship, and the calendar. Because every rule names its good, the complexity dissolves the moment the household reads the purposes aloud.
