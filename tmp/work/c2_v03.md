**FAITH IN WHAT NO EYE REACHES**
The first mark of the people the Book guides is that they believe in what no eye has reached: ***“who believe in the unseen, establish prayer”***. The unseen is not one item on a list. It covers God Himself, His angels, the books He sent, the meeting with Him after death, and every promise and warning the Qur'an carries about a world the senses cannot enter. Early readers explained the phrase in the same direction: Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Suddī are reported to have read it as belief in God and in what He sent, including the Last Day, and the Companions and their students treated the hidden as the first door of faith rather than its last resort. Trusting what cannot be seen is not a weakness of the mind. It is the way every person already lives, since a farmer plants in a season he cannot verify and a traveller steps onto a plane he did not build. Faith in the unseen simply names the same trust and gives it the truest object.
**PRAYER AS A FIXED APPOINTMENT**
The verse joins belief to practice with a second mark: established prayer. The wording pictures something set up and kept standing, not a feeling that comes and goes, and the Qur'an repeats the pair of prayer and giving in several places, as in (8:3 — **“those who establish prayer and donate from what We have provided for them”**). A prayer that is established has a time, a place and a body in it, which is why the community began with a fixed number of prayers and a fixed direction, and why the duty is described as standing rather than as feeling. Picture a shop that opens at the same hour every day in a street where the neighbours set their watches by it. The people around such a shop organise their own day by its rhythm, and a believer's prayer does the same work for him. Fasting and almsgiving each have their own seasons, but prayer keeps its appointment every day, so that no week can pass without a man standing in front of his Lord.
**SPENDING OUT OF WHAT WAS GIVEN**
The third mark closes the sentence: ***“and donate from what We have provided for them”***. The wording does not say out of what they earned, and it does not say out of what they own. The phrase is out of what they were provided, so that the money in a man's hand is treated as stock left with him rather than treasure he created. The Qur'an returns to the claim in a command that has the last day in view, (2:254 — **“Donate from what We have provided for you”**), and it binds the same duty to the prayer with a single verb in (2:43 — **“Establish prayer, pay alms-tax”**). A closed fist is not neutral. Wealth that never moves grows heavy on the one holding it, because a man who refuses a small outflow soon measures his safety by the size of his store and stops trusting the Provider.
**WHY THESE THREE STAND TOGETHER**
The order of the three marks is not random, and the reason matters for anyone who tries to choose among them. Belief in the unseen gives the other two their ground, because a man who spends and prays while believing that nothing sees him is performing for an audience he does not have. Prayer keeps the link alive, and the Qur'an gives it fixed hours rather than leaving it to convenience (4:103), which means the believer stands in front of his Lord with his hands open at the same points of every day. Giving takes that posture into the street, the market and the family, where the poor and the neighbour actually wait. The Book also names the poor and the traveller among those with a claim on what a man holds, as at (2:177 — **“who give charity out of their cherished wealth”**), so the third mark is not charity in the sense of a leftover gift. It is a right that a household keeps in mind the way it keeps a bill, and it can be paid this week, quietly, out of whatever the Provider has already placed within reach.
