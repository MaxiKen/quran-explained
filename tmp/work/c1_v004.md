**TWO WAYS OF RECITING ONE WORD**

The fourth sentence names God as ***“Master of the Day of Judgment”***, and the first word of it is read in two ways that the community has received and recited. One reading gives the word as *mālik*, an owner; the other gives it as *malik*, a king. Both are transmitted readings of the same word, and both are true of God, so no reader is asked to choose between them. The difference between the two is not a difference about God but a difference of emphasis. An owner holds a thing and may dispose of it, and a king rules and commands; every king is an owner in this sense, while not every owner is a king. Those who preferred *malik* said that it is the more emphatic, because kingship is said of people and not only of property. Those who preferred *mālik* said that it reaches further, because ownership covers everything owned, while kingship belongs to the government of persons. The verse leaves both readings standing, and a person who recites it in either way has recited the same truth. The next word settles what kind of day is meant. *Al-Dīn* in this place is requital, the day on which every deed is repaid, and the earlier authorities explained it as the day of reckoning and the settling of disputes between created beings. Ibn ʿAbbās said of it that people will be judged according to their deeds, and that no judge will sit there except God.

**WHY THIS DAY IS SINGLED OUT**

A reader may ask why the verse ties mastery to one day, when God is master of every day. The answer the reports give is that on that day the claim of every other owner will be stripped away. Today a person speaks of what is his, defends it in courts, and writes it into documents; on that day the documents will mean nothing and the titles will belong to one. The Book states it as a question put to a silent assembly: (40:16 — **“Who does all authority belong to this Day? To Allah—the One, the Supreme”**). It states it again in a verse that gathers the whole earth into one hand: (39:67 — **“the ˹whole˺ earth will be in His Grip, and the heavens will be rolled up in His Right Hand”**). It states it once more in the plainest of its sentences: (82:19 — **“all authority on that Day belongs to Allah ˹entirely˺”**). The silence of that day is described in several places, and the description supports the same reading. No one will speak without permission: (78:38 — **“None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true”**), and again: (11:105 — **“no one will dare speak except with His permission”**). A day on which no one may speak is a day on which no one may claim. A report carried by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, on the authority of Abū Hurayrah, has the Prophet describing that day in these words: *"Allah will grasp the earth and fold up the sky with His Right Hand and say, 'I am the King; where are the kings of the earth?'"*

**THE DAY THAT DISCIPLINES THE PRESENT**

What a person believes about the last day changes how he spends the present one, and the verse puts that change in a single word. A merchant who knows that a full audit is coming keeps his ledger as he trades. He does not wait until the accounts are called for, because a ledger kept honestly in the course of the year is a different object from a ledger reconstructed under pressure. The knowledge that the Day of Judgment belongs to a master disciplines the ordinary transactions of a life. It tells a person that no wrong will go unrecorded simply because it went unnoticed, and that no good will go unpaid simply because it went unthanked. It also puts the ambitions of the powerful in their place. The verse names a day that no king can postpone and no court can overrule, and the naming is a comfort to everyone who has been treated unjustly by the strong. The Book turns this belief into a command for the present: (2:254 — **“a Day when there will be no bargaining, friendship, or intercession”**). The verse is recited in the middle of the sūrah's praise, so that the reader praises the Lord who is master of the day on which the accounts are settled. The order of the words teaches something in itself: the praise comes first, and the judgement is named as part of the reason for praise.

**A COURT THAT NEVER LOSES A FILE**

Think of a court whose files cannot be lost, whose witnesses cannot be bribed, and whose judge cannot be replaced. Such a court would make an honest man easy and a dishonest one uneasy, whether or not he ever appeared in it. The Day of Judgment works on human conduct in exactly that way, and the verse names its owner so that the reader knows whose court it is. A person who begins his prayer with this sentence begins it inside a courtroom that is not yet in session, and the knowledge steadies him or warns him according to what he carries. These days the world is loud about the things that can be measured and quiet about the things that are being recorded, and the verse speaks into that quiet. It does not ask a person to live in fear of a tyrant's whim. It asks him to live in the presence of a master who is perfectly just and who never sleeps through a hearing.
