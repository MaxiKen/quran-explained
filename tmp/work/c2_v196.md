**CONTRACTS OF ARRIVAL**

The contracts of the passage are drawn phrase by phrase, from arrival to the reminder: ***“Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage”***, ***“for Allah. But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺”***, ***“then ˹offer˺ whatever sacrificial animals you can afford.”***, ***“And do not shave your heads”***, ***“until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination.”***, ***“But if any of you is ill”***, ***“or has a scalp ailment ˹requiring shaving˺”***, ***“then compensate either by fasting, charity”***, ***“or a sacrificial offering. In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage”***, ***“and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford. Whoever cannot afford”***, ***“that ˹offering˺, let them fast three days during pilgrimage”***, ***“and seven after returning ˹home˺”***, ***“completing ten. These offerings are”***, ***“for those who do not live near the Sacred House.”***, ***“And be mindful of Allah”***, ***“and know that Allah is severe in punishment”***. The order of the contracts is the order of the traveller's day: the intention, the blockage, the animal, the hair, the combination, the counting, and the mindfulness. Each phrase meets the pilgrim at the hour of the journey it actually governs. The purpose Ibn ʿAbbās states for the two rites governs the whole sequence.

Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah. opens a passage of contracts, and the first contract is completion. The two rites are named together and aimed at Allah, so the intention is the offering's first ingredient. Ibn ʿAbbās states the purpose of the two rites in exactly those terms: they are performed sincerely for Him and carried to their full completion, and the completion is what the imperative demands. The verse does not describe arrival as the achievement; arrival is where the contract begins. The pilgrim who reaches the precinct and quits the rites has entered the contract without finishing it, and the law follows the unfinished contract with its remedies. Because the two rites share the verse, the minor pilgrimage borrows the major one's discipline and the major one keeps its own completion. The worship is a promise with a shape.

**PREVENTED BY ENEMY**

The first remedy answers the pilgrim who is stopped on the road: But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺, then ˹offer˺ whatever sacrificial animals you can afford. The prevention named in the reports is the prevention by an enemy — the community was held back from the House — and the law responds to the blocked pilgrim with an offering rather than a reproach. The offering is measured by capacity, so the poor traveler is inside the ruling as well as the wealthy one. The sequence preserves the contract by substituting its object: the journey ends, the offering continues it. Because the prevention is not the pilgrim's fault, the remedy carries no blame, only the animal. The blocked traveler learns that the law has a place for him at the very moment the road refuses him.

**HAIR AND ITS RANSOM**

The ritual of the hair is fenced with a timing rule: And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination. The shave is the rite's completion signal, and the offering must arrive before the signal is given. The verse then answers the pilgrim whose body breaks the rule's patience — illness or a scalp ailment — with a ransom measured in the familiar three: fasting, charity, or a sacrifice. The ransom formula appears in the legislation of violations at the House, where the penalty for the broken discipline of the ihram runs through the same three currencies (5:95 — **“fasting so that they may taste the consequences of their violations. Allah has forgiven what has been done. But those”**). Because the ransom is available, the ailment is a case and not a catastrophe. The hair waits for the offering or is bought back; the two paths keep the rite intact.

**COMBINING THE TWO PILGRIMAGES**

The verse turns to the case of peace, where the pilgrim can perform both rites together: In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford. The combination is legislated as an economy of travel, and the offering remains the constant that both rites share. The permission to combine arrived with the practice preserved in the reports of the farewell pilgrimage, where the Prophet taught the rites to the assembled thousands. Because the combination is offered in times of peace, the war's prevention and the peace's ease are both answered inside one verse. The application is to plan the rites by the verse's ladder — completion, prevention, combination — and to know where the traveler stands before the first step. The rite that is designed with its exceptions is a rite the whole community can keep.

**COUNTING THE FASTS**

The pilgrim who cannot afford the offering receives his own arithmetic: let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning ˹home˺, completing ten. The counting is stated in full so that nobody has to guess, and the split — three on the road, seven at home — respects the traveler's condition. The verse's numbers are a mercy of precision: the penance is bounded, dated, and completable, which is the shape of every ease in the passage. Because the ten are divided by the state of the person, the law legislates around the body rather than against it. The one who reads the numbers as a burden has missed their direction: they are the ceiling of the obligation, not its floor. The fasts are a bridge the poor pilgrim walks home on. The three-and-seven arrangement is also a lesson in the sequencing of duty: the road's share is kept on the road, and the home's share waits for the home. The verse treats the traveler's energy as a real quantity, and the legislation bends around it without cancelling the debt. The arrangement is one of the clearest demonstrations in the passage that the law measures people before it measures them against the law. Because the counting is done for the traveler, the duty arrives as a schedule rather than as a burden. The one who keeps the ten days in their two homes has kept the whole contract.

**SEVERITY ALONGSIDE MERCY**

The close of the passage refuses to end in softness alone: And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment. The severity is placed beside the mercy of the remedies so that the rulings are kept for their Giver's sake and not only for their convenience. The reminder of the punishment is the verse's final instrument, aimed at the pilgrim who would skip the offering once the rites are done. Because the mercy and the severity share the close, the pilgrim leaves the passage with both hands full: the remedies of the law and the fear of the Lawgiver. The application is to settle the contract before leaving the precinct — to shave only when the offering is safe, and to count the fasts before counting the days home. The rites end where the verse ends, in mindfulness. The reminder of the punishment at the close is the verse's final service to the pilgrim: it keeps the contracts alive in the memory after the road has emptied. Severity at the end and mercy in the middle is the passage's whole architecture, and the pilgrim who has walked it once will recognize the shape in every ruling that follows. Because the contracts outlast the rites, the journey is judged after the traveler is home. The passage's protections — the blocked pilgrim's offering, the ailment's ransom, the poor traveler's counted days — form one policy of accommodation that runs from the road to the doorway. The policy exists because the rites were designed for real travelers with real bodies, and the design is visible in every exemption. The one who finishes the rites in any of these forms has finished them lawfully.
