**A MOCKERY TURNED BACK ON ITS OWNER**

The verse answers the laughter, and it answers it in a single sentence: ***“Allah will throw their mockery back at them, leaving them to continue wandering blindly in their defiance”***. The word for throwing back belongs to the language of trade and exchange, which means the mockery is returned to the sender like a coin rejected at the counter. Nothing in the sentence suggests a God who is wounded by a joke; the offence is handled the way an accountant handles a forged entry, by sending it back to the page it came from. The Book tells the Prophet in another sūrah that the mockers are not his problem to settle, (15:95 — **“Surely We will be sufficient for you against the mockers”**), and that division of labour is a mercy to everyone who has ever been laughed at for belief. A man who has been ridiculed for his prayers should not be surprised to learn that the answering is not his task. His task is to keep praying, and the verse leaves the laughter where it belongs.

**THE BLIND WALK THAT FOLLOWS**

The second half of the sentence describes the condition that comes after the laughter, and its last words name the walk that follows. The word for defiance carries the sense of water rising past its banks, which means the man is not standing still in his unbelief; he is being carried by it further than he intended. Another passage uses the identical image of a man left to wander, (7:186 — **“leaving them to wander blindly in his defiance”**), and the picture is of a traveller who never sits down to check his direction. A blind man in a familiar house manages for a week and then breaks an ankle on a chair that has always been there, and the verse is describing that ankle rather than the furniture. The punishment is not an added misfortune from outside; it is the ordinary consequence of walking fast in the dark and pretending otherwise.

**WHY GOD DOES NOT ANSWER AT ONCE**

A reader may wonder why the reply to mockery is not instant, and the sentence answers that question by its shape. If the answer came in the same minute as the joke, the laughter would stop for fear and the man would never see what he had become. The delay is what makes the verse a mercy to the honest and a sentence on the dishonest, since the honest man uses the interval and the other man spends it. This is why the Book tells the Prophet that he is not a keeper over people and cannot compel them, because a faith produced by force would prove nothing about the man who held it. A farmer who demands that his crop appear the day after sowing proves only that he does not understand a field, and the patience of God with the mockers belongs to the same order of things. The delay is a test with a deadline, and the deadline is not announced.

**WATCHING FOR THE SAME BLINDNESS**

Anyone who reads the verse today can watch for the same condition in himself, and the signs are not dramatic. A man starts by laughing at a practice he once kept, then stops defending an opinion he once held, and finally cannot remember why the whole subject ever mattered to him. That path is the walk the verse describes, and its early stages are reached by steps rather than by leaps. The remedy is to catch the drift while the road is still visible, which is why the Book keeps its warnings attached to ordinary habits like prayer and speech rather than to large crises. A person who notices that his own jokes have turned against the things he claims to believe has found the verse pointed at him, and the finding is a kindness, because the man who can still see the direction he is walking is not yet the man the verse is about.
