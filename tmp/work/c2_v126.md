**SAFETY ASKED FOR BEFORE PROVISION**

The verse records the next thing Abraham said at that place, and it is a request for two things at once: ***“And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord, make this city ˹of Mecca˺ secure”*** and ***“and provide fruits to its people”***. The prayer asks for safety before it asks for provision, which places the security of the settlement ahead of its comfort, and the Book's order in the sentence is worth noticing. The request is made for a city rather than for himself, which fits the role the previous verses assigned to him as a model for others. Another passage records the same settlement being made with the same two concerns in view, and it shows how the prayer was answered in the geography of the valley: (14:37 — **“I have settled some of my offspring in a barren valley, near Your Sacred House”**). What the reader receives here is a picture of a founder who intercedes for the place he has built.

**PROVISION FOR THE PEOPLE WHO BELIEVE**

The prayer names the people for whom the provision is asked, and the clause restricts the request to those who believe: ***“those among them who believe in Allah”*** and ***“and the Last Day.”***. The Book reports the restriction because it is part of the prayer as Abraham made it, and the answer that follows will widen the provision while keeping the spiritual condition where it was. The pairing of belief in Allah with belief in the Last Day is the Book's standard way of describing a complete faith, and it appears throughout the sūrah in descriptions of the people who answer the call. The same pairing, in the note that carries Ibn ʿAbbās's name, describes the community praised earlier in this sūrah, and the verse here places the combination in the mouth of the founder. A reader should notice that the request was for provision to those who believe rather than for belief in those who are provided for.

**THE ANSWER ABOUT THOSE WHO DISBELIEVE**

The sentence then reports the divine reply, and the reply grants the prayer in a broader form while leaving the outcome of the two groups different: ***“He answered, “As”***, ***“for those who disbelieve, I will let them enjoy themselves”***, ***“for a little while”*** and ***“then I will condemn them to the torment of the Fire. What an evil destination!”***. The provision is extended to the disbelievers for the duration of their lives, and the extension is stated as a decision rather than as an oversight. Another passage describes the same safe settlement being used as an argument by people who had not accepted the guidance: (28:57 — **“Have We not established for them a safe haven ˹in Mecca˺”**). The verse's answer therefore separates the two gifts that the prayer had joined, since security and provision were given widely while the destination was left to what each person did with his time there.

**A HOST WHO SEATS A GUEST BEFORE SHOWING HIM OUT**

Think of a host who lets a guest enjoy the meal for a while even though he knows the man will be asked to leave at the end of it. The verse's answer about the disbelievers has that shape, and the phrase about a little while keeps the whole arrangement inside a horizon that the guest cannot see. The comparison explains the absence of any condemnation in the sentence, since what is being described is a temporary provision rather than a reward. Anyone who has seen a man mistake the duration of his comfort for the quality of his position can see what the sentence is guarding against, and the guard is the clause about the destination. The difference between being provided for and being accepted is what the sentence asks him to notice.

**THE ENJOYMENT THAT IS ONLY FOR A WHILE**

The answer closes with a judgement on the place to which the disbeliever is finally sent, and the exclamation is short: what an evil destination. The Book uses such exclamations sparingly, and their appearance here marks the seriousness of the ending that follows the temporary provision. The whole reply leaves the reader with the two horizons in view at once: the food on the table and the door at the end of the meal. Anyone who has been told that the goods of this world are given without regard to merit will find the sentence consistent with the sūrah's argument, since provision and acceptance have been separated from the beginning. What remains after the verse is the prayer that opened it and the answer that widened it, and the reader is left to decide which of the two horizons he is living for.
