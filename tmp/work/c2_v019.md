**THE STORM, THE THUNDER, AND THE FINGERS IN THE EARS**

The sūrah moves to a second picture of the same people: ***“Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder”***, ***“and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap”***, ***“for fear of death.”***, and ***“And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺”***. The rain in a desert land is life, and the sky it falls from is the mercy the Book is always pointing to. Around that rain, however, sits everything that frightens: the dark, the noise, and the flash. The people in the picture are not running to the rain. They are covering their ears so that they can survive the night without having to deal with it, and the verse ends by noting that the storm is not happening outside God's reach. The Book says of the same voice elsewhere: (13:13 — **“The thunder glorifies His praises, as do the angels in awe of Him. He sends thunderbolts,”**). The image fits the situation it follows. The revelation has arrived among a people who have heard its promises and its warnings at close range. What they want from it is the material benefit, and what they cannot stand is the part that summons them to change. So they shelter in the community, take the rain and refuse the thunder, and the verse leaves them standing there with their hands over their ears. A reader who has ever accepted the comfort of a religion while ignoring the part of it he did not wish to hear knows the position exactly.

**WHY THE FEAR IS NOT FAITH**

Notice what the thunder produces in them. It is fear, and the fear is real enough to make a man stop his ears, but it is the fear of consequence rather than the fear of God. That distinction runs through the whole Qur'an, and it explains why awe alone saves nobody. The people in this storm are reacting to danger the way anyone would; the moment the sound passes, their hands come down and their lives continue as before. The verse names their motive, ***“for fear of death.”***, and by naming it strips away the appearance of seriousness. What they are protecting is not their souls but their survival, and the Book is clear that the two things are not the same.

The last phrase puts the whole scene inside a larger frame: ***“And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺”***. The word describes a circle drawn around a person who does not know he is being circled. Every attempt to shelter himself from the message on his own terms is being counted while he makes it, and the verse states this without anger. That calm is what makes it frightening: no threat is needed when the position is already surrounded, and no noise is required from a power that has no rival. The reader is meant to feel the difference between the man who thinks he is hiding and the God he thinks he is hiding from, since no pair of hands can cover the ears of the one who is being addressed.

**THE USE OF FEAR AND THE USE OF TRUTH**

The picture also raises a practical question about how the Book works on a reader. Fear of punishment can move a person to change a habit for a while, and the sūrah does not pretend otherwise, since the storm makes the crowd put their hands over their ears and take shelter. But the fear in this image produces no repentance, because it is aimed at the discomfort rather than at the Lord. What the passage recommends instead is the response the earlier verses described: certainty about the Hereafter, prayer kept when inconvenient, and giving measured in the open. Those are the acts of a person who has listened to the rain and understood what it is for. A reader today can use the distinction honestly. If his religion is a set of habits he performs so that nothing bad happens to him, then his hands are on his ears in the sense that matters, since nothing in the practice is reaching the place where decisions are made. Today he can test himself in one small matter by asking whether he would keep the practice if he were sure no danger followed from letting it go. The answer does not need to be comfortable for the exercise to be useful, and the storm is described in this sūrah precisely so that the question can be asked in good time.
