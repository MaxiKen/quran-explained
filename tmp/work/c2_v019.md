**A SECOND PARABLE SHOWS FEAR WITHIN THE GIFT**

The first picture ended with a dead fire. The next begins with water from above: ***“Or ˹those caught in˺ a rainstorm from the sky with darkness, thunder”***. Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn Masʿūd, Mujāhid, and Qatādah all understood the central image as pouring rain. Rain gives life, yet a person caught in a night storm may notice danger before benefit. This tension makes the scene fit people who come near revelation but meet it with divided hearts. The Qur’an descends as guidance and mercy, while its commands, warnings, and demands expose what they wish to keep safe. They want whatever shelter faith may give without yielding to the Lord who sends it. The parable does not call revelation dark or harmful. The storm contains water and light; the darkness belongs to their doubt and the fear with which they face what should revive them. In the created world, lightning itself can awaken both hope and fear, and God is the One who (13:12 — **“shows you lightning, inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear, and produces heavy clouds”**). A sound heart receives both sides and still sees the Giver. Their hearts treat every grave sign as a threat to escape. This is why the benefit can be present while their response remains ruled by fear.

**THREE SIGNS MEET A DIVIDED HEART**

The storm holds three features at once: ***“and lightning. They press their fingers into their ears at the sound of every thunder-clap”***. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the darkness as the trials and confusion disclosed in the Qur’an, the thunder as its rebuke and threats, and the lightning as its clear teaching and promise. Other early readings likewise connect darkness with doubt and hypocrisy. These links do not remove the force of the real storm. They let its mixed force show a mixed response. Light draws the eye, thunder shakes the heart, and darkness makes each next step unsure. The fingers in the ears reveal which part they most want to stop. They do not wait, listen, and learn whether the sound marks danger or brings needed rain. They try to shut out the warning itself. The wording pictures the whole finger thrust in, though only its tip can enter, which makes the panic vivid. Yet blocking the ear does not still the sky. In the same way, refusing a recited warning cannot cancel what it warns about. The hypocrites hope that unheard truth will lose its claim on them. The effect is less readiness for the danger and less ability to receive the mercy carried in the same revelation.

**FEAR OF DEATH MAKES THEM CHOOSE A DEADLIER SHELTER**

Their frantic act is ***“for fear of death.”*** On the surface, a violent thunderclap can make a traveller fear that the storm will kill him. Within the parable, the warning of judgment and the cost of sincere faith feel like threats to the life the hypocrites have built. Ibn ʿAbbās says they feared being drawn to the Qur’an, for true response would end the false stance by which they served both sides. They also feared the punishments announced to disbelief, but sought relief by stopping the message rather than stopping its cause. Fear thus turns against its proper work. It should move a person away from ruin; theirs moves them away from the warning that marks ruin. No hand over an ear can bar death. The direct reminder is, (62:8 — **“The death you are running away from will inevitably come to you.”**). Nor can fear justify the lie that makes the final meeting worse. A believer also fears judgment, but joins fear to hope and action. He listens, seeks pardon, and changes course. The hypocrite uses fear to protect the course itself. This difference matters today when a person avoids a verse, adviser, or fact because it threatens comfort. Relief gained by not listening is not safety. The reason is that a warning guards a person only when it is heard and allowed to guide. Ignoring it may mean losing the last clear alarm before harm.

**NO SHELTER FALLS OUTSIDE DIVINE POWER**

The scene closes by moving from their hands to divine power: ***“And Allah encompasses the disbelievers ˹by His might˺”***. Ibn ʿAbbās joins God’s full knowledge to the certainty that He will gather them for judgment. To encompass is more than to stand near. No route falls outside His knowledge, no shelter resists His power, and no hidden fear or plan slips beyond His account. The storm may seem to close around the travellers, but God already holds both the storm and those within it. This ending also corrects the instinct behind their act. They behave as though danger lies in the sound and safety lies in a small wall made by their own fingers. The true issue is their bond with God. Another concise verse states, (85:20 — **“But Allah encompasses them from all sides”**). Flight within His creation cannot become flight from Him. Therefore, the sound response is not to silence warning but to seek the mercy to which warning points. When revelation brings promise and threat together, faith does not tear them apart. It lets promise guard against despair and threat guard against ease in sin. The hypocrite hears only what endangers his mask; the believer hears a Lord who sends rain, light, warning, and a road through the storm.
