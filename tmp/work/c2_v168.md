**EAT WHAT IS LAWFUL AND GOOD**

***“O humanity! Eat from what is lawful”*** addresses all people with an order to eat, which is itself the answer to a piety that forbids what God has given. The permission is doubled by quality: ***“and good on the earth”***, so the lawful is not merely permitted but wholesome and pleasant. Then the verse names the source of the invented prohibitions with ***“and do not follow Satan’s footsteps. He is truly your sworn”*** and closes with the single word ***“enemy”***. In the reading transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās, the embellishments of the enemy concerned exactly these goods — the cultivation of land and the raising of cattle — declared forbidden by whispering rather than by revelation. The structure is gift, boundary, and diagnosis. The order matters, because the gift comes first: the people are fed before they are warned, so the warning arrives from a generous hand rather than from a grudging one.

**ANSWERING INVENTED TABOOS**

The distortion had taken the form of piety: the more a person forbade, the holier they appeared, and the only cure for that competition is a divine permission that cannot be outbid. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the setting in concrete terms — the claim covered cultivation and cattle alike, and the verse reopens what the whisperings had closed. The reason the verse answers with permission rather than argument is that the additions were not a heightening of devotion but an insult to the Lawgiver. A law that grows without its author is a law that has found a new author, and the verse names the new author as the enemy. The order to eat is therefore an act of legislation as much as an act of kindness: it restores the boundary of the law at the same time as it fills the table. That is why the enemy's method is named as footsteps: a path walked step by step, so that each small prohibition leads the walker further from the open ground of the gift. The permission is also a test of gratitude, because the person forbidden everything can perform holiness at no cost while the person permitted everything must practise thankfulness all day. The lawful is also named good, which places pleasure and piety on the same side. The earth is a table set by a host, and the meal is one of its invitations. (16:114 — **“So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you, and be grateful for Allah’s favours,”**)

**DISCIPLINE VERSUS LEGISLATION**

A hearer may ask whether refusing food cannot be a legitimate discipline of the soul. Discipline is legitimate as a choice and blasphemous as a law. The one remains inside the permission and simply uses it lightly; the other rewrites the permission, and that rewriting is the enemy's work. Fasting today is discipline; declaring bread unlawful forever is legislation. Because the permission is God's to give, it is also God's to revoke, and a servant's veto cannot register in His register. The verse protects both gratitude and authority: gratitude by ordering the good to be eaten, authority by reserving the definition of lawful to its source. The lawful is called good as well, so pleasure and piety are not rivals. The earth is a table set by a host.

**NAMED AS AN ENEMY**

The enmity named here is expanded in (35:6 — **“so take him as an enemy. He only invites his followers to become inmates of the Blaze”**), and Ibn ʿAbbās's transmission states that the devil's whisperings against cultivation and cattle are the specific content of the footsteps to avoid. The application is to treat the good as good: to eat gratefully, to work honestly, and to hold every prohibition to the standard of the text that enacts it. The footsteps to avoid are the ones that trade a generous law for a flattering strictness. The trade flatters the trader and starves the household, and the verse reopens the table it closed. Because the enemy is named, the reader can refuse him by name. The table is reopened as a gift, and the gratitude is its reply. The verse keeps both sides of that answer in view: eat from the good, and remember the hand that made it good.
