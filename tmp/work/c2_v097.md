**AN OBJECTION TO THE ONE WHO BROUGHT IT**

The quarrel is closed in the next verse by naming every party at once, so that the objection to one messenger becomes an objection to them all: (2:98 — **“Whoever is an enemy of Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel,”**). The verse answers the quarrel with a messenger by describing what the revelation does: ***“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know”***, ***“that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah's Will, confirming what came before it”***, ***“a guide and good news”***, ***“for the believers.””*** The objection behind the verse is not stated, and the Book does not need to state it, since the reply identifies the accusation precisely enough: the community had made the identity of the angel a reason to reject the message. The answer rephrases the whole affair in terms of function, and the functions listed are four: the revelation came down, it was placed in the heart, it confirms what came earlier, and it guides and gives good news. A quarrel about a name is answered with a description of what the named one has actually done.

**THE BEARER, THE HEART, AND THE PURPOSE**

A messenger's identity is not a trivial matter, since trust in the message depends on knowing who carried it and what kind of act the carrying was. The Book therefore does not dismiss the question; it answers it by naming the bearer, the destination, and the purpose in one sentence. The destination is named as the heart, which is where belief is formed and where the community's hardness had already been located earlier in the sūrah. The same constellation of ideas returns in the verse that follows this one, where the angels are named one by one and enmity toward any of them is answered in the same breath. A reader who has refused advice because of its source can see the Book's method here, since the message and the messenger are treated as parts of one act rather than as separate issues.

**CONFIRMATION AS THE ANSWER TO SUSPICION**

The revelation is described as confirming what came earlier, which is the strongest available answer to a community that already possessed scriptures. A revelation that confirmed their own books could not have been rejected on the ground that it disagreed with them, and the Book has already made this point several times in the sūrah. The naming of guidance and good news does the rest of the work, because these are the things a reader needs and the things the message claims to provide. Nothing in the verse defends itself with a miracle or an argument from power; the defence is a description of function, and the function is addressed to the community's own stated needs. That technique is used wherever the Book is accused of something, and it is why the answer here feels final despite its brevity.

**THE PATIENT WHO DOUBTS THE CHEMIST**

Think of a person who needs a medicine and refuses it because he does not like the pharmacist who prepared it. The Book's answer to the community in this verse is to name what the preparation contains and what it does, which is the only relevant consideration for anyone who needs the medicine. The angel's standing is not diminished by the community's dislike, and the revelation's contents do not change because the hearer was expecting another messenger. The reader who wants to apply the passage can ask whether he has ever rejected a piece of information on the ground of its carrier while knowing that the information was sound. The Book's handling of the objection shows what such a refusal looks like when it is examined, and the examination is what the verse performs.

**THE STATE OF THE ARGUMENT**

The sentence settles the dispute for anyone who is willing to weigh functions rather than reputations, and it leaves the objector with the option of continuing to refuse on grounds that no longer concern the message. The Book's placement of the verse here, after the account of a community's reluctance to part with life, shows that the two objections belong to the same disposition. A person who does not want the message will find a reason in the messenger, and the Book removes that reason without asking the person to like the messenger. What the reader takes away is a way of separating a claim from the person who delivers it, and the practice of that separation is the beginning of fairness. The next verse deals with those who extend the enmity to God and the angels together.
