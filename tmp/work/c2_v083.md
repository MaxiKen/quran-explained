**THE SEVEN TERMS OF THE FIRST PLEDGE**

The covenant is recalled in its Torah form, and the list it carries is a constitution in miniature: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none”***, ***“but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans”***. The first term is God's own right, and the glosses define its content as three duties in one: affirm the unity, attest the messengers, and act by the books. This is the foundation, the ethicists of the tradition say, without which no other work is accepted. The second term is the first of the creatures' rights, and the arrangement itself is the teaching. The books mention the rights of parents beside the rights of God, so that gratitude to the two is named in one breath: (17:23 — **“For your Lord has decreed that you worship none but Him. And honour your parents.”**). The Arabic phrase for the second term is strikingly spare. Its surface sense is and toward parents virtue, the verb understood rather than written, and the grammar of the ellipsis was noted as an understood command. What follows walks outward in circles: the kin whose name in Arabic means the possessors of nearness, then the orphans whose definition in the juridical handbooks is the minor who lost the father who provided, then the needy named in almost every passage where relatives and orphans are named. The sequence is a lesson in the sociology of obligation. Worship begins at the altar and ends at the street.

**THE COURTESY THAT TRUTHFULNESS REQUIRES**

The fourth term is speech, and the verse grants it the same expansion the others received: ***“and the needy”***, ***“speak kindly to people”***, ***“establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.””***. The kind word in this covenant is not decoration. The glosses unpack it as commanding right and forbidding wrong while staying truthful about the status of the awaited prophet, with kindness in act toward the orphan and the poor. The manners clause was pressed into service in a scene the tradition keeps for its tone. When a student complained to ʿAṭāʾ of his own harshness toward people of unsound doctrine and said he dealt with them roughly, the scholar pointed him back to the kind words this verse prescribes and answered: *"Allah has commanded us to speak to people politely. When Jews and Christians all are to be treated like this, would this commandment not apply to a Muslim?"* The pedagogy of the prophets is the same. The books record Moses and Aaron sent to the worst tyrant of their age with instructions to use gentle words. The tone of any modern caller to any faith is therefore a doctrinal matter and not a trait of personality. Prayer and the alms-tax then close the list. The vertical covenant carries its social dividend, the five daily meetings and the share that keeps the poor inside the community's arithmetic. The list as a whole refuses to separate what it joins. Theology is followed immediately by parents, and worship immediately by wages, because the covenant contracts for both tables at once.

**ORDINANCES THAT CARRY NO EXPIRY**

The doctrine that emerges from the list is a doctrine of permanence. These ordinances are the principles of religion that every revealed law has commanded, since they answer the general interests of every time and place, and the means to a favor is itself a favor. The tradition therefore treats them as un-abrogated in substance even where the specific statutes of earlier laws were replaced, and the catalogue keeps reappearing in the later surahs in nearly the same order. The ranking of deeds inside the covenant was shown in the report the two major collections record from Ibn Masʿūd: *"I asked the Messenger of Allah which action is best. He said: Prayer at its proper time. I said: Then which? He said: Goodness to parents. I said: Then which? He said: Jihad in the cause of Allah."* Two of the three answers stand inside the verse's own list. The breach is then stated in the verse's own tense — ***“But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away”***, ***“except for a few of you”***, ***“and were indifferent”*** — and the addressing of descendants in the second person is part of the passage's forensic style. The third-person reading makes the sentence a plain historical report. The second-person reading makes the covenant a standing summons that every generation must sign or breach. The few who refused the turning-away were identified in the earliest glosses by name, the converts of the first hour from among the scholars of the community. The indifference of the majority is the measure of what the pledge was worth in practice.

**THE OATH THE TEXT ATTACHES ITSELF TO**

One grammatical detail from the legal language of the covenant deserves recording, because it shows how seriously the form was argued. The commandment stands as if attached to an oath, and Sibawayh's analysis of the syntax turns the list itself into sworn testimony rather than counsel. A covenant here takes its name from the root of binding with an oath, the kind of assurance that secures a promise against the promiser's temper. The transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās keeps the same definition: the pledge taken is a pledge secured by oath. The hardness that required such forms was itself noted in the tradition. A people who resisted every plain command until they were met with fearful oaths have revealed, by the manner of their binding, the manner of their turning. The judgment this passage performs is therefore not a surprise inspection but a contract trial. The trial file carries the document the signatories themselves signed. For any later house that treats its founding covenant as heirloom rather than obligation, the closing words of the verse remain the sharpest commentary. The pledge is still in force, the signature is still legible, and the indifference is still counted.
