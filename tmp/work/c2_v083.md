**TERMS THAT NAMED THE WEAK FIRST**

The pledge taken from this people is read out here in its own words: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none”***, ***“but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans”***, ***“and the needy”***, ***“speak kindly to people”***, ***“establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.””***, ***“But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away”***, ***“except for a few of you”***, ***“and were indifferent”***. The list is remarkable for what it puts beside the worship of God, since it names parents, kin, orphans, the poor, and then every person a speaker might address. A covenant in the ancient world usually bound a people to a king and settled what they owed him; this one binds them to a set of obligations whose beneficiaries are mostly people without power. The worship of the One comes first and the treatment of the weak comes second, and the Book does not separate the two into different documents. The same covenant is recalled in another sūrah with the leaders appointed to witness it: (5:12 — **“Allah made a covenant with the Children of Israel”**). Ibn ʿAbbās unfolded the covenant as exclusive worship, dutiful care for parents and kin, generosity to the vulnerable, truthful speech, prayer and the due share of wealth.

**THE ORDER OF THE TERMS AND WHY IT MATTERS**

The sequence begins with the exclusivity of worship and moves outward in circles of decreasing closeness, and the arrangement is not accidental. A person who has settled the question of whom he serves is then asked about those who depend on him, starting with his parents and widening to strangers; the Book elsewhere repeats almost the same list in almost the same order when it wants to describe the good life: (4:36 — **“Worship Allah ˹alone˺ and associate none with Him.”**). The movement from the divine to the domestic is a warning against a piety that stops at the first item, since the Book treats a claim of devotion that leaves the household and the street untouched as incomplete. The same chapter teaches that righteousness is not a matter of turning toward one direction or another but of what a person does with what he has: (2:177 — **“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east”**). The terms of the covenant are therefore a single fabric, and the reader who pulls on one thread will find the others attached.

**KINDNESS TO PARENTS AS A COMMANDMENT IN THE MIDDLE OF A LIST**

Among the items stands a command that the Book repeats in its own right in another sūrah, where honouring parents is joined to the decree that God alone is to be worshipped: (17:23 — **“For your Lord has decreed that you worship none”**). The inclusion of orphans and the needy in the same sentence is a reminder that a community's treatment of those without protectors is one of the measures the Book applies to it repeatedly. The verse then reports that the children of Israel walked away from these terms and were indifferent, and the word for indifference covers the whole attitude: not hostility, not denial, but the condition of someone who has heard the terms and cannot be moved. The Book's charge is therefore not only that a people broke a rule but that they stopped caring about the obligations placed on them.

**A CHECKLIST A READER CAN USE**

Think of a man who keeps the fasts, attends the congregation, and has not spoken to his own mother in two years, and who would describe himself without hesitation as a religious person. The covenant in this verse is arranged to catch exactly that inconsistency, because it places the worship of God and the duty to parents in one sentence and makes the reader deal with both at once. The Book does not claim that the second earns the first or that the first excuses the second; it lists them as parts of one agreement, and it reports the consequences of treating the agreement as a menu. A reader who wants an honest reading can take the six items in the verse as a checklist for the coming week and see how much of his religion survives the exercise. The Book's charge against the people in this passage was indifference, and the remedy for indifference is a concrete act performed where it is visible.

**THE FEW WHO DID NOT TURN AWAY**

The verse does not end in an accusation without exception; it records that a few of the people kept to the covenant, and the Book's habit of noting the remnant is one of its most consistent mercies. The majority turned away and were indifferent, and the few who did not are named in a single clause that has kept hope for the community in every generation since. The same pattern appears where the Book says that some among them were upright and recited the revelations at night, and where the reader is warned against treating a whole people as a single character. What the reader should take from the clause is that the covenant remained available and that fidelity was possible inside the same community that broke it. The chapter's argument never becomes a condemnation of a lineage; it stays a matter of what each person does with the terms he has been given.

**WORSHIP AND THE STREET IN ONE SENTENCE**

The lasting value of this passage for a reader of any community is its refusal to separate devotion from conduct. The list in the verse begins in the sanctuary and ends in the marketplace, and the same movement occurs in the chapter's later teaching about spending, giving, and dealing fairly. The people addressed had a covenant whose terms they knew, and they turned away from a document that asked them to be kind to orphans and to speak well to others, which is a remarkable thing to abandon. A reader who finds himself drawn to the first term and reluctant about the rest can see in this verse what the Book thinks of a religion with one item left. The few who kept the whole list are mentioned, and the reader is invited to count himself among them. Today, devotion is tested at the bedside of a parent, in speech to a stranger and in the handling of money owed to the poor.
