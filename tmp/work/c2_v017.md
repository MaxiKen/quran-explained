**THE FIRE THAT LIGHTS THE ROAD AND THEN GOES**

The sūrah now gives the situation a picture: ***“Their example is that of someone”***, ***“who kindles a fire”***, ***“but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness”***, ***“unable to see”***. The fire is not a luxury in the desert; it is warmth, safety, and the ability to see the ground underfoot, and the man who lights it is thinking only of the light it will give him. Then the light is removed at the moment when he has come to rely on it. The verse does not explain the removal here, and it does not need to: the reader has just been told about people who used the light of belief for the benefits it brought and kept their real allegiance somewhere else. The detail that the light is taken away when it lights up all around them carries the whole weight of the image. Nothing was wrong with the fire, and nothing was wrong with the road. What failed was the arrangement by which the man had made himself comfortable in a place he never intended to stay. A person can attend, contribute, and be counted for years on the strength of a borrowed light. The verse shows how the borrowing ends, and it does so with an image that every reader already knows from ordinary life.

**AN IMAGE THE READER CAN FEEL**

A verdict would tell the reader what to think; the image makes him feel the position he is being warned about. That is why the Book returns to it later with a near-identical picture of a flame: (24:40 — **“Or ˹their deeds are˺ like the darkness in a deep sea, covered by waves upon waves,”**), where the darkness is layered until nothing can be seen. The two passages are describing the same end state, and both are built from things a person handles without thinking: fire, water, light, blindness. The Book is not trying to impress the reader with difficulty. It is trying to make the loss imaginable, because a loss that can be imagined can still be avoided. The picture also answers a question the previous verses raise. Why does God not simply leave such a person with his small advantages and settle the account at the end? The answer the image gives is that the advantages were never his own. The light came from the mission he was pretending to serve, and when the pretence is all that remains, the light goes with the thing he was pretending about. The verse is not describing an arbitrary withdrawal. It is describing a supply that was never routed through him in the first place.

**LOOKING FOR ONE'S OWN LIGHT**

A reader can test the image on his own case with a question that costs nothing. If the community he belongs to and the people he admires were removed from his week, what would remain of his religion at home, alone, on an ordinary evening? If the answer is a genuine practice, the light is his own and the fuel is in his hands. If the answer is a set of habits he keeps because others keep them, then the fire in the picture is worth thinking about, not as an accusation but as an opportunity, since the fuel can still be gathered. The verse leaves the man at his darkest point on purpose, so that the reader takes the warning while the fire is still burning. A second lesson sits beside the first. Other people's light should be used to light one's own flame rather than to be borrowed indefinitely, and the household, the study circle, and the honest friend all serve that purpose. Today that can begin with a small decision: one practice kept where no one can see it, named to no one, kept for no audience. The point is not secrecy; the point is to find out where the light is coming from. A person who discovers after that test that he was borrowing has found something worth more than the comfort of not asking.
