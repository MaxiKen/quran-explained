**THE INNER CONDITION IS TURNED INTO A SCENE**

The account now shifts from direct charge to a picture: ***“Their example is that of someone”***. A parable lets the hearer see in one moment what the prior verses traced through many acts. There is night, a needed flame, a brief ring of sight, and then a loss more stark because light came first. The single figure can stand for the whole group, much as one scene can show the shared state of many people. Their details may differ, but the pattern is one: they approach the signs of faith, gain from being near them, and fail to receive the light as an inward guide. The parable does not ask the reader to match each speck of the scene to a separate event. Its main force lies in the movement from need to light and from light to chosen loss. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the worldly side through the safety their public claim gave to person, family, and wealth. Ibn ʿAbbās also places the full loss at death, when an outward claim can no longer supply what only true faith gives. Both stages expose the same flaw. They used the setting of light without making its truth their own.

**A FIRE GIVES REAL HELP FOR A SHORT TIME**

The figure is one ***“who kindles a fire”***. He does so in darkness because fire can show danger, mark a path, give warmth, and calm fear. In the same way, the rise of faith around the hypocrites brings real signs and real worldly goods. They hear the Qur’an, see the Prophet’s life, join the Muslim group, and receive the public rights tied to their claim. The light around them is not false merely because their use of it is false. This point keeps the parable from becoming a claim that they met no guidance at all. They came close enough to see for a time, but treated what they saw as a tool for safety rather than a truth to obey.

There is also a difference between being lit from within and standing for a while in light made by another. Early explanation notes that the flame was obtained for a need, not held as a secure source within the man himself. That detail suits a borrowed profession of faith. Words and company can place someone near the effects of belief, yet neither can replace trust in the heart. The Qur’an describes a sounder gift as life joined to (6:122 — **“a light with which they can walk among people”**). Such light directs steps because God has given life to the person who bears it. The hypocrites seek the shelter of the lit circle while refusing the bond that would let them walk by its truth.

**THE USEFUL LIGHT GOES WHILE THE HARM REMAINS**

The turn comes ***“but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness”***. The timing sharpens the loss. They have seen enough to know what light does; then the good they tried to borrow is gone. The sentence begins with one fire-maker and turns to the group whose state he pictures. This prevents the reader from losing the hypocrites inside the image. God is the One who removes the light, just as He had left them to range in revolt after their own repeated refusal. The wording names the light as what is taken. Early readers drew from this that the useful part is lost while the painful result remains. Fire can shine and burn; they sought its shelter but end with no light to guide them. Doubt, disbelief, fear, and the cost of deceit now close in. This is just because they did not lose a sincere faith against their will. They kept rejecting the purpose of the light while trying to retain its side benefits. The Last Day will make the difference plain. Believing men and women will have (57:12 — **“their light shining ahead of them and on their right”**), while a borrowed public place will not produce that light. What survives then is what faith formed, not what a mask once gained.

**NO SIGHT REMAINS FOR THE WAY OUT**

The last words are ***“unable to see”***. Ordinary eyes are not the issue. The figure cannot find his road when the flame dies, and the people pictured cannot find guidance while they guard the cause of their dark state. Another parable describes deeds beneath waves and clouds as (24:40 — **“Darkness upon darkness! If one stretches out their hand, they can hardly see it.”**). Layers make the loss worse: doubt clouds truth, lies guard the doubt, mockery resists advice, and evil company praises the whole course. Each layer makes return seem less needful. The image is therefore a warning against living on light that belongs only to one’s setting. Today, a person may grow up near scripture, use the speech of faith, and enjoy trust among believers while the heart avoids surrender. Nearness is a gift, but it is not a substitute for response. The right answer is not fear without hope. Believers themselves are told to repent and ask, (66:8 — **“Perfect our light for us, and forgive us. ˹For˺ You are truly Most Capable of everything.””**). Light is kept through sincere faith, pardon, and steps taken by it. The parable urges the reader to receive the truth before familiar words become a mere glow around a heart that still chooses night.
