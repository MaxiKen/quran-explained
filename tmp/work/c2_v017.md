**THE FIRST OF THE TWO PICTURES**

The sūrah now sets the whole passage in a picture: ***“Their example is that of someone”***, ***“who kindles a fire”***. The image is a man in the dark who lights a fire, sees the ground around him and feels safe, and then loses the light in a single moment. The first readers of the passage applied every part of the picture with care. Ibn ʿAbbās described a man who lit the fire to protect his family and property, and who saw what surrounded him and felt secure just before the flame went out, and he compared that to the pretenders whose safety and standing came from their public profession. Al-Suddī and others added that the fire was borrowed rather than owned, which is why it could be taken away without the man losing anything of his own. The picture is placed after the verse about the trade, and the placing is deliberate, since the loss of the light is the visible form of the loss described in the ledger. A reader who has just been shown a bargain with no profit is now shown the man who made it standing in the dark, and the second image is meant to be remembered with the first.

**THE MOMENT THE LIGHT IS TAKEN**

The middle of the verse holds the reversal: ***“but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness”***. The wording does not say that the fire went out by itself, and it does not say that the man put it out, since the sentence traces the act to God. That is the point of the picture for a reader who wonders why the pretenders were not exposed and punished at once: the light was given for a term, and the term belonged to the One who gave it. The Book shows the same scene at the end of the road in a sūrah where the pretenders ask the believers for a share of their light: (57:13 — **“Wait for us so that we may have some of your light.”**). Those men had lived by a borrowed light in this world without owning it, and the scene shows what happens when the borrowing ends. What turns on the picture is that a person's standing in a community is not the same thing as his standing before God.

**THE CONDITION THE PICTURE LEAVES BEHIND**

The verse closes with the state of the man in the dark: ***“unable to see”***. The early teachers read the phrase as confusion about the way and fear of what surrounds him, since a person in darkness cannot move and cannot defend himself. The Book uses light and darkness for the two conditions of the heart in a verse that contrasts them directly: (6:122 — **“a light with which they can walk among people”**). The difference between the two is the difference between walking and standing still, and the pretender who has lost his light is described as a man who cannot take a step. The reason the sūrah uses a picture instead of a verdict here is that the reader can hold the image longer than he holds a sentence, and it is the image that comes back to him when he is tempted to let his public face get ahead of his private state.

**A CAMPER WHO BORROWED A LANTERN**

Think of a man who sets out across a field at night carrying a lantern he borrowed from a neighbour, and who plans his route by its light while saying nothing about where the lantern came from. The light serves him for as long as the owner allows it, and the moment it is taken back the field is exactly what it was before. The picture fits the pretenders because their light was never theirs, and it fits the reader who is honest about his own dependence, since everything he sees by is given to him. These days a person can build a whole reputation out of a light that belongs to a community rather than to himself, and the verse quietly asks whose the lantern is. What follows is the second picture, which handles the kind of pretender who is not left standing in the dark but is carried along by forces he cannot control.
