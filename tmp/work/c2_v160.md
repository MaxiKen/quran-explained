**THE DOOR THAT OPENS AFTER THE WARNING**

The curse pronounced in the previous verse is followed at once by an exception, and the exception has conditions attached to it: ***“As for those who repent, mend their ways”***. Repentance is described in the Book as a turning, and the sentence requires more than regret, since the second condition is the repair of what was damaged by the concealment. The same conditions are stated elsewhere in the Book with the same promise attached to those who meet them: (3:89 — **“As for those who repent afterwards and mend their ways, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**). The order matters, because the turning comes first and the mending follows it, so the amendment is treated as the proof of the repentance rather than as a separate achievement. The warning was never meant to be the final word on the people it described. The bankrupt report preserved by Muslim warns that prayer and fasting do not cancel injuries inflicted on other people, so repairing the public harm belongs to repentance rather than standing outside it.

**REPAIR THAT REACHES THE TONGUE**

The conditions do not end with the repair of conduct, since the sentence goes on to require that the concealed matter be brought into the open: ***“and let the truth be known, they are the ones to”***. The offence had been committed with the tongue and with the keeping back of knowledge, so the remedy must be performed with the same organ in the opposite direction, and the person who hid what people needed is asked to publish it. The repair therefore reaches beyond the offender's own record to the harm that his silence caused among those who trusted him. The Book treats the concealment of revealed knowledge as a breach of a covenant, and a breach of that kind is not undone by a private apology. What the verse requires is that the well be opened again where it was covered.

**MEETING THE TURNING WITH A TURNING**

The sentence then names the response from the other side, and it speaks in the first person: ***“whom I will turn ˹in forgiveness˺”***, closing with the two names that guarantee the promise: ***“for I am the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”***. The Arabic behind the two halves is one root used twice, so that the servant's turning and God's turning are placed side by side, and the sentence makes the meeting between them the whole subject. The same pairing appears at the beginning of the chapter in the account of the first human beings, where the acceptance of repentance is named as an attribute of God: (2:37 — **“so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”**). The promise is therefore not an innovation introduced for these offenders but the settled manner of God with those who turn. The door offered here has its handle on the inside.

**A CLERK WHO REOPENS A FALSIFIED LEDGER**

Think of a clerk today who hides a mistake in the accounts by writing a false entry, and who months later brings the page back to his employer and asks for the record to be corrected. The correction costs him more than the silence did, because it exposes what the false entry concealed, and yet it is the only thing that returns the books to the truth. The verse asks for exactly that kind of correction, since the truth that was hidden must be made known again and the harm that followed from the silence must be repaired as far as it can be. Anyone who has had to admit a long-kept silence knows how much the third condition costs. What the sentence promises is that the cost is met with forgiveness rather than with punishment.

**A REMEDY SHAPED TO THE OFFENCE**

The remedy is shaped to the offence in every part: the turning answers the persistence, the mending answers the corruption, and the disclosure answers the concealment. This is why the verse can follow the curse so closely without contradiction, since the curse falls on the state that the offender refuses to leave, and the forgiveness meets the state he chooses instead. The Book repeats this pattern after its harshest warnings, and it leaves the door open for as long as the person is alive to walk through it. The passage on concealment therefore ends with the possibility of its repair rather than with the satisfaction of the curse. The choice the warning was spoken to produce is what the sentence leaves.
