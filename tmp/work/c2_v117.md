**THE ORIGINATOR NAMED FIRST**

The claim of the previous sentence is answered by naming God as the one who brought the heavens and the earth into being, and the name chosen describes invention rather than assembly: ***“˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens”*** and ***“and the earth!”***. The term used belongs to the vocabulary of creating something without a prior model, which is the answer to a claim about offspring in two ways: the originator is not the father of what he originates, and what he originates owes him everything. The Book's argument in the previous verse rested on ownership, and this sentence supplies the ground of that ownership by naming the act that produced it. A reader who has followed the sequence has now been given the reason the claim cannot stand, and the reason is the relation between a maker and what he makes. What the reader receives here is a title rather than an argument, and the title settles the matter.

**DECREE DESCRIBED IN A SINGLE WORD**

The sentence then describes how this originator acts when he decides something: ***“When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, “Be!”***. The contrast with the claim of the previous verse is exact, since the Book has just denied that God has offspring and now shows how everything that exists came to be: by a command. No intermediary, no partner and no material is named, and the absence is the whole content of the clause. Another sūrah states the same principle in similar words, and it also draws the conclusion that a child is impossible for such a maker: (40:68 — **“When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it”**). The Book uses this sentence wherever it needs to establish that creation depends on God's word alone, which is why it appears in the answers to several claims about divine sonship. A reader who has understood the clause has understood why the claim of the previous verse could not survive.

**BE, AND IT IS**

The command is quoted in the verse itself, and the sentence closes with the result following immediately on the word: ***“And it is”***. The Book places no interval between the decree and the existence of what is decreed, and the two words that end the sentence carry the whole of its force. The same pair of words describes the coming into being of a child in the annunciation to Mary, where the impossibility is answered in exactly this form. The use of so short a sentence for so large a claim is deliberate, since the Book's manner in these verses is to state the ease of the act rather than to describe its mechanics. The picture left behind is one of a command that is not followed by effort.

**A BUILDER WHO SPEAKS THE HOUSE INTO PLACE**

Think of a builder who does not lay a single brick but describes a house and finds it standing where he spoke of it. The verse's clause about the decree works at a larger scale and with no comparison intended, since the point of the image is that the result has no relation to labour. The comparison also makes clear why the two sentences of this passage belong together: the one denies that God has a family, and the other shows that what exists came to be without the involvement that a family would imply. Anyone who has watched a person try to account for the world without a maker can see how much the Book's answer depends on this single clause. What the verse offers is not an explanation of creation but the plainest statement of who created.

**THE PAIR OF VERSES AND WHAT THEY SETTLE**

Read together, the two sentences answer the claim of offspring in two stages, and each stage removes a different part of it. The ownership of everything rules out the possibility of a derived divine nature, and the command that produces everything rules out the need for any such derivation in the first place. The Book's practice in these verses is to answer a doctrinal claim with a statement about God's relation to the created order, and the two statements here are as brief as any in the sūrah. A reader who wanted a longer argument has the short one to work with, since the sentence about the decree leaves nothing for a partner to do. What remains for the reader is the title with which the passage opened, and the ease with which the Book has disposed of the claim.
