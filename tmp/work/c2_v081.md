**A PARTICLE THAT TURNS THE VERDICT**

One particle turns the whole passage over, and the grammarians made it a study text: ***“But no! Those who commit evil”***. The cry of certainty that opens the verse answers the claim that closed the last one, and the form of the answer was argued with the precision the question deserved. Sibawayh held that this particle and its affirmative twin are not nouns but particles like the simpler turn of correction, and the verse's usage here is a reply to the saying that the Fire would touch them only for counted days. The Kufan analysts read the form as that simpler turn with a euphonic letter added for the pause, the added letter carrying an affirmation inside it: the one particle negates a negation and the other affirms what was negated. The effect is surgical, because the claim was itself a negation — the Fire will not touch us — and this answer both cancels the cancellation and installs the opposite. The tradition presses the logic further: the universal statement stands as the major premise of a syllogism whose minor clause is left out, and the elision is more eloquent than spelling it would be. The point of the construction is that a claim about time is answered with a claim about character, since days can be granted by concession but character cannot be granted at all. What follows is therefore not a competing arithmetic of days but a law of persons, and the law states itself once for everyone in the same sentence. The book of the covenant's own summary puts the same rule in contract form — (98:6 — **“Indeed, those who disbelieve from the People of the Book and the polytheists will be in the Fire of Hell,”**) — notice who is named in it: membership buys no quota.

**WHAT SURROUNDS THE SOUL AT ITS END**

The verse then describes the condition of the condemned: ***“and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever”***. The middle phrase is a picture of geometry as much as morality, and the early generations read the surrounding three ways that nearly converge. One reading, transmitted by Abū Hurayrah and others, takes the surrounding as associationism itself: the idolatry wraps the person as a wrapper encloses its content. A second, from al-Rabīʿ b. Khuthaym through the teachers of Kufah and shared by al-Suddī, takes it as the state of one who dies without repenting, the wrongs closing over the record before it is opened. A third, held by Mujāhid, Qatādah, al-Hasan, and al-Rabīʿ b. Anas among others, takes it as the major sins that master their doer. The difference between the three readings is slight in practice, since each describes the same final posture of the soul from a different angle. The Arabic verb itself can mean to encircle, to enclose, or to comprehend, and the picture the glosses share is spatial: sin becomes the outer wall of the self, so that whatever good remains is inside the wrong rather than the wrong inside the good. In other words, the question at the end is not how long the record is but which side of the self the record sits on. A line of transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās through one of his students puts the whole reading in one report: *"whoever does what you did and disbelieves as you disbelieved until his unbelief surrounds whatever good he had — those are the companions of the Fire, they will abide therein forever"*. The companion report about accumulation, transmitted by Ibn Masʿūd with the collection of Aḥmad, agrees: when small sins gather, they bring about destruction. The last word of the verse then does the work the whole exchange demanded. Where the claim counted days, the verdict says forever, and the covenant-mountain passage had already ruled that the term is not up to the claimants: (11:108 — **“and the earth will endure, except what your Lord wills—a ˹generous˺ giving, without end”**). This is why the verse can close with a single word: the word carries the weight of a whole calendar. For a modern reader who keeps a private ledger of tolerable offences, the passage answers with the physics of the soul rather than a toll: what surrounds you at the end is what you built.
