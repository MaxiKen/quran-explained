**A CORRECTION: EVIL IS NOT A SHORT VISIT**

Two words end the discussion that the previous verse's confidence had opened: ***“But no! Those who commit evil”***, ***“and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever”***. The Book's denial is total, and its correction is not about arithmetic; it replaces a term of days with a permanence, and it attaches that permanence to a condition rather than to a lineage. The people described are those who have been enveloped by their misdeeds, and the image is of something that has closed over the offender rather than of a single act recorded against him. That distinction is the verse's real subject, since it separates the person who slips and returns from the person whose conduct has become the atmosphere he lives in. The claim about a limited punishment is answered by a description of a life that was never limited.

**A LIFE CLOSED OVER BY ITS OWN DEEDS**

The phrase translated here as engrossed or enveloped carries the sense of a thing that surrounds a person on every side, and the early teachers read it as a description of a man whose bad deeds have become his environment. This is a different category from the person who errs, regrets, and corrects himself, and the Book keeps the two apart in almost every passage about punishment. The same permanence is stated in another place where the residents of the Fire are named and their stay described without a term: (2:39 — **“But those who disbelieve”**). A reader who wants the verse to apply to someone else should notice that the condition is measured by what a person's life has become, which is not a judgement he can make about another man's interior. The Book's warning lands on the one person whose conduct he can actually inspect.

**WHY PERMANENCE IS THE RIGHT ANSWER TO PRESUMPTION**

The community had claimed a term of days, and the Book answers with forever, and the answer is not arbitrary. A person who has decided in advance how long his punishment will last has decided that his conduct cannot reach a certain severity, and that decision is itself a form of presumption about God. The claim was comfortable and it made repentance less urgent, which is exactly what the Book is warning against in this passage. The next verse sets the believers beside this description, and the contrast shows that the permanence is attached to a state of being and not to a label. A reader who takes the correction seriously will stop negotiating the terms of a judgement that has not yet happened and start examining the conduct that would determine them.

**A FLOODED ROOM AND THE WATER NOBODY NOTICES**

Think of a household that has stopped noticing the water rising through its own floor, and whose members argue about whether the damp will last the winter. The verse's word for being engrossed describes the people in that house, and the Book's answer to their argument is that the water is not seasonal. The comparison is uncomfortable because it suggests that the community's debate about the duration of punishment was itself a symptom, since a person whose conduct had not swallowed him would not be calculating the length of the penalty. A reader who finds that he has an opinion ready about how long he would deserve to be punished has already begun the calculation, and the verse invites him to set it down and look at the state of the room instead. The Book's treatment of the claim is therefore an examination of the claimant.

**THE RESIDENTS NAMED AND THE ALTERNATIVE HELD BACK**

The verse ends with a description and leaves the reader to turn the page for its counterpart, which is a structure the Book uses often and which keeps the reader moving. What has been established here is that the permanence of the Fire is tied to a life that has been taken over by wrongdoing, and that a claim to a shorter term is a claim about one's own case. The passage does not tell a reader how to measure the degree of envelopment in his own life, and no measure is required of him, because the Book's guidance throughout the sūrah has been to act on what is clear and to leave the accounting to God. The following verse names those who live in the Garden forever, and the two verses together form a single picture with the reader standing between them. What he does next is the subject of the rest of the chapter.
