**REMEMBERING THE SIGNS**

***“Ask the Children of Israel how many clear signs We”*** establishes remembering the signs. The verse asks the Children of Israel to remember how many clear signs Allah gave them. These signs include deliverance from Pharaoh, the splitting of the sea, manna and quails, the cloud of shade, water from the rock, the victories and guidance granted through their prophets, and the clear revelation sent to them. The question is not seeking information Allah lacks. It is an indictment of forgetfulness and ingratitude. A sign—āyah—is both a miracle and an indication. It should direct the heart toward its divine Giver. The problem was not that the signs were too few; it was that many people treated repeated favours as temporary conveniences and returned to disobedience once danger passed. Allah’s favour—niʿmah—is not simply comfort. It includes guidance, rescue, provision, knowledge, and the opportunity to serve. When a community receives these gifts, its responsibility increases. It must preserve the revelation, act justly, teach the next generation, and respond gratefully. The Qur’an repeatedly reminds Banū Isrāʾīl of their favours while also acknowledging that some among them were righteous and others were not. The warning is therefore not racial or collective. It addresses a historical pattern of receiving guidance, violating covenant, and claiming privilege while neglecting obedience. (2:174 — **“Indeed, those who hide Allah’s revelations,”**) Because remembering the signs frames favour creates responsibility, favour creates responsibility returns attention to remembering the signs.

**FAVOUR CREATES RESPONSIBILITY**

***“have given them. And whoever trades Allah’s favour—after receiving it—˹for”*** deepens favour creates responsibility. Muslims are equally accountable when they receive the Qur’an and Sunnah but treat them as decoration. Exchanges Allah’s favour means trading guidance for something lesser. A person may exchange truth for political influence, revelation for social approval, worship for wealth, or justice for group loyalty. The language of exchange connects this verse with the earlier warnings about selling the Book for a small price. The exchange can happen through neglect as well as active denial. A blessing may be lost when a person uses it against its purpose. Knowledge becomes a curse if it produces arrogance, wealth becomes a curse if it funds oppression, and authority becomes a curse if it protects corruption. Gratitude preserves a blessing by directing it to Allah’s obedience. Allah is severe in punishment because deliberate ingratitude after clear signs is a serious betrayal. This severity does not contradict His mercy. He gave signs, reminders, prophets, and opportunities to repent. Punishment follows persistent refusal, not a single moment of weakness or an honest question. The phrase is a warning rather than a reason for believers to celebrate another community’s punishment. Muslims should learn the principle and fear for themselves. The Qur’an says that Allah does not change a blessing granted to a people until they change what is within themselves. (8:53 — **“This is because Allah would never discontinue His favour to a people until they discontinue their faith.”**) Since exchanging favour for loss follows remembering the signs, favour creates responsibility links purpose to exchanging favour for loss.

**EXCHANGING FAVOUR FOR LOSS**

***“disbelief˺ should know that Allah is indeed severe in punishment”*** clarifies exchanging favour for loss. A community that wants its blessings preserved must maintain gratitude and justice. A miracle alone does not force submission. Pharaoh saw signs and still became arrogant; the Qur’an describes him and his people as rejecting them inwardly despite recognizing them. Signs clarify evidence, but pride can make a person reinterpret or conceal it. This is why spiritual purification matters alongside knowledge. The servant asks Allah for a heart that recognizes blessings before they disappear. He remembers past rescue, thanks Allah for present guidance, and does not demand a new miracle every time obedience becomes difficult. Muslims have received the greatest sign in the Qur’an, along with the Prophet’s example and countless daily favours. They must not assume that membership in the ummah makes them immune from the verse. If they conceal revelation, divide its teachings, abandon justice, or use religious language for worldly gain, they are exchanging favour for loss. The proper response is remembrance, gratitude, repentance, and service. A person who sees a blessing in his faith should make it available through good character and truthful teaching rather than using it to humiliate others. Think of remembering the signs through an heir who sells a piece of family land for a season of easy living. (27:14 — **“And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly.”**) The severe punishment matters, which means exchanging favour for loss returns the severe punishment to remembering the signs.

**THE SEVERE PUNISHMENT**

The money is spent before the year ends, the land is fenced by a stranger, and the man explains to his children how the sale was forced on him. Remembering the signs confronts anyone treating favour creates responsibility as remote; under remembering the signs, such a person has been given a truth in his youth and has traded it for the approval of a group. The verse gives him a ledger with one entry and asks him what price he received. Because the signs are counted by the Giver and not by the receiver, remembering the signs shows that no one has been given so little that he can plead ignorance. The Book puts the same severity in the verse that follows, so that remembering the signs reveals the gift and the account held by the same hand. A further passage states the law of the earlier community in a single line, and exchanging favour for loss invites comparison with the report of the signs. Favour creates responsibility makes remembering the signs practical; exchanging favour for loss therefore matters. Today, inherited knowledge and social opportunity become trusts whose gratitude appears through service rather than entitlement.
