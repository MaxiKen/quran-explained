**THE COVENANT-CASTING AS HABIT**

The section's last question is asked in the interrogative of exasperation, and its syntax is the syntax of a census taken too many times: ***“Why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside?”***. The repeated-time construction returns the passage to its oldest rhythm — every time — and the answer hidden inside the question is that the casting-aside is not an incident but a department. The noun for the group is the noun of the faction, and the factional shape of the breach matters. A covenant is not usually broken by a whole house at once. It is discarded by an active third while the rest keep the paper. The verb of the casting is the verb of throwing behind the back, the posture of the man who will not look at what he is abandoning. The earliest glosses on the pattern keep the same two-layer reading the section has used throughout. The deed belongs to the faction, and the responsibility travels to the majority that let the faction carry the mail.

**THE PATTERN ACROSS THE WHOLE DOCKET**

The verse does not name which covenant, and the tradition hears the silence as deliberate. The docket of the section already holds the covenant of the mountain with its seven articles, the civil covenant of blood and homes and ransom, the pledge whose signatories bore witness against themselves, and the standing question of the covenant taken at the origins of the race itself. One line of analysis holds that the pledge was taken from the loins of the first father: (7:172 — **“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord brought forth from the loins of the children of Adam their descendants”**). Another holds that it was sworn in the maturity of every generation on the lips of the prophets. The plural of the unnamed covenants is therefore the section's own archive, and the casting-verse is the archive's index. The transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās keeps the pledge's terms in their plainest form: shed not the blood of your people, nor turn your people out of their dwellings. The two clans whose houses the later fighting emptied are named as the clause's first witnesses. The turn-away of the earlier passage was already counted in the same currency: (2:83 — **“orphans and the needy; speak kindly to people; establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.” But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except”**). The witness-clause of the civil pledge is the closing entry: (2:84 — **“you gave your pledge and bore witness”**). Read the index as a whole and the habit appears in its full chronology. The covenants were not forgotten. They were filed and then discarded by the hand that signed them.

**THE MAJORITY-WORD AND ITS WORK**

The second sentence of the verse closes the census: ***“In fact, most of them do not believe”***. The rhetorical question that opened the passage is answered with the flatness of a headcount, and the headcount is the point. The claim of the claimants throughout the section has been a claim about belief. We believe in what was sent to us; we are the beloved; the fire is limited for us. The section's last word withdraws the claim at its root with the majority-verb. The glosses press the arithmetic. The faction breaks the covenant, and the majority do not even reach the threshold the word belief requires. That is the reason the covenant-breaking keeps recurring across the generations. The whole class of such breakers is named at the head of the surah, and the naming keeps nothing back: (2:27 — **“those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained,”**). The doctrine of the earlier reply-verse applies its final turn. The faith that divided the books into accepted and refused parcels was already named as the refusal of belief itself. What follows from the majority-word is the passage's parting verdict on the whole trial. The problem was never a missing signature on the covenant. The problem was a census in which belief itself was the minority report.

**THE VERDICT BEHIND THE STATISTICS**

The placement of the headcount at the section's end deserves its own reading, because the sentence does rhetorical work beyond its arithmetic. The journey from the woe of the writing verse to this census is a journey from the deed to the diagnosis. The pages were forged, the prices were paid, the covenants were discarded, the prophets were killed, the angel was tried, the signs were denied — and the final word explains why the machinery kept running. The disbelief named in the last sentence is the engine, and every covenant-casting was the engine's exhaust. The tradition reads the sequence the way one reads a trial that ends with the defendant's character rather than the defendant's acts. The acts were already entered in evidence. The character is what makes the next acts inevitable. In other words, the section does not end on a crime. It ends on the disposition that manufactures crimes.

**THE CLOSE OF THE HUNDRED-VERSE ARC**

The passage that began with the secret counsels of the pricing-house closes with a public census of belief, and the arc of the hundred verses is the arc of a single trial. The opening questions asked who the custodians were and what they wrote. The closing question asks who believed at all. A later community — including the ones assembled today — that keeps the vocabulary of covenant in its liturgy while managing the covenant's articles by faction will meet the same headcount in advance. What turns on the census is everything the section has built. The covenant is not a heritage to be carried but a belief to be practiced, and the practice is the only signature the last day will read. The next pages of the revelation open on the magic the trial's losers preferred to the message. The book that casts its covenants behind its back always has a sorcerer waiting at the door.
