**A HEART HARDER THAN THE STONE IT SAW**

The sign has been given and the chapter turns immediately to the state of the people who received it: ***“Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock”***, ***“or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers”***, ***“others split, spilling water”***, ***“while others are humbled in awe of Allah.”***, ***“And Allah is never unaware of what you do”***. The comparison is not between a heart and any stone, but between a heart and the stones that do something, since the verse names the rocks that give water and the rocks that fall in awe and leaves the hardened heart with neither of those capacities. That is the force of the phrase about being harder: a stone can be softer than a heart if the stone responds to its Lord and the heart does not. The reader who watched water come out of stone earlier in this sūrah is now asked to compare the rock's obedience with the people's, since the same event is retold with the command that produced it: (2:60 — **“And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, “Strike the rock with your staff.””**). Ibn ʿAbbās connected the hardening to the refusal that followed even after the victim had been revived and his killer disclosed; a witnessed sign had not produced submission.

**WHY STONE BECOMES THE COMPARISON**

The choice of comparison is deliberate, because the community had just watched a stone produce twelve streams at the command of God. Any stone in that history was a better servant than the hearts that had watched the miracle and gone on as before, and the verse says so without softening the language. The same image is used elsewhere in the Book to contrast the receptive and the closed, and the verse's three clauses move from the rocks that pour, to the rocks that split, to the rocks that submit, so that the reader ends on the most surprising member of the list. The early teachers read the passage as a description of hearts that had stopped responding after receiving proof, and they placed it beside the signs given to this people in the wilderness. A reader who has heard the same stories and felt nothing new for years should recognise the position he is in.

**THREE KINDS OF STONE, THREE KINDS OF RESPONSE**

The verse sorts stones by their behaviour, and the sorting is a small lesson in itself. One pours out rivers, another breaks open and lets water through, and a third stands in awe of its Maker; the first two are useful, and the third is simply obedient. Human beings who have received a revelation are expected to do at least as much as the rocks they have seen, and the verse's accusation is that they have managed less. The Book uses the same rank in another place where the mountains are described as humbled and the earth as speaking, and it does not treat those images as decoration. What the reader should carry away is not that the comparison is harsh but that it is available: a person who has hardened still has the material of a softer thing inside him, and the verse does not say the condition is permanent.

**THE WARNING IN THE LAST CLAUSE**

The final sentence is short and takes the discussion out of the hands of the people discussing it: God is never unaware of what they do. The Book places this clause after the description of hearts because the audience might otherwise assume that hardness is invisible and costless, a private condition with no consequences attached. The whole section of this sūrah is written against that assumption, since the previous verses have just shown a killer exposed and a concealment revealed. A reader who finds his own heart described in the comparison should read the clause as a comfort as much as a threat, because the One who is aware of the hardness is also the One who can remove it. The verse ends without offering a remedy, and the next verses explain where the hardness came from.

**WHEN THE LIVING CENTRE CEASES TO YIELD**

Think of a person who has been in the same argument for ten years and can no longer hear the other side's sentences as arguments at all; the words arrive as noise and leave without effect. That is the condition the verse describes, and it is not produced by a single event; it is the result of repeated refusal to be moved. The people of this chapter had received water from stone, food from the sky, and a dead man raised in front of them, and the Book's verdict on them is that their hearts came out of it harder than the objects that had obeyed. A reader can test the same condition in himself by noticing what he has stopped responding to: a verse he no longer hears, a warning he has explained away, a duty he has agreed with for years. The Book's answer to a hardening heart is not a new miracle; it is the same message, heard again with the intention of being changed by it. In the present day, repeated exposure to suffering can harden a conscience unless attention is deliberately restored.
