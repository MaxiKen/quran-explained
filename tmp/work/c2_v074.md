**THE HEARTS THE SIGNS COULD NOT WET**

The address of you in this verse has drawn a real dispute, and both candidate referents are in the earliest glosses. Ibn ʿAbbās takes the hearts as the hearts of the slain man's heirs, and the tale gives that reading its force. When their kinsman rose and named them, they answered with a denial made in full knowledge. Al-Awfi transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās the scene in these terms: *\"when he was asked who had killed him, he said that his nephews had killed him; then he died again, and they denied the truth while they knew it\"*. The other reading, standing with Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Qatādah, and the mass of the learned, takes the address to the Children of Israel entire — the people whose bent the whole section has been trying. A second tilt of the same question then shows in the reading of the crowd as such. The scold can stand against those who stood at the scene and watched a corpse speak, and it can stand against the later folk who heard the tale read and turned from what it teaches. The gap between the two referents is small, since a group's set way is just what makes a kin's denial at the sharp hour. The layered reading lets the verse speak to the event and to the heir of the event at once. What turns on the pronoun is the reach of the warning and not its heart. The charge names the heirs who lied, or the folk whose bent made the lie cheap. The illness found in both is the same hardening after the clearest sign.

**WHAT HARDNESS MEANS AND WHY STONE IS THE MEASURE**

The hardening is named with a verb whose root kin drags dryness and the withered state, and the glosses gather its sense like wounds on one frame. The heart is told of as dried and withered, which is the going of mercy and softness from it. Another sense has it grown coarse. A third has it blackened. The likeness then does the diagnosing: ***“Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock”***, ***“or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers”***. The like is to the stone in its hardness and its barrenness toward the weight of deeds, and the strange word at the head of the line marks how far the state stands from what one would think. The hardening comes after the one event whose whole aim was to lift it. The joining of the second clause reads as a plain and or as the sharper nay, and both keep the climb: like stone, or past stone. The choice of stone over the harder metals is instructive, because the stone still keeps the chance of what the heart has lost. Iron is harder and it is not the gauge. The stone is, since the stone in this very passage does three things the hardened heart refuses to do.

**THREE KINDS OF STONE, THREE DEGREES OF ALIVE**

The list of stones is scaled with a careful hand. The first pour gushing rivers — the image at the top of the scale, a rock giving drink to beast and man, the very figure of a life-bearing heart. The second ***“others split, spilling water”***, giving less but still giving. The third ***“while others are humbled in awe of Allah.”***. The water in the image is the water the Book treats as life and the means of life in every place. It is sent down from the sky, it rises from beneath the ground, and it is figured inside the making of live things themselves. So the rock which gushes is a dead thing bearing what the living need, and the contrast with the heart that bears nothing is full. The falling of the third kind is then the top of the scale and not its floor, since a thing that fears is a thing still addressed by its Lord. The Book presses that wakefulness onto the most firm things it can name: (59:21 — **“Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain, you would have certainly seen it humbled”**). The mountain of Moses furnishes the old picture of the same truth, where the set sight levelled the peak to the ground before the man who asked to see: (7:143 — **“If it remains firm in its place, only then will you see Me.” When his Lord appeared to the mountain,”**). The point is that the whole made world is awake to its Author. It is the human heart alone in this passage that sleeps.

**WHEN EVEN STONE REMEMBERS ITS LORD**

The tradition about living stones is not confined to this passage, and its reports draw the picture from every side. The Prophet's own words about the greeting stone — *\"I know a stone in Makkah that used to greet me before I was made a prophet, and I know it even now\"* — are handed down in Muslim. The mountain that answered affection — *\"This is a mountain that loves us, and we love it\"* — stands in the two Ṣaḥīḥ collections. The palm-stump that wept beside the pulpit, answered with the Prophet's embrace and his words *\"It weeps for the remembrance it used to hear\"*, is handed down by Tirmidhī and Nasāʾī. A further reading of the three kinds of stone takes them as three kinds of human weepers. There are those who weep much, those who weep little, and those whose weeping stays in the heart and never reaches the face. The psychology of the likeness is exact. Even the mountain's surrender is drawn into the argument, since the whole of creation is described as hymning its Author in voices the audience cannot hear: (17:44 — **“that does not glorify His praises—but you ˹simply˺ cannot comprehend their glorification. He is indeed Most Forbearing, All-Forgiving”**). Some of the early readings hold that all three kinds of stone in the verse act out of fear of God, the gushing and the splitting included. In other words, the list is not really mineralogy. It is a ladder of response laid against a people, and the stones hold each rank above the one the hearts have picked.

The verse shuts the likeness by turning from the metaphor to the account: ***“And Allah is never unaware of what you do”***. The closing phrase is read from two sides by the same voice. Ibn ʿAbbās takes it as the word that God will not leave the wrongs unpunished, and he also reports the sense that the what you do holds what is hidden. These are the denials spoken in the heart while the tongue answers the questioner. The handed-down form shifts the closing verb itself, moving from you do to they do as the charge widens from the crowd at hand to everyone who acts like it. Against the certainty of the reckoning stands the patience of the pause, since the same clause has been read as respite: God is not heedless, and He gives time till the set term. The two readings balance without cancelling: the unawareness is shut out, and the haste is not implied. For people who hear the passage in every age the application is exact and unsentimental. These days the pattern of hardening is not a tale about one family or one town. It is the standing risk of anyone who watches a sign arrive and files it away unopened, and the closing clause is the reminder that filing is also doing.
