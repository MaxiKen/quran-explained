**THE VERDICT ON THE PORTRAIT JUST DRAWN**
Four marks were laid out over the three verses before this one, and the sūrah now gives its verdict on the people who carry them: ***“It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord”***. The sentence is a seal rather than a new item, and its weight lies in the pronoun, which means the verdict cannot be borrowed from another company. The guided are not a vague crowd somewhere in history. They are the specific people just described, the ones who trust what they cannot see, keep the prayer standing, give out of what they were given, and accept the revelation that reached them along with what came before it. Al-Fātiḥah asked for a road, (1:6 — **“Guide us along the Straight Path”**), and this verse shows who ends up walking it. The answer to the prayer is a portrait, and the portrait is drawn so that a reader can measure himself against it rather than wonder about the company.
**THE PRICE AND THE PRIZE**
The second half names the destination: ***“and it is they”*** — the very same people, no others — ***“who will be successful”***. Success in the Qur'an is not a mood and not a prize handed out at random; it is the state of a person who obtains what he hoped for and escapes what he feared, and the sūrah later treats that state as the fruit of the same discipline. One passage announces the whole company in three words, (23:1 — **“Successful indeed are the believers”**), and another ties the outcome to obedience under pressure, (24:51 — **“It is they who will ˹truly˺ succeed”**). The same promise closes a command about giving to relatives, the poor and the traveller, (30:38 — **“and it is they who will be successful”**), which shows how wide the road is: it runs through a man's wallet and his family before it reaches his reward. Another passage names the same company, where striving with wealth and life meets the identical verdict (9:88), because the promise is attached to the labour rather than to the result.
**TWO VERDICTS, ONE ROAD**
The verse also settles a question that troubles anyone trying to live well in a confusing time. Is guidance a private feeling, or can a person know whether he is on the road at all? The sūrah answers with marks that can be checked, and it repeats the verdict at the end of the next verse group for the rejecters, so that the reader is given two finished portraits rather than a riddle. Picture a merchant who judges his business by how he feels about it rather than by whether the stock moves, the accounts balance and the customers return, and the difference between the two men is exactly the difference between a feeling and a mark. The marks in this verse work the same way, which is why the sūrah offers them as a portrait rather than as a mystery. A man who finds himself believing what he cannot see, praying when nobody watches, and giving without an audience has evidence in his own week, not merely hope in his heart.
**WHERE THIS LEAVES A READER TODAY**
The closing promise is worth carrying into the coming week, because success is the thing every person is already chasing by some other name. The Qur'an does not tell a believer to stop wanting success; it tells him where the road runs and lets him walk it, and the reason it can be so calm about the outcome is that the road has been walked before him. A household that keeps the two verses above it in view can test itself without drama: the unseen is believed when nobody is watching, the prayer stands when the morning is busy, the giving continues when the month is tight, and the Hereafter is treated as a real appointment. None of that requires a special gift or a perfect record, and all of it can begin again today, which is what makes the promise a mercy rather than a burden.
