**A FACE TURNED TOWARD HEAVEN**

The verse begins with what God saw in His Prophet, and the sight is described in words the Prophet himself would have recognised: ***“Indeed, We see you ˹O Prophet˺ turning your face towards heaven. Now We will make you turn towards a direction ˹of prayer˺”*** and ***“that will please you.”***. The longing is named before it is answered, so that the desire is dignified before it is granted. Nothing in the sentence says that the Messenger had complained or asked; what it says is that God saw him turning, and the answer came to the turning rather than to a request. The reading credited to Ibn ʿAbbās describes the turning as a waiting for Gabriel to arrive with the command about the House, and the description fits the reply that follows. What stands in the scene is a petition heard before it was spoken.

**THE SANCTUARY LONGED FOR**

The direction that would please the Messenger was the one belonging to Abraham's sanctuary, which the sūrah has just spent a long passage describing as the first house of worship. The communities of Medina had their own orientations, and the announcement that the believers would face the ancient House settled a matter that had been unsettled since the migration. The earlier account of the patriarchs in this sūrah has shown that the House was built as a refuge for all who came to pray, which makes the change of orientation a return rather than an innovation. Someone who has followed the chapter from the account of Abraham's prayer will see why the answer to the longing was also a claim on a heritage. The change was a restoration in the eyes of the community that had been waiting for it, which is what the sentence settles.

**TURNING TOWARD THE HOUSE**

The command that follows is doubled, since it is given to the Messenger and then to everyone with him: ***“So turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque ˹in Mecca˺”***, ***“wherever you are, turn your faces towards it. Those”*** and ***“who were given the Scripture certainly know this to be the truth from their Lord.”***. The change is absolute, and the clause about the believers' location removes any exception for distance, land, or sea. Another passage states the same command in nearly the same form and adds the reason it was given, which shows how central the sentence is to this part of the sūrah: (2:150 — **“Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque”**). The sentence's final clause then lays a claim about the people of the earlier scriptures, which the following verses will take up: they knew what the command confirmed, and the verse says so before they are accused of hiding it.

**A DEED ARRIVING AFTER LONG WAITING**

Think of tenants who have lived in a rented house for years and who one day receive the deed to the house they had always regarded as theirs. The verse's change of direction has that quality for the community, which had been facing the sanctuary of another people while the House of its own ancestor stood in the south. The comparison also explains the clause about the People of the Scripture, since a deed transferred is best understood by those who knew its history. A long anticipation ending in a plain command is the shape of the sentence, and the command is plain because the matter had been settled long before. The moment of arrival is what the passage holds out.

**KNOWLEDGE HELD BY THE OTHERS**

The verse closes by naming God's awareness of what the others do, and the clause is placed after the statement about their knowledge rather than before it: ***“And Allah is never unaware of what they do”***. The pairing is deliberate, since the sentence has just said that the communities knew the truth of the change, and the closing words state that their conduct with that knowledge is being recorded. The combination repeats where the Book addresses what the People of the Scripture knew and what they concealed: (2:146 — **“Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺ as they recognize their own children”**). A sentence is given that grants the knowledge, names the command, and leaves the accounting to God. The next verses draw out the consequences of that knowledge for the people who held it.
