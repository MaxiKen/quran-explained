**BOTH EXITS CLOSED**

***“They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will”*** opens the account of duration and intensity together. The two negatives that follow close both doors a prisoner would try: ***“not be lightened”*** removes mitigation, and ***“nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺”*** removes postponement. The previous verse delivered the sentence; this one describes its administration. Because relief and adjournment are named and denied, the horror is not only that the punishment is severe but that its administration is constant. Ibn ʿAbbās's report has it that the doom will not be lifted or reduced, and that it will not be deferred.  Every form of hope the punished might form is answered by name, and the naming is done before the hope can be spoken.

**NOT EVEN A BLINK**

The denial of lightening is total in my reading — not an instant of relief separates them from the doom. Human courts normally offer exactly the two mercies this verse removes: sentences shortened for good conduct, and starts postponed pending appeal. The stacking of the negatives therefore anticipates the precise hopes of the punished and forecloses each one. In the administration described here the hours count the same on the first day and the ten-thousandth, and no season brings the matter to a close. The horror is the removal of every curve in the graph. Ibn ʿAbbās's wording is stark: the doom is neither lifted nor reduced, and no deferral is granted once the matter is settled. The smallest unit of relief the mind can invent is the unit reported as refused, so the verse closes the door on the microscopic as well as on the infinite. Both denials are administrative rather than descriptive: they speak of what will be done with the sentence rather than of what the Fire is like. An administration without a schedule of grace is the state the two negatives describe. (33:65 — **“to stay there for ever and ever—never will they find any protector or helper”**)

**WHERE HOPE BELONGS**

A hearer may ask whether "forever" can be reconciled with a mercy said to embrace everything. The two statements are reconciled by their different addresses, because the mercy is offered inside a life where it can be received; this verse describes the account after the offer has been refused at its last door. That is why the severity is announced alongside verses of hope rather than instead of them. The pair of passages prices the present correctly. This is the difference between medicine available at the pharmacy and medicine at the post-mortem — the same substance, differently timed, is either gift or memory. No hearer can claim the text was unclear about which side of the door hope belongs to. The verse also settles the question of whether the punishment might be imagined as a series of episodes with rests between them; the denial of delay removes the rests along with the end. Because the two denials stand in the same breath, neither can be read as an exaggeration of the other — lightening and deferral are separate mercies, and each is refused separately. In the administration described, the punished find no season of relief and no procedural gap to enter. The steadiness is the horror, and the horror is meant to be read while steadiness is still available on the other side.

**THE ADMINISTRATION DESCRIBED**

The companion verses fix the same steady state, and the renewal of torment is drawn out in (4:56 — **“Surely those who reject Our signs, We will cast them into the Fire. Whenever their skin is burnt completely,”**). The verse also answers the fantasy of gradual cooling, since a punishment that could taper might be imagined as finite; the denial of lightening removes the taper along with the end. The mind's last refuge — that the sentence might soften by attrition — is closed by name. What remains is the same intensity repeated without interruption. The verse thus describes eternity in the only way a temporal reader can grasp it: as a rate that never changes. Ibn ʿAbbās reports that no respite is granted in which to repent or to prepare a defence. The application is sober: every day of life is the only period in which the administration of mercy is still possible. The two closed exits make the open door of the present valuable beyond measure.
