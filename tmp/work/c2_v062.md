**WHO IS COUNTED, AND WHAT IS ASKED OF HIM**

The verse widens the field at the moment a reader expects it to narrow: ***“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and”***, ***“the Last Day and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And”***, ***“there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”***. Four communities are named, and the condition that follows cuts across all four of them, since the promise is attached to belief and good conduct rather than to a label. The naming is not a compliment paid to each group; it is a statement that the categories people use to sort themselves are not the categories the reward is sorted by. The Sabians were a small and obscure community in the region, which is exactly why their appearance in the list is significant, since a group with no political weight is included on the same terms as the others. The sentence closes with the two things the Day removes for those who meet its condition, and both are things people carry every day: fear of what is coming, and grief over what is gone.

**TWO READINGS OF THE WIDENING**

This verse has been read in two ways, and the difference is worth knowing because both readings are held by serious people. One line of reading holds that it describes the position before the final revelation arrived, so that Jews, Christians, and Sabians who lived faithfully within their own dispensations are addressed as a community that has since been replaced by the arrival of the Qur'an. Another line holds that the conditions named remain open to anyone in any time, and that a person who truly believes in God and the Last Day and lives well is not excluded by the name his community carries. The Book itself supplies the check on both readings in a later sūrah, where the same list is repeated with the same promise, and where the order of the names is even rearranged: (5:69 — **“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians and Christians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and”**). The rearrangement is a small signal that the list is not a ranking. Also carried in the early reports is that the verse came down in answer to a question about the fate of people who had followed earlier prophets, and that the answer described their case rather than abolishing the conditions of belief.

**BELIEF, THE LAST DAY, AND THE WORK BETWEEN THEM**

The two beliefs named are joined to a deed, and the deed is not left vague: ***“whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah”*** is placed beside good-doing, so an empty claim, producing nothing in a person's conduct, is not the case the verse is describing. The Last Day is named here as it is named throughout the chapter, because a person's certainty about the meeting shapes what he does with his time and his money. The same combination of faith and good work appears elsewhere in the Book as the one doorway that admits anyone at all: (2:112 — **“But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah”**). The order matters too, since faith comes first and the good deed is its fruit rather than its price; a reader who reverses the order turns the verse into a scheme of payment and loses the mercy it is describing. A second sūrah reports the same principle for the People of the Book individually rather than by group: (3:199 — **“Indeed, there are some among the People of the Book”**). The Book never treats the group as the unit of judgement, even when it addresses people in groups.

**FEAR AND GRIEF, THE TWO THINGS THAT ARE REMOVED**

The last clause is worth a paragraph of its own, because it describes the two things a believer is spared: ***“there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve”***. Fear looks forward and grief looks backward, and between them they consume most of the anxiety a person carries. The reward described here is not a description of gardens, which comes elsewhere in the chapter; it is the removal of the two emotions that make a person unable to enjoy what he has. The early teachers noted that the verse puts the two together because the human being is rarely free of both at once, and that the promise is made in the same breath as the requirement, so that the reader sees them as parts of one arrangement. This is also the place where the comparison with the merchant in the market sits comfortably: a man who has been guaranteed the safety of his goods behind him and a place to sleep ahead of him can trade all day without either of those two weights on his chest.

**ONE STANDARD SET BEFORE EVERY COMMUNITY**

A reader may finish the verse and ask where the boundary now runs, and the honest answer is that the verse gives the test rather than the roster. The terms it sets are faith in God and in the Last Day, together with good conduct, and it does not add membership of a particular community to the list. That is why communities that read this verse often end up applying it to themselves first, since the conditions are demanding enough to keep anyone busy. The same sūrah returns to the question later with a harder edge, warning that a claim of belonging will not substitute for the work: (98:6 — **“Indeed, those who disbelieve from the People of the Book”**). The verse is therefore best read as an invitation extended before a warning, and as a reminder that the One who will judge on that Day has described in advance what he will be looking at. What remains on the reader's side is to check his own two hands rather than his neighbours' labels, since the promise is written for whoever meets the terms, and no one can answer the terms on another's behalf.
