**A DOOR OPENED AFTER THE VERDICT**

The account has just closed a people under a stamp of disgrace, and the very next line refuses to let that ending be read as a closing of the sky: ***“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians”***. The Book pairs warning with promise in this way as its settled practice, so that a hearer who has just watched a punishment is not left with the thought that mercy has run out. The placement answers a misgiving the previous verse could easily leave behind. A whole people has just been marked for conduct of that kind, and the question whether return is still open to anyone has to be settled at once; the verse itself settles it. The door is named again here for every community, and the condition standing at its threshold is set out for all of them together. What the verse promises is also placed against what the previous verse removed. Disgrace was stamped on the wrongdoers there; here the reward waits with the Lord of those who qualify, and the two endings could not stand farther apart. A community is therefore never condemned as a community in this Book; it is condemned for what its wrongdoers did, while its believers are answered by name.

**WHO STANDS INSIDE THE NAMED GROUPS**

The list of names has been read from more than one side, and the differences are transmitted early rather than invented late. The first group, those who believe, is read by the received gloss as those who affirm the Prophet, and it is also read by an early authority, Sufyan al-Thawri, as those whose belief is on their tongues alone, which is why they are counted beside the others and then left to the condition. The name of the second group carries its own history, since the form of it in Arabic is traced to the eldest son of Jacob and to the turning the name records. The third name points back to the town of Jesus and to the disciples’ claim to be helpers of the faith. The fourth name has drawn the widest disagreement: ***“and Sabians whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah”***. Descriptions of them run from a sect that shaves the middle of the head and reads the Gospel while worshipping the angels — Ibn ʿAbbās gives that picture — to people with no scripture or rite at all, and the sounder placement among the transmitted views keeps them inside the Christian fold rather than outside every religion. Later communities claimed the name for themselves, and a claim of that kind settles nothing about what the verse meant when it was sent. The parallel listing elsewhere of the same four groups confirms that these are the communities of the revelation’s own world: (5:69 — **“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians and Christians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good,”**). The order of the names shifts between the two passages, and the condition never shifts.

**THE CONDITION GOVERNS THE LABELS**

Everything turns on the qualification: ***“and the Last Day”***. Belief in God is bound here to belief in the final accounting, and deeds are bound to both. The wording then seals the connection: ***“and does good will have their reward with their Lord.”***. The condition is spoken in the singular and answered in the plural, a grammar that lets one person and a whole people hear themselves addressed by the same sentence. The label of a community is therefore not the passport the verse describes; the condition is. That is why the passage must be read with the Book’s own later rule: (3:85 — **“Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them,”**). The report of the sequence is explicit. Ali ibn Abi Talhah transmits from Ibn ʿAbbās that this later verse was revealed after the one under discussion, and the conclusion carried by that sequence is that no deed is accepted from anyone after the final message except on its terms; before it, every people was answered on the terms their own message brought. Two readings have therefore stood in the field. The first takes the verse as the standing rule for every age, so that any sincere monotheist who believes in God and the Last Day and does good is rewarded. The second reads it as the announcement of God’s practice toward the righteous of earlier communities in their own times, with the invitation now answered only by the faith the final message carries. The reading followed here is the second, because the sequence of the two verses is transmitted by a Companion, and because belief in God and the Last Day is measured by what the messengers of God taught and not by the sincerity a person claims for himself. What the first reading guards is not lost in the second: no servant of God in any age was ever wronged, and the righteous of the earlier communities were never refused their reward.

**WITHOUT FEAR AHEAD, WITHOUT GRIEF BEHIND**

The promise closes on the two absences that define peace: ***“And there will be no fear”***. The absence is ordered forward and backward in the transmitted glosses. Fear belongs to what has not yet arrived, and grief belongs to what has already passed; the people of this promise are secured against both directions at once. One early gloss gives the two their furthest reach — no fear when death is overcome and the Fire is closed, and no grief for anything left behind in the world. The same double assurance is given to the close servants of God elsewhere: (10:62 — **“There will certainly be no fear for the close servants of Allah, nor will they grieve”**). And it is placed on the lips of the angels at the moment it is needed most: (41:30 — **“Surely those who say, “Our Lord is Allah,” and then remain steadfast, the angels descend upon them, ˹saying,˺ “Do not fear,”**). The closing clause of the verse under discussion states the same end in plain words: ***“for them, nor will they grieve”***. What follows for the reader who has travelled from the stamp of disgrace to this sentence is the shape of the whole passage. The Book has just shown a people ruin their own standing by refusing guidance; it now shows that the standing itself is offered again to anyone who meets the condition. The reward is held with the Lord, which means no negligence mislays it and no power takes it, and the two absences stand guard over it until the day it is paid.
