**A TRUE PART CAN HIDE A FALSE WHOLE**

The command ***“Do not mix truth with falsehood”*** concerns more than a wholly false statement. Ibn ʿAbbās explains it as mixing facts with lies. The danger is that sound teaching lends credit to what has been added to it. A hearer may recognize one true part and accept the rest without seeing the change. Mixing thus differs from an open refusal of truth: it can preserve the appearance of belief while turning belief away from its proper object. The force of the command is to keep the boundary clear. God’s revealed word must not be blended with an invented claim and then presented as one message from Him. The earlier command to believe in revelation gives this duty its setting. Those who know a Book should help others distinguish what it teaches from what it does not. Because their knowledge gives their words weight, they can make falsehood harder to detect than someone who speaks without such standing. That weight is a reason for care, not a right to recast the truth.

Abū al-ʿĀliyah gives a pointed example: some acknowledged Muhammad as a messenger but claimed that his mission was not to them. The admission retained a true part, while the added restriction denied the reach of his call. The Quran states that reach in his commanded address to humanity: (7:158 — **“I am Allah’s Messenger to you all.”**). The false restriction therefore changes the duty that follows from admitting the truth. It is not a harmless qualification. A person who accepts only a messenger for someone else can appear fair while refusing the message directed to himself. Ibn Zayd also applies the warning to mixing the revealed Torah with what people wrote into it. The examples differ, but both protect the line between a gift from God and a human addition. This is why a correct quotation does not by itself make an explanation correct. A statement placed beside it can narrow, reverse, or hide what the quotation establishes. Faithful teaching must preserve the meaning as well as the words.

**WHAT IS LEFT UNSAID CAN ALSO MISLEAD**

The prohibition continues, ***“or hide the truth”***. Distortion and silence are two different ways to keep people from the same good. The first gives them a mixed account; the second withholds the part they need. Ibn ʿAbbās relates this concealment to knowledge of the Messenger and what he brought, already found in the scriptures held by those addressed. A later verse describes that recognition and then adds, (2:146 — **“Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly”**). The wording identifies a group; it does not charge every person of a lineage with an identical act. The grammar here can join two separate prohibitions, or describe the combining of distortion with concealment. The broader reading is clearer because each act is itself wrong. The ban does not mean that either practice becomes lawful when carried out without the other. Someone who tells no lie but hides a truth he owes may still mislead. Someone who states that truth and surrounds it with false claims has not discharged the trust merely by mentioning it.

The final word, ***“knowingly”***, makes the charge more serious. The rebuke falls on people who possess the relevant knowledge, not on someone who simply has not learned the answer. Their knowledge should have restrained them from the deed. Instead, they used the advantage it gave them to leave others without clear guidance. This is why learning can increase responsibility rather than offer a defence against it. The Quran repeats the challenge to the People of the Book: (3:71 — **“Why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide the truth knowingly”**). Its question exposes the clash between knowing and hiding. It also guards against treating a scholar’s status as proof that every statement is true. ʿAlī’s counsel is to recognize truth and then recognize its people, rather than make the rank of people the measure of truth. That counsel does not abolish the need for sound teachers. It denies them the right to overrule what is known to be true. The trust belongs to the knowledge they bear, not to the prestige that gathers around them.

**WISE DISCLOSURE DOES NOT ALLOW A FALSE ANSWER**

The duty to make truth clear is not a demand to say everything to everyone in the same form. In a report carried by al-Bukhārī, ʿAlī urges teaching people what they can understand, lest the way it is presented lead them to deny God and His Messenger. In Muslim’s introduction, Ibn Masʿūd likewise warns that a report beyond a people’s understanding may become a trial for some of them. The point is to help truth reach its hearers, not to keep it from them for private gain. A teacher may choose the order and level of an explanation, or decline to guess at what he does not know. Such care is not the knowing concealment blamed here. Since a needed answer may be clear from a situation itself, duty also need not wait for someone to ask a polished question. Where people are being misled, the need can call for clarification. None of these distinctions allows a false answer to replace a true one. Caution about when to speak cannot make lying a form of faithful teaching.

Repair must address the harm done by the silence or the mixture. A private change of mind may leave the public error in place. The Quran’s promise of pardon in the later passage includes those who (2:160 — **“repent, mend their ways, and let the truth be known,”**). Repentance, reform, and disclosure belong together because the wrong had kept others from guidance. The repair should make clear what was false, restore what was hidden, and stop presenting personal additions as revelation. This does not require filling every lesson with remote disputes that its hearers do not need. It requires a true account of the matter on which they are entitled to guidance. The difference is between serving their understanding and managing it for the speaker’s advantage. The verse began with a mixture and ended with knowledge. Its demand is that knowledge be used to undo confusion rather than to manufacture it. Only then does possession of scripture perform its proper work: making the path of obedience clearer for those who hear it.
