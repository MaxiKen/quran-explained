**DOUBT INVITED TO PRODUCE ITS PROOF**

After naming the gifts, the sūrah turns to the message itself and answers a doubt with an invitation to test it: ***“And if you are in doubt about”***, ***“what We have revealed to Our servant,”***. The servant named here is the Prophet, and the title is worth noticing, because the Book calls him a servant at the moment it stakes everything on the revelation he carries. Ibn ʿAbbās and the earliest teachers read the doubt as belonging to the pretenders and to those who had heard the message and hesitated, and the verse does not treat the doubt as a crime in itself. The Book states the same invitation in two other sūrahs with the number of sūrahs they were asked to produce named in each, which shows that the challenge was repeated as the message was repeated. What turns on the wording is that the reader is not asked to swallow the claim; he is told to test it with the tools he already has.

**WHAT THEY WERE TOLD TO BRING**

The invitation is specific: ***“then produce a sûrah like it”***. The demand is not for a book or for a poem but for something of the same kind as the shortest unit of what had been revealed, which means the challenge stands within the reach of anyone with a language and a claim. The Book repeats the same demand in two places with the helpers and the honesty clause attached, and one of them shows exactly how much help the challengers were permitted to gather: (10:38 — **“Produce one sûrah like it then, and seek help from whoever you can—other than Allah—if what you say is true!”**). A later sūrah widens the field to include every human being and every jinn, which removes the excuse of a small tribe in a small country: (17:88 — **“they could not produce its equal, no matter how they supported each other”**). The reason the challenge is confined to a single unit is that the claim being tested is specific, and the test is meant to be answerable rather than impossible.

**THE HELPERS THEY WERE TOLD TO CALL**

The verse allows the doubters to bring support: ***“and call your helpers other than Allah”***, ***“if what you say is true”***. The last clause places the whole matter under the truthfulness of the people making the claim, and the Book uses the same clause at the end of the other two versions of the challenge. Ibn ʿAbbās understood the helpers as the deities the doubters called on for support, and others took them as the witnesses and the men of eloquence whose testimony they were relying on. The reason the verse does not forbid them to consult anyone outside God is that the point is already decided by the invitation, since no ally of theirs can supply what the challenge requires. A reader who has ever been challenged to match a piece of work knows the difference between an open test and a closed one, and this one is left open on purpose so that the failure cannot be blamed on the rules. The people who took up the challenge left no sūrah behind, and the silence has held for fourteen centuries, which is the answer the verse was waiting for when it named the clause.

**A CRAFTSMAN WHO DARES THE TOWN TO COPY HIS WORK**

Think of a potter who sets a bowl in the market and tells every potter in the town to make its match, offering to pay for the clay and to allow any tool they like. The offer would be pointless in a market where a hundred men could do the work, and it is decisive in one where none of them can. The verse makes the same offer with the message and with the whole of a community's talent, and the offer has been open ever since. These days the text is available to more readers and in more languages than at any point in its history, and the invitation stands where it stood, which is why the doubt of a modern reader is met with the same test as the doubt of the first hearer. A person who takes the challenge seriously will read the Book with the question in his hands rather than with a verdict already written, and the sūrah leaves him free to do exactly that.
