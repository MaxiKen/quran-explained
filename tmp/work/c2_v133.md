**A DEATHBED QUESTION PUT TO THE FAMILY**

An appeal to the reader's own knowledge stands at the head of this verse, and it takes the form of a question: ***“Or did you witness”*** and ***“when death came to Jacob?”***. The communities that argued about the ancestors had not been present at any of these scenes, and the Book's question quietly removes them from the position of witnesses. The scene itself then begins with the dying man, and his first concern is not his property but the worship of the household he is leaving. Another passage records the same man as one of the patriarchs to whom the way was given, which places this conversation inside the line the sūrah has been tracing: (2:132 — **“This was the advice of Abraham—as well as Jacob—to his children”**). The question at the start of the verse is therefore aimed at everyone who claims to know this family from the outside.

**THE FATHER'S OWN WORDS**

The dying man's question is recorded in his own words, and it asks about the future rather than the present: ***“He asked his children, “Who will you worship after my passing?”***. The question is about loyalty after the asker is gone, which makes it a test of what the household carried in itself rather than of what it owed to a living father. The patriarchs come back to this point again and again in the Book, since the danger for a family of prophets is that the inheritance becomes a name rather than a worship. Anyone watching a family's practice weaken within a generation of the founder's death will understand why the question was asked in that form. What the verse shows is a man checking the foundation at the last possible moment.

**THE REPLY FROM THE SONS**

The children's reply follows at once, and it names the object of worship with precision: ***“They replied, “We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers”*** and ***“Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac”*** and ***“the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit.”***. The answer moves from a personal God to a family tradition and then to the unity of that God, the sequence this chapter has been building from its opening verses. The reply also ends with submission in the first person plural, exactly the posture the founder had asked for in prayer and performed when he was commanded. The same list of names appears in another passage as the company whose revelation the believers are told to accept, which shows how the family and its descendants belong together: (42:13 — **“what We decreed for Abraham, Moses, and Jesus”**). What the reader hears in the answer is a household that knew what it was handing on.

**A HOUSEHOLD CALLED TO THE BEDSIDE**

Think of a man who calls his sons to his bed and asks them not whether they will keep the business but whether they will keep what the business was for. The scene in the verse is that kind of exchange, and the Book preserves it because the answer given there is the answer the sūrah has been asking its own readers to give. The brevity of the reply suggests a household in which the question had been settled long before it was asked, which is the opposite of the hesitations the sūrah has reported elsewhere. Anyone who has been present at such a conversation will know how much it reveals about a family, and what the patriarchs left behind is disclosed in this exchange. What the verse offers is a picture of a tradition being confirmed at the point where it could most easily have been lost.

**UNITY AND SUBMISSION IN THE LAST WORDS**

The last words of the reply are the most important in the verse, since they name the unity of God and the family's own submission in a single sentence. The phrase about the One God answers every later attempt to divide the family's religion among communities, and the clause about submission describes the response that the whole account has been illustrating. Several accounts of the prophets in the Book close with the same two elements, and here they come from children standing beside a dying father. What the reader should carry from the scene is that the answer given at that bedside is the answer the sūrah has been inviting him to give, and the invitation has been in front of him since the chapter's opening. The next verse tells him what that inheritance means for his own account.
