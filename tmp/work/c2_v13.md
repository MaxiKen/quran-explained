**THE INVITATION AND THE TOWNSPEOPLE**
The scene repeats the shape of the earlier one with a different subject on the table. The call goes out in the street, ***“And when they are told, “Believe”***, and the sentence is cut off by the answer before it can finish. The people being pointed at are the first Muslims, the men and women who had already paid for their faith in the currency the city understood. A later verse of the same sūrah makes the point by asking who would turn away from the faith of Abraham except a fool (2:130). The invitation is generous because it offers a tested road rather than an untested theory, and it is humbling because it asks a newcomer to walk behind people whose lives had already been altered by the road. A young man learning a trade is told to work beside the men who have done the job for twenty years. The instruction is not an insult to his intelligence; it is a shortcut past a hundred avoidable mistakes.
**A WORD THROWN AT THE BELIEVERS**
The answer is not a question, though it wears the shape of one: ***“as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe”***, ***“as the fools believe?””***. Its whole edge is a single word, and the word is thrown at the believers rather than at the idea. Calling a man a fool in a public argument is cheaper than answering him, which is why the sneer is doing the work of an argument the speaker does not have. The men being mocked were the ones who had lost homes, trade and standing for the message. The insult therefore rebounds on the man who uttered it, since he was measuring intelligence by the price a person was willing to pay, and by that measure his own caution looked clever to him. A spectator who mocks the workers carrying a stretcher off a field is not demonstrating insight; he is demonstrating that he has never carried one. The crowd sees the difference even when he does not.
**FOLLY, WEIGHED PROPERLY**
The verse does not return the insult in the same coin; it redefines the word. ***“Indeed, it is they”*** ***“who are fools, but they do not know”***. Folly in the Book is not a low score on a test; it is a man's ignorance of his own interest, which means folly is measured by outcomes rather than by vocabulary. The sharpest proof of it is a bargain that trades what lasts for what does not, and the sharper the bargain, the deeper the folly. A man who sells the deed to his house for the price of a good dinner has been clever at the table and ruined by the morning, and the Book applies that image to the soul without hedging. The verdict is therefore not an insult returned; it is a diagnosis, and a diagnosis is meant to be used. That is why the sentence ends by naming what the men lack rather than by naming what they deserve.
**A SHORT STEP DEEPER INTO THE DARK**
The closing words here are a step darker than the closing words of the verse before, and the difference between them is worth weighing. That verse ended with men who fail to perceive, and this one ends with men who do not know, which is the heavier verdict, since perception can return to a man who has lost the habit while knowledge of one's own ruin is slower to restore. The pattern belongs to the whole portrait: every verse closes with the men being told what they cannot see, and the reader is left with less room to stand on the side of the observer. A reader who smiles at the men in the portrait has already placed himself among them. The passage is built to make that smile uncomfortable.
**WHO DECIDES WHAT A WISE MAN IS**
The question this verse settles is still open in every marketplace, and it is answered the same way wherever a crowd is asked. Wisdom is measured by the returns it produces, so the man with the largest balance is counted the cleverest, and a person who fasts on a Thursday is counted quaint. The Book turns the ruler around and measures a life by what it costs its owner and what it keeps for him afterwards. That is why the believing community has always looked foolish to a world that counts only what it can see. Any reader can settle the question today with a week of honest accounting, because the way a man spends his evenings and his money answers it long before his opinions do. The verse does not ask him to despise the marketplace; it asks him to stop letting the marketplace decide what a wise man is.
