**THE EXCHANGE THEY MADE AND THE PRICE ATTACHED**

The passage ends with a description of what the community had actually done, put in commercial terms: ***“These are the ones”***, ***“who trade the Hereafter”***, ***“for the life of this world.”***, ***“So their punishment will not be reduced”***, ***“nor will they be helped”***. The Book names the transaction because the earlier verses had shown people selecting the parts of a covenant that suited them, and a selection is a form of purchase. What they gave up is described as the life to come, and what they took is the present arrangement with its advantages, which the reader has just seen in operation as ransom money and political position. The two closing clauses then remove both of the consolations a punished community usually hopes for: the sentence will not be lightened, and no ally will arrive to help. The Book states this without softening it, because the offence was not a mistake about facts but a deliberate exchange of one life for another. The same bargain is described with the worldly goods named first in another sūrah: (11:15 — **“Whoever desires ˹only˺ this worldly life and its luxuries,”**).

**THE TERMS OF THE TRADE THEY MADE**

A reader might object that the people in the previous verses kept many observances and were not, in their own eyes, rejecting the covenant entirely. The Book's accounting is different, since it treats the deliberate setting aside of a known term as the sale of the whole arrangement; a document that a person edits whenever it inconveniences him has already been exchanged for whatever he preferred. The same trade is described elsewhere with the goods named more plainly, where the covenant and the oaths are said to be sold for a small price: (3:77 — **“Indeed, those who trade Allah's covenant”**). The Book's language leaves no room for the idea that the transaction was inadvertent, and it is this deliberateness, not the size of the profit, that fixes the price named in the verse's closing words.

**THE PUNISHMENT THAT CANNOT BE SHORTENED**

The clause about the punishment not being reduced responds directly to the claim the community had been making about itself in the earlier verses of this passage. They had asserted that the Fire would touch them for a limited number of days, and the Book answers by stating that the sentence will not be lightened at all, which shows that the earlier claim was not a private comfort but a doctrine about the terms of judgement. The second clause takes away help, and the Book uses the same pairing in other passages about people whose conduct had closed their own doors. What a reader should notice is that both clauses are about the future, and the verse's verdict is given in the present tense as a description of what the community had already become. The Book's precision here is deliberate: it does not describe a people who will be punished, but a people who have already made the exchange that determines it.

**A CONSTABLE WITH A BILL OF SALE**

Think of a man who sells the deeds to his own house to pay for a season of gambling, and who keeps living in it for a while and cannot understand why the buyer's agents keep walking through the rooms. The community in this passage had traded its share of the next life for advantages in this one and had not moved out, which is why the Book's picture includes both worlds in a single sentence. The disorder of that arrangement is what the reader is being shown: a person may enjoy the goods he acquired while believing that nothing has changed. The verse names the consequence so plainly that the illusion cannot survive a careful reading, and the question of what the reader himself has been buying is left for him to answer.

**NO REDUCTION AND NO RESCUE**

The verse ends by naming what will not happen, and the Book's method of describing absences is worth noticing here. It says the punishment will not be reduced and no help will come, because those are the two hopes that keep a person comfortable while he continues in the conduct that earned the sentence. A reduced penalty is a negotiation, and an arriving helper is an escape, and both are removed in the same sentence so that the community's confidence is dismantled at its foundations. A reader who has been relying on either of those hopes, in any form, has the verse addressed to him rather than to the people of a distant century. The passage closes here, and the next verse returns to the favours and failures of this community with a fresh account of Moses and the messengers sent after him.
