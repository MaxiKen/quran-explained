**THE VOICE THAT CHANGES AT THE DOOR**

The verse catches a man in the act of being two people, and it reports both of him without comment. The first visit is recorded first: ***“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.””***. There is nothing wrong with the words themselves, since a believer would say the same thing, and the sentence leaves the reader to notice that the words cost this speaker nothing. The second visit is the one that explains the first: ***“But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you”***, ***“we were only mocking.””***. The confession in the second room is the verse's masterstroke, because it tells the reader that the man knows exactly what he is doing. He is not confused about his loyalties or uncertain about the message; he is performing, and a performer knows the difference between the stage and the dressing room. That is why the passage wastes no time on argument with him, and why the reader is asked to watch rather than to debate.

**ONE MAN, TWO CONGREGATIONS**

The two rooms belong to two companies, and each company gets the version of the man that suits it. With the believers he is a face in the congregation; with his own people he is a comedian who mocks the whole business of faith. Picture a man who laughs at a joke in one room and tells the truth about his opinion in the next. The picture is uncomfortable because it is ordinary, and the verse takes the ordinary case seriously rather than treating it as a small failing. Another sūrah reports the same double entry with a different detail, since the company at the door in the morning is not the company at the table at night (63:1), and the Book treats the habit as a single condition rather than as a series of isolated slips. A man who can say two different things in two rooms has already decided which room he believes in.

**THE ADMISSION INSIDE THE JOKE**

The last words of the verse are the most damning, since they do not deny the deceit; they name it and then shrug. ***“we were only mocking.””*** is the whole account of the man's religion, and the mockery in it is aimed at the believers rather than at the creed. That is a different sin from doubt, and a heavier one, because it lives on company and depends on applause. A man who cannot believe quietly but can laugh loudly is telling his neighbours exactly how much of his own claim he holds. The Book elsewhere warns the community about this kind of intimacy, since the man who shares the table may be sharing nothing else (3:118), and the warning is the practical face of the verse. Trust is not a theory here; it is a question of who is told what, and a reader can answer it about himself in a minute.

**A READER'S OWN TWO VOICES**

The test this verse sets is available today to anyone willing to be embarrassed by it, and it needs no audience but the truth. A person can write down two sentences: what he says about his religion in front of believers, and what he says about it where nobody is watching his mouth. If the two sentences differ, the difference is his condition, and the verse has already told him what to call it. The remedy does not begin with shame; it begins with honesty about which room he actually lives in, because the man in the verse was cured of nothing by being flattered. A community that keeps its doors open to such a man does so in hope, since the same Book that exposes the two voices also keeps calling him back to one of them, and it is the voice that costs him something to say.
