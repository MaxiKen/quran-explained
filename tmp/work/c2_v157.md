**A DOUBLE GRANT MENTIONED IN ONE BREATH**

The first gift given to the patient is stated in the plural: ***“They are the ones who will receive Allah’s blessings”***, followed at once by the second: ***“and mercy. And it is they”***. The blessings of God upon a person include the forgiving of his faults and the mention of his name in a place better than any gathering on earth, so the term is wider than the ordinary sense of a kind word. Another passage shows the same gift being poured out upon the believers, where the blessings of God and the prayer of the angels are joined together: (33:43 — **“He is the One Who showers His blessings upon you—and His angels pray for”**). The order of the two gifts is not accidental, since the blessing comes first and the mercy follows it, as though the acceptance of the servant preceded the pardon of his shortcomings. What the verse gives the patient, then, is not a payment for services rendered but a state of favour in which their losses are wrapped.

**GUIDANCE ADDED AS THE THIRD GIFT**

The list does not close with mercy, because a third gift is named in the same sentence: ***“who are ˹rightly˺ guided”***. Guidance is placed beside blessing and mercy because the three answer the three things a human being needs when he has been struck: he needs to be accepted, he needs to be forgiven for the impatience he felt, and he needs to know where he is going next. A short passage near the beginning of the sūrah uses the same words to describe those who follow the Book and act upon it, so the description here is the highest one the sūrah knows: (2:5 — **“It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord, and it is they who will be successful”**). The guidance is stated in the present tense, which means it is granted with the patience and not only after it. The sentence therefore puts the grieving person among the guided while he is still grieving.

**A REWARD THAT MATCHES THE RESPONSE**

Each part of the reward answers a part of the patience that has been described: the blessing answers the confession of belonging, the mercy answers the pain that the confession did not erase, and the guidance answers the direction that the return implies. This threefold matching is why the passage can move on after this verse without further explanation, since nothing in the account of the testing has been left unprovided for. It is the Book's way to name the reward in terms of the need it meets rather than in quantities, and that way is at work here. The community is therefore taught that endurance is not a loss quietly accepted but a transaction whose profit is known and fixed. What the patient receive is not compensation measured against the thing they lost but a state of favour in which the loss is transformed.

**A CARAVAN THAT LOSES A PACK ANIMAL**

Think of a caravan crossing a desert that loses one of its pack animals on the second day, and of what actually keeps the travellers moving: not the recovery of the load, which does not happen, but their knowledge of the destination and their trust in the guide who set the route. The travellers who stop to mourn the animal without limit are those who forget the destination, and the ones who continue are not the ones who feel less but the ones who remember more. The verse gives the patient the same three things the caravan needs: a master who owns what was lost, a forgiveness for the grief, and a road that still leads somewhere. Anyone who has had to travel through a hard season will recognise which of the three carried him. What the verse does is name them all before the journey is over.

**THE SENTENCE THAT CLOSES THE PASSAGE ON TESTING**

Read with what comes before it, this verse ends the section that began with the command to seek help through patience and prayer. The losses were named, the words of the patient were given, and the reward is now stated, so the account is complete in the order in which a person meets it. The passage does not close with a description of the trials but with a description of those who pass through them, which is the Book's consistent habit when it treats the suffering of the community. The community is left with the assurance that the three gifts are attached to the sayings of its own mouth at the hour of loss. What remains after this sentence is not a counsel of endurance in the abstract but a settled promise with a clear address.
