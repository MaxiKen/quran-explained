**WHAT KEEPS THE WISH OFF THE LIPS**

The forecast of the refusal comes with its own map of the mind: ***“But they will never wish”***. The no carries the full weight of the first word — never at all, on any terms — and the gloss handed down from Ibn ʿAbbās gives the refusers their cause in one line of court logic. They know the prophet they speak to, and they deny him all the same. The men know the prophet they are with. That knowing is in the file from the passage before. The wish for death would be a wish for a meeting their own record has made dreadful. The plain terms of the dare stand in the verse before this one: (2:94 — **“then wish for death if what you say is true!””**). The commentary puts the counter-fact in bare numbers: had the wish been made that day, not one of that people would have lived — and that is just why the tongue stayed still. The shape of the refusal is thus a confession in the grammar of self-guard. A man will not knock at a door he believes opens onto his own trial, and the silence of the lips testifies to the reading of the file. What follows from the never is that the dare of the last verse stands on the table still, undated and unanswered. The wish is not hard to say; it is hard to say honestly.

**THE HANDS THAT FILL THE POCKETS OF FEAR**

The second clause names the brake with the verse's own body idiom: ***“for that because of what their hands have done.”***. The hands send forth what the ledger reads back, and the glosses hear the cause of the cowardice just there. The deeds stand between the man and the door, so the same mouth that claims the world to come refuses to travel toward it. The same men are staged again on the day of the crowd's regret: (2:167 — **“The ˹misled˺ followers will cry, “If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them as they disowned us.””**). The parallel decree of the revelation on the love of this fleeting life draws the same portrait: (17:18 — **“Whoever desires this fleeting world ˹alone˺, We hasten in it whatever We please to whoever We will; then We destine them”**). The mind the two passages share is worth stating without softening. The fear of death in the claimant of paradise is the conscience's own arithmetic. The claim speaks of inheritance; the hands remember the forgery. The reason the verse names the hands and not the heart is that the docket is manual. The deeds were done with these, and the memory of them keeps the wish unmade.

**AWARENESS DIRECTED AT THE WRONGDOERS**

The close of the line attaches knowledge to the class with the care of a charge sheet: ***“And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers”***. The name of the knowing here is the name of the knowledge that holds its objects in full. The elative form in the Arabic does the work of the superlative: a knowledge whole in its grasp of who the wrongdoers are and what the wrongdoers did. The placement of the clause after the refusal is deliberate, since the refusal claimed the cover of anonymity. We are the chosen; we are the beloved; the record is buried in the fathers. The verse answers with a knowledge that names the class by its deeds. The tradition's rendering of the last verb keeps both sides in the frame — whether the line is read as His awareness of what they do or of what the wrongdoers are — because the knowledge does not only watch the act; it files the actor. The effect on the reader standing at the end of this long cross-examination is the effect of a courtroom that has stopped speaking and started writing.

**THE OFFER THAT NEVER EXPIRES**

The two-verse experiment remains the section's most portable instrument, and the tradition treats it as such. The claim of the exclusive home, the counted days, the guaranteed cohort — each family of claims meets the same test, and the test never requires a scholar to administer it. For a congregation today that has quietly replaced the wish for the meeting with a portfolio of postponements, the passage holds the same mirror up and asks the same question in the same register. The difference between the two postures — the faith that would pronounce the wish and the faith that will not — is the difference the whole passage has been building toward, and it is measurable in a single syllable. In other words, the verdict of the experiment does not wait for the last day. It is pronounced in the silence of the wish, on the day the claim was made.
