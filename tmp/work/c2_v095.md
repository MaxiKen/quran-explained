**THE WISH THAT WAS NEVER MADE**

The Book supplies the answer before anyone speaks, and the demand that produced it stands in the verse just before this one: (2:94 — **“then wish for death”**). What the challenge produces is a refusal: What the challenge produces is a refusal: What the challenge produces is a refusal: ***“But they will never wish”***, ***“for that because of what their hands have done.”***, ***“And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers”***. The test asked for a wish, and the verse reports that the wish will not be made at any time, which makes the absence of the answer into the answer. The reason given is not a doubt about the next life but the record of what the people have already sent ahead of them, and the Book names their own hands as the source of the reluctance. The last clause then states that this record is fully known, so the reader understands that the reluctance is not a hidden motive discovered by an accuser but a fact about the people themselves. Ibn ʿAbbās tied their refusal to the deeds their own hands had sent forward, making the absence of the wish evidence drawn from conscience rather than from argument.

**WHY AN UNCLEAR ACCOUNT MAKES A MAN HESITATE**

The phrase about hands is the Book's regular way of describing accumulated conduct, and here it explains an emotion rather than assigning a penalty. A person who suspects that his account is not in order will not ask for it to be brought forward, and that is the condition the verse describes. The same reasoning appears in the sūrah's earlier warning about the Day when no soul can help another, where the emphasis falls on what each person has carried to that meeting. The confidence these people held about the next life was evidently never a confidence about their own record, and the two could be held together only while nobody asked the question plainly. The test in the previous verse was chosen because it forces those two things into the same sentence.

**A KNOWLEDGE THAT LEAVES NOTHING OUT**

The closing clause is short and it does two things at once: it states that the wrongdoing is known, and it quietly declines to describe the people as anything else. The Book uses this formula wherever a conduct has been established beyond dispute, and it uses it at the end of verses that a reader might otherwise treat as a debate. Here the clause also protects the reader from the impression that the challenge was a piece of rhetoric which the Book won by default, since the reason for the silence is given in the same breath. A reader who has ever kept quiet when a question would have required an honest answer has the mechanism in front of him, and the mechanism is being described as something God already sees.

**A MAN WHO WILL NOT READ HIS OWN STATEMENT**

Think of a partner in a firm whose books have been called for and who finds reasons why next year would be a better time. The verse's phrase about hands is the call for the books, the refusal to wish is the postponement, and neither step requires the firm's accounts to be set out in the passage. The point of the passage is that a person's reluctance is itself information, and the information is available to anyone watching. A reader can test the same thing in himself whenever he avoids a question that would require him to state his position clearly, since the avoidance usually has a reason attached to it. The verse names the reason and then leaves the reader with it, and the next verse shows how far the reluctance can go.

**WHAT AN UNANSWERED CHALLENGE SHOWS**

The verse is one of the Book's clearest demonstrations of a claim being tested by conduct rather than by argument, and the demonstration works because the silence is total. The community had an opportunity to settle the matter in its own favour with a single sentence, and the Book records that the sentence would never be spoken. Anyone who has seen a confident assertion collapse the moment it required a commitment has watched the same structure at a smaller scale. No further verdict on the claim is required, since the conduct of the claimants has done that work already. What remains for the reader is to notice the places in his own life where a similar test would produce a similar silence, and the next verses continue the description of what this community preferred to the wish they were invited to make.

**THE PREFERENCE REVEALED BY RELUCTANCE**

The verse leaves the reader with a portrait of a people whose attachment to the present was stronger than their confidence about the future, and the Book does not soften the diagnosis. That attachment is not unusual, and the account of those who traded the next life for this one has already covered the same ground. What makes this passage distinct is the way the preference is exposed: not by an accusation but by an invitation that the people declined to accept. The reader who notices how much of his own behaviour is shaped by not asking certain questions has understood the verse, and the Book's placement of it here, immediately after the covenant and before the account of magic that follows, shows that the whole section is about the difference between a claim and a life. In the present day, avoidance of the final account often grows in proportion to the unfinished wrongs a person refuses to face.
