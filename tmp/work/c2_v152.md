**THE REMEMBRANCE THAT RETURNS**

A covenant in miniature stands at the centre of the passage. ***“remember Me; I will remember you.”***. Two verbs. Two sides. One circuit. Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss reads the remembering as the performance of acts of obedience. The return of the remembering is the granting of the garden. The same transmission gives the harder form of the bargain. Remember Allah in the season of ease. He remembers you in the season of hardship. The circuit is therefore not decorative. It is a supply line, and the supply is drawn against a promise written in the first person. What follows is that the smallest phrase spoken on a tongue has an address at the other end. The addressing is the mercy of the design. The point of the paired pronouns is intimacy with procedure. The remembering goes up. The remembering comes down. The effect is that the servant's dhikr is a meeting rather than a technique. The comfort-clause states where the returned remembering is first felt (13:28 — **“whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort”**). The comfort is the receipt of the circuit.

**THE THANK THAT CLOSES THE CIRCUIT**

The gratitude clause completes the loop. ***“And thank Me, and never be ungrateful”***. The thank is the remembering in its social form. The rejection named at the end is the verse's single prohibition. The gloss of Ibn ʿAbbās takes the thank as thanks for the blessings bestowed and the rejection as the stopping of that thanks. al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record the return-promise in the report of the Prophet: *"whoever remembers Me in himself I will remember him in Myself and whoever remembers Me in an assembly I will remember him in an Assembly more excellent than his"*. The private and the public remembering both draw a matching return. The circuit has two switches. The heart can open it. The tongue can open it. The bargain of gratitude is proclaimed with its stakes in full elsewhere (14:7 — **“And ˹remember˺ when your Lord proclaimed, ‘If you are grateful, I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful,”**). The increase follows the thanks. The circuit is economic in the strictest sense. The supply grows with the gratitude.

**THE INGRATITUDE THAT IS A THEFT**

The closing prohibition deserves its own warning-post. Never be ungrateful. The early commentators keep the warning lashed to the blessings just delivered. The qiblah. The messenger. The direction and the teacher. To receive the favour and withhold the thanks is the theft the verse names. For the modern reader the small verse is a complete manual of the inner life. Remember. Thank. Do not cut the circuit. This age is fluent in mindfulness and mute about the one being remembered. The second clause fixes the address of the practice. A memory technique returns calm. A meeting returns a gaze. The difference between the two is the difference between an exercise and a prayer. The returned remembering is the proof that the meeting is real. The covenant stands open at both ends. Remember Me. I will remember you. The circuit is older than the reader, and the line is still live.

**TWO SWITCHES, ONE LINE**

The engineering of the covenant is simple enough to carry in a pocket and deep enough to spend a life on. The remembering is the current. The thanks is the load. The prohibition at the end is the fuse. The effect of the three together is a complete circuit diagram of the inner life. The point of the assembly-clause in the transmitted report is that the circuit is not embarrassed by company. The private meeting draws its return. The public meeting draws its matching return. The reason the gratitude is paired with the remembering is that the two are the same act in two registers. The tongue remembers. The conduct thanks. For the modern reader the diagram replaces a whole shelf of manuals. This age runs on forgetting, and its industries are tuned to interrupt the current. The verse installs the practice that survives the interruptions. The line is old. The switches are in the reader's hands. The promise at the other end is written in the first person.
