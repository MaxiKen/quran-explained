**AN EXCHANGE BETWEEN LORD AND SERVANT**

The verse names a relationship in which the two halves answer each other: ***“remember Me; I will remember you.”*** The command is not merely to speak a formula but to keep the Lord in mind through obedience, and the promise that follows is the assurance that the servant is not forgotten in return. What God's remembrance of a servant means is left open to the widest reading, since it includes mercy, protection, and the reward stored up for him, and the sentence places the whole exchange on the ground of generosity rather than of debt. A nearby passage makes the same promise in the form of a response to those who call: (2:186 — **“When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me: I am truly near. I respond to one’s prayer”**). The distance between the two parties in the verse is therefore closed from the side of the one who is nearer than the suppliant expects.

**REMEMBERING IN EASE AND IN HARDSHIP**

Two weights belong to the command that the English cannot carry at once: the remembrance is an act of obedience, and the answering is a bestowal of mercy, so that what passes between the two is not a transaction of equal parts. Ibn ʿAbbās gave the phrase the sense of remembering God in ease and plenty, with the promise that He remembers His servant in hardship and distress. That reading makes the verse a preparation for the difficulties that a community inevitably meets, since the time to lay up the remembrance is before the need for it arrives. The sentence therefore trains the believers in a habit rather than in a single act, and the habit is precisely what the passage has been forming in them since the direction was changed. What is remembered in prosperity becomes the ground of steadiness in adversity, and the verse states both halves of that arrangement.

**THANKING AS THE OTHER HALF OF THE MATTER**

The second half of the verse turns the same relationship outward: ***“And thank Me, and never be ungrateful”***. Gratitude is placed beside remembrance because the two together name the whole response of a servant to a generous master, the one in the mind and the other in the conduct. The Book ties thankfulness to increase in a passage that leaves no doubt about the stakes: (14:7 — **“I will certainly give you more. But if you are ungrateful,”**). The warning against ingratitude is not a threat added to a gift but a description of what closes the hand that has been generous. The verse ends, therefore, on the response that keeps the gift flowing, and the community is left with a practice that lies fully within its power.

**NAMING THE GIVER AT THE DOOR**

Think of a man who receives a gift every morning at his door and who, before he eats, says the giver's name aloud so that the household knows where the bread came from. The habit costs him nothing, yet it changes how he carries the day, because a man who names his giver is not free to pretend that he fed himself. The verse calls the community to exactly that habit, and it adds the promise that the name is known on the other side as well. A house that stops naming the giver soon begins to treat the gift as an inheritance, and the warning against ingratitude is aimed at that drift. The sentence is short, but it holds a household's whole attitude in its two commands.

**THE PRACTICE THAT CARRIES THE BLESSING**

Read with what comes before, the verse closes the account of the favours by naming the response they were meant to produce. The messenger has been sent, the Book and the wisdom have been taught, and the direction has been fixed; what remains is that the community remember and give thanks. The Book returns to that response at the end of other passages of blessing, and it never treats the gifts as ends in themselves. The sentence is therefore a hinge on which the whole favour rests, since a blessing that is not answered does not remain a blessing in the life of the receiver. The passage leaves the community with a practice it can keep and a promise that it is kept in turn.
