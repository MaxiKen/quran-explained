**THE WORST TRADE PRICED**

***“They are the ones”*** points back to the merchants of the previous verse and confirms their accounts. The verse states the first exchange with devastating plainness — ***“who trade guidance for misguidance”*** — and follows it with the second, ***“and forgiveness for punishment. How persistent are they in pursuit”***, ending the question on the destination: ***“of the Fire”***. Every line of the ledger reverses the sensible direction of trade, so the traders lose on both sides of every transaction. The verse pauses on the word "persistent" because persistence is normally praised; here it is the measure of a ruin kept up by daily effort. Because the traders work hard at the exchange, the amazement is genuine: effort of that size could have purchased guidance instead. The ledger is short enough to memorise and severe enough to measure a life against. The amazement in the final question is itself part of the argument: the persistence required to keep this trade going is beyond ordinary recklessness.

**AMAZEMENT AS INSTRUCTION**

The question is phrased to provoke wonder in the believers at how patiently these people commit sins that necessitate their punishment, and in reality they have no patience for the Fire at all. The amazement is calibrated — it marvels at the effort of sin, not at the endurance of consequence. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the first exchange in economic terms: they purchased disbelief at the price of faith, and torment at the price of pardon. Misguidance is not a neutral good; it is the subtraction of the only asset that survives, and the punishment received in exchange is the exact invoice of that subtraction. The two lines of the ledger mirror each other — guidance and pardon are the possessions of the hereafter, and the traders have swapped both away in one sitting. Nobody makes this trade once accidentally; the persistence the verse names is a habit of business. (4:44 — **“Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures yet trade it for misguidance”**)

**PATIENCE THAT IS NOT PATIENCE**

A hearer may ask how anyone could be patient enough for such a trade to be described as persistent. The verse itself answers that the patience is imaginary — a persistence of habit rather than of endurance, which the traders will discover on the day the invoice is opened. Sin is easy to repeat when each repetition feels costless, and the trading floor of the present hides the settlement behind its noise. One person patiently builds a house while another patiently digs a pit; the effort is real in both cases, and only the destination differs. Because the verse addresses the believers' amazement, the intended audience is expected to look at its own trades and reverse any line written in the same direction. The larger consequence is that persistence cuts both ways — the same habit that buys misguidance can be turned to buy the way back. The amazement is pedagogical: the reader stands where the verse stands and looks at the ledger with its eyes.

**RECKONING THE LEDGER**

The end of the ledger is spoken by the Judge Himself in (78:30 — **“˹So the deniers will be told,˺ “Taste ˹the punishment˺, for all you will get from Us is more torment.””**). The finding Ibn ʿAbbās reports is that the pardon prepared for them would have been theirs had they not concealed the matter, which prices the second line of the exchange in advance. Wonder is the first step of caution, so the verse teaches fear by teaching astonishment first. The application is to review the ledger personally: hold no position that trades away guidance for advantage or forgiveness for the pleasure of the offence, because the exchange rates never favour the trader. The review is the verse's demand and its gift at once, since a ledger read in time can still be rewritten. Because the amazement of the verse is aimed at the believers, the reader is expected to come away careful rather than entertained. The Fire is pursued with a persistence that deserves astonishment. The astonishment is meant to end in caution. The reader who leaves the verse amazed has taken the first step the verse designs for him, and the second step is the review of his own trades.
