**A RECOGNITION AS CLEAR AS FAMILY**

What the people who had received scriptures knew about the Messenger is stated here, with a comparison drawn from ordinary life: ***“Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺”*** and ***“as they recognize their own children.”***. The comparison is drawn from the most familiar recognition a person has, which makes the knowledge asserted here the opposite of a vague impression. This stretch does not charge the others with missing information; it charges a group among them with choosing concealment after recognition. Another passage makes the same claim in a sentence about the scripture they had been given, which is a pattern the Book uses in more than one place: (6:114 — **“Those who were given the Scripture know that it has been revealed”**). What the sentence establishes is the level of certainty the communities possessed. Ibn ʿAbbās applied the recognition to people who knew the Prophet's description as distinctly as a child among other children, yet noted that a party knowingly concealed it.

**THE OBJECT OF THAT RECOGNITION**

The recognition had a content, and part of it has already been described in the account of the covenant that bound them to publish the scripture. The descriptions of the coming prophet in the earlier books are what the sentence refers to, and the Book treats the match between those descriptions and the Messenger as a matter of settled knowledge rather than of interpretation. The comparison with a father's recognition of his own children implies the kind of certainty that does not need to be argued into a person, which is why the following clause is about conduct rather than about doubt. The terms are exact for the act they name: a claim acknowledged and then buried. What the verse leaves no room for is the plea that the communities did not know.

**THE GROUP THAT HIDES WHAT IT KNOWS**

The conduct is then named in a single clause: ***“Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly”***. The word for the concealment covers an act of putting something out of sight, and the clause adds the adverb that removes every excuse, since the hiding is done with knowledge. The Book has already described the same offence in its account of the covenant sworn to make the scripture known, and this sentence shows the offence continuing into the community's dealings with the Prophet himself. The qualification about the group is important, since the sūrah has also described people among the same communities who were upright and followed what they had. Anyone who has seen a document quietly withdrawn from circulation will recognise the act the sentence names.

**RECOGNITION FOLLOWED BY SILENCE**

Think of a family that meets a young man in the street, knows him to be the son who was given away years before, and walks past him in silence. The verse describes a recognition of exactly that kind and an act of concealment that follows it, and the two clauses stand together so that neither can be read alone. The comparison explains the severity of the charge against these communities, since the failure named is not ignorance but the burial of knowledge. Anyone who has watched a man withhold the identity of someone he recognised can see how the situation is constructed. What the verse offers the reader is the difference between knowing and acknowledging. The difference between knowing and acknowledging is the lesson on offer.

**A TESTIMONY PUT AWAY**

The truth is the object of the concealment, which means what was hidden was not an opinion but the thing itself. The same word is used for the revelation throughout the sūrah, and the sentence places it in opposition to a group's conduct rather than to their understanding. Another passage describes the same concealment as the reason for a curse pronounced by the tongues of the prophets, which shows how seriously the Book treats the handling of knowledge: (3:187 — **“when Allah took the covenant of those”**). A sentence is left that grants the communities everything except honesty. The verses that follow turn to the question of what the community itself is to do with the truth it has received. Present-day recognition can coexist with concealment when professional or communal cost makes an acknowledged truth inconvenient to state.
