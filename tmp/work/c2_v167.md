**REGRET WITHOUT A SECOND TRIAL**

At regret without a second trial, attention falls on ***“The ˹misled˺ followers will cry, “If only we could have a second chance, we would disown them”***, ***“as they disowned us.””***. The followers respond to the leaders’ disavowal with a wish for another opportunity: If only we had another chance. They now recognize that the leaders could not help them and that their obedience was misplaced. They promise to reject those leaders just as the leaders have rejected them. But the wish comes after the test has ended. The Qur’an repeatedly presents such regret to teach the living. Regret is useful when it leads to repentance and change, but regret after death cannot create new deeds. A wise person listens to the warning before the consequences are visible. He examines the people he follows, the assumptions he inherits, and the desires that govern his decisions while correction remains possible. The followers’ words imply an admission of responsibility. They do not simply say, *Our leaders ruined us*; they say that if returned, they would disown them. This shows that they know they participated in the alliance. They chose to follow, defended the leaders, and perhaps benefited from the false system. A person cannot surrender his moral judgement to another and then expect to be free of accountability. This does not deny that leaders can manipulate, threaten, or deceive. Allah judges coercion, knowledge, capacity, and influence perfectly. But ordinary claims of *I was only following orders* do not excuse a person who could recognize and refuse wrongdoing. The Qur’an asks people to use hearing, sight, and reason rather than surrendering their conscience. (2:166 — **“˹Consider the Day˺ when those”**) Because regret without a second trial governs 2:167, deeds become sources of regret arises from regret without a second trial. It follows that returning to the present completes regret without a second trial through deeds become sources of regret.

**DEEDS BECOME SOURCES OF REGRET**

Deeds become sources of regret draws together ***“And so Allah will make them remorseful of their misdeeds.”***. Allah will show them their deeds as regrets. The phrase suggests that the very actions in which they once found pride, pleasure, or belonging will become objects of pain. Wealth gained through injustice, loyalty offered to falsehood, time wasted in mockery, and opportunities for good deliberately abandoned will all appear in their true form. The Qur’an says that a person will see what he sent ahead. Deeds are not erased by death or hidden behind public reputation. When the curtain lifts, the person sees how every choice shaped his final state. They will never leave the Fire describes the final punishment of those who die upon disbelief and persist in the allegiance to falsehood described in the passage. The permanence is not a denial of Allah’s mercy; it is the consequence of meeting Allah in a state that rejects Him. Muslims affirm the warning while entrusting the detailed judgement of individuals to Allah’s perfect knowledge and justice. The verse should produce seriousness about the Hereafter, not a culture of cruelty. The Prophet ﷺ grieved over people’s rejection and sought ways to guide them. Believers warn because they want people saved, and they invite through wisdom, mercy, and clear evidence rather than celebrating another person’s destruction. (67:10 — **“And they will lament, “If only we had listened”**) The reason returning to the present follows deeds become sources of regret lies in regret without a second trial. Therefore, returning to the present returns practical force to deeds become sources of regret.

**RETURNING TO THE PRESENT**

***“And they will never ˹be able to˺ leave the Fire”*** mark the turn toward returning to the present. The phrase if only we had another chance should be transformed into action now. A person who recognizes a harmful influence can leave it. Someone who followed a false opinion can seek sound knowledge. A community that has defended injustice can acknowledge it, repair the harm, and change its structures. Repentance is not merely feeling ashamed; it is breaking the alliance with falsehood before it becomes permanent. The believer should also remember that influence is reciprocal. A follower can strengthen a leader by praise, money, votes, silence, or repeated sharing of harmful speech. Refusing to participate in falsehood is a form of worship. Supporting a good leader is likewise a good deed when the support remains within truth and accountability. Imagine watching a recording of a bridge collapse and seeing the warning signs that were ignored before the fall. The viewer aches to enter the scene, stop the traffic, and redirect every car, but the recording cannot be reopened and the consequences cannot be revised. The regret described here has that closed quality. A person living this week still possesses what the people in the scene seek—time in which attachment can be tested, harmful loyalty broken, and a different road taken before deeds become only memories. (33:66 — **“If only we had obeyed Allah”**) In other words, returning to the present answers regret without a second trial.
