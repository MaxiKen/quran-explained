**WEALTH UNDER A NAME**

The verse's phrases name the property, the trick, and the knowledge in order: ***“Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly”***, ***“nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property, knowing”***, ***“that it is a sin”***. The walk moves from the ownership to the courtroom to the conscience, and each phrase deepens the charge by naming one more layer of the act. The anatomy of the crime is the indictment's evidence, and every phrase adds a layer to the charge. The event named on Ibn ʿAbbās's authority stands behind the whole sequence. The property named first is somebody's actual livelihood, the trick named second steals it through the courthouse, and the knowledge named third convicts the hand that signed the paperwork.

Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly is a negative built on an ownership the passage admits before the prohibition starts: the wealth belongs to one another, which means the household is full of property with names attached. The consumption prohibited is the quiet kind — the margin taken without a contract, the share reduced without a witness. The second prohibition sharpens the first by adding the courtroom: nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property. The bribe is described as deliberate and the target as a portion, so the corruption is calculated rather than impulsive. The verse refuses the whole pipeline from theft to verdict, because the verdict is where theft acquires a document. Because the command is addressed to believers, the corruption being policed is the corruption inside the community's own transactions.

**THE BRIBE DISGUISED AS LAW**

The bribe's particular evil is its costume: the money moves in the dark and returns as a ruling, so the victim is robbed by an authority he is required to trust. The verse sees through the costume by describing the purpose — to devour a portion of others' property — which is theft's purpose wearing judgment's clothes. The legislation of trusts and judgment stands against exactly this exchange, commanding that trusts be returned to their owners and that people be judged with justice (4:58 — **“Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people,”**). The bribed judge is the anti-witness of the previous passage: instead of standing firm against payment, he stands for sale. Because the ruling family of verses calls judgment a trust, the purchased verdict is a double theft — the property and the law at once. The corruption cannot be repaired by the ruling it produced; the money must go back and the case be heard again.

**KNOWING MAKES IT WORSE**

The verse's last words carry the indictment: knowing that it is a sin. The knowledge is part of the act, so the corruption is done with the scale in the doer's hand. This is the difference between the ignorant transgressor and the calculating one, and the verse addresses the second. The report of Ibn ʿAbbās names the event behind the law — two men and an estate dispute in which the property was consumed in vanity — and the law refuses to let the paperwork of such cases stand. Because the sin is known, the excuse of confusion is removed before it is offered, and the only remaining question is the doer's repentance. The One who counts the portions counts the knowledge with them.

**FIRE AS THE ACCOUNTANT**

The threatened account is drawn in the imagery the Qur'an reserves for the devourers of other people's property: the consumption of the unjust is answered with consumption of another kind. The orphan's wealth, swallowed without right, is described as fire carried in the belly, and the image returns in the legislation of the vulnerable's property (4:10 — **“Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies.”**). The equality of the image is the justice of it: the mouth that swallowed the wrong share receives the share it deserves. The application is therefore practical and not poetic — to examine the estate, the contract, and the courtroom before the money moves, because the examination done in this world is cheaper than the one prepared for the next. The honest man's margin is small and his fear is large; the corrupt man's margin is large and his account is fire. The verse's warning therefore works in both directions at once: it discourages the bribe and it comforts the victim, since the verdict purchased in this world is scheduled for reversal in the next. Because the sin is known at the moment of the payment, the repentance must include the money returned and the case reopened. The estate that was devoured is owed its portion back before the bellies are emptied of anything else. The verse's audience includes the officials as well, since a marketplace without bribes is built by the refusals of the judges as much as by the honesty of the traders. The one who refuses the envelope protects the whole street's faith in the courthouse.
