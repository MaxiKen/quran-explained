**THE SANCTITY OF WEALTH**

The verse develops the sanctity of wealth with ***“Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly”***. After discussing the fairness of bequests and the discipline of fasting, the Qur’an sets a broad rule for economic life: do not consume one another’s wealth through false or unlawful means. *Consuming* is wider than eating. It includes taking, using, controlling, or benefiting from property without a legitimate right. Wealth belongs to Allah ultimately, and people hold it as a trust governed by law and ethics. The prohibition covers theft, fraud, embezzlement, deception in trade, exploitation of the vulnerable, breach of trust, false claims, unlawful gambling, bribery, interest in its prohibited forms, and any contract that is not based on genuine consent or a lawful subject. The Qur’an elsewhere permits trade by mutual agreement, showing that Islam does not condemn wealth or commerce; it condemns injustice in obtaining them. The verse specifically mentions presenting wealth to authorities—present wealth as a bribe to authorities—so that a person may consume another’s property. This includes bribing judges, officials, police, regulators, mediators, or anyone with power to alter a decision. A bribe can make falsehood appear lawful in court, but a judgement does not transform stolen wealth into a rightful possession before Allah. The Prophet ﷺ cursed the briber and the one who takes the bribe, as reported in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī and Sunan Abū Dāwūd. The condemnation protects the integrity of justice. If legal outcomes can be purchased, the poor lose access to their rights, the powerful become untouchable, and society’s trust collapses. (4:29 — **“O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally”**) Because the sanctity of wealth governs 2:188, even a fraction remains sinful arises from the sanctity of wealth. It follows that honest trade and mutual consent completes the sanctity of wealth through even a fraction remains sinful.

**EVEN A FRACTION REMAINS SINFUL**

At even a fraction remains sinful, attention falls on ***“nor deliberately bribe authorities in order to devour a portion of others’ property, knowing”***. The knowing mentioned here makes the act deliberate: even a portion of another person’s wealth remains unlawful. A person may not have taken the entire property; a single dishonest share is enough to be a grave sin. Someone who wins a case through a false witness, hidden evidence, or a paid official may receive only a fraction, but the fraction remains unlawful. The word *knowingly* emphasizes moral responsibility. A person who genuinely makes an innocent mistake should correct it when it becomes clear. The one who knows the claim is false yet proceeds cannot hide behind a favourable legal ruling. The Prophet ﷺ warned in the Ṣaḥīḥ collection of al-Bukhārī, with the report also in Muslim, that disputants may present arguments more persuasively than the other, and that a judgement in their favour does not make what they take lawful. Courts determine rights through human evidence and procedure, but Allah knows the underlying reality. A successful lawsuit is not a licence to take what a person knows belongs to another. Likewise, losing a case does not always mean that one’s moral claim was false; a person may need to seek appeal or another lawful remedy. The believer respects judicial process while remembering that divine judgement is more complete. This principle applies to modern contracts and institutions. A company may comply with a technical rule while hiding a material fact from customers. An employer may use unequal bargaining power to take advantage of workers. A public official may direct contracts to friends. If the wealth is acquired through deception or abuse of trust, its outward paperwork does not make it *lawful*. (4:58 — **“Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners”**) The reason honest trade and mutual consent follows even a fraction remains sinful lies in the sanctity of wealth. Therefore, honest trade and mutual consent returns practical force to even a fraction remains sinful.

**HONEST TRADE AND MUTUAL CONSENT**

Honest trade and mutual consent draws together ***“that it is a sin”***. The Qur’an permits trade by mutual consent, but consent must be informed and free from coercion and fraud. A seller discloses defects, uses accurate weights, honours warranties, and does not manipulate a desperate buyer. A buyer pays what was agreed and does not exploit a seller’s mistake. Both parties remain accountable to Allah after the document is signed. The Prophet ﷺ passed by a pile of food and put his hand into it, finding hidden wet grain underneath. He said, Whoever deceives us is not one of us, a report that Muslim preserves in his Ṣaḥīḥ. The hadith makes transparency a religious obligation, not merely a commercial preference. Bribery is especially destructive because it converts public authority into private property. A person may pay to obtain a licence, avoid a penalty, win public land, secure a job, or defeat a rightful claimant. Even when the payment is described as a *gift,* its timing and purpose may reveal that it is a bribe. There is a legal distinction between a person who pays under coercion to recover a right or avoid oppression and the person who pays to obtain what he has no right to receive. The exact judgement requires qualified advice and evidence. The general rule remains: no one may purchase injustice, and officials may not demand payment for duties they are already obliged to perform. (5:8 — **“O believers! Stand firm”**)

**REPENTANCE AND RESTITUTION**

A person who has consumed unlawful wealth must repent and return what can be returned. If the owner is known, restitution is required. If the money cannot be returned directly, the person should seek qualified guidance about disposing of it in a way that removes the unlawful benefit, while not treating charity as a shortcut that excuses deliberate theft. Public institutions should investigate corruption and recover stolen funds. The fasting passage reminds us that worship and wealth are connected. A person cannot use prayer, fasting, or pilgrimage to cleanse a property right that he refuses to restore. The rights of people require repair. A court order obtained by bribery is like a stolen coat fitted with an official badge. The badge may silence objections in public, but it does not change the owner or cleanse the theft. Legal success and moral right can therefore separate. Present-day contracts, procurement, litigation, and online transactions create sophisticated ways to consume another's wealth while preserving paperwork, yet the verse addresses the knowing conscience behind the file: a judge's mistaken ruling cannot make false property lawful before God. (83:1 — **“Woe to the defrauders”**) In other words, repentance and restitution answers the sanctity of wealth.
