**A STRIKE WITH A PART OF WHAT WAS SACRIFICED**

The instruction is one sentence long and it ends the case: ***“So We instructed, “Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs”***. The pronoun of the strike returns to the slain man. It carries the masculine sense of the person rather than the feminine form of the soul-word. The scene gathers three dead things at once: a dead man, a dead limb, and an animal already slaughtered. The transmission of Mujāhid fixes the moment in one line of narrative: *\"he struck him with the cow's thigh and he rose alive and said that so-and-so had killed him, then he returned to his death\"*. Another account has the blow fall from the tongue or the tail of the animal. In that account the revived man names a killer or two among his own cousins before he dies again. The two killers were denied the inheritance and were later killed. The differences between these reports are held without strain, because none of them touches what the verse makes plain. The instrument of the case was the flesh of the sacrifice. The demonstration was a return to life granted for a day.

**THE PART NOBODY WAS TOLD TO KEEP**

The specifying instinct that ran through the hunt now reappears among the interpreters, and the tradition handles it with a discipline of its own. The scholars differed over which organ was used. One names the tongue, since the tongue is the instrument of speech and the dead man was about to speak. Another names the base of the tail, since the human frame is said to be assembled there. Others name the thigh, or a bone from her bones. Al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Ibn Jurayj both record the dispute over the matter. Against all of them stands the certain minimum — an organ from her organs. The epistemic rule the reports carry is the sharpest thing in the material. Any part of the cow would have produced the miracle, so the unnamed part carries no benefit in religion or in life. Had it carried benefit, the Book would have named it. The matter was left open because it was delivered open, and the learned response is to leave it exactly so. The reason the rule matters reaches far past this episode. The reader who demands the withheld details of every reported sign is demanding what the report deliberately declines to give.

**THE LAWYERS TAKE UP THE DEAD MAN'S WORDS**

The revived man's sentence — my blood is with so-and-so, so-and-so killed me — entered the legal tradition as an argument, and the argument divided the schools. In the transmissions of Ibn Wahb and Ibn al-Qasim, the great early jurist Malik cited the slain man's words as proof for the collective oath procedure in homicide. That is the fifty-oath mechanism by which a victim's kin press a murder claim when direct proof is missing. The rejection came from al-Shafi and the majority of the scholars. They held their own position correct and refused the probative analogy. The standing law of murder is drawn tightly, as the verse on retaliation shows: (2:178 — **“O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder—a free man for a free man,”**). A miracle-bound declaration sits outside that ordinary evidence. The difference between the two sides turns on exactly that boundary. In this story the dying declaration was certified by a device no courtroom can reproduce — a corpse revived by the flesh of a divinely commanded sacrifice. It therefore stands as the testimony of a miracle and not as a model for witnesses. What follows for the legal mind is a caution in both directions. The event may illuminate the moral weight the dying man's words were given. It may not be pressed into a rule of proof the standing law never drew from it.

**ONE SOUL REVIVED, EVERY SOUL PROMISED**

The closing clause does the work of a creed: ***“so that you may understand”***. The understand here is glossed from one direction as belief in the resurrection after death. From another direction it is the reasoning that follows from a single example. The One capable of reviving one soul is capable of reviving every soul: (30:50 — **“Surely That ˹same God˺ can raise the dead. For He is Most Capable of everything”**). The reader is asked to pass from the witnessed case to the promised one. The chapter builds this argument with visible architecture, returning to the life-after-death demonstration more than once. There is the raising after the first death. There are the thousands who fell and rose. There is the traveller who passed a town in ruins and watched its people stand again. There is the patriarch who asked to be shown how the dead are given life and was answered with four birds: (2:260 — **“And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead.” Allah responded, “Do you not believe?””**). Even the complaint the doubters raise about decayed bones is staged in the book only to be answered: (36:78 — **“And they argue with Us—forgetting they were created—saying, “Who will give life to decayed bones?””**). Each return presses the same point from a new angle. What happened before can happen again, and the precedent is meant to carry the reason forward. The modern reader who accepts the logic of precedent in every other field will find the chapter asking only that the logic be applied without a special exemption for death.
