**A STRIKE WITH A PART OF WHAT WAS SACRIFICED**

The instruction is one sentence long and it ends the case: ***“So We instructed, “Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life, showing you His signs”***. The pronoun of the strike returns to the slain man, carried on the masculine sense of the person rather than the feminine form of the soul-word, and the scene gathers three dead things at once: a dead man, a dead limb, and an animal already slaughtered. The transmission of Mujāhid fixes the moment in one line of narrative: *"he struck him with the cow's thigh and he rose alive and said that so-and-so had killed him, then he returned to his death"*. Another account has the blow fall from the tongue or the tail of the animal, and the revived man naming a killer or two among his own cousins before he died again. The differences between these reports are held without strain, because none of them touches what the verse itself makes plain: the instrument of the case was the flesh of the sacrifice, and the demonstration was a return to life granted for a day.

**THE PART NOBODY WAS TOLD TO KEEP**

The specifying instinct that ran through the hunt now reappears among the interpreters themselves, and the tradition handles it with an instructive discipline. The scholars differed over which organ was used: one naming the tongue, since the tongue is the instrument of speech and the dead man was about to speak; another the base of the tail, since the human frame is said to be assembled there; others the thigh, or a bone from her bones. Against all of them stands the certain minimum — an organ from her organs — and the narrated epistemic rule is the sharpest thing in the material. Any part of the cow would have produced the miracle, so the unspecified part carries no benefit in religion or in life; had it carried benefit, the Book would have named it; the matter was left vague because it was delivered vague, and the learned response is to leave it exactly so. The lesson reaches far past this episode, since the reader who demands the withheld details of every reported sign is demanding what the report deliberately declines to give.

**THE LAWYERS TAKE UP THE DEAD MAN'S WORDS**

The revived man's sentence — my blood is with so-and-so, so-and-so killed me — entered the legal tradition as an argument, and the argument divided the schools. In the transmissions of Ibn Wahb and Ibn al-Qasim, the great early jurist Malik cited the slain man's words as proof for the validity of the collective oath procedure in homicide, the fifty-oath mechanism by which a victim's kin press a murder claim when direct proof is missing. The rejection came from al-Shafi and the majority of the scholars, who held their own position correct and refused the probative analogy; (2:178 — **“O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set for you in cases of murder—a free man for a free man,”**) marks how tightly the standing law of murder is drawn, and a miracle-bound declaration sits outside its ordinary evidence. The difference between the two sides turns on exactly that boundary. In this story the dying declaration was certified by a device no courtroom can reproduce — a corpse revived by the flesh of a divinely commanded sacrifice — which means it stands as a miracle's testimony and not as a template for witnesses. What follows for the legal mind is therefore a caution in both directions: the event may illuminate the moral gravity with which the dying man's words were treated, and it may not be pressed into a rule of proof the standing law never drew from it.

**ONE SOUL REVIVED, EVERY SOUL PROMISED**

The closing clause does the work of a creed: ***“so that you may understand”***. The understand here is glossed from one direction as believing in the resurrection after death, and from another as the reasoning that follows from a single example: the One capable of reviving one soul is capable of reviving every soul, and the reader is asked to pass from the witnessed case to the promised one. The chapter builds this argument with visible architecture, returning to the life-after-death demonstration more than once — the raising after the first death, the thousands who fell and rose, the traveller who passed a town in ruins and watched its people stand again, and the patriarch who asked to be shown how the dead are given life and was answered with four birds (2:260 — **“And ˹remember˺ when Abraham said, “My Lord! Show me how you give life to the dead.” Allah responded, “Do you not believe?””**). Each return presses the same point from a different angle: what happened before can happen again, and the precedent is meant to carry the reason forward. The modern reader who accepts the logic of precedent in every other field will find the chapter asking only that the logic be applied without a special exemption for death.
