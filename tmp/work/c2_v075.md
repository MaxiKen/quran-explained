**AN EXPECTATION THAT HAS TO BE GIVEN UP**

The verse asks the believers a question about their hopes, and the answer is left with the reader: ***“Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you”***, ***“though a group of them would hear the word of Allah”***, ***“then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it”***. The charge is specific, and it is not that this group rejected what it heard; the charge is that the meaning was grasped and then altered deliberately. The believers are being asked why they keep offering trust to people whose practice has already been described, and the question is a correction aimed at the trusting rather than at the untrustworthy. The Book treats misplaced confidence as a fault of its own, since a community that never learns from a pattern of deception will keep paying for the same lesson. The sentence assumes that the record is sufficient evidence and that no further proof is required before a reader adjusts his expectations.

**WHAT IT MEANS TO CORRUPT A WORD KNOWINGLY**

The phrase about understanding before altering is the hinge of the verse, because it removes the excuse of ignorance from the offence. A person who changes a text he does not understand may be careless; a person who changes what he has grasped is doing something else, and the Book's later description of that conduct is blunt: (2:79 — **“So woe to those who distort the Scripture with their own hands then say,”**). The same charge is laid out again elsewhere, in wording that describes the distortion being done with the tongue so that listeners take it for the Book: (3:78 — **“There are some among them”**). The verse therefore does not describe a dispute about interpretation, since interpretation leaves the words in place. It describes a change made to the wording itself by people who knew what the wording had been.

**HOW THIS PEOPLE'S HABIT AFFECTS A READER**

A reader should notice how much of the chapter's account of this community is built on this one charge, since the distortion is named here and returns at the end of the sūrah in a prayer about not being made to bear what others have twisted. The practical consequence for a reader is a caution about trust: an honest disagreement can be worked with, but a group that has been shown to rewrite what it receives cannot be relied upon to convey what it is told. The Book's instruction here is not to hate the people concerned; it is to stop expecting from them a fidelity they have already demonstrated they do not practise. A community that keeps hoping otherwise will find its own message changing in their hands, which is the loss the verse is trying to prevent.

**THE TRUSTING PARTY AND ITS HABIT**

Think of a lender who has been repaid late three times and still counts the fourth promise as reliable, and whose friends have stopped warning him because he does not want to hear it. The Book's question in this verse is the friend's question, and it is asked of the believers because they are the ones who keep extending credit. The verse does not ask them to become suspicious of everyone, since it speaks of a group among the people and not of the whole people; it asks them to calibrate their expectations to what they have seen. The record of the previous verses is the evidence, and anyone who has read the argument so far knows that the question has only one sensible answer. What the Book wants from its own community is a clear-eyed trust, and a clear-eyed trust is one that has been adjusted by experience rather than by wishful thinking.

**A TRUST THAT NEVER LEARNS FROM ITSELF**

There is a wider lesson in the question for any community that carries a message, and it concerns the difference between goodwill and judgement. Goodwill keeps the door open and hopes the best of people, and it is a virtue; judgement notices when the same words are received and returned differently, and it is a duty that goodwill needs. The people described in this verse had the scripture in their hands and the understanding of it in their heads, and they chose to pass on something else, which is why the verse names the knowing as the aggravating factor. A reader who keeps a record of his own dealings can see the value of the Book's sharpness here, since the community that learns from one deception will not need to learn from the same deception again. The verse closes the question without answering it, and the silence is the answer.
