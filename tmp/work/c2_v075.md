**A HOPE THE HISTORY CANNOT CARRY**

The verse that follows the catalogue of signs turns, for once, on the people who received them best: ***“Do you ˹believers still˺ expect them to be true to you”***. The opening interrogative is one of rejection, and the tradition unpacks its force as a single instruction: do not be so eager, for their record is already written. The expression of longing at the head of the sentence is stronger than hope. The tongue of the language marks it as the soul's strong attachment to attaining its object, arising from the intensity of desire and the force of the will — and that is precisely the disease being diagnosed in the address, a longing for the belief of a people whose book of signs has already closed, which means the desire is spent on a door that history has shut. The address itself is disputed in the best way. The received views run from the Prophet and the believers together, to the believers alone, to the Ansar in particular, whose alliance and neighbourhood with the Jewish tribes had made them eager for their conversion; the transmitted view from Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil takes the line as directed to the Prophet alone, carrying the comfort of do not grieve at their belying you. The difference between the readings softens the edge rather than dulling it, since every candidate for the address shares one posture: standing at the end of a long persuasion and still hoping it might work.

**WHO THE GROUP IS THAT HEARD AND ALTERED**

The reason the hope is being sent home is then given in one clause of history: ***“though a group of them would hear the word of Allah”***, ***“then knowingly corrupt it after understanding it”***. The identity of the group is settled in two directions across the glosses, and both deserve keeping. Mujāhid, Qatādah, ʿIkrimah, and al-Suddī take the group as the scholars of the community in its later generations — the class who heard the revelation recited and then altered what it carried. The transmitted view from Ibn ʿAbbās and Muqātil places the group at the mountain, as the seventy men chosen by Moses for the appointment of his Lord, and the distortion charged to them is precise: they returned to their people declaring that their Lord had promised forgiveness where the commandments could not be borne, a softening of the law pronounced in the very presence of its descent. The narrated examples of the later alteration run to two items above all: the description of the awaited prophet, which was rewritten, and the penalty that had stood in the text and was taken out. The word they were heard hearing is the Torah itself, recited to them by Moses, and the hearing is not metaphorical — the tradition has them listening to the recitation as the verses arrived.

The crime in the clause is layered past translation, and every layer matters. The distortion is reported as reaching the words themselves, or the sense standing behind them, or both at once, and the two verbs of the clause frame it as a deliberate act of the learned. The report of Qatādah keeps the whole sequence in one line: *"they are the ones who used to hear the words of God and then alter them after they had understood and grasped them"*. The double qualification attaching to that act is the moral centre of the verse. The corruption came after understanding, so that error is ruled out; and it came knowingly, so that the excuse of haste is ruled out too. What turns on those two words is the whole difference between a mistake and a crime, since the alteration of a text one has understood is a decision made against one's own knowledge. The Book traces the same behaviour to its root in the covenant's own ledger — (5:13 — **“But for breaking their covenant We condemned them and hardened their hearts. They distorted the words of the Scripture”**) — and the hardened hearts of the preceding verse and the distorted words of this one are therefore the same condition seen from inside and outside.

**THE CONTEMPORARIES AND THE RECKONING OF COMPANY**

The verse's logic then reaches past its first audience, and the narrated context makes the reach practical. The believers had taken great pains over the conversion of their neighbours, and the counsel here is not coldness toward a people but realism about a pattern. The contemporaries of the Prophet may not have stood at the mountain or rewritten the pages, and the tradition still counts them into the account on one principle: a generation that does not abhor the misdeeds of its forefathers is counted among their company. The standard applied is neither inheritance nor ancestry but approval, and by that standard the party addressed in the verse is anyone who keeps the silence that makes alteration safe. The two identifications of the group converge exactly here, because the scholars who alter and the publics that consent are one machinery; the first supplies the distortion and the second supplies the market for it. The reader today who has watched a text handled carelessly by those paid to guard it will find the verse's expectation-management reading as current advice: persuasion has a duty, and it also has a horizon set by the honesty of its audience, because neither half of the equation belongs to the persuader alone.

The immediate demonstration of the diagnosis arrives in the following movement of the passage, where the private and public faces of the group separate — (2:76 — **“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.” But in private they say ˹to each other˺,”**). The pattern there is the pattern named here in embryo: speech arranged by audience, knowledge treated as a bargaining chip rather than a trust. The closing words of the present verse — after understanding it, knowingly — thus stand as the verdict that the next scene will illustrate, and the illustration is drawn from ordinary conversation rather than from any miracle. What follows for those who read the passage as a manual of trust is exact. Knowledge of revelation carries two custodial duties at once: it may not be altered, and it may not be withheld for advantage, and the verse indicts the group on both counts in a single sentence.
