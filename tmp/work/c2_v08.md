**A CLAIM MADE WITH THE DOOR STILL SHUT**
The sūrah has just described people who refuse the Book in the open, and it now turns to people who never refused it with their mouths. The opening words set the tone: ***“And there are some”***. Those words do not sweep a nation or a class into a single judgement, and they say little about how many such people there are, which keeps a reader from guessing at numbers and turns his attention to the description that follows. Reports from the earliest generation place these people inside the community at Madinah, attending what the believers attended while carrying something else in the chest. A face in the second row of a prayer line is not evidence about the heart behind it, and this verse exists because the difference had become impossible to see from the outside. The claim that follows is therefore examined in public, in front of anyone who has made the same claim, and the examination itself is a kindness to a person who would rather not be deceived about his own state. The Book keeps a long register of this type of man, and nothing in that register is gentle (9:67).
**A CONFESSION WITH A PIECE MISSING**
What these people announce is not a denial but a confession, and the confession is carefully built out of two halves: ***“who say, “We believe in Allah”***, ***“and the Last Day,””***. Their words name God and they name the Last Day, and the Messenger is absent from the list. A man who refuses to lie outright still has to leave something out, and what was left out here is the part that would have committed him. The two items kept in the claim are the two that cost nothing to say in that city, since a man who has already decided to hide his allegiance will begin by naming the parts of it that no one will challenge. Picture a merchant who swears by his own good name while avoiding the name of the man he cheated, and the construction in front of the reader is the same one the verse reports. Belief in God in general is easy, and the test arrives with the particular messenger a person is asked to follow, which is why the verse shows a man arranging his words to pass inspection without passing through the door. This is why the exposure of him is a favour rather than a humiliation.
**THE DISGUISE INSIDE THE HOUSE**
The last words of the verse are a verdict rather than a description: ***“yet they are not ˹true˺ believers”***. Hiding behind an appearance is not a small matter in the Book, because it damages the one thing a religious life needs, which is a person whose inside and outside agree. The Qur'an returns to the type in a sūrah of its own, (63:3 — **“their hearts have been sealed, so they do not comprehend”**), and the destination of the habit is named there without softening, (4:145 — **“Surely the hypocrites will be in the lowest depths of the Fire”**). A man who keeps two accounts of himself is not merely being untidy; he is choosing which of the two he will finally be, and the choice hardens with use. The verse speaks to the reader today as directly as it spoke to the row of worshippers it describes, since the temptation to be counted among a people without paying their price is older than any city.
**WHAT A TRUE CLAIM COSTS**
A claim like this one is not cured by better arguments, because nothing is being argued; it is cured by the fear of being seen as one actually is. The Book offers a practice that closes the gap, and the practice is cheap: do the small things when nobody is watching, and let the private record catch up with the public one. The Prophet ﷺ described the disease in a report carried by Bukhārī and Muslim on the authority of Abū Hurayrah, listing three signs of the hypocrite: a lie when he speaks, a broken promise, and a betrayal of a trust. Anyone who measures himself against those three signs will find something to repair long before he finds a reason to feel superior, and the verdict in the verse turns from a label into a diagnostic. A man can begin that repair today with one promise kept and one lie refused, because the distance between the two accounts narrows with every small act of agreement between them.
