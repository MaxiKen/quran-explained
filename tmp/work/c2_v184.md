**A MEASURED OBLIGATION**

The sequence reaches a measured obligation through ***“˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days.”***, ***“But whoever of you is ill”***, ***“or on a journey”***. Fasting is prescribed for a limited number of days, not as a permanent burden. The wording prepares the believer for a duty with a clear beginning and end. Islam does not ask the body to abandon nourishment indefinitely. It disciplines appetite within a defined period so that the servant can worship with steadiness rather than destructive excess. The verse also reveals the law’s attention to capacity. The sick and the traveller are permitted to delay the fast and make up the same number later. Illness is assessed according to whether fasting worsens the condition, delays recovery, or causes serious hardship, and a trustworthy medical opinion can help. Travel has legal details in the Sunnah and fiqh; the concession is not a licence for casual inconvenience, but neither should a traveller be shamed for using the mercy Allah granted. The obligation is not cancelled; it is transferred to a time when the person can fulfil it. A person should make up the fasts before the next Ramadan when reasonably able, while someone whose illness is permanent has a different ruling explained in the juristic tradition. The requirement of equal days reflects justice and mercy. The person does not owe more because he was ill, and he does not lose the worship because his circumstances temporarily changed. Allah values the intention to obey and provides a lawful route to completion. (2:183 — **“so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺”**) Because a measured obligation governs 2:184, the ransom for inability arises from a measured obligation. It follows that why performance outweighs substitution completes a measured obligation through the ransom for inability.

**THE RANSOM FOR INABILITY**

The ransom for inability takes shape in ***“then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺.”***, ***“For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺.”***, ***“But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better”***. The verse mentions a ransom—*fid-yah*—for those who can only fast with great difficulty. Classical scholars discussed the scope of this category, especially in relation to the early stages of the fasting legislation and to people with permanent inability. The established legal application includes an elderly person or one with a chronic condition who cannot safely fast and cannot reasonably expect recovery; such a person feeds a needy person for each day, according to the relevant school and local guidance. A temporary illness is generally treated by making up the fast later, while a permanent inability calls for feeding rather than a physically harmful fast. People should not impose a universal ruling on medical situations without considering the person’s condition. Allah does not require worship that causes serious harm. The general principle is that voluntary generosity is rewarded, but it must not contradict the concessions Allah has provided. Extra good includes feeding more than one needy person, providing nutritious meals, supporting a fasting family, or helping someone make up missed obligations. Charity should preserve dignity. It is better to offer good food without reminding the recipient of the favour or making the poor perform gratitude before people. (4:29 — **“O believers! Do not devour one another’s wealth illegally”**) The reason why performance outweighs substitution follows the ransom for inability lies in a measured obligation. Therefore, why performance outweighs substitution returns practical force to the ransom for inability.

**WHY PERFORMANCE OUTWEIGHS SUBSTITUTION**

The wording for why performance outweighs substitution is ***“for them. And to fast is better”***, ***“for you, if only you knew”***. Fasting is better for you, if only you knew encourages those who are able to fulfil the ordinary obligation rather than searching for excuses. It does not condemn the sick or traveller who takes the concession. The verse places encouragement alongside mercy: the healthy resident fasts; the person with a legitimate excuse delays or feeds according to his condition. The believer should avoid both extremes. One extreme treats every discomfort as permission to abandon the fast. The other treats illness, pregnancy, old age, or serious medical danger as a test of religious toughness. The Prophet ﷺ sometimes fasted while travelling and sometimes broke his fast, as recorded in Muslim’s Ṣaḥīḥ and al-Bukhārī’s record, teaching that the lawful concession is part of obedience. Fasting requires care for health. The pre-dawn meal, hydration during the permitted hours, moderation at the evening meal, and attention to medical advice help the servant worship responsibly. The Qur’an does not praise self-harm. The Prophet ﷺ said that the body has a right over the person, a report preserved by al-Bukhārī in his Ṣaḥīḥ, and this right remains during Ramadan. Care is especially important for diabetes, pregnancy, breastfeeding, chronic illness, physically demanding work, and mental health conditions. A person should ask a reliable scholar and qualified clinician. Religious commitment is not measured by ignoring genuine danger. Counted days are like a prescribed course of medicine: limited enough to complete, serious enough to transform, and adjusted when illness makes the normal dose harmful. A patient does not call the adjustment a rejection of treatment, nor does he invent his own schedule without need. The concessions of fasting carry this measured mercy. For believers today, the verse resists both harsh performance that ignores genuine incapacity and casual avoidance that turns a licence into a habit, keeping worship joined to honesty. (2:195 — **“Spend in the cause of Allah”**) In other words, why performance outweighs substitution answers a measured obligation.
