**COUNTED DAYS**

Taken phrase by phrase, the passage legislates its ladder step by step: ***“˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days.”***, ***“But whoever of you is ill”***, ***“or on a journey”***, ***“then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺.”***, ***“For those who can only fast with extreme difficulty, compensation can be made by feeding a needy person ˹for every day not fasted˺.”***, ***“But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better”***, ***“for them. And to fast is better”***, ***“for you, if only you knew”***. The order of the rungs is deliberate: the command, then the two excuses, then the payment, then the preference, so nobody reads the ladder upside down. The household climbs the ladder in this order, and the order is drawn for the households that struggle most. The dispensation Ibn ʿAbbās describes is governed by this sequence.

˹Fast a˺ prescribed number of days. answers the anxiety of an open-ended obligation: the days are numbered, and the counting is part of the mercy. The verse continues with the first exemption — But whoever of you is ill or on a journey — and the pair covers the two conditions a human being cannot schedule. The substitution is symmetrical: then ˹let them fast˺ an equal number of days ˹after Ramaḍân˺, so the ledger is preserved without burdening the person whose body or road prevents the act. Ibn ʿAbbās's account of the dispensation is precise: the one who misses fasts the same number on other days, and the one who cannot fast at all feeds a poor person for each day missed. The structure of the ruling is a ladder of capacities — fast if you can, make up if you miss, feed if you cannot — and nobody is left outside the law's design (73:20 — **“and night. He knows that you ˹believers˺ are unable to endure this, and has turned to you in mercy.”**).

**BREAKAGE AND ITS PAYMENT**

The middle case — the person who can fast only with extreme difficulty — is where the verse's charity appears most clearly. Compensation by feeding is offered first, and the option of feeding is framed as an ease rather than as a lesser act. The report of Ibn ʿAbbās applies the ruling to the old man and the old woman who cannot endure the fast: they feed a poor person for every day, and the feeding replaces the hunger they can no longer carry. Because the payment is food, the exemption teaches solidarity as well — the body that cannot worship with hunger still feeds the body that has no food at all. The ruling thus converts incapacity into provision for the poor, which is a redistribution designed by mercy. The one who has wealth learns that the month's discipline can be kept with his hand when the stomach refuses.

**BETTER WHEN VOLUNTARY**

The verse closes with the case of the person who volunteers more — But whoever volunteers to give more, it is better for them — and then returns the reader to the core act: And to fast is better for you, if only you knew. The comparison is stated, not argued: fasting is better, and the verse says so in the grammar of a teacher who knows the students will ask. The volunteer is praised without the obligatory being diminished, since the ladder of capacities already set the minimum. The wisdom behind the preference is left to the reader's experience — the fasting person knows what the month does to his attention, his temper, and his gratitude. Because the verse says "if only you knew," the knowledge it points to is available to anyone who keeps the month rather than merely studies it. The best argument for the fast is the character of the one who finishes it.

**KNOWLEDGE CHANGES THE RULING**

The verse's arrangement — illness, travel, incapacity, volunteering — is a small course in how revelation reasons. Each case receives its ruling because the case was known before the ruling was written, and the mercy is therefore structural rather than improvised. The application is to consult the ladder before despairing: the person who cannot fast has been seen by the law and given a door. The traveler's ease is not a loophole but a provision, and the ill person's make-up days protect both the body and the account. Because the rules are published in advance, nobody must invent an exemption in the dark of his own conscience. The hardship clause of the wider legislation stands behind all of this, since the religion carries no hardship its own Book has not already relieved (22:78 — **“and laid upon you no hardship in the religion—the way of your forefather Abraham. ˹It is Allah˺ Who”**). The verse's ladder is therefore worth reading slowly in the case of a household with an ill parent: the ruling has already met that parent and offered him a door. Because each rung of the ladder is published, the family's decision is made against a text rather than against its own guilt. The published ladder also removes the authority of the family's impatience: nobody can invent a harder rule than the verse allows.
