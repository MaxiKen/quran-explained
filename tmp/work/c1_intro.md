This sūrah is the opening of the Book, and the community has never known it by any other place than the head of the muṣḥaf. The reports give it several names, each taken from a function it performs. It is the Opening of the Book (Fātiḥat al-Kitāb) because the Book begins with it, and the Mother of the Qur'an (Umm al-Qur'an) and the Mother of the Book because the rest of the Book is built upon it, as a settlement is built out from its centre. It is the Seven Oft-Repeated (al-Sabʿ al-Mathānī) because it holds seven verses which are recited again and again in every unit of prayer, and the Qur'an names the gift in a verse addressed to the Prophet: (15:87 — **“the seven often-repeated verses”**). It is also called the Praise (al-Ḥamd), the name it takes from its own second sentence, and the Prayer (al-Ṣalāh), because the Prophet said of it, as Abū Hurayrah reports, that a prayer in which the Opening of the Book is not recited is a deficient prayer (al-Bukhārī, Muslim). A report carried by Muslim gives the sūrah a higher honour still: Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet said that God says, “I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves, and My servant shall have what he asks,” and the report then follows the recitation sentence by sentence, with the answer of God placed beside each one.

The early scholars took up the question of where the sūrah was revealed, and the greater number of them held it to be Makkan. ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib, Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah and most of the Companions are reported to have held that position, and the case is supported by the verse that names the seven oft-repeated verses, which belongs to the Makkan revelation by the agreement of the scholars. Mujāhid held it to be Madinan, alone among the early authorities, and later writers counted the view a slip on his part rather than a position with a case behind it. Some held that it was revealed twice, once at Makkah when the prayer was made obligatory and once at Madinah when the direction of prayer was changed, so that the community would know that its place in the prayer had not changed with the direction. Whatever the date of its coming down, its rank in the religion was settled from the first days: it is recited in every unit of every prayer, it stands at the head of the written Book, and the reports about its recitation are carried in the two collections that the community has received with the fullest acceptance.

The arrangement of the sūrah runs in three movements. The first movement speaks about God in the third person: praise belongs to Him, He is Lord of all the worlds, He is the Most Compassionate and the Most Merciful, and He is Master of the Day of Judgement. The second movement, the middle verse, turns the address around: the servant now speaks to God directly and says that it is Him alone he worships and Him alone he asks for help. The third movement is the petition that the two earlier movements have been preparing: guidance to a straight path, described in the terms of the people who walk it. The turn at the middle verse never turns back, and the sūrah does not return to speaking about God after the servant has begun to speak to Him. Its wording also moves from the widest horizon to the narrowest: from the Lord of all the worlds, through mercy and the Day of Judgement, to one person asking for one road. The arrangement is not an accident of composition. Because the servant is taught to open with praise before he asks, the sūrah models the order of supplication itself, and because the praise is put in his mouth before the request, he learns that the one to whom he will turn has already been described to him.

What the sūrah carries as a whole is the creed of the religion in seven short verses, and the request that gives that creed its direction. It states that God is one, that He is the Lord of everything, that mercy is His settled attribute and judgement is His settled work, and it places the servant before Him as a worshipper who has nothing to rely on but help from the one he worships. Then it gathers the whole religion into a single request, which is guidance, because a person who has been given everything else still needs the road, and knows it. The last verse describes that road by the people who travel it rather than by its features, and names the two deviations that have drawn people off it. One of the earliest Muslims described the opening sentence of this sūrah as a statement that God loved for Himself, was pleased with for Himself, and loves to have repeated, and the community has repeated it in every prayer since. The sūrah teaches the reader what to say, in what order to say it, and what to ask for when everything has been said.
