Everything in the Book that follows rests on these seven lines. They are recited in every unit of the daily prayer, and the sūrah is known by three names that each describe a role it plays. It is the Opening, because the Book begins with it. It is the Mother of the Book, because the whole of the revelation grows out of what it says. It is the seven often-repeated lines, because no other passage is repeated as often in worship, and that last name comes from the Qur'an's own wording about verses read again and again. The shortness of the sūrah is part of its purpose: twenty-five words set out the shape of the whole religion, and the hundred and thirteen sūrahs that come after fill that shape in. A reader who learns these lines has been given the plan of the building before he walks through it.

The sūrah moves in two halves, and the reader is the one who travels between them. The first half speaks about God: praise, lordship, mercy, and the Day when accounts are settled. The second half speaks to Him: worship, dependence, and a request for guidance that will not be answered inside these seven lines but across the rest of the Book. A single line carries the turn where the servant stops describing and begins addressing, and everything after it is spoken in the first person. The Prophet ﷺ reported that his Lord said, *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves,"* as Muslim carries it. That report explains why the sūrah cannot be read as a statement about God alone. It is a conversation with two voices in it, and the second voice belongs to the person reading.

One question waits at the door of the sūrah, and it is honest to name it before the verses are read. The line that begins the chapter, the naming of God before anything else, is agreed by everyone to be part of the Qur'an, since it stands inside the letter Solomon sent to the queen of Sheba. What is debated is whether it is counted as the first verse of this sūrah or as a marker separating one sūrah from the next. The difference shows in prayer, where one school recites it quietly and another recites it aloud as part of the opening. Both positions come from the practice of the Companions, and each is carried by reports the reader can check. The disagreement is worth knowing about, and it is not worth fighting over, because no position on it changes what the words themselves ask of the person saying them.

Every day the prayer returns the reader to the same seven lines, and this is why their meaning is rehearsed rather than learned once. The words are said in the morning before the day has shown its face and at night after it has spent itself, so the same sentences have to hold both moods. They praise when a person has reason to be pleased and they ask when he has reason to be afraid. Between those two, they keep a course steady that no single day could be trusted to keep on its own. A sūrah repeated that often stops being a text and becomes a habit of speech, and a habit of speech shapes what a person notices and what he reaches for. Read it slowly, then, and it will not stay a formality for long.
