**PARDON DOES NOT CANCEL THE EARTHLY CALLING**

After the acceptance of repentance, the order to leave is heard again: ***“We said, “Descend all of you!”***. Its return does not mean that Adam’s prayer failed. The earlier command described the place of settlement and the hostility he would face. This one sets out the guidance by which life there must be lived. The order is repeated because each statement explains a different side of the descent. There is no need to invent two journeys from two separate gardens to account for the repetition. Earth was already named in the first command. More deeply, the earthly calling preceded the lapse itself: (2:30 — **““I am going to place a successive ˹human˺ authority on earth.””**). Forgiveness restores Adam’s relation to his Lord without removing that calling. The whole account holds these truths together. Sin has consequences, pardon is real, and God’s purpose is not overturned by the tempter’s scheme. Since more than one person is addressed, the promise reaches beyond Adam’s private future. It opens the course of human life in which Adam’s children will need the same Lord’s teaching.

**TEACHING GIVEN FROM GOD REQUIRES A HUMAN RESPONSE**

The promise begins, ***“Then when guidance comes to you from Me,”***. The source matters: the guidance is from God, not a rule Adam’s children devise simply to comfort themselves in exile. Abū al-ʿĀliyah explains it through prophets, messengers, clear signs, and plain teaching. Al-Suddī points to God’s revealed Book. The readings agree because a messenger brings the Book and teaches what the Lord asks of His people. God does not provide earth as a home and then leave its people with no word about their end. The Quran describes the wide reach of this gift: (16:36 — **“We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, “Worship Allah and shun false gods.””**). The guidance names both the Lord to worship and the false objects to refuse. It is therefore more than skill at finding food or building a house. Those skills sustain earthly life; revelation teaches how that life should answer to its Maker. The work of the messengers also makes the demand clear, (4:165 — **“so humanity should have no excuse before Allah after ˹the coming of˺ the messengers.”**). Receiving such teaching is a mercy that brings responsibility with it.

The condition is ***“whoever follows it,”***. The difference between hearing and following is the response to what has been heard. The guidance must be believed where it tells the truth, obeyed where it commands, and heeded where it warns. Otherwise, its arrival alone would make every hearer safe, and the next verse’s warning to those who reject it would lose its force. Because the promise is tied to following, the arrival of guidance calls for a choice rather than granting safety without one. This does not make guidance a prize earned without God’s help. The preceding verse has already shown that even the way to repent is taught by Him. It means that His gift is received through a real response. The parallel promise states, (20:123 — **“whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray ˹in this life˺ nor suffer ˹in the next˺”**). Ibn ʿAbbās explains that promise as freedom from being led astray in this life and from misery in the Hereafter. The two stages are joined. The path is not merely admired now and then recovered at death; it is followed through the life for which the direction was given.

**SAFETY CONCERNS THE END, NOT A LIFE WITHOUT ALARM**

Those who follow are promised that ***“there will be no fear for them,”***. Fear looks toward a harm still ahead. Ibn Zayd applies the assurance to what lies before a person after death, the great concern at the end of earthly life. The follower of guidance will not be abandoned to the punishment from which the message warned him. This cannot reasonably mean that a believer never feels alarm during the present trial. The Quran says of a prophet, (20:67 — **“So Moses concealed fear within himself”**). That fear did not place Moses outside divine guidance. Nor does fear of God contradict the promise. Reverence moves a person to obey; the promised safety is the outcome of that path, not a ban on every feeling along it. This is why the assurance must not be used against those in grief or danger. Being frightened does not disprove a believer’s faith. The question settled here is where following God’s guidance leads, beyond the threats that earthly life contains.

**THE LOSS LEFT BEHIND WILL NOT RULE THE FINAL HOME**

The promise also reaches backward: ***“nor will they grieve”***. Grief concerns a good lost, a hurt endured, or a hope that was not fulfilled. At the final home, such loss will no longer govern the life of those admitted to God’s mercy. Their own words make that end clear: (35:34 — **““Praise be to Allah, Who has kept away from us all ˹causes of˺ sorrow.”**). This is more than being spared a future threat. It is release from the sorrow carried out of the past. Yet the assurance does not require a cold heart in this world. Jacob could turn his sorrow toward God and remain faithful: (12:86 — **““I complain of my anguish and sorrow only to Allah,”**). The promise gives sorrow an end, not a verdict that every mourner has strayed. Fear and grief thus span the two directions in which distress reaches. God’s guidance leads beyond both. Adam’s descendants begin on earth with a warning about their enemy, but they are not sent without a way home. The closing assurance supplies the hope that makes their earthly duty something more than the management of loss.
