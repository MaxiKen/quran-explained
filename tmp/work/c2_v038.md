**A PROMISE MADE TO EVERYONE LEAVING THE GARDEN**

The descent is spoken again, and this time it carries an undertaking: ***“We said, “Descend all of you!”***, ***“Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows it, there will be no fear”***, ***“for them, nor will they grieve”***. The repetition of the departure is not idle, because the second telling is addressed to a crowd rather than to a household. All of you, says the sentence, and that address reaches everyone who would ever be born into the world below. Into that address the Lord places a promise: guidance will come, and the promise is made before anyone has asked for it. The Book is describing a road that has been supplied with signs before its travellers set out. The last clause then defines what is being offered in the only terms a person can verify. No fear and no grief: one looks forward and the other looks back, and between the two of them lies the whole of a person's unease. What follows guidance in this sentence is not prosperity or safety or length of days. It is the removal of the two weights that make the present unbearable and the past unforgettable, and the Book treats those two removals as the substance of the promise rather than as its decoration. A reader who has carried either weight understands immediately how much is being offered.

**THE SAME PROMISE IN OTHER WORDS**

The Book repeats the sentence in several places, and each time the repetition anchors the reader a little more. In the sūrah of the Heights the condition is described with a moral clause attached: (7:35 — **“O children of Adam! When messengers from among yourselves come to you reciting My revelations—whoever”**), and the promise closes the same way there, with no fear and no grief for the ones who mend their ways. Even the children of Israel are given the same terms in this very sūrah when their own account is described: (2:62 — **“Indeed, the believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and”**), and the ending of that verse repeats the two negations word for word. The wording stays constant because the offer does not change with the audience. The guidance in the promise is not a private intuition. The Book consistently attaches it to messengers and to a revealed scripture, which is why the promise appears in practical terms in the sūrah of Ṭā-Hā: (20:123 — **“Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray ˹in this life˺”**). The pattern is the same everywhere. A message arrives, a person follows it, and the fear and the grief lose their grip. Nothing in the arrangement depends on a person's ancestry, his language, or his position, which is what makes "all of you" such a strange and generous word in a story about an expelled household.

**A WEEK WITHOUT THE TWO WEIGHTS**

A reader who wants to put this sentence to work can ask what his week would look like if the two weights were actually gone. The fear of what may come is usually carried in the form of hoarding and hustling, and the grief over what has passed is usually carried as resentment or as a story told about oneself. Both of them occupy a great deal of room, and both of them are named in the promise as things a follower of guidance does not have to keep. The Book does not ask a person to pretend that the losses did not happen. It asks him to follow the guidance, and it says the following is what makes the difference. The exercise is to take one recurring fear and one recurring grief and to trace each of them back to a decision that was made without reference to the guidance that has arrived. That tracing is not an accusation, and the verse does not present it as one. It presents the promise first, in the sentence spoken as the household left the garden, and it leaves the reader to notice that the offer has been standing ever since.
