**THE PARADOX OF RETALIATION**

At the paradox of qisās, attention falls on ***“There is ˹security of˺ life”***. The statement there is life for you in legal retribution is concise and profound. At first, people may imagine that responding to death with a death only multiplies loss. The Qur’an explains that measured, lawful accountability protects the living. When society knows that intentional murder has a serious and proportionate consequence, potential killers are restrained, victims’ families are given a recognized path to justice, and private revenge is less likely to spread. It means a proportionate response, not uncontrolled vengeance. The law follows the act without exceeding it. This balance protects the sanctity of life while acknowledging that a community that refuses to confront murder may allow more lives to be destroyed. The verse’s public benefit depends upon justice in application. A punishment imposed on the wrong person, on weak evidence, or through a corrupt process does not produce life; it produces another injustice. The accused has rights, the victim’s family has rights, and society has a right to safety. Qualified judges must establish guilt and distinguish intentional killing from accident, self-defence, coercion, and other circumstances. The verse cannot be used as permission for mobs, families, militias, or individuals to execute people. Private retaliation often kills the innocent and turns one crime into a feud. Islamic legal authority exists precisely to prevent emotions and tribal power from controlling life and death. (2:178 — **“a free man for a free man, a slave”**) Because the paradox of retaliation governs 2:179, mercy and prevention arises from the paradox of retaliation. It follows that mindfulness as the goal completes the paradox of retaliation through mercy and prevention.

**MERCY AND PREVENTION**

Mercy and prevention draws together ***“for you in ˹the law of˺ retaliation, O people of reason”***. The previous āyah calls this an alleviation and mercy. The Qur’an’s objective is not a culture of death; it is a society in which life is so protected that murder carries a measured and unavoidable accountability. Modern discussions sometimes focus only on the punishment and overlook prevention. The verse also requires addressing conditions that contribute to violence: injustice, impunity, corruption, lack of protection, and the easy availability of means of harm. A community that seeks *life* must protect the vulnerable before a murder occurs and support families afterward. The appeal to *ūlī al-albāb*—people possessing sound understanding—shows that the wisdom of the law requires reflection. Superficial emotion may see only revenge; deeper understanding sees protection and mercy. These people look beyond immediate sentiment to consequences, rights, and the preservation of social order. Sound understanding also avoids the opposite superficiality: using the verse as a slogan without learning its conditions. The people of understanding study the whole Qur’an, the Sunnah, the legal system, and the moral objectives of justice. (5:32 — **“if they killed all of humanity”**) The reason mindfulness as the goal follows mercy and prevention lies in the paradox of retaliation. Therefore, mindfulness as the goal returns practical force to mercy and prevention.

**MINDFULNESS AS THE GOAL**

***“so that you may become mindful ˹of Allah˺”*** mark the turn toward taqwā as the goal. The purpose is so that you may become mindful—*laʿallakum tattaqūn*. Legal accountability should cultivate *mindfulness*, awareness of Allah that restrains wrongdoing even when human law cannot see. A society needs courts, evidence, and enforcement, but it also needs hearts that fear Allah, value life, and refuse violence in private. Mindfulness affects every stage: the potential killer restrains himself; witnesses tell the truth; investigators refuse torture; judges resist bribery; heirs do not demand unlawful revenge; officials protect the accused from abuse; and the public does not celebrate cruelty. Without mindfulness, even a formally correct rule can be corrupted by power. In many cases, a victim’s family may pardon or accept compensation, and such mercy can heal wounds when it is voluntary and safe. The law protects the right to seek retribution while also honouring the right to forgive. Life is served by both accountability and reconciliation, according to the circumstances. Forgiveness should not be forced upon families or used to protect powerful offenders. Nor should a family that seeks justice be shamed as lacking mercy. The verse presents a lawful balance in which the rights of all parties are recognized and Allah remains the final judge of intentions. A strong railing on a high bridge restricts movement at the edge precisely because it protects movement across the bridge. No sensible traveller calls the barrier an enemy of freedom after seeing the drop below. Just retaliation is presented in that protective way: its measured certainty deters the first killing and prevents revenge from multiplying it. For societies today, the claim requires both seriousness about murder and seriousness about due proof, equal standing, and limits, because an arbitrary penalty cannot preserve the life the verse addresses. An explanation transmitted from al-Suddī through Abū Mālik takes the promised life to include people being held back from killing one another, which makes deterrence part of the verse’s own moral logic. (17:33 — **“Do not take a ˹human˺ life”**) In other words, mindfulness as the goal answers the paradox of retaliation.
