**TWO WORDS GIVEN IN REPLY TO A COVENANT**

The pledge is renewed and the reply is reported without softening: ***“And when We took your covenant”***, ***“and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to”***, ***“that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you”***, ***“and obey,” they answered, “We hear”***, ***“and disobey.” The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts”***. The answer is a sentence that sounds like acceptance and ends in refusal, and the Book quotes it whole because the shape of the sentence is the shape of the community's condition. The command had asked for two things, holding and obeying, and the reply keeps the first half and discards the second. The same words are recorded again in a later sūrah as a saying of this people, which shows that the phrase was remembered as part of their own speech rather than invented by an accuser: (4:46 — **“Some Jews take words out of context and say, “We listen”**).

**FOUR WORDS AND WHAT THEY CONCEDE**

The reply is remarkable for its honesty, since a community that meant to deceive would have said only the first word. What the Book reports is a group of people who know exactly what is being asked and who state their position in the plainest available form. The early teachers read the pair as an admission that the mountain compelled an outward agreement rather than an inward one, so that the second word explained the first. That reading fits the scene recounted above, where the mountain was held overhead and the agreement was extracted under a weight. The verse therefore records a decision taken in full view of the terms, and the reader who has watched a person agree to something while making clear he will not do it has the same transaction in front of him.

**A HEART IN WHICH THE CALF HAD TAKEN ROOT**

The clause that follows explains the answer, and it uses the language of growth: ***“The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts”***, ***“because of their disbelief”***. The image is of something planted that has spread, and the Book dates the planting to the episode in the wilderness when the image was made and worshipped. A later passage describes the consequence in the same vocabulary, connecting the covenant-breaking with a hardening that followed it: (5:13 — **“But for breaking their covenant We condemned them”**). The two answers were therefore consistent with each other, since a heart occupied by the calf had no room for the command that forbade it. The order of the two words matters: love of the calf is given as a result of disbelief, so the sequence runs from refusal to affection, not the reverse.

**A CLOSING ADDRESS TO THE CLAIMANT**

The same charge, of a covenant honoured in words while the messengers who carried it were killed, is laid elsewhere in exactly those terms: (5:70 — **“Indeed, We took a covenant from the Children of Israel and sent them messengers.”**). The sentences here end with an address that turns the community's own claim against it: ***“because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do”***, ***“if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!””*** The claim is not denied; it is examined, and the examination is done by the community's own conduct. If the scripture in their hands was what they said it was, the conduct it would prompt should look different from the killing of prophets and the worship of a calf. A reader who has heard a claim of loyalty measured against the fruit it produces will recognise the method, and the Book uses it here without a single additional argument. The question is the last word of the verse, and it is left where the reader can pick it up.

**AN APPLICATION THAT ANY COMMUNITY CAN RUN**

The passage is not only about one people's history, since the sentence it examines is available to anyone: agreement in words while the intention goes the other way. Think of an employee who accepts a policy in a meeting and describes the same policy as absurd over lunch, or a congregation that recites the creed and treats the obligations that follow as optional. The Book's report of this answer is valuable precisely because the answer is so short, and short sentences reveal what longer ones conceal. A person can take the last occasion on which he said that he had heard a command and ask whether the second half of that sentence was also true of him. What the verse records is what such a test looks like when it is conducted in the open.

**WHENCE THE CONFIDENCE CAME**

The community's confidence in its own position is the last thing to notice here, since the claim about the Torah is made by people whose conduct is being described in the same verse. The Book treats that confidence as a symptom rather than a strength, and it does so because the claim had become a shield for the conduct. The chapter's earlier verses have shown the same arrangement in other forms, where a doctrine about a short punishment made repentance unnecessary and a claim about an exclusive future supported a comfortable present. What this verse adds is the mechanism: a person can hold a scripture in his hands and let it stand for the obedience he has not given. The Book's question interrupts that arrangement by asking what the belief actually prompts, and the answer is left for the reader to supply.
