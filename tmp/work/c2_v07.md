**A STAMP ON THE VESSEL THAT CARRIES KNOWLEDGE**
The first half of the verse names an act of God upon the organ that decides: ***“Allah has sealed their hearts”***. The earliest explanations take the seal as a stamp rather than a lid, and that picture is worth holding, because the heart carries what it knows the way a jar carries water, and a stamp is set on the thing that holds. A lid, by contrast, belongs to a door, and a door can be opened from the outside by anyone with a key. The distinction keeps the reader from imagining a lock fitted by a stranger. What is closed here is the place where understanding is kept, and the reports from Mujāhid and Qatādah carry the same sense: the sealed heart no longer understands, and the closing followed a loyalty those people had already given away. A report from Qatādah says that when they obeyed the devil, the devil took charge of them, and the seal came after. Read that way, the sentence describes an ending rather than a beginning.
**THE EAR THAT STOPS ANSWERING FIRST**
The verse turns next to the ear and then to the eye, and the order is not accidental, since a message reaches a person through hearing before it reaches him through sight. The Book names the same loss in another place as it describes a people whose ears are stopped, (17:46 — **“and deafness in their ears”**). A man who stops answering his door stops hearing the bell eventually, because the habit of refusal trains the sense that carries the news. The verse is careful with its wording at this point, since the ear is not said to be destroyed but to be shut with the rest, and a shut ear can still register noise. What it loses is the ability to be moved by what it hears, and that ability is the only part of hearing that ever mattered in this matter. Anyone who has watched a person sit through a warning and rise unmoved has seen the condition the verse describes, and the condition belongs to the whole person rather than to one organ.
**A COVER LAID OVER THE EYE**
The rest of the sentence gathers the remaining faculties and names the consequence: ***“and their hearing, and their sight is covered. They will suffer a tremendous punishment”***. A cover, again, is gentler than a blow. The eye still works, the world still arrives, and the sign inside the world is missed, because the covering is laid between the man and the meaning of what he looks at rather than between his eye and the object. Another passage lists the same three faculties with the ear first and the cover named plainly, (45:23 — **“sealed their hearing and hearts, and placed a cover on their sight”**), and an earlier sūrah describes a veil laid over hearts while the ear is stopped, (18:57 — **“We have certainly cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend this”**). The sentence closes with a punishment described as tremendous, and the word is placed where the faculties were, which tells the reader what the punishment answers. It answers a life spent refusing what those faculties were built to receive, and its size is measured against the gift that was spoiled.
**THE HUMAN STEP COMES FIRST**
Linking the two halves of the verse gives the reader a warning rather than a mystery. The Book states the order of events in more than one place, and in each of them the human step comes first, (61:5 — **“So when they ˹persistently˺ deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate”**), and the principle behind that order is spelled out in another sūrah, (13:11 — **“Indeed, Allah would never change a people’s state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state”**). Nothing here reads as an arbitrary sentence passed on an innocent man. It reads as the harvest of a field that was left unwatered, and the reader who fears the outcome has been handed the remedy in the same breath, since the faculties named in the verse are the ones he still has today. A young man who spends his evenings feeding his ear on mockery should not be surprised when the same ear stops warming to the call, because the senses follow what they are given. The verse warns him in time, and that is what a warning is for.
**FOUR GATES TO KEEP**
Four habits follow from this verse for anyone who takes it seriously. The first is to keep the ear busy with what it was made to receive, since the Book repeats the warning about hearing wherever it describes a wasted faculty, (7:179 — **“ears they do not hear with”**). The second is to act on what is heard quickly, because understanding that is never used goes the way of an unused muscle. The third is to keep company that speaks plainly, since a man's ear takes its habits from the room he sits in. The fourth is to keep asking, and to keep asking again, because the door this passage describes shuts on the person who has stopped asking. A reader can test that in his own home today, and the test costs him nothing but attention.
