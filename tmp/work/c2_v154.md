**A SENTENCE THE TONGUE MAY NOT REPEAT**

The verse takes up a sentence that people were repeating about their own dead and forbids it: ***“Never say that those martyred in the cause of Allah are dead”***. The prohibition is directed at the tongue first, because that is where the harm begins, but it reaches the belief behind the tongue as well, since a man who is convinced of the finality of such a death will speak of it in the same way. The occasion reported for the verse is the talk that followed the first battle, when the loss of fourteen men, six of the emigrants and eight of the helpers, was repeated as though those men had simply been extinguished. The explanation credited to Ibn ʿAbbās traces the talk to people who wished to discourage the believers by dwelling on what the dead had lost. The verse therefore does not forbid grief; it forbids a way of describing the dead that makes their sacrifice look like a subtraction.

**THE LIFE THAT THE SENTENCE ASSERTS**

Against that description the verse sets a plain assertion: ***“in fact, they are alive!”***. The life intended is not a metaphor and not a memory kept by the community, since the Book places it with their Lord and describes them as provided for, which is a state and not a reputation. A later sūrah states the same truth with an addition that answers the question left open here: (3:169 — **“Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead. In fact, they are alive with their Lord, well provided for—”**). The difference between the two statements is the difference between being told that they live and being told where and how they live, and the community is given the second only after it has accepted the first. What the verse corrects, then, is not an opinion about the afterlife in general but a way of speaking about a particular group of people.

**THE STATE THAT THE EYE CANNOT REACH**

The verse ends by naming the limit of ordinary perception: ***“But you do not perceive it”***. Their condition is not hidden because it is doubtful but because the senses are not built to reach it, and the reports that describe the spirits of the slain as birds moving freely in the Garden are attempts to give the community some picture of a state it cannot inspect. The difference between them and other dead is therefore a difference in what they are given, not in what can be seen at a grave. Nothing in the sentence asks the believers to treat death as unreal; it asks them to stop treating it as the end of the account. The verse leaves the matter with God and leaves the community with a corrected tongue. Among the matters of the unseen that lie beyond inspection, this condition is placed in the treatment found in Ibn Kathīr, and the believer is asked to accept what has come down rather than to imagine it.

**A VILLAGE THAT GIVES A SOLDIER UP FOR DEAD**

Think of a village that gives up a young man for dead because no letter has reached it from the distant country where he serves, and of the emptiness that settles on the household once the neighbours start saying that he is gone. The report is not malicious in most mouths, yet it changes how the family lives, and it would be overturned in a moment by a single letter. The verse intervenes at exactly that point, since the report of the community about its own dead was shaping its courage in the field. Anyone who has watched a rumour of loss do more damage than the loss itself will understand why the sentence is addressed to speech. What the verse supplies is the letter that the village did not have.

**A CORRECTION THAT STEADIES A COMMUNITY**

Read with the verses before it, the sentence belongs to the training by which the community is prepared for what it will face. The command to seek help through patience and prayer is followed at once by a correction of the way its losses are described, which suggests that words are part of the equipment. An army that counts its dead as annihilated will not stand for long, and the same is true of a community, so the verse removes the despair at its source. The Book returns to the theme of the slain in several places, and it always returns with the same correction and the same promise. The passage therefore teaches the believers both how to endure and how to speak while they endure.
