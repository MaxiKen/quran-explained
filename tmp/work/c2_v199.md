**STANDING WITH EVERYONE**

The order of the phrases is itself the lesson of the pilgrim's last hour: ***“Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims.”***, ***“And seek Allah’s forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. The walk performs the pilgrim's last hour in order: the descent with the crowd and the speech of repentance together, and the order is the rite itself. The emptying plain is the deadline, and the verse keeps the clock visible to the crowd. The custom described by Ibn ʿAbbās is what the first phrases correct. The crowd's movement and the private speech of repentance are joined in the verse so tightly that neither can be performed without the other on that day. The joining is the pilgrim's last instruction.

Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. is a one-sentence correction of a tribal habit. The Quraysh used to stand at Muzdalifa and refuse to descend with the other people, and the verse commands the descent with the multitude as one body. The wording recorded by Ibn ʿAbbās keeps the direction clear: go forth from where the people go forth — the people of Yemen and the rest returning together. The command is about posture as well as geography: the pilgrim stands with the crowd rather than above it. Because the rite is designed for the assembled multitude, the exclusivity of the descent was a violation of the assembly's meaning. The verse restores the pilgrim to the human race at the very moment of the worship.

**A TRIBE'S DISTANCE**

The custom being corrected is a form of religious class — the tribe that stands apart at the rites — and the correction is a democratization of the worship. The descent ordered with the people is the rite's way of erasing the distance the tribal mind loves. Because the order is addressed to those who had made a custom of the distance, the verse treats custom as a candidate for correction. The application is to look for the modern versions of the Muzdalifa habit: the rites kept for the family's prestige, the charity kept inside the circle, the mosque kept for the clan. The one who descends with the crowd learns the humility the plain was designed to teach. The verse's road is the crowded one. The crowd is the point: the plain collects the proud and the humble in one garment. The descent with the multitude is therefore a rehearsal of the equality the rites exist to teach, and the rehearsal is performed with the feet. Because the order is given to the tribe that stood apart, the verse's sharpest edge is aimed at religious prestige. The application is to descend from one's own high ground with the same speed the Quraysh were ordered to descend from theirs.

**FORGIVENESS AS THE EXIT**

The second command of the verse is interior where the first was public: And seek Allah's forgiveness. The rite's conclusion is a prayer rather than a ceremony, and the prayer is aimed at the One who forgives the failure of the rites themselves. On Ibn ʿAbbās's reading, the forgiveness is commanded after the descent so that the pilgrim leaves the plain with a clean account. The pairing of the two acts — the descent with the people and the seeking of forgiveness — is the pilgrim's final posture. Because the forgiveness is sought at the exit, the journey's failures are settled before the traveler reaches home. The application is to end the rites where this verse ends them: among the people and in the speech of repentance.

**MERCY AT THE END OF RITES**

The closing names — Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. — guarantee the answer to the verse's own command. The seeker of forgiveness is met by the names that promise the forgiveness he asks, and the passage closes like a completed circuit. The promise appears in the wider legislation of the return, where the same forgiveness and bounty are offered to the one who fears the stinginess the road tempts him toward (2:268 — **“while Allah promises you forgiveness and ˹great˺ bounties from Him. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing”**). Because the names follow the command, the pilgrim's last word on the plain is answered before it is spoken. The application is to trust the answer: to descend, to ask, and to leave with the names in the ears. The rites end in mercy because mercy designed them. The verse's pairing of the public descent and the private prayer is worth carrying into every gathering the reader leaves. The crowd and the conscience are settled in one motion, and the motion is the pilgrim's last rite. Because the forgiveness is promised by name, the exit from the plain is an exit into mercy, and the traveler carries the names home as the journey's final provision.
