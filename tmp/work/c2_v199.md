**LEAVING THE OLD PRIDE**

Leaving the old pride takes shape in ***“Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims.”***. The verse instructs the pilgrims to depart from where the people depart. Classical exegetes connect this to the Quraysh and allied groups who, before Islam, distinguished themselves from the rest of the pilgrims by refusing to stand at ʿArafāt, stopping instead at Muzdalifah because of their claimed special status as people of the sanctuary. Allah removed this aristocratic boundary and commanded them to join the wider human assembly. The lesson is larger than that particular custom. Sacred worship is not a stage for inherited privilege. The wealthy, powerful, local, foreign, learned, and ordinary all stand as servants before Allah. A tribe’s closeness to the Kaʿbah, a family’s religious reputation, or a person’s wealth does not give him permission to invent a superior route through the rites. *Where the people depart* also teaches conformity to the established communal rite. Hajj is not redesigned around personal symbolism. The Prophet ﷺ said, *Take your rites from me,* and demonstrated when to stand, depart, pray, stay, and move. The pilgrim learns from the Sunnah and qualified scholarship rather than making unusual practice a badge of spiritual distinction. Following the people here does not mean following a crowd into error. It means joining the rightly established rite instead of preserving an arrogant pre-Islamic exception. A Muslim avoids both blind conformity and self-important innovation. (2:197 — **“surely the best provision is righteousness.”**) Because leaving the old pride governs 2:199, seeking forgiveness at the end of the rite arises from leaving the old pride. It follows that mercy after imperfection completes leaving the old pride through seeking forgiveness at the end of the rite.

**SEEKING FORGIVENESS AT THE END OF THE RITE**

The wording for seeking forgiveness at the end of the rite is ***“And seek Allah’s forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. After the standing at ʿArafāt and the departure, the pilgrims are told to seek Allah’s forgiveness—seek God’s forgiveness. Even after a major act of worship, the servant does not assume that his performance is perfect or that he is entitled to reward. He recognizes shortcomings in intention, attention, patience, and treatment of others. Seeking forgiveness is not an admission that Hajj was worthless. It is an expression of humility and a request that Allah cover faults, accept the worship, and allow its lessons to continue. The Prophet ﷺ would seek forgiveness after prayer, and the Qur’an instructs him to glorify and seek forgiveness after the completion of his mission. The command to join the people and seek forgiveness links social humility with spiritual humility. A person cannot claim closeness to Allah while looking down on the crowd. Hajj exposes the illusion of status: all pilgrims need water, shade, guidance, and mercy. The one who pushes the weak or treats service workers with contempt has contradicted the communal lesson of the rite. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah does not look at bodies or wealth but at hearts and deeds, which appears in Muslim’s Ṣaḥīḥ collection. The pilgrimage garment and gathering remind people that their true rank is determined by mindfulness, not by passport, family, or income. (49:13 — **“O humanity! Indeed, We created you from a male”**) The reason mercy after imperfection follows seeking forgiveness at the end of the rite lies in leaving the old pride. Therefore, mercy after imperfection returns practical force to seeking forgiveness at the end of the rite.

**MERCY AFTER IMPERFECTION**

Allah is *All-Forgiving* and *Most Merciful*. The pilgrim may have made mistakes, lost concentration, argued, or struggled with the logistics of the journey. He repents, repairs harm, and hopes in Allah’s forgiveness. Mercy is not a reason to repeat avoidable wrongdoing, but it prevents one error from turning into despair. A community should help pilgrims learn rather than shame them for honest mistakes. If a serious legal violation occurs, the person seeks qualified advice about its remedy. He does not conceal harm behind the claim that Hajj is already complete. The departure from the sacred places is also a test. A pilgrim may feel spiritual emotion in Makkah and then return to the old habits that harmed his family, employees, or neighbours. The command to seek forgiveness invites a continuing relationship with Allah after the journey. The accepted Hajj should leave a person more humble, truthful, and just. The real proof is not the souvenir or title but the changed conduct. A person who leaves the sanctuary with less pride and more mercy has understood the verse’s direction. It is like a procession that admits every group through the same gate corrects the pride of those who once demanded a private entrance. The command to depart with the people places privileged ancestry back inside the common human crowd, and the command for forgiveness keeps even correct ritual from becoming a claim of perfection. Present-day status can create its own special routes through religious life—wealth, passport, office, or lineage—but the verse joins worshippers in one movement and one need for pardon. (53:32 — **“So do not ˹falsely˺ elevate yourselves. He knows best”**) In other words, mercy after imperfection answers leaving the old pride.
