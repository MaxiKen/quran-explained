**PHASES IN THE SKY**

From first phrase to last, the answer moves from the sky to the doorway: ***“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, “They are a means”***, ***“for people to determine time”***, ***“and pilgrimage.” Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors. Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺.”***, ***“So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors”***, ***“and be mindful of Allah”***, ***“so you may be successful”***. The order of the walk is itself the argument: the phases are demoted to a calendar before the doorway is commanded, so the reader carries no omen to the door. The reader who walks these phrases leaves with two commands and one definition to live by. Ibn ʿAbbās's reading of the moon-question follows the same route.

They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. records a question about irregularity: the moon thins, swells, and vanishes, and the questioners want to know what the irregularity means. The answer removes the omen and installs the instrument: Say, They are a means for people to determine time and pilgrimage. The phases are a clock and a calendar, not a message about anyone's fortune. The sun and the moon run by measure everywhere the Qur'an describes them, and the measuring is the point (10:5 — **“He is the One Who made the sun a radiant source and the moon a reflected light, with precisely ordained phases,”**). Because the question came from a people who read irregularity as portent, the reply is a liberation: the sky keeps time and the believer keeps his deeds. The moon is demoted from oracle to clock, and the demotion is a mercy.

**TWO DOORS OPEN**

The verse then pivots to a practice of the questioners themselves: they entered their houses from the back doors during the ritual state, treating the front door as ritually charged. The reply dismantles the superstition at its root — the ritual state does not reverse architecture — and the command is stated plainly: So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors. The practice shows how a piety of taboos grows: a prohibition nobody legislated becomes a custom, and the custom becomes a test of devotion. The verse answers the whole trajectory by pointing to the doorway of the house and saying, in effect, that the pious person enters normally. The cosmos and the household are governed by one law, and the law is not startled by a door. Because the superstition is corrected alongside the moon-question, both are examples of one error: reading divine meaning into things God has made functional.

**PIETY IS NOT A RITUAL WALK**

The verse's definition is among the most quoted in the chapter: righteousness is not the back-door entrance but the character described after it. The ritual gesture is displaced by the interior disposition — mindfulness — and the displacement is complete enough to change the layout of the house. The companion tradition preserved in al-Bukhārī warns against inventing rituals the law never legislated, and the back-door practice is exactly such an invention. The piety of taboos flatters its practitioner because the taboos are visible; the piety of mindfulness is quieter and harder. Because the verse keeps the definition negative first — righteousness is not this — the reader learns to suspect his own theatrical habits before trusting them. The test of a ritual is whether it makes the person kinder at the door he walks through every day.

**ENTERING WITH INTENTION**

The close gathers the passage into one instruction: and be mindful of Allah so you may be successful. The mindfulness named at the end is the same element the fasting legislation names as its purpose, and the consistency is the reader's map. The application is to enter — the house, the month, the marketplace — as one person, with the same face at the door and behind it. The phases keep the calendar, the doors keep the house, and the mindfulness keeps the person; the three lines of the verse are one system. Because success is attached to mindfulness rather than to ritual dexterity, the poorest practitioner can be the most successful. The moon does not know your name; the One who set its phases does. The verse's two answers therefore share one theology: the sky is made functional and the person is made moral. The reader who consults the phases for his calendar is doing exactly what the verse teaches, and the reader who consults them for his fortune has wandered into the error the verse corrects. Because the correction arrives as a command about doors, the superstition is defeated by the ordinary — the most reliable weapon the revelation owns. The same weapon answers the modern superstitions that dress themselves in the language of science while reading the sky for luck. The verse sends the reader back to the clock and the conscience. The two returns are the verse's whole method: the sky is demoted to a clock so that the conscience can be promoted to the judge.
