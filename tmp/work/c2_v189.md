**THE MOON AS A MEASURE OF TIME**

The moon as a measure of time begins from ***“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the phases of the moon. Say, “They are a means”***, ***“for people to determine time”***. The people asked the Prophet ﷺ about the changing shapes of the moon. The answer directs attention to its practical purpose: the lunar phases are measures of time—*appointed times*—for people and for Hajj. The new moon marks the beginning of a lunar month, and its phases provide a visible calendar for fasting, pilgrimage, waiting periods, contracts, and other acts of worship. The verse does not reject scientific curiosity about the moon’s physical causes. It answers the question according to the guidance people most need: how the signs of creation regulate human life and worship. The Qur’an frequently invites observation, but it also reminds people that knowledge should serve gratitude and obedience rather than vanity. The Islamic calendar is tied to lunar observation. Ramadan, Hajj, festival, and the waiting period of a divorced or widowed woman are connected to months and phases. This allows people in different climates and nations to share a ritual calendar without depending upon one political empire or a single agricultural season. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the month begins with sighting the crescent and that if it is obscured, the preceding month is completed as thirty days, in the authentic reports of the authentic records preserved separately by al-Bukhārī and Muslim. Scholars have discussed the role of calculation, regional sightings, and communal authority. The verse’s purpose is unity and reliable timekeeping, not endless conflict over methods. (9:36 — **“So do not wrong one another during these months.”**) Because the moon as a measure of time governs 2:189, a strange custom corrected arises from the moon as a measure of time. **A STRANGE CUSTOM CORRECTED**

***“and pilgrimage.” Righteousness is not in entering your houses from the back doors. Rather, righteousness is to be mindful ˹of Allah˺.”***, ***“So enter your homes through their ˹proper˺ doors”*** establish a strange custom corrected. The verse then mentions a custom of entering houses from their backs during pilgrimage or after entering consecration. Reports from early Muslims explain that some people regarded this as a sign of piety. Allah corrects the superstition: righteousness is not found in an invented ritual. *Enter houses through their doors* restores ordinary good sense and lawful practice. The lesson is broader than the physical doorway. People often attach holiness to unnecessary difficulty, unusual clothing, secret formulas, or inherited gestures while neglecting justice and obedience. Revelation distinguishes genuine worship from religious performance that has no evidence. The command to use doors also carries an ethical meaning. A person should approach matters through their proper means: knowledge through learning, reconciliation through honest conversation, wealth through lawful work, and private spaces through permission. Trying to enter through the back can symbolize evasion, intrusion, manipulation, or seeking a result without accepting the lawful process. The Prophet ﷺ taught that asking permission is required before entering homes and that a person should not look inside when permission is refused, as recorded in the authentic records preserved separately by al-Bukhārī and Muslim. Physical privacy and moral transparency are both protected by the principle. **TAQWĀ IS THE SUBSTANCE OF RIGHTEOUSNESS**

For taqwā is the substance of righteousness, the decisive wording is ***“and be mindful of Allah”***, ***“so you may be successful”***. Righteousness is the way of one who is mindful of Allah. The verse echoes 2:177, where *birr* was defined through faith, charity, prayer, promises, and patience. Here, the same term corrects an outward custom. Mindfulness is not a theatrical display of hardship; it is protecting oneself from Allah’s displeasure through truthful obedience. A Muslim should therefore ask whether a practice has evidence and whether it produces justice, humility, and mercy. If a person enters through the door but lies, steals, or violates privacy, he has observed the outer instruction while missing its moral purpose. The physical and spiritual meanings should support each other. The question about the moon may also teach that people’s questions are sometimes redirected toward their deeper need. Human beings ask about appearance, measurement, and unusual phenomena; revelation answers with worship, time, and accountability. This does not belittle the questioner. It teaches him to connect information with purpose. Muslim educators can follow this method by answering clearly and then showing how knowledge affects faith and action. The Qur’an is not merely a storehouse of facts. It is guidance for the person who wants to live well. The lunar phases are like the marks on a public ruler: they make time measurable for ordinary people without requiring a private mystery. Entering a house through its door gives the same lesson in conduct—use the purpose God gave, not an invented display of difficulty. Present-day religion can still prize impressive detours, obscure rituals, or superstition over direct obedience. The verse joins sky and doorway to teach that mindfulness is practical, intelligible, and willing to abandon customs that merely look devout. (36:39 — **“As for the moon, We have ordained ˹precise˺ phases”**) In other words, taqwā is the substance of righteousness answers the moon as a measure of time.
