**THE OPEN-AIR CATALOGUE**

***“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens”*** begins a catalogue that never leaves the observable world. The verse moves through ***“and the earth”*** and into ***“the alternation of the day”***, ***“and the night”***, then turns to human commerce itself with ***“the ships that sail the sea”*** and their cargo, ***“for the benefit of humanity”***. Nothing in the list is hidden or rare, which means the demand for one spectacular sign is answered by an inventory of spectacles already in every eye. The catalogue reads as an answer to a demand for proof, and the answer is given in kind: the proof asked for is the proof already standing in the open air. The demand for the exotic is answered with the essential.

**SIGNS STACKED IN ORDER**

The arrangement walks from the largest frame down to the household table. As Ibn ʿAbbās reports, the alternation of night and day includes their increase and decrease through the seasons, so even time is shown to be administered rather than automatic. After the ships the verse turns skyward with ***“the rain sent down by Allah from the skies, reviving the earth after its death”***, and then to the population of the revived land with ***“the scattering of all kinds of creatures throughout”***. The earth needs the rain, the rain wakes the soil, the soil spreads the creatures, and the system is kept running by ***“the shifting of the winds”*** and ***“and the clouds drifting between the heavens”*** and ***“and the earth”***. The summary is compressed into ***“˹in all of this˺ are surely signs”*** for ***“for people of understanding”***. Comprehension is the response the signs require, and the verse names the reader's task as plainly as it names the signs. (31:31 — **“Do you not see that the ships sail ˹smoothly˺ through the sea by the grace of Allah”**)

**WHY NO SINGLE MIRACLE**

A hearer may ask why no single miracle is granted instead of this long inventory. The objection assumes that one miracle would be more persuasive, but a wonder is witnessed once while a sign is read daily. The catalogue functions so that nobody stands outside the evidence; the alternation of day and night witnesses at every moment, and the loaded ship witnesses at every port. Because each item is ordinary, the excuse of rarity is removed; because each item is essential, the excuse of irrelevance is removed as well. The reader who cannot find a sign in the ship's cargo or the rain's return would not find one in a splitting moon either, because the problem would be the eye and not the evidence. This is the difference between an event that startles a crowd and a system that educates a lifetime. The verse would rather teach than startle. The inventory also answers the demand for signs at the right scale, because the demand was for a private spectacle while the answer is a public system. A private spectacle can be denied by the witnesses who missed it; the alternation of night and day cannot be missed by anyone alive. The catalogue therefore shifts the question from the availability of proof to the disposition of the reader, which is the only variable the verse leaves open. The signs themselves are unanimous, and the comprehension named at the close is the reader's contribution.

**READING THE ORDINARY**

The dead ground is drawn as evidence in its own right in (36:33 — **“There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat”**). Ibn ʿAbbās transmits the note that the clouds are subservient between heaven and earth, moving wherever the command sends them, and that the winds change direction by the same ordinance — sometimes carrying torment and sometimes mercy. The application is practical: look at the ordinary with the attention others reserve for the extraordinary, because the ordinary is where the administration of the One is most continuously visible. The signs were never hidden; what changes is the eye that reads them. The verse trains the eye rather than supplying the missing object. The comprehension the verse names is available to anyone willing to think through what they already see. The verse leaves the willing reader with no missing evidence and the unwilling reader with no available excuse.
