**A PRAYER TAUGHT BY THE ONE IT ASKS**

The sūrah has praised, described and declared; now it asks, and the thing it asks for is guidance: ***“Guide us along the Straight Path”***. Nothing is mentioned about food, health or victory, and this is deliberate, since the reader is being shown what a person should want first. A road is worthless to a traveller who cannot find it, whatever supplies he carries, and the request assumes that the destination matters more than the luggage. Notice also who is speaking: the worshipper who has just said that worship and help belong to God alone now asks for something only God can give. Every earlier line was building the posture that makes this request possible, and the request is the first thing done in that posture.

The word translated as guidance is not a single act but a course of leading. It begins with the general direction given to everything created, continues with the explanation of right and wrong that reaches a person through revelation and conscience, and reaches its highest point when God fixes a person on the road and keeps him there. The prophets themselves are described as guided and as guiding others, so the request cannot be about religious information alone. A person may know the way and still not walk it, which is why the prayer has to be made by everyone, including those who are already far along: what is asked is that the walking continue, not only that the map be handed over.

**THE REQUEST IS ABOUT TOMORROW AS MUCH AS TODAY**

Nothing here suggests that guidance is settled once and put away. The line is said in every prayer, several times a day, by people who have already accepted the religion. The reason is plain when the request is read closely: the reader is asking to be guided in the hours that have not happened yet. The knowledge he has is not the same as the strength he will need when the temptation arrives, and today's certainty does not pay for tomorrow's decision. So the prayer keeps re-opening a matter that pride would prefer to consider closed. The Prophet ﷺ told his companions that this request is met with the answer *"My servant will have what he asks for,"* as Muslim carries it. If the request had to be made only once, a lifetime of repetition would make no sense.

Something else is worth noticing in the words chosen. The path is described as straight, and the picture behind the word is a road with no forks in it, clear from end to end. Anyone who has driven in an unfamiliar city at night knows the difference between a road and a turnoff: the first lets a person relax, and the second forces him to choose with the little light he has. Because the sūrah asks for the road rather than for a sign, it teaches the reader to want the whole route correct and not only the next step. The Qur'an explains the same thing elsewhere in a single sentence: (6:153 — **“Indeed, that is My Path—perfectly straight. So follow it and do not follow other ways, for”**). The crooked alternatives are there in the world, and the prayer is the answer to them.

**ONE VOICE STANDING FOR MANY**

The reader asks in the plural: guide us, not guide me. A person who prays only for himself can improve in isolation and remain useless to the people around him, but the opposite is not true, since asking for a group includes the one asking. The plural also keeps a small grammar of humility alive. The Book itself has guided people through other people: a teacher, a parent, a friend who mentioned something at the right moment. Asking on behalf of all of them acknowledges the road the reader actually travelled, which was never a private route. It also asks for the outcome rather than the ownership of it, and that is the difference between wanting to be right and wanting to arrive.

The practical weight of the line shows up when a person is certain about a matter and the certainty is wrong. History is full of sincere people who were zealous on a road that turned, and this verse is the reader's protection against joining them. Today a person can say it in the morning and then watch the day present the options the prayer was about: the shortcut that skips an obligation, the lie that saves face, the account that can be paid late. Guidance is not an idea in those moments; it is the choice made when nobody is watching and the answer is not obvious. The sūrah asks for the whole of that, and it asks first. Because the request is made before the day has shown its hand, it reaches the hours that have not been planned for, and those are the hours that decide most lives. Nobody can pray for a particular temptation in advance, but anyone can ask for the road before he reaches the fork.
