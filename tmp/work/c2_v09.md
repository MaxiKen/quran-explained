**A PLAN THAT NEVER LEAVES THE ROOM**
The verse describes an attempt before it reports its failure: ***“They seek to deceive Allah”***. The attempt is described with a verb whose root sense is hiding something away. The related noun names a hidden place, and the choice of that word fits a man who works in the dark and expects the dark to keep his confidence. What the sentence does not do is treat the attempt as harmless. This is why it states the plan plainly and follows it to the ledger where it will be written, and that ledger is not the one the planner had in mind. Picture a man who hides a banknote from his own accountant by slipping it into his own pocket; the manoeuvre is complete and the money is still gone. That is why the description of the act is exact. A man who hides unbelief inside a public confession is not merely mistaken about his standing; he is trying to run a transaction on a table he does not own.
**WHO IS COUNTED IN THE LEDGER**
The second half widens the target and then collapses it: ***“and the believers, yet they only deceive themselves, but they fail to perceive it”***. The deception is aimed at God and at the congregation, and the sentence finds it landing on the man who launched it. Another passage reports the identical attempt and gives the answer in three words of pure reversal, (4:142 — **“but He outwits them”**). God cannot be misled, and no believer can read a heart, so the only party left to fool is the man himself, and the Book says that is precisely what happens. The difference between a failed trick and a successful one is therefore the difference between a man who tried to cheat God and a man who cheated himself, and the second is the worse of the two. A trader who inflates his own ledger to impress a partner he cannot deceive ends up running his business on false numbers. The same traders of the sūrah turn up again with one face for the believers and another for their allies (2:14). The verse applies that logic to the soul. The deception is not a transaction with God that fails, but a transaction with himself that works, and that is why the hypocrite's loss is so complete.
**THE ONE PERSON THE TRICK MISSES**
The closing clause is not a punishment added at the end; it is the diagnosis that makes the rest intelligible, which is why the same wording ends the verse that follows this one (2:12). A man cannot guard against a flaw he does not believe he has, and the loss of that belief is the deepest part of his condition. Elsewhere the Book describes the same people at prayer, going through the motions for an audience and barely remembering God at all (4:142). The picture that leaves is a man fully occupied with an audience that went home hours ago. Picture a man tidying his room for a guest who cancelled and told him so in a message he never opened. The verse is severe with him because the alternative is worse. A person who never opens the message keeps the room, the guest and the mistake, and the whole of that ruin is contained in his not perceiving it.
**THE MIRROR A READER HOLDS**
Any reader can run the test this verse describes on himself today, and the price of it is a little honesty. Three questions do the work: does the face he presents to the community match the one he lives with at home, does he do in public what he would refuse unwatched, and would he keep the claim if it cost him money and friends. The Book is not squeamish about the answer, because it would rather wound a reader into repair than flatter him into ruin, and the repair begins the moment he stops defending the gap. A man who admits that his religion is thinner in private than in public has already stepped out of the condition the verse describes, since that confession requires exactly the perception the verse says is missing. That is why the mirror is the whole remedy here, and a person who looks into it honestly will not need to be told what to change.
