**THE ANSWER OF A CREATURE WHO KNOWS ITS LIMITS**

The angels answer, and the whole shape of their answer is a lesson in how to speak about oneself: ***“They replied, “Glory be to You!”***, and then ***“We have no knowledge except what You have taught us. You are truly the All-Knowing, All-Wise.””***. They begin by clearing their Lord of any shortcoming, and only then do they state their own position. The order matters. Before admitting a limit, they affirm the perfection of the one who set it, which removes any suggestion that the limit is a fault in the teacher. Then comes the admission itself, and it is expressed without embarrassment and without excuse, which is a harder combination than it sounds. The second sentence is not only humility; it is a definition. What the angels possess is described as taught knowledge, which means they hold it by gift rather than by right. That single word protects them from the confusion that has ruined better-equipped creatures, since a person who forgets that his knowledge was given will soon treat it as proof of his own importance. The Book elsewhere reports the same confession from other servants of God, and the wording runs in the same channel: everything a creature knows is a loan whose terms were set by the lender. The angels end by naming two attributes of the one they address, and both of them are exactly what the situation requires.

**THE PAIR OF NAMES THAT SETTLES THE MATTER**

The knowledge of God answers the question of who can test, and the wisdom of God answers the question of why the test was arranged as it was, because a fair examination needs both a competent examiner and a reason worth being examined for. The angels had wondered about the coming of a creature that would spill blood, and the answer they receive will never be a full blueprint. What they are given instead is the assurance that the one who arranges the examination is both fully informed and perfectly wise, which is enough for a creature. A reader who wants the whole plan explained before he consents to it will find the angels' reply unsatisfying, and he should notice that they did not find it so. The Book uses the same pair of names in other places where a person is asked to trust a decision he cannot see the end of, and the effect is the same each time. In one account of the creation, the answer given to the worry about bloodshed is precisely this sentence, and the readers of the verses have taken it as the model of how a believer responds to what he does not understand: honesty about his own limits, and confidence in the names of his Lord. The verse here adds nothing to that formula, and it needs nothing. It is a complete reply to a question that was never answered with information.

**SPEAKING ABOUT WHAT ONE DOES NOT KNOW**

The practical value of this verse is the discipline it teaches about the two most common failures in religious speech. The first is pretending to know what has not been given, whether by inventing an answer or by repeating a confident guess as though it were settled. The second is refusing to acknowledge what has plainly been given, which produces a false humility that is just another kind of performance. The angels do neither. They name what they have, credit it to its source, and stop. A person who manages that combination in a single conversation about God has understood the verse better than any explanation of it can convey. For a reader, the test sits in the next ordinary disagreement, where he can notice where he is speaking from knowledge and where from impression, and to say so out loud when the second is the case. The Book is not embarrassed by the sentence "I do not know". It places the words in the mouths of angels who stand in the presence of their Lord, and it treats the admission as part of their dignity rather than a defect in it. A reader who can say it in the right place has learned something the verse was written to teach.
