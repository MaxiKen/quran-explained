**HALF A PLEDGE AND A FULL PRICE**

The accusation arrives with a contrast, and every clause of it is a picture: ***“But here you are, killing each other”***, ***“and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin”***, ***“and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you”***, ***“as captives, you still ransom them”***, ***“though expelling them was unlawful”***, ***“for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture”***, ***“and reject the rest?”***. The same community that had pledged not to spill its own blood is doing it, and the same community that ransomed its captives is the one that put them in chains. The Book's charge is not that they kept nothing of the covenant; it is that they kept the clause that cost little and honoured them, and discarded the clause that protected the people they had decided to fight. The question that ends the sentence is the sharpest in the passage, and it exposes a way of handling a scripture that survives in every generation. The pledge they were breaking is recorded in the verse before this one: (2:84 — **“And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant”**).

**THE RANSOM THAT EXPOSED THE CONTRADICTION**

Ransoming captives was a recognised duty under the law given to this people, and the verse names it because the practice proves that the community still regarded the scripture as authoritative in the matters it liked. The Book's argument turns on that admission: a people who will pay money to free a captive from their own nation have already accepted the law that made him a brother, and cannot then claim that driving him out was permitted. The same inconsistency is described elsewhere in the sūrah where the community's leaders are shown treating part of what they were given as binding and part as negotiable: (5:44 — **“Indeed, We revealed the Torah, containing guidance and light,”**). What the verse reveals is a habit of selective obedience, and it is the habit rather than any single crime that the Book sets out to correct in this passage on the covenant.

**WHY SELECTIVE FAITH IS TREATED AS UNBELIEF**

The final question uses the language of belief and rejection, and the reader might wonder why a partial acceptance deserves that vocabulary. The answer is in the structure of the verse: the people were not accepting a set of rules and doubting others; they were using the rules they kept as evidence of their standing while breaking the ones that interfered. The Book treats that as a decision about who has authority over a person, and it says as much when it accuses them of rejecting what does not suit them. The same charge is laid in another passage where the object of the trade is named as the covenant itself: (3:77 — **“Indeed, those who trade Allah's covenant”**). A person can test the charge by looking for the command he has quietly granted an exemption and asking what that exemption is worth in the currency the Book uses.

**DISGRACE IN THIS LIFE, PUNISHMENT AFTER**

The verse does not leave the offence without a stated consequence, and it divides the consequence into two instalments. The first is disgrace in the present life, which the reports of history attach to the loss of a kingdom and the long condition of a community living under other people's rule; the Book states the same sentence for this people in a later sūrah: (3:112 — **“They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go,”**). The second is described with a superlative, and the Book places it after the first so that no reader assumes the whole account is settled in this world. The question that carries the sentence is stated in full: ***“Is there any reward”***, ***“for those who do”***, ***“so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life”***, ***“and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment?”***, and the closing clause seals the record: ***“For Allah is never unaware of what you do”***. The closing sentence confirms that the record is kept: God is never unaware of what they do. A reader who has watched a group claim a religious identity while treating its members badly will find that the two instalments are already visible in the first, and the verse names the second so that the account is not mistaken for a finished one.

**THE DRIVER WHO OBEYS ONLY THE CHEAP SIGNS**

A driver who obeys every sign he passes, since obeying them requires nothing, treats the speed limit as advice because it costs him time. The people addressed throughout this passage had the same relationship with their covenant: the parts it kept were the parts that gave it standing, and the parts it broke were the parts that would have constrained its conduct toward its own people. The Book's question exposes the arrangement without needing to add a punishment first, because the reader can see what the arrangement makes of the document. A person who has quietly excused himself from one command while keeping the rest can hear the question addressed to him, and the honest answer is that he has made himself the editor of the terms. The verse's charge is against that editorship, and it treats it as a rejection of the scripture rather than a negotiation with it.
