**A FIGHT FOUGHT FOR THE ALLY'S HONOR**

The opening pronoun of reproach was analyzed for its own grammar, because the shape of the address carries the accusation: ***“But here you are, killing each other”***, ***“and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin”***. The particle that introduces the sentence marks estrangement in the very occurrence and not delay in time. The thing is happening, and its happening is the shock. The emphatic pronoun places the house in the dock as its own defendant. The verb of mutual assistance carries an assimilated spelling and an unassimilated variant, and its range is the range of the charge. It means helping one another against them in sin, and the glosses split the sin into disobedience toward God and injustice toward people. The crime is thus an infringement of two rights at once: the right of God broken in the defiance, and the right of creatures broken in the pain. The geometry of the scene is what makes it infamous. Men of one household fought under the flag of a foreign ally against members of their own household, then returned to empty the houses of their own kin. The passage does not describe a war between religions. It describes a war in which religion was the loot.

**THE PARANTHESIS THE STATUTE HIDES**

The middle of the verse is built like a legal instrument, and the language specialists found its joints worth mapping: ***“and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you”***, ***“as captives, you still ransom them”***, ***“though expelling them was unlawful”***. The ransoming clause comes with two reading variants in the consonantal tradition, one on the captives and one on the act of delivering them with money. The glosses stress that this ransoming was itself one of the pledged articles. The forbidden-expulsion clause is then tied grammatically back to the expulsion verb, with the ransoming statement sitting between them as a parenthesis. The statute forbade the expulsion in exactly the same way it forbade abandoning the ransom. The practice's own logic is preserved in the tradition in the form of the questions the practitioners were asked. When they were asked why they fought men and then paid for their release, they answered that ransoming was commanded. When they were asked why they fought at all, they answered that they feared the humiliation of their allies. The exchange is the whole moral picture in miniature: piety in the account books, and honor in the battle line. The pattern of listening to scripture only when it speaks conveniently is drawn to the same scale elsewhere in the revelation: (4:46 — **“Some Jews take words out of context and say, “We listen and we disobey,” “Hear! May you never hear,””**).

**THE ALLIANCE MAP BEHIND THE CHARGE**

The historical map is given in the tradition with names, and it turns the general charge into a courtroom exhibit. The city's two clans had fought their old wars of the age of ignorance, and three Jewish factions had entered the alliance system. One faction allied to one clan, one to the other, and one held kinship with both. When the alliances flipped across generations, the combat itself crossed the community's own bloodlines. The men of one faction would fight beside their patrons against fighters who included their own people. They would kill their own and empty their homes at the rout. They would then buy back the prisoners of their own side when the fighting stopped. The disgrace this worldly life promised them is recorded in the tradition as having landed in their own later history in the form the sentence prescribes. A clan was slain after its allies had marched on it, and a clan was marched out of its houses into exile with the tax of submission imposed on the remainder. The particle of the sequence therefore does rhetorical work beyond chronology. It says: and it is you, of all people, who do this. For a house that reads the passage now, the alliance map is the warning against the oldest habit of party spirit: arranging the faith around the patron and then billing the faith for the war.

**THE HALF-BELIEF THE QUESTION NAMES**

The indictment then rises from the deed to the doctrine the deed implied: ***“for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture”***, ***“and reject the rest?”***. The earliest glosses hear two charges in the question, and both stand. The creed of the faithful names the same split-mind in its own terms: (4:150 — **“Surely those who deny Allah and His messengers and wish to make a distinction between Allah and His messengers, saying,”**). In the specific register, the believers-in-part are those who kept the article commanding the ransom of captives while discarding the articles that forbade the killing, the expulsion, and the alliance-assistance. This is a docket of observance chosen by convenience. In the general register, the question names the whole method of taking from the book what suits the whim and leaving what rebukes it — the very posture the revelation indicts elsewhere: (4:150 — **““We believe in some and disbelieve in others,” desiring to forge a compromise”**). The structure of the accusation is the one the final revelation states as a creed, where the faithful define themselves by refusing the selection altogether: (2:285 — **“and His messengers. ˹They proclaim,˺ “We make no distinction between any of His messengers.” And they say, “We hear”**). What follows from the pairing is the doctrine of the book as an indivisible trust. To believe part and reject part is not a lesser belief. It is a different religion wearing the first one's vocabulary. The judgment this passage performs is therefore not on ignorance but on the editor's pen, the same pen the earlier woe already priced.

**TWO FLOORS OF DISGRACE**

The sentence of the verse is then set on two floors, and the architecture of the punishment is deliberate: ***“Is there any reward”***, ***“for those who do”***, ***“so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life”***. The first floor is historical. The earliest glosses read the worldly disgrace as killing and captivity, the community paying for its selective practice in the currency it had minted. The men who ransomed prisoners of war by the book were led away as prisoners themselves. The second floor is jurisprudential, and the pairing with the first is the point. The disgrace of history answers the disgrace of doctrine, so that the public shame is the visible form of the inner break. The comparative in the verse's second floor, the punishment named as the most severe, was read by the language specialists as the elative form doing superlative work. It is not merely severe. It is the severe itself. The structure echoes the doubled sentence of the writing-verse earlier in the passage, where the deed and its wage each received their own woe. Here the world and the last day each receive their own disgrace.

**THE WATCH THAT KEEPS THE FULL ACCOUNT**

The close answers the practice's hidden theory with one sentence: ***“and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment?”***, ***“For Allah is never unaware of what you do”***. The hidden theory of selective observance is always the theory of the unwatched ledger: that the unobserved articles cost nothing and the unexamined motives weigh nothing. The assurance of the verse withdraws the theory at its root, and the transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās states the refusal of the discount plainly: *"He is not reducing your punishment because of what you do"* — and the same authority adds the second register, that the accounting also covers what was concealed. The chain of readings on the final verb keeps both parties in view. Whether the sentence reads that He is not heedless of what you do or of what they do, the reach of the watch is the same. The effect on a modern congregation is procedural rather than spectacular. A house today that keeps its charter's comfortable articles and files away the costly ones is standing in exactly this dock, and the closing line is the court's assurance that every article is on the books.
