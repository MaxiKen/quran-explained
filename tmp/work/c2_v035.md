**THE HOUSE WITH ONE TREE FENCED OFF**

The story turns to the first household and to its single restriction: ***“We cautioned, “O Adam!”***, ***“Live with your wife in Paradise”***, ***“and eat as freely”***, ***“as you please, but do not approach this tree”***, ***“or else you will be wrongdoers.””***. The permission comes before the prohibition, and its breadth is part of the scene. Every tree in the garden is available, the food is free, and the instruction to settle and enjoy is given without a condition. Against that background stands one tree, and the wording does not say "do not eat from it" but "do not approach it", which fences off the road as well as the fruit. A boundary drawn that carefully is a kindness rather than a tease, because it keeps a person away from the road that leads to the line. The Book gives the same instruction in the same terms elsewhere, and the phrasing there makes the limit a matter of approach rather than of appetite. Reports from Ibn ʿAbbās place Adam and his wife together under this one limit. Their shared residence therefore included shared duty, which means neither abundance nor companionship removed the need to obey the boundary set by their Lord.

The Book uses this same scene in other sūrahs, and the details given there show how the test was meant to work. In one account the warning is paired with the identity of the one who would try to undo it: (7:20 — **“Then Satan tempted them in order to expose what was hidden of their nakedness. He said,”**), and in another the temptation is quoted as an offer of something more than they had: (20:120 — **“But Satan whispered to him, saying, “O Adam! Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality”**). The reader who puts the passages together sees the design plainly. A generous provision, a narrow limit, and an enemy whose whole work is to make the limit look like deprivation.

**WHY ONE TREE WAS ENOUGH**

A reader may wonder why a garden full of permission needed a prohibition at all. The answer the passage gives is that a creature with real choice needs a place to exercise it, and a limit is where love becomes visible as obedience, since obedience without a boundary would be a word without a test. The instruction was not given because the fruit was harmful in itself; it was given so that the relationship had a shape a person could keep or break. That is what makes the disaster in the next verse comprehensible, since the wrong in the story was not a poisoned meal. It was the decision to treat one clear boundary as though it were a negotiable detail. The verse names the consequence in advance, and it does so in the language of the law rather than the language of sentiment: whoever crosses the line is a wrongdoer. Nothing in the sentence suggests that the penalty would fall immediately, and nothing suggests it would be forgotten. The Book treats the warning as complete and leaves the choice to the two people standing in front of it. Everything that follows in the story is the outworking of a decision made by creatures who had been told exactly what it would cost.

**THE LIMITS A READER KEEPS AT ARM'S LENGTH**

The practical value of the verse is that it forces a comparison a reader may not want to make. Every life has a wide field of permitted things and a small number of boundaries drawn tightly around them, and the boundaries are usually the ones a person spends his attention on. That is precisely the inversion the scene warns against. The garden was given for enjoyment, and the two people in it lost the whole of it over the one thing they were told to leave alone. The Book does not present the limit as a burden; it presents the limit as the place where a person's answer to his Lord becomes visible. Today the useful exercise is to name one boundary that has been kept at arm's length, in the hope that a technical distance would count as obedience. The wording of the verse closes that gap in advance: what is forbidden is the approach, not only the act. A reader who accepts the fence where it stands has learned the lesson the two residents of the garden learned too late, and he has learned it while the trees on his side of the fence are still his to enjoy. Think of a wide orchard with one fenced corner: abundance makes the fence easier to honour, not harder to understand. The single limit exposed whether the gift would be received on the Giver's terms or turned into a claim of ownership.
