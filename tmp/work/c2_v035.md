**A HOME SHARED BEFORE THE EARTHLY JOURNEY**

A wife and a home follow the honour shown to Adam. God addresses him directly: ***“We cautioned, “O Adam!”*** ***“Live with your wife in Paradise”***. The welcome includes his wife; she is not outside the gift or merely watching him receive it. Both belong in this dwelling. The Quran later recalls humanity’s shared beginning in a single soul, (4:1 — **“and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women.”**). Marriage thus enters the account before life on earth begins. It provides companionship and a shared dwelling under God’s care. The address first names Adam, then joins his wife to him. The commands about eating and the tree will address them both. This change matters because each is allowed the food and each is bound by the limit. Nor does being told to live in a place mean owning it without condition. A host may grant a guest the use of a home without giving away the house. Adam receives a dwelling from its Lord, and must live there under that Lord’s word.

Two readings of this dwelling need to be kept distinct. The heavenly-Paradise reading takes it as the Garden later promised to believers. The earthly-garden reading objects that the blessed will never be made to leave Paradise: (15:48 — **“nor will they ever be asked to leave”**). Yet that promise concerns their final reward, not every earlier entry into the Garden. Adam was there under a test, before the earthly life already announced for him. His stay need not carry all the terms of the final reward. The heavenly reading is adopted here. The definite reference to Paradise points to the known Garden, and the account of Adam’s entry and exit fits that sense. In Muslim and al-Nasāʾī, Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet named Friday as the day Adam was created, admitted to Paradise, and taken out of it. The report fixes those events without supplying a map of the unseen. The account requires belief in the dwelling God granted, not guesses about a route, an earthly site, or the length of every stage.

**FREE ENJOYMENT DOES NOT REMOVE EVERY LIMIT**

Permission is broad: ***“and eat as freely as you please,”***. Ibn ʿAbbās explains this as ample living, with room to eat where and when they wished. The food is not described as a scarce ration earned through hard work. It is ready for their use and brings no strain. A second account spells out the ease: (20:118 — **“Here it is guaranteed that you will never go hungry or unclothed”**) and (20:119 — **“nor will you ˹ever˺ suffer from thirst or ˹the sun’s˺ heat.””**). Hunger, lack of clothing, thirst, and heat will belong to the burden of earthly life, not to this welcome. The command to eat therefore gives leave; it does not force them to keep eating. The words about their own wish show that freedom plainly. Mujāhid’s gloss of food taken without account also belongs within this broad grant. It cannot cancel the tree’s stated exception. God gives much and withholds one thing. Since the allowed food is so plentiful, need cannot make the forbidden tree their only means of life. The setting makes the test clear: enjoyment of a gift must not be confused with freedom from the Giver’s rule.

The limit is stated before either person crosses it: ***“but do not approach this tree,”***. The warning reaches the approach to the tree, not only the act of eating. This shuts the path by which desire might become an excuse to take what was withheld. The command also points to a known tree. Its identity was clear to the two people addressed, even though its kind is not given to later readers. Reports name wheat, a vine, or a fig tree, but those differing names cannot all settle the question. No detail needed for obedience is missing. Adam and his wife knew what to avoid; later readers know that a clear limit was set. The story does not teach that the tree had an evil power of its own. Its place in the test came from the command concerning it. That is why curiosity about its species cannot replace attention to the warning. The useful knowledge is where God set the boundary and why a servant must not treat wide permission as permission without end.

The consequence is made explicit: ***“or else you will be wrongdoers.””***. The final clause ties wrong to crossing the limit; it is not merely advice about which food tastes best. Ibn ʿAbbās explains the harm as harm done to themselves. They would put their own good at risk by turning from the One who had provided it. Their later confession repeats that judgement: (7:23 — **““Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves.”**). This link keeps the story from being read as a contest between a harsh command and a harmless appetite. The command protected their welfare within a gift already full of ease. It also joined them in responsibility. Both had heard the warning, and both would need to return to God after the lapse. Neither can be excused simply by pointing to the other. The distinction from Iblīs remains important: a fall into wrong need not become a defence of wrong. Here the danger is named in advance so that the two may keep clear of it, not so that mercy may be closed to them forever.
