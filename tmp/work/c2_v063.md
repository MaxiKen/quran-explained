**A MOUNTAIN HELD OVER A COVENANT**

The pledge in this verse is taken under a weight that can be seen, and the setting is part of the lesson: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you”***, ***“and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to”***, ***“that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you”***. A covenant is usually a matter of words, and the Book reports here that words were reinforced by a sight the people could not mistake. The mountain was lifted and held above them, and another sūrah describes the same event with the detail that they thought it would fall on them: (7:171 — **“And ˹remember˺ when We raised the mountain over them”**). What the reader should notice is what the lifted mountain was meant to produce, since it produced agreement rather than conviction, and the Book reports the agreement honestly in a later passage where the same people say they heard and disobeyed. Awe and obedience are two different things, and the verse is careful to show a generation that had the first in abundance while the second was still missing.

**WHAT THEY WERE TOLD TO HOLD**

The command itself is short, and each part of it deserves its own reading: ***“and observe its teachings”***, ***“so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.””*** The people were told to take hold of what had been given them, which is a stronger image than reading or hearing it, and then to observe what it teaches, which is the working out of that grip in daily conduct. The final clause names the outcome rather than the demand, and the outcome is mindfulness, so the observance is a road to something rather than an end in itself. The same scene appears in a passage that places the lifted mountain beside the command to enter the gate with humility: (4:154 — **“We raised the Mount over them ˹as a warning˺ for ˹breaking˺ their covenant”**). The two accounts together show that this was not a first agreement but a renewal of one already broken, and a renewed pledge taken under a lifted mountain is a measure of how much had already been lost.

**THE PLEDGE TOLD AGAIN IN A THIRD PLACE**

Readers of this sūrah meet the scene again a few pages on, and the third telling adds the answer the people gave: (2:93 — **“And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺,”**). The repetition is not wasted space; the Book returns to the scene because the community's later conduct has to be measured against the pledge it made at the foot of that mountain. A reader who treats the three tellings as one will hear the agreement as a single event, and the Book's habit of returning to it will look like an editor's duplication rather than an author's insistence. What is being established is a fact about the community's own record, and it is established from more than one direction, which is how the Book treats everything it wants a reader to take seriously.

**WHAT STANDS BEHIND A PLEDGE TAKEN IN FEAR**

There is a plain comparison here with the promises a person makes when he is frightened. A man in a hospital corridor promises a lifetime of worship for one clear scan, and the promise is sincere while the fear lasts; the same man a year later will not be able to recall the exact wording of what he offered. The Book's account of the mountain is a record of that pattern on a national scale, and the mercy in it is that the pledge was still accepted, which is what the next verse says outright. The verse also fixes a principle that runs through the whole chapter: what is taken by awe has to be kept by choice, and the keeping is the part that is tested. A reader who has made promises in a hard season can take the lesson without despair, because the following verse names the door that remains open after the turning away.

**A COVENANT TAKEN AND A COMMAND REPEATED**

What the passage leaves behind is a picture of a people standing under a mountain with a scripture in their hands and a promise on their lips. The words of the command are the same words the reader has been given in this Book, and the outcome named at the end, mindfulness, is the goal the whole sūrah has been working toward since its opening lines. That is why the scene is placed where it is, between the favours and the failures, because it names the thing that was available to that community and to every community after it. The verse does not say the mountain produced obedience; it says the covenant was taken and the command was given, and the reader already knows what happened next. The honest use of the paragraph is therefore not to admire the miracle but to notice how close the command is to the one he carries, and how much of it he has been holding without observing.
