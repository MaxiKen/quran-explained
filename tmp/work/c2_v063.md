**A PLEDGE EXACTED WITH ITS OWN SKY**

The record returns to a day of binding: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from you”***. The Arabic form behind the covenant here carries the sense of a firm assurance, a binding that holds by oath or by pact, and the noun is spoken of in the singular because the same pledge was taken from each person there. What the pledge contained is stated at length elsewhere in this chapter: (2:83 — **“And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives,”**). Monotheism and conduct were sealed together in it, so the covenant was never a bare confession but a course of life. The occasion the accounts give is the return of Moses with the tablets, when the people refused to act on what he brought until they had heard God attest it themselves. Seventy men were then chosen to go and hear, and the account records that they returned as witnesses to the Book but added words of their own to what they had heard, promising themselves ease that God had not promised. A pledge confirmed with words of that kind is exactly the kind a mountain has to settle.

**A MOUNTAIN HELD OVER A REFUSAL**

The confirmation is described without decoration: ***“and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to”***. The mountain was lifted and fixed over their heads like a canopy or a cloud, and the fuller account records their certainty that it was about to fall: (7:171 — **“And ˹remember˺ when We raised the mountain over them as if it were a cloud”**). Ibn ʿAbbās gives the scene its plain shape: the mountain was pulled out and raised above them for the taking of the covenant. A question rises at this point and is settled by the text itself. The Book forbids compulsion in religion: (2:256 — **“Let there be no compulsion in religion, for the truth stands out clearly from falsehood. So whoever renounces false gods”**), yet here force stands over a people’s heads. The reason the two do not contradict is that these were not outsiders being converted. They had already believed in their prophet and already covenanted to act on the Book, and the raising of the mountain is the enforcement of a pledge against a party on the edge of breaking it, which is how every legal order treats the rebel under its own pact. The timing of the two events has been read two ways, and both are transmitted. One reading places the raising after the pledge was broken, so that the mountain is the answer to the breach; another places it with the taking itself, as the verse’s own joining of the two clauses suggests. What turns on the difference is small, because both readings describe the same God and the same severity: a covenant in this history is not a formality that a people may quietly outgrow.

**TWO HALVES OF A DUTY KEPT TOGETHER**

The command given under the mountain is complete in two halves: ***“that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you”***. The holding is charged with strength and seriousness, and the transmitted glosses give the charge its weight. Al-Ḥasan takes the object as the Torah itself; Mujāhid takes the command as strict adherence to it; Abū al-ʿĀliyah and al-Rabīʿ b. Anas fill out the second half in the same direction: ***“and observe its teachings”*** is reading the Book and putting it into practice, not carrying it as a relic. The phrase carries memory and action in one word, so that a scripture observed is a scripture recited, understood and obeyed. The pair of commands also fixes the order of the two halves. Holding the Book without observing it produces the empty witness; observing it without holding to it produces the amended text. The seventy had already shown where the second road ends when they added to what they heard. What the scene demands of any community holding a scripture is therefore double, and the demand does not age: the text is kept as it was given, and it is lived as it reads.

**TAQWĀ AS THE PURPOSE OF THE WHOLE ARRANGEMENT**

The purpose clause names the aim of every part of it: ***“so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺.””***. The mindfulness named as the purpose here is given its content by the early glosses as warding off God’s wrath and torment through obedience. That is why the covenant was exacted and the mountain raised. A law secured by awe produces a people who govern themselves when no mountain is visible. The hope is not that they keep the Book under threat; the point is that the discipline of keeping it forms the habit that needs no threat. The later answer of the same people shows how rare that outcome was: (2:93 — **“they answered, “We hear and disobey.””**). Hearing without obeying is the failure this verse was written to prevent. What follows for a reader of any scripture is the measure the verse installs. The gift is the Book; the duty is to hold it unaltered and to practise it; the purpose behind both is a heart that stands in awe of its Lord, and every element that does not serve that purpose has been left out of the arrangement.
