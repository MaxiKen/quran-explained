**A GENERAL RIGHT OF KIND TREATMENT**

***“Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced”*** establishes a general right of kind treatment. After detailing dowries, waiting periods, reconciliation, and widowhood, the Qur’an gives a broad ethical command concerning divorced women: they are entitled to a suitable provision—matāʿ bi-l-maʿrūf. The verse reminds the community that divorce does not erase the woman’s dignity or leave her responsible for all the financial and emotional costs of a marriage’s end. The provision can include a compensatory gift, maintenance owed during the relevant waiting period, housing, clothing, and practical support according to the circumstances and the specific legal category of the divorce. The detailed obligations differ depending on whether the marriage was consummated, whether a dowry was specified, whether the divorce is revocable, and whether there are children. A qualified scholar and legal adviser should examine the case rather than applying one amount to every woman. Maʿrūf means what is fair, recognized as good, and consistent with revelation and sound custom. A wealthy man should not offer a token gift while keeping wealth for himself, and a poor man is not required to give what would destroy his ability to survive or support dependants. Fairness considers the woman’s needs, the husband’s means, the length of the marriage, and the rights already established by contract and law. The command is not a payment for the woman’s silence. **MAʿRŪF AND ABILITY**

***“women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺”*** deepens maʿrūf and ability. It does not allow a husband to purchase permission to insult, abuse, or conceal wrongdoing. It is an expression of responsibility as the relationship ends. If the couple have children, support for the children remains a separate obligation and must not be counted as a favour to the mother. A marriage may end, but the former spouses remain human beings with rights. The husband must not expose private matters, manipulate children, withhold documents, or use financial dependence to continue control. The wife must also avoid false claims and unlawful seizure, but the presence of conflict never removes the moral requirement of justice. The Prophet ﷺ said that the best people are those best in character to their families, as Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī records. That character is tested not only while affection is strong but when disappointment and resentment appear. A dignified provision can prevent poverty, reduce hostility, and help a woman rebuild her life without being treated as discarded property. The verse calls the provision a duty upon the muttaqūn. Taqwā makes a person fulfil rights even when a court cannot compel him and when relatives will praise him for doing less. It also prevents the recipient from turning a lawful claim into revenge or public humiliation. Both parties remember that Allah sees the settlement and knows whether it was voluntary, fair, and sufficient. **DIVORCE DOES NOT CANCEL HUMANITY**

The mindful person settles accounts transparently, pays what is due promptly, and does not force the other spouse to beg. He may give more than the minimum because he remembers the years of shared life and the favour that once existed. Generosity is especially valuable when the marriage ends without a clear legal remedy for every loss. This command echoes 2:236, where a woman divorced before consummation and without a fixed dowry is given a suitable gift, and 2:237, where generosity is called closer to taqwā. It also completes the warning in 2:231 against retaining a woman to harm her. The Qur’an’s family law repeatedly joins legal precision with ethical excellence. The later verses of Sūrah al-Ṭalāq explain maintenance and housing according to ability. These passages should be read together. A Muslim cannot cite one verse to minimize a right while ignoring the wider Qur’anic requirement of kindness. Sometimes a former husband cannot provide enough, or he has died, disappeared, or become abusive. The community, relatives, zakāh institutions, and lawful public systems should help divorced women and children. The Qur’anic ethic is not satisfied by telling a vulnerable woman to be patient while those with means refuse support. Social support should preserve dignity. It can include housing, legal assistance, education, childcare, counselling, and protection from harassment. **A DUTY UPON THE MINDFUL**

The aim is to restore independence rather than create a new form of dependence. Think of a general right of kind treatment through a household that moves out of a rented flat and scrubs the floors before the keys go back, though no clause in the lease asks for it. The next family walks into a clean house, and the landlord tells the story for years. A general right of kind treatment confronts anyone treating maʿrūf and ability as remote; under a general right of kind treatment, such a person has paid every sum the law demanded and left his former wife to arrange her own life in silence. The verse gives her a provision and puts the duty on the mindful, and it asks him which list he wants his name to be on. Because the sentence joins the money to the fear of God, maʿrūf and ability makes a general right of kind treatment show that the last payment of a marriage is made to the Lord rather than to the woman. Today, fair provision after divorce includes material help, dignified speech, and procedures that do not turn separation into social punishment.
