**STOPPING HOSTILITIES**

***“But if they cease”*** establish stopping hostilities. This brief verse places a decisive limit on the preceding permission to fight. If the hostile party ceases its aggression, the reason for fighting ends. Muslims are not commanded to continue violence merely because the enemy once attacked, because hatred has grown, or because victory is now possible. The door to cessation is open, and Allah’s forgiveness and mercy are named at the moment when fighting stops. *Cease* includes stopping attacks, ending persecution, accepting a truce, withdrawing from aggression, or moving toward a reliable peace according to the circumstances. The exact legal arrangement may require negotiation and guarantees, but the moral principle is plain: war is not an end in itself. Allah’s forgiveness is mentioned even in relation to former aggressors. This does not erase the rights of victims or prevent lawful accountability for crimes. It shows that repentance, restraint, and the end of injustice can change the relationship. If Allah opens the way to forgiveness for those who stop and return, believers should not treat every opponent as permanently beyond mercy. The Prophet ﷺ demonstrated this principle by accepting the repentance and allegiance of former enemies, granting amnesty when circumstances allowed, and refusing to turn every conflict into hereditary vengeance. Justice may require compensation or prosecution, but revenge for its own sake is not the Qur’anic goal. (2:190 — **“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you”**) Because stopping hostilities governs 2:192, peace is not weakness arises from stopping hostilities. It follows that the inner application completes stopping hostilities through peace is not weakness.

**PEACE IS NOT WEAKNESS**

For peace is not weakness, the decisive wording is ***“then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. Some people imagine that stopping when the enemy stops is a sign of weakness. The verse teaches the opposite. Restraint requires confidence in Allah and discipline over anger. A leader who continues a war solely to preserve prestige or gain territory has moved beyond the cause permitted by revelation. Peace must be genuine and safe, not a pause used to prepare an attack. The Qur’an allows caution and verification. But suspicion cannot become an excuse to reject every offer, break every agreement, or keep a population in permanent fear. Allah’s mercy is reflected in reducing bloodshed when the conditions for safety exist. Forgiveness in this context does not mean pretending that persecution never happened. The injured may need safety, property restored, missing people returned, and crimes investigated. A ceasefire protects life immediately; reconciliation may require a longer process of truth, reparations, and trust-building. The verse gives the direction—stop aggression and open mercy—while justice determines how rights are restored. The Qur’an praises pardon when it leads to reform, but it does not demand that victims expose themselves to renewed harm. A community can forgive emotionally while maintaining lawful safeguards. Mercy includes preventing future victims. (8:61 — **“If the enemy is inclined towards peace, make peace with them.”**) The reason the inner application follows peace is not weakness lies in stopping hostilities. Therefore, the inner application returns practical force to peace is not weakness.

**THE INNER APPLICATION**

The verse also applies to personal disputes. When an opponent stops insulting, stealing, or attacking, the believer should not keep escalating merely to win. If a family member apologizes, a neighbour ends a feud, or a colleague withdraws a false accusation, accepting peace may be more beloved to Allah than rehearsing the old injury. This does not require trusting a person blindly. Boundaries, evidence, and accountability can remain. The moral change is that the believer no longer seeks harm as an independent goal. the All-Forgiving and *the Most Merciful* teach that Allah loves the path of return. Forgiveness covers the past when repentance is sincere, while mercy guides the servant into a better future. A believer who has been protected from aggression thanks Allah by refusing to become an aggressor himself. The verse also comforts those who fear that ending conflict will surrender their rights. Allah knows the difference between strategic retreat, lawful truce, and cowardly abandonment of the vulnerable. The community takes necessary means while trusting that Allah’s mercy is greater than the apparent advantage of continuing violence. A hand released from a weapon changes the situation like a storm that has passed beyond a harbour: emergency measures cannot be continued merely because fear remains vivid. When hostile action ceases, the reason for fighting also changes. The verse leaves judgement of hidden hearts to God and governs visible conduct. In present-day cycles of retaliation, that rule is difficult but necessary; refusing every opening for cessation turns defence into vengeance and treats past aggression as permission without end. (4:90 — **“then Allah does not permit you to harm them”**) In other words, the inner application answers stopping hostilities.
