**IF THEY CEASE**

The short verse divides cleanly into its two phrases, and both are needed: ***“But if they cease”***, ***“then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. The first phrase is the condition and the second is the promise, and neither phrase can stand in the verse without the other. Both halves of the short verse carry the mercy together, and the walk spends a breath on each. The two destinies Ibn ʿAbbās draws out are the two phrases in sequence. The mercy of the short verse is the mercy of a gate that stays open while the person can still walk, and the walk keeps the gate visible from both sides. The two halves of the verse hold the condition and the promise in one breath.

The whole verse is one condition and two names: But if they cease, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. The brevity is the mercy's posture — the gate is stated without decoration so that the one who turns can see it at once. The cessation named is the cessation of the conflict described in the surrounding passage, and the forgiveness is aimed at those who stop and turn. On Ibn ʿAbbās's reading, the short verse carries two destinies: forgiveness for the one who turns to Allah in life, and mercy for the one who dies in the state of repentance. The pairing of the names covers the whole path of the return — the turning and the ending alike. Because the condition is placed between two commands to fight, the reader cannot mistake the war's purpose: the purpose is the cessation.

**FORGIVENESS IN THE SHORT VERSE**

The verse's placement does the argument: it follows the permission to fight and precedes the command to end the persecution, so the war is bracketed by mercy on both sides. The structure answers the accusation that the passage loves violence — the passage gives violence a stopping point in the very center of its grammar. The forgiveness offered is not a reward for defeat but a door for reform, and the door stays open while the person can walk. The application is to read the two names as the war's intended end-state: the fighting is a medicine whose health is the forgiveness. Because the names are all-forgiving and most-merciful, the returning enemy is treated as a guest rather than as a prisoner of the verse. The short verse is the passage's conscience. Its brevity is worth imitating in the moments the reader must offer an exit to an opponent: the shorter the sentence, the sooner the door is seen. The verse models the speech of mercy as much as the doctrine of it. The reader who offers an exit in the verse's own economy — short, open, and unconditional in tone — is imitating the rhetoric the revelation uses for repentance.

**THE SWORD OF TWO EDGES**

The verses on either side give the stopping condition its teeth: the fight continues until the persecution ends, and the moment it ends the hostility must end with it. The community's adversary in the spiritual register is described in the same period as the party that causes the forgetting of remembrance, and the forgetting is the persecution of the heart (58:19 — **“causing them to forget the remembrance of Allah. They are the party of Satan. Surely Satan’s party is bound to lose”**). The one who fights to restore remembrance cannot then fight the one who has remembered. Because the sword's second edge cuts the warrior's own aggression, the verse trains an army that can stop. The stopping is the harder skill and the one the verse rewards with the divine names.

**CEASEFIRE AND REPENTANCE**

The close of the reading returns to the condition: the ceasefire is the political act, and the repentance is the interior one, and the verse treats them as one motion. The one who stops the outward persecution but keeps the inward enmity has met half the condition; the forgiveness is attached to the whole of it. The application is to offer the exit before the battle as well as after it, because the gate named in the verse does not depend on who is winning. Because the names are attached to the condition rather than to the battlefield, the mercy is available on the day of weakness as well as the day of strength. The verse keeps the light on for the returning. Because the condition is placed at the center of the military passage, the war's own scripture dates the peace, and the dating is the mercy's appointment. The two names at the end are also the answer to the shame that keeps people from returning at all. Because the forgiveness is all-forgiving and the mercy is most-merciful, the one who turns is met by an attribute rather than by a grudge. The gate is small in words and total in reach. The application is to believe the names as they stand: to treat the returning person as the person the names describe, and to leave the verdict to the One who signed it.
