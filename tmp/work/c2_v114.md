**THE WORST WRONGDOING NAMED IN A QUESTION**

The verse opens with a question that measures a deed against every other deed, and it asks it in a form that expects no answer: ***“Who does more wrong than those”***. The Book uses this opening when it wants to place a piece of conduct beyond comparison, and the conduct here is not disbelief in the abstract but an act committed against a place and a name. The question is framed as a comparison, which means he is being asked to look for a worse offence and to find none. The verse's placement continues the sūrah's account of the communities that received scriptures, and it turns from their disputes to the harm some of them inflicted. What the reader is given first is a scale, and the deed is placed on it before it is described.

**THE NAME PREVENTED FROM BEING MENTIONED**

The deed is then spelled out, and its first part concerns speech: ***“who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship”***. The offence is not the neglect of worship by those who were charged with it; it is the active prevention of others from mentioning God where He is worshipped. The Book's attention to the divine name runs through the sūrah, and this verse treats access to it as a matter of right rather than of favour. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās connects the verse to the destruction of the sanctuary by a foreign power and to the obstruction the believers faced at the Sacred Mosque, which shows how the two halves of the deed belong together. The offence named first is an offence against speech, since the places are built for the mentioning of the name.

**STRIVING TO PULL THE PLACES DOWN**

The question adds a second charge to the first, and it escalates from obstruction to demolition: ***“and strive to destroy them?”***. The word for striving describes an effort that does not stop at a single act, which is why the verse attaches it to a campaign rather than an incident. The Book elsewhere describes the protection of houses of worship as a reason for the defence of a whole community, and it names the same kind of aggression in that connection: (22:40 — **“those who have been expelled from their homes for no reason”**). What the verse shows is that hostility to a place of worship is treated as hostility to what is mentioned in it, and the second charge therefore explains the first. Someone who has seen a community lose its place of gathering knows what the sentence is describing.

**ENTERING IN FEAR, OR NOT ENTERING**

The next clause states the consequence for the offenders, and it is phrased as a right that has been forfeited: ***“Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear.”***. The sentence does not describe a penalty imposed from outside; it describes the condition the offenders have brought on themselves, since a place they have attacked can no longer be a place of confidence for them. Another passage describes who is fit to maintain such places, and its answer turns on belief and conduct rather than on possession: (9:18 — **“The mosques of Allah should only be maintained by those who believe”**). The Book's logic here is that the guardianship of a sanctuary is a moral qualification rather than a legal title, and the verse's sentence removes both from those who fight it. A reader can see how the two communities that disputed in the previous verse are answered here by a standard about deeds.

**DISGRACE NOW AND PUNISHMENT LATER**

The verse closes by naming the two parts of the offender's lot, and it separates them by time and by severity: ***“For them is disgrace in this world”*** and ***“and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter”***. The pairing of an immediate shame with a deferred penalty is the Book's customary treatment of those who damage what belongs to God, and the same combination of the two outcomes appears in another passage about those who attack the community: (5:33 — **“This ˹penalty˺ is a disgrace for them in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter”**). The verse therefore does not leave the matter to the day the previous verse named; it states that the loss begins in the present, in the standing of those who act this way. The sentence shows the seriousness with which the Book treats the protection of worship, since it matches the offence with a loss in both worlds. The next verse turns from the attackers to the breadth of the One they were attacking, and it answers the restriction of worship to particular places.
