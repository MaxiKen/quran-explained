**THE ADDRESS TURNS AROUND**

Up to this sentence the sūrah has spoken about God, and in this sentence it begins to speak to Him: ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”***. The change is the hinge of the whole chapter, and the earlier authorities marked it. Until here the words were report and praise; from here they are address and request, and the sūrah never returns to the third person again. Nothing in the wording announces the turn, and that is part of its effect. Praise that never became address would leave the servant outside the sentence, looking at his Lord from a distance rather than standing in front of Him. A person reciting the sūrah finds himself carried from speaking about his Lord to standing in front of Him without a single word of transition. The Book uses the same movement elsewhere and names it plainly: (19:93 — **“There is none in the heavens or the earth who will not return to the Most Compassionate in full submission”**). The servant speaks as one of a company and not as a single man. He does not say *I worship*, and the reports explain the force of that choice. It joins the reciter to every other worshipper, so that he stands in a row rather than alone. It also keeps him from the self-importance of a private conversation, since a person who says *we* has already admitted that he is one of many. The plural also answers a difficulty that the solitude of private prayer creates, because the servant may imagine that he is the only one standing there. Some said that the choice gathers the whole creation into the sentence, since every created thing is a servant of God whether or not it knows it, and the human being is given the honour of saying on their behalf what the rest of them cannot say in words.

**THE OBJECT PLACED BEFORE THE VERB**

***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”*** carries its exclusiveness in the order of the Arabic words, and the order is the first thing the early teachers explained. The object is placed before the verb, and in Arabic that placement restricts the action to the object alone: it is You we worship, and no one else. The same construction then appears in the second half of the verse, so that worship and the request for help are both sealed against every rival. The word for worship itself deserves the explanation it received. It names the utmost humility before one who is loved and feared, and its root carries the sense of subduing. Ibn ʿAbbās explained the first half of the verse as turning to God as the only God and obeying Him, and he explained the second half as asking His help in that worship and drawing confidence from Him in obedience. The religion the Book teaches is gathered here, since the verse gives God what belongs to Him and then admits that the servant cannot deliver it without help. The order also teaches that a person's own strength is a gift rather than a possession, because the help he asks for is asked from the one he serves. The Book states the purpose of creation in the same terms: (51:56 — **“I did not create jinn and humans except to worship Me”**), and it announces the same demand through every messenger: (21:25 — **“There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me, so worship Me ˹alone˺”**).

**WORSHIP AND THE STRENGTH TO CARRY IT**

The verse joins ***“You ˹alone˺ we worship”*** to ***“and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help”***, and the order of the two halves teaches its own lesson. A person might think that a command to worship needs only to be heard and obeyed, and the verse corrects that thought by making the servant ask for assistance in the same breath. The help requested reaches further than the prayer mat. It is help in worship and help in everything else, and the reports describe the earliest readers asking for both. Ibn ʿAbbās read the request as help in the worship itself and as confidence drawn from God in obedience, and that reading keeps the two halves tied together. The Book commands the same pair: (2:45 — **“And seek help through patience and prayer”**), and it joins the two in a sentence addressed to the Prophet: (11:123 — **“So worship Him and put your trust in Him”**). Some of the earliest Muslims said that the Fātiḥah is the secret of the Qur'an and that these two words are the secret of the Fātiḥah, and the saying explains itself. A person who has said them has declared who he serves and who he relies on, and those two declarations are the whole of the religious life. The first half of the verse also clears the ground of every partner. Whatever a person leans on besides God, whether wealth, strength, or the opinion of others, is denied a share in the sentence before it is even mentioned.

**THE HAND THAT HOLDS THE TOOL**

Picture an apprentice who works under a master craftsman. He brings the tool and the material to the bench, and he does the work that has been set for him. He cannot make the wood, and he cannot make his own hand steady, and both came from the one who taught him. The verse is the apprentice's sentence, and its two halves match the two things he must do. He must work at the task that was assigned, and he must ask the one who assigned it for the steadiness to do it. A person who tries to carry the first without the second burns out, and a person who asks for the second without doing the first is asking for nothing. The gathered voice of the sentence does the same work in a person's private life that the prayer hall does in a community. These days a person is trained to be self-sufficient and told that asking for help is a weakness, while the verse makes asking for help part of worship itself. The servant says both halves in every unit of prayer, and the two halves stay in their order: the worship first, and the request for help immediately behind it.
