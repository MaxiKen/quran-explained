**A FOURTH QUESTION AND THE ONE CLAUSE THAT SAVED THEM**

The questioning has become a rhythm, and the fourth round opens exactly as the others did: ***“Again they said, “Call upon your Lord”***, ***“so that He may make clear to us”***. What they ask for now is the animal's occupation rather than its body — does she graze freely or is she used in labour — and that shift explains the shape of the answer waiting in the next verse (2:71 — **“He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should have been used neither to till the soil nor water the fields;”**). The glosses on their question supply the two categories exactly: does she feed freely from her own pasture, or is she put to work in the fields, and the people want to know which occupation would disqualify the animal before they buy. The distinction is not trivial in a farming economy, since a working animal would stand in the fields on any day the search passed, while a grazing animal would be loose on the range and harder to claim.

The narrated counting of the exchange puts the matter in order: had they slaughtered whichever cow came to hand at the beginning, that would have been enough; the first question restricted them by age, the second by colour, and each restriction stood as a charge on the asking. The counting in the earliest narrative layer treats this demand as a third failure of understanding after the first two, each one costlier than the last. This third demand then seeks disclosure beyond the disclosure already given, which means the people have decided the first answer did not match their need. The obstinacy reaches the point where the text lets them describe their own difficulty without disguise, and their own words become the evidence of the pattern.

**WHEN CATTLE LOSE THEIR FACES**

Their excuse is stated in the verse as a fact of perception: ***“which cow, for all cows look the same to us.”*** The animal answering the description is abundant in every herd, so the confusion is real rather than invented, and the likeness of cattle to one another is a proverb in the language itself — a transmitted saying compares trials to the faces of cows, each resembling the other. The grammar of their complaint keeps the whole species in view: the collective form stands for the entire kind, so that what has become confused is cattle as such and not one stray animal. The guidance promise that closes their sentence carries two readings in the earliest glosses. Ibn ʿAbbās takes it as guidance to the fitting description of the cow, and a second view in the same transmission takes the guidance as guidance to the exposure of the murderer himself, so that the right one named at the end of their sentence may be the victim's answer rather than the animal. A transmitted reading in this line keeps the confusion open rather than closed: al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and al-Araj are reported with a form that turns the likeness into something still arriving, as though the herd would go on losing its faces until the command was obeyed. The point is that the difficulty has been manufactured at every stage, since the species was never the obstacle; the obstacle was the catalogue their own questions assembled.

**THE PROVISO THAT OUTLIVED THE QUESTIONS**

Everything in their sentence is bargaining except its last breath: ***“Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.””***. That clause is the one part of the exchange the transmitted account treats as decisive, and the report attached to it is startling in its counterfactual force. Abū Hurayrah transmits the saying in this form: *"By God, had they not made the proviso, it would never have been clarified for them until the end of time"*. The reason the clause outweighs the questions is that it concedes the ground the whole exchange had been contesting. Four times they had asked for more, and every request presumed that the matter lay in their hands to specify; the proviso returns the specification to the One who issued the command. Without that surrender the chain of descriptions would have stood as an exhibit of stubbornness with no terminus, and the murder would have stayed buried under the search. That is why the narration marks the utterance as the condition of the clarification itself, rather than a decoration of piety at the sentence's edge.

For the reader today the lesson is exact in its proportions: the phrase attached to plans is not a courtesy, and the community that says its clause while doing its work keeps the door of outcomes in the hands of its Lord. A planner who attaches the clause and then bargains with a clear command has learned the words without the surrender they encode, and the narration is careful to separate the two. The entire apparatus of the sign then ends where revelation always ends, in understanding rather than possession — (2:73 — **“showing you His signs so that you may understand”**) — and the clarification that finally arrived arrived to a people who had, by their own clause, asked to be guided rather than to be satisfied.
