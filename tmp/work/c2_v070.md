**THE THIRD QUESTION AND THE LATE IF GOD WILLS**

The same request is made a third time, and this time it carries an admission: ***“Again they said, “Call upon your Lord”***, ***“so that He may make clear to us”***, ***“which cow, for all cows look the same to us.”***, ***“Then, Allah willing, we will be guided ˹to the right one˺.””*** After two specifications they still do not know what they are looking for, and they say so, and the sentence shows what the earlier questions had done to them. There is a word in the third clause that stands out, because the phrase acknowledging God's will appears here for the first time in the whole exchange, and it appears after the demands rather than before them. The promise to be guided is made at the end of a request for more information, and the order of the two says something about the state of the people asking. The Book reports their words as they were spoken, and the reader who has negotiated with a clear duty will hear the tone.

**WHY ALL COWS HAD COME TO LOOK ALIKE**

The complaint is true, and that is what makes it instructive: two specifications had turned the herd into a puzzle. A reader who wants to see how the narrowing works can take the arithmetic himself, since an age range removes the old and the young, a colour removes every other shade, and what is left is a crowd of animals that differ in ways the questioners had not thought to ask about. The reports from the early teachers describe this state of affairs plainly, and one of them notes that the people had made their own task hard when a single cow of any description would have satisfied the command. The same pattern is visible in the account of the golden calf, where the people who could not wait for a prophet found an image to follow that led them nowhere: (7:148 — **“his people made from their ˹golden˺ jewellery an idol of a calf”**). A community that keeps asking for signs ends up unable to read the ones in front of it.

**THE PLACE OF IF GOD WILLS IN A PROMISE**

The clause about God's will is the one hopeful element in the verse, and its position is the lesson. A person who says that he will act, God willing, has placed his intention under the knowledge of the One who knows whether it will happen, and the words carry no credit unless they shape the conduct that follows. Here they come after three requests for clarification, which is late, and the reader can see the difference between a phrase used to cover a delay and a phrase used to hand one's plans over. The same community is described elsewhere in the chapter as having said that it heard and then disobeyed, and the gap between the saying and the doing is the chapter's recurring subject. What makes the clause in this verse worth noticing is that the promise was kept, since the animal was finally killed, and the Book records both the hesitation and the outcome.

**A PERSON WHO ASKS FOR ONE MORE DETAIL**

The application is easy to see, because the pattern does not require a herd or a prophet. Think of a man who will begin praying once he has arranged his day properly, and then once he has bought the right mat, and then once he has learned the correct words, and who finds after a year that the days are as disordered as they were and the mat is still unused. Each request is reasonable and each answer removes an excuse, and the result is a person who knows more about prayer and does less of it. The Book's account of the cow is the classic description of that temperament, and the people in it are not portrayed as stupid; they are portrayed as unwilling and inventive. A reader who recognises himself in the third question can do the one thing the questioners eventually did, which was to stop asking and act on what they already had.

**AN OBJECTION THAT CORRECTS ITSELF**

The verse is unusual because the people correct themselves inside the sentence they use to delay, and the reader is left with both halves of their state: the demand for more clarity and the admission that they will need God's help to reach the animal. That combination is closer to the human condition than either half alone, and it is why the passage has been read as a description of hesitation rather than of unbelief. The next verse supplies the last of the specifications and the people's response to it, and the response shows what the hesitation was worth in the end. For a reader, the useful thing in this page is the moment he notices that his own questions are also a form of speech addressed to God, and that they will be answered in a way that leaves him with the duty he started with.
