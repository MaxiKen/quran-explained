**MANNA REFUSED IN FAVOUR OF ONIONS**

A free supply of food is set aside for the taste of a vegetable stall, and the request is made as though it were reasonable. The people open their case with a complaint about sameness: ***“And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses!”***, ***“We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺.”*** The meal they had grown tired of was the manna and the quails, food that arrived without planting, harvest, or market, and it had come to them in a place where nothing grows. Their words are addressed to a prophet rather than to the One who had been feeding them, and the whole request is framed as a favour Moses could do for them if he would only ask. Nothing in the verse suggests hunger; what it reports is weariness with a thing that had never failed. Anyone given a steady good long enough to overlook it will recognise the complaint before its sentence ends, because the complaint is not about the food but about the repetition of it. Ibn ʿAbbās read the better provision as the manna and quails of the wilderness and the lower request as ordinary crops available in settled towns; the rebuke concerns the exchange, not the lawfulness of vegetables.

**THE REQUEST FOR ORDINARY CROPS**

Then the demand is spelled out, and the list is the point: ***“So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth”***, ***“for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils”***, ***“and onions.” Moses scolded ˹them˺, “Do you exchange what is better”***. The items named are cheap, common, and heavy: the food of people who work the soil and flavour their bread with what they can buy in the market. Every one of them requires ground to plough, water to carry, and a season to wait through, and the people asking were standing in a desert. The offer they were turning down had no such conditions attached, and they wanted the burden back, since a field they had tilled themselves would feel like their own in a way that falling food never could. The same appetite is described in the earlier chapter of this story, where the people who had been brought out of Egypt ask for the produce they had known there and are answered by the command to go and eat what they please: (2:58 — **“And ˹remember˺ when We said, “Enter this city”**). The earlier verse had offered exactly what they now demanded, freely and on the spot, and they had left it behind.

**A SHORT REBUKE THAT CLOSES THE CASE**

Moses does not argue with the request; he asks a question that exposes it: ***“for what is worse?”***, ***“˹You can˺ go down to any village”***, ***“and you will find what you have asked”***, ***“for.” They were stricken with disgrace”***. The rebuke is short and complete, and its force lies in a comparison the people had not made: what they wanted to trade was better than what they wanted to receive. The permission that follows is not a concession but a sentence, because going down to a village means leaving the arrangement under which they were fed without effort, and the Book has already told the reader what a settled life under a village's economy looks like. A later passage marks how this account was received by generations that came after, when the same community is described as condemned for exactly the pattern of conduct named here: (5:78 — **“The disbelievers among the Children of Israel were condemned in the revelations of David”**). The verse treats the exchange as the beginning of a long descent rather than as an ordinary complaint about food.

**DISGRACE, MISERY, AND THE REASON GIVEN**

The sentence at the end of the request is the heaviest in the verse and each of its parts is named in order: ***“and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah”***, ***“for rejecting Allah’s signs”***, ***“and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺”***, ***“for their disobedience and violations”***. Disgrace and misery are attached to a people rather than to an individual, and they are described as something stamped on them, which is how the early teachers explained the words: one glosses the first as a tax laid on them and the second as the dress of poverty, while a later reading notes that a nation stripped of its own rule carries both wherever it settles. The Book then gives the cause, and the cause is not the vegetable request. It is the rejection of signs and the killing of prophets, crimes that had been going on for generations before anyone asked for lentils. The same indictment is repeated elsewhere in the Book with the same two charges joined: (4:155 — **“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah’s signs, killing the prophets unjustly,”**). A reader who stops at the onions will miss what the verse is actually counting.

**THE MECHANISM OF A SLOW REVERSAL**

What the passage shows is that communities do not fall in a single act; they fall by trading a good they have stopped noticing for something smaller they can call their own. The exchange is almost never presented to them as a rejection of God, and in this story it is presented as a menu. The early teachers read this account as a warning about contentment, but the mechanism they describe is broader than taste in food, since the same reversal happens with guidance that has become familiar, prayer that has become routine, and a community that starts measuring its health by what it produces rather than by what it obeys. The Book's answer to such a demand is not a lecture but a permission, and the permission permits exactly the thing desired, which is why it is a punishment. A reader can test the pattern cheaply in his own case by naming the goods he has received without effort and noticing which of them he has begun to describe as boring, since the sentence that follows that discovery is the one this verse was written to interrupt. Today, constant choice can train appetite to despise sound provision merely because it has become familiar.
