**WEARINESS WITH A GIFT, DRESSED AS A REQUEST**

The turn begins inside the people’s own mouths: ***“And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses!”***. Weariness had replaced gratitude by the time this was said. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī describes them as bored and impatient with the food they had been given, remembering the diet of their old life in Egypt and longing to return to it. What made the complaint sound reasonable to them is the next clause: ***“We cannot endure the same meal ˹every day˺.”***. The meal they call one was in fact two things, and the phrasing is a known Arabic usage in which the dual is spoken of as one; another account gives the reason as their habit of eating each with the other until the two behaved as a single dish. The word they use for endurance also deserves attention, since it carries the sense of holding the self back inside a strait, and their sentence is therefore a refusal to be patient rather than a report of harm. Nothing in the provision was spoiled or scarce. The complaint is that the gift repeated itself, and a gift that repeats itself is still a gift.

**THE MARKET LIST THAT NAMED THEIR OLD LIFE**

The demand follows its complaint immediately: ***“So ˹just˺ call upon your Lord on our behalf, He will bring forth”***. The phrasing already carries its own judgement, because it turns the Lord of the heavens into the arranger of a greengrocer’s stock. What they ask for is then listed item by item: ***“for us some of what the earth produces of herbs, cucumbers, garlic, lentils”***. The herbs named are plants that grow without a standing stalk, and the fifth item of the list has been read most often as garlic; Ibn ʿAbbās gives that gloss, and the transmitted reading of Ibn Masʿūd pronounces the same word in the form that means garlic outright. Other readings of that item exist, one of them taking it as wheat used for bread, and one taking it as covering every grain and seed that is eaten. The list itself tells the history. These are the foods of settled peasant life, and they are exactly what the people had eaten in the land of their servitude. What follows in the sentence carries their tone back to them: ***“and onions.” Moses scolded ˹them˺, “Do you exchange what is better”***. The question of the rebuke is grammatical as well as moral, since the form of it in Arabic is built for reproach rather than for information.

**A TRADE MEASURED IN BOTH DIRECTIONS**

Moses answers the request by naming what it would cost: ***“for what is worse?”***. Manna and quails descended without sowing or shearing, pure and daily renewed, while the vegetables they asked for would have to be dug from ground that owed them nothing. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī adds that they remembered the food of their forebears and the pull of old habits moved them more than any need. The scolding is therefore also a valuation, and the valuation runs against their own appraisal of their lives. A person who trades descending bread for a field’s labour has not merely changed a menu; he has re-ranked what gift is and what toil is. Moses also shows his measure as a leader in the same movement. He rebukes them sharply and then carries their request to his Lord, since care for a people does not end when their demand is wrong. The answer that follows is itself shaped like their demand. Permission arrives, but the permission is the beginning of the fall they had chosen.

**GRANTED, AND THEN MARKED**

The permission is short: ***“˹You can˺ go down to any village”***. The verb for going down covers entering a place and need not describe a change of elevation, and the town named in the order is indefinite in its grammar, so the reading that takes it as the proper name of Egypt is held back by the shape of the word itself. Ibn ʿAbbās reports two senses for the order: a return to where they had come from, or entry into whatever town would take them. What they find there is only what they asked for: ***“and you will find what you have asked”***. The vegetables were available in towns all along; the wilderness had not deprived them of cucumbers, but of the unearned sweetness they had learned to despise. Granting the request is therefore itself the first stroke of the answer. They are sent down to buy with labour what they had been given without it, and the sending has the shape of exile rather than of favour. The reason the permission grieves a careful reader is that it marks the moment a people moved from being carried to being made to walk.

**STRICKEN WITH WHAT STICKS TO A PEOPLE**

The verse then states the outcome without softening: ***“for.” They were stricken with disgrace”***. The blow is named as a stamp rather than as a passing event. Disgrace of this kind behaves like a dye that has entered the cloth; later washing moves the dirt but the colour remains. The pair of afflictions is completed in the next words: ***“and misery, and they invited the displeasure of Allah”***. The transmitted glosses give the two their shapes. One takes the disgrace as the tribute imposed on the subdued and the misery as the dress of poverty that never left them. The cause clause names two crimes and weighs both: ***“for rejecting Allah’s signs”***. Rejection after seeing the sea part and the stone give water is not ignorance but a decision, and a decision of that kind is what seals the distance between a people and their Lord. The second crime is heavier in the counting: ***“and unjustly killing the prophets. This is ˹a fair reward˺”***. The Book returns to this charge against them in its own ledgers of their covenant: (4:155 — **“rejecting Allah’s signs, killing the prophets unjustly”**). Killing a prophet is the crime that answers mercy with murder, and the word of God given to warn them became the blood they spilled to silence the warning. The closing line then repeats the cause as the verdict: ***“for their disobedience and violations”***. The repetition itself is deliberate, since an account that ends twice on the cause refuses to let the punishment be read as arbitrary. What the verse therefore settles is a pattern that outlives the camp. The same proclamation stands in the record of this people: (7:167 — **“And ˹remember, O Prophet,˺ when your Lord declared that He would send against them others who would make them suffer terribly”**). A stamp set by that much refusal is not removed by wealth or time alone; it is removed only by the return that undoes the cause.
