**THE OTHER END OF THE SAME SENTENCE**

The promise has a counterpart, and the sūrah states it without softening: ***“But those who disbelieve”***, and ***“and deny Our signs will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.””***. Two conditions are named, and the order matters. The first is a refusal, and the second is what refusal becomes once it has something concrete to reject. The Book does not describe the people in this line as uninformed; the signs are named as things they have denied, which means the signs were there to be met. The sentence is the closing bracket of the opening scene of the chapter, and it lands with the same finality as the promise that precedes it. The word for residence in this verse is the same word the sūrah used for the wrongdoers in the garden, and it is also the word used in this chapter for the people who are settled in their unbelief. The Book likes to keep that thread visible, because it wants the reader to see that the two destinies in the opening verses are not a pair of moods but a pair of tenancies. One group is promised that fear and grief will be lifted from them; the other is told what their permanent address will be. Nothing in the arrangement is hidden, and nothing about it is negotiable after the fact.

**THE ARGUMENT THAT LEADS TO BOTH ENDINGS**

The order of the sūrah's opening chapters of thought is worth tracing, because the reader has now passed through all of it. First the Book was described as a guide for the mindful. Then the readers were divided into the guided, the sealed, and the double-minded. Then the whole of humanity was called to worship, challenged to produce a sūrah, warned about the fire, and given good news about the gardens. Then the story of Adam and Iblīs supplied the mechanism behind all of it: a command, a choice, a fall, and a return. Now the two endings are stated one after the other, and the reader has been given every piece of information he needs to understand what the choice involves. The Book is not unusual among scriptures in naming a punishment, and it is unusual in the amount of reasoning it places in front of the warning. A person reading this chapter has been argued with, reminded, shown his own biography, told about his enemy, and given the terms of forgiveness before the fire is mentioned a second time. That sequence is the answer to anyone who says the Book threatens first and explains later. The explained version is exactly what the sūrah has been building from its opening line, and the two verses about the residents of each destination are simply where the argument lands.

**DENYING SIGNS IN AN ORDINARY WEEK**

The phrase about denying signs can sound far away from a reader who has never formally rejected a scripture, and the Book uses it in a way that brings it home. A sign is anything that points beyond itself, and a person denies signs whenever he explains away what he has plainly seen: the answered prayer he attributes to coincidence, the limit he crosses while telling himself the matter is not settled, the verse he avoids because it has asked him for something. None of that is dramatic, and none of it looks like unbelief at the time, which is precisely why it is dangerous. The verse names the endpoint rather than each step toward it, and the reader is expected to supply the steps from his own week. Today the honest exercise is to take one sign that has been explained away and to stop explaining it. The verse does not ask anyone to feel certain about what comes after death; it asks him not to deny what he has already been shown. A person who can do that much has separated himself from the group described in this line, and he has done it at the only moment available to anyone, which is the one he is living in now.
