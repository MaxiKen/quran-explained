**REJECTION IS NOT ANOTHER WAY OF FOLLOWING**

The promise of safety has a stated opposite: ***“But those who disbelieve”*** ***“and deny Our signs”***. These words describe a response to the guidance just promised. Ibn ʿAbbās connects the denial with the scripture and the messenger sent with it. The signs are not empty labels for private feelings. They are the evidence, revealed words, and clear teaching by which God makes His message known. To deny them is to reject what they establish. Disbelief can take the form of refusing a command or treating a true message as false. It need not always arise from a lack of information. Pharaoh’s people supply a later example: (27:14 — **“And, although their hearts were convinced the signs were true, they still denied them wrongfully and arrogantly.”**). That passage concerns their own case, but it shows why knowledge and submission cannot be treated as the same thing. Because pride may resist a truth already recognized, the cure is not always another fact. The will to obey also matters. This is why the warning follows guidance rather than replacing it. God shows the way and states the result of rejecting it. The two verses do not offer two equally sound paths with different outward customs.

**THE WARNING MUST BE KEPT WITHIN ITS STATED TERMS**

A person who stumbles and repents is not described by this verse merely because a fault occurred. Adam’s accepted return has just made that distinction plain. The threat is for those who reject the truth, not for every person who has done a wrong. A reader also cannot know each person’s heart or what teaching truly reached him. God sets a rule for His judgement: (17:15 — **“And We would never punish ˹a people˺ until We have sent a messenger ˹to warn them˺”**). Since God judges each case justly, His broad warning grants no right to declare the fate of each person. Its seriousness remains intact without such claims. The threat concerns those who die in unbelief. It does not shut the way back before death. The call to return is clear: (39:53 — **“Do not lose hope in Allah’s mercy, for Allah certainly forgives all sins.”**). Hope does not cancel the threat, because repentance means turning back rather than insisting that God’s signs are false. The difference between Adam and Iblīs has already shown the two movements. One owns the wrong and receives mercy; the other makes pride a defence of refusal.

**AN ABIDING HOME, NOT A PASSING ENCOUNTER**

Those described ***“will be the residents of the Fire.”***. These are its people, bound to it as their lasting home. The distinction matters because a believer may be punished for a sin without being condemned to stay forever. Muslim carries Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī’s report that the Prophet spoke of two groups: the Fire’s abiding people and others punished for their sins. The latter are reduced to a state like charcoal before leave for intercession is granted. The report therefore does not promise an exit to those whom this verse describes as remaining forever. It distinguishes another group from them. Nor does it make sins harmless for those who believe. Even where a person will at last come out, the pain of the Fire is not a slight matter. The reading must preserve both truths: the warning here has a defined object, and faith is no excuse to treat disobedience lightly. The verse’s description of residence prepares the reader for the final words, which settle how lasting that relation to the Fire will be.

The end is explicit: ***“They will be there forever.””***. Long duration and unending duration are different claims. Here the latter is meant. Another account describes the path of those who persist in disbelief and wrong as leading to Hell, (4:169 — **“to stay there for ever and ever.”**). Nor does death provide release there: (35:36 — **“where they will not be ˹allowed to be˺ finished by death, nor will its torment be lightened for them.”**). Because these verses speak of life under judgement and of a stay without end, they rule out a merely long sentence. The warning names a real outcome, not just the shame of knowing that a wrong choice was made. At the same time, the story has placed this outcome after the teaching of repentance and the promise of guidance. Human life does not begin with a verdict that every descendant of Adam is condemned. It begins with provision, a warning about the enemy, a taught path of return, and a message to follow. The final division turns on the response to that message. The coming account of the Children of Israel will bring that choice into the history of a community already blessed with revelation.
