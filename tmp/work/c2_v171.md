**HEARING WITHOUT MEANING**

***“The example of the disbelievers ˹not responding to the Messenger’s warning˺ is like a flock not comprehending”*** draws the simile in one stroke: the animals hear their shepherd fully and understand nothing. The verse isolates the acoustic facts with ***“the calls”*** and completes the picture with ***“and cries of the shepherd. ˹They are wilfully˺ deaf, dumb”*** and its conclusion, ***“and blind so they have no understanding”***. The warning reaches the ear and stops there. The failure is not in the message but in the receiving faculty itself, and the simile locates the blockage downstream of the voice. The shepherd's side of the image is worth pausing on, because the shout is the posture of care: a shepherd who has stopped caring stops calling. The animals hear the cry of one who still wants them home, and the verse measures the gap between that cry and the answer it receives. Because the caller is a shepherd, the warning is also a summons back to the fold, and the deafness is therefore a refusal disguised as a hearing problem.

**THE SHEPHERD'S CRY**

The one who calls shouts at something that hears nothing except a call and a cry — a sound without its meaning. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the pastoral side of the comparison: the shepherd's speech to camels and sheep is unintelligible to them even when he says "eat" or "drink". That report explains the closing triad, because the faculties named describe what understanding would have been doing had it been present. The warning works like a loudspeaker wired to nothing — the air carries it perfectly and the receiver converts none of it. The deafness, dumbness, and blindness are not anatomical; the eye and ear work, but the understanding behind them is switched off by decision. The warning itself is honoured by the image even while the hearing is indicted. (7:179 — **“Indeed, We have destined many jinn and humans for Hell. They have hearts they do not understand with,”**)

**MANNERS VERSUS EFFECT**

A hearer may ask whether comparison to animals is not excessive for people who listen politely. The comparison is about effect after the sermon, not manners during it. Polite listening that changes no judgment is acoustically perfect and morally inert, which is the state the simile describes; the flock also listens attentively to the cry. The image removes the compliment listeners give themselves for hearing the words, because hearing the words was never the requirement. The outcome hangs on the gap between a person who can repeat the warning and a person who can be moved by it. The animal comparison is drawn from the audience's own daily life, because every household kept livestock and every shepherd spoke to animals that did not understand him. The hearers know exactly how a flock behaves at the call, and the verse places them in the field from the other side of the sound. The pastoral image is therefore drawn from the audience's own expertise and returned to them as a mirror. Because the shepherd is the one who shouts, the honour in the image belongs to the warning, and the dishonour belongs to the hearing it receives.

**TESTING THE HEARING**

The comparison returns in shorter form in (25:44 — **“Or do you think that most of them listen or understand? They are only like cattle—no, more than that,”**). Ibn ʿAbbās sets the report out thus: the shout and the cry are all the animals receive from the shepherd's full speech. Put plainly, the verse grades by response, not by attendance. The application is to test one's own hearing by its effects: the sermon that produces no change in judgment, practice, or fear has entered the ear the way the shepherd's voice enters the field. The test is honest because it measures the changed life rather than the softened mood; a hearer cannot claim comprehension while the comprehension leaves no trace. The sound is not the meaning. For that reason the verse closes on understanding rather than on obedience. The meaning is what the warning does to the life that receives it, and the verse measures the change. The measurement is generous: it counts comprehension rather than eloquence, so the simplest hearer stands to pass and the polished one stands to fail.
