**A SICKNESS THAT HAS A NAME**
The verse opens with a diagnosis rather than a symptom: ***“There is sickness in their hearts”***. The earliest teachers read the sickness as doubt, and more than one of them is named for that reading: Ibn Masʿūd counted it as doubt, and Ibn ʿAbbās read it the same way. The reading fits the passage, since a man who trusted what he said would not need to say it so often. Fever and doubt are alike in one respect, which is that both are worse than they look to the person carrying them, because the body adjusts to its own illness and calls the adjustment normal. Picture a man who has run a low fever for a month and now calls the weakness his natural strength. That is why the disease is named here before the penalty is. A reader has to see the condition before he can understand the treatment, and a wound that has been given a name can no longer pass for a feature of the personality.
**THE INCREASE NOBODY ASKED FOR**
The middle of the sentence describes a growth: ***“and Allah ˹only˺ lets their sickness increase. They will suffer a painful punishment”***. The sickness is not static, and the increase is not an arbitrary addition imposed from outside; it is the working out of the condition, which is why the man's own choices feed it every day. Another passage traces the same spiral in a single line, (9:125 — **“But as for those with sickness in their hearts”**), and a third shows the sick heart choosing its company by instinct, (5:52 — **“racing for their guardianship”**). The mechanism is ordinary. Every time a man defends his position with a lie, the lie costs him a little more and the position becomes a little more precious, and the result is the increase the verse describes. The sentence then turns without warning from the disease to the verdict. That turn is the effect of a condition that has run its course, and the reader is meant to hear the two halves as one movement rather than two separate announcements.
**THE PENALTY AND THE LIE IT ANSWERS**
The verse's last words attach the penalty to one deed among many: ***“for their lies”***. Every lie in this passage has been religious in nature, since the claim of faith was itself the lie, and the Book prices the penalty accordingly. Trust is the substance a religious community is built out of, and a man who forges it damages the whole structure rather than the one room he was standing in. A forged signature on a deed ruins the document rather than the clause it changed, and the fraud costs the buyer far more than the sum he was cheated of. The verse tells the reader that God takes the false claim seriously enough to name it as the reason for a punishment. That is why the passage ends on the lie rather than on the doubt, and why the last part of the man to fall appears to have been his mouth. The same company is described in another sūrah swearing to a claim its members do not hold (63:1).
**THE CHECK A READER CAN RUN**
Anyone who reads this verse can measure his own temperature today, and the measurement takes an honest hour rather than a doctor. The question is not whether a person has doubts, since doubt that is admitted and brought to the Book is a beginning rather than a disease. The question is whether he has arranged his life so that the doubt is never disturbed. Two plain signs mark that arrangement: he avoids the company that would ask him hard questions, and he grows angry when the subject of his own honesty comes up. A man who notices either sign has found the sickness early, and the early stage is where the Book wants him to find it. The cure is not a performance of certainty but a return to the practices that carry a person past his own moods, which is why the sūrah will spend so much of its length on what a believer does rather than on how he feels.
