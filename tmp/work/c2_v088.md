**A WRAP PLEADED IN OPEN COURT**

The excuse arrives in the imperative mood and the mock register: ***“They say, “Our hearts are unreceptive!””***. The noun is one of the strangest in the passage, and the language specialists earned their keep on it. In the common reading it is the plural of a form meaning sheathed — hearts in their covers, as a sword sits in its scabbard and an uncircumcised man is called by the same adjective. These are hearts screened from understanding and discernment. A second reading thickens the vowel of the plural and hears a different plural pattern. On that reading the line becomes a boast rather than a shrug: our hearts are filled with knowledge and need nothing you bring. The transmitted glosses keep the range visible. Mujāhid gives a covering over the heart. ʿIkrimah gives a stamp upon it. Abū al-ʿĀliyah gives a heart that does not comprehend. The mockers' own intent — the claim of satiety, that the vessel is full and the message is surplus — is the reading Qatādah and Ibn ʿAbbās transmit when they render the cry as: our hearts are filled with knowledge and need nothing you bring. The syntax around the cry was itself remarked. The sentence turns from direct address to the third person in the middle of the reproach, a shift the analysts read as deliberate distancing. The speakers are dropped from the honor of being addressed while the quotation of their words is still on the air. The parallel placement of the same cry elsewhere in the revelation keeps the diagnosis consistent: (17:46 — **“We have cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend it—and deafness in their ears. And when”**). What follows from the metaphor is worth pausing on. A wrapped heart is not an empty one. It is a full one under seal, which is precisely the shape of the danger the verse describes.

**THE CAUSALITY THE REPLY REVERSES**

The rebuttal is one particle long and a whole doctrine deep: ***“In fact, Allah has condemned them”***, ***“for their disbelief. They have”***. The particle that turns the sentence is the turn of correction the passage has used before — no, not that — and the correction here is causal as well as factual. The mockers had pleaded the wrapping as the excuse for the disbelief: our hearts were already sheathed, so the message could not enter. The answer reverses the arrow. The disbelief is the cause, and the sheathing is its effect. The phrase for the condemnation places the curse precisely on the disbelief rather than on any original defect of heart. The glosses draw the reversal out. The curse is the removal of mercy and the degradation that follows rejection. Or it is the seal placed on hearts as punishment for what they chose. Or it is the deprivation of every kind of righteousness. None of the readings describes a manufacturing fault. The reply therefore removes the only defense the excuse contained. A man may answer for his capacities; he cannot answer with them. The reason the reversal matters is that it relocates the trial entirely. The question is no longer what the heart was made of but what the will did with what arrived. The passage that recorded the cry of the sheathed hearts in another assembly makes the sequence explicit in its own grammar: (4:155 — **“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah’s signs, killing the prophets unjustly, and for saying, “Our hearts are unreceptive!”—it”**). Note where the saying sits in the list: last, after the deeds. The family of the concealers is named at the condemnation's other end: (2:159 — **“Those who hide the clear proofs and guidance that We have revealed—after We made it clear for humanity in the Book—will be”**).

**WHAT LITTLE BELIEF IS MADE OF**

The closing phrase is a miniature of the whole diagnosis. The faith that remains after the seal is measured, and the measurement itself was debated: ***“but little faith”***. The emphatic particle in the Arabic intensifies the smallness — little indeed is what they believe — and the analysts split on the unit of the littleness. One reading takes the count in persons: only a few of them believe. The other takes it in quantity: their faith is minute, a residue of doctrines held in common — the Lord, the last day, the reward and punishment Moses foretold — while the confirmations that would complete it were refused. The doctrine of faith in the tradition distinguishes the two senses carefully. Lexically the word can name certitude about anything at all. Legally it names the assent that affirms everything the law requires to be affirmed. The satanological parallel is the tradition's coldest illustration. Knowledge of the truth without acceptance of it is the exact condition that intensifies the guilt, since even the adversary knows. The little faith is therefore not a step on the ladder but the rung that was kept while the rest of the ladder was returned. The difference between the two readings is pastoral rather than speculative, since each measures a different kind of small. One counts the faithful; the other weighs the faith. For a congregation today the closing phrase is the sharpest judgment in the verse. Partial belief is not scored as progress here; it is scored as the measure of what was withheld.
