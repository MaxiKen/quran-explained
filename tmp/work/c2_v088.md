**AN EXCUSE NAMED IN THE HEART**

An explanation of this community's position, in its own words, is set down in four short phrases: ***“They say, “Our hearts are unreceptive!””***, ***“In fact, Allah has condemned them”***, ***“for their disbelief. They have”***, ***“but little faith”***. The claim is that the fault is not in their choice but in the organ of understanding, and the Book's reply rejects the excuse and names a cause that belongs to them. What they describe as a condition is described by the Book as a sentence, and the sentence is attached to the disbelief that produced it. The last phrase is the one that should stop a reader: their share of faith is small rather than absent, which means the door was not nailed shut and the hearts were not beyond reach. The verse refuses the excuse and stops short of a final verdict, both within one short sentence.

**THE MEANING BEHIND THE ANNOUNCEMENT**

The same claim about unreceptive hearts is reported again elsewhere, attributed to a group that had broken its covenant, and the Book answers with the same connection between the saying and the outcome: (4:155 — **“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah's signs,”**). The excuse has a long history in religious communities, since it converts a refusal into a defect of nature and leaves the speaker with nothing to answer for. The Book's treatment of it is consistent: it does not argue about the condition, it points to the conduct that preceded it. A reader who has heard someone explain his own hardness as a temperament he was born with has the shape of the argument in front of him, and the Book's answer is that hardness is manufactured by choices long before it feels like a birthright.

**A SENTENCE AND THE DISBELIEF BEHIND IT**

The phrase about being condemned carries the sense of something driven away rather than a casual refusal, and the Book uses it for people who have been put outside the mercy they had been offered. The verse does not say that God acted without cause; the cause is named in the next two words, and the cause is their own disbelief. This is the same sequence the sūrah has followed throughout its account of this people, where every punishment is attached to a conduct rather than to an identity. Faith is described as small rather than absent, and the phrase leaves room for belief that was present in this people and left largely unused. A reader who is tempted to read the verse as a description of people who were doomed from the start should notice that the hearts are described as holding too little rather than nothing at all.

**A LOCKED DOOR WITH A KEY STILL HANGING BESIDE IT**

Think of a house whose owner has convinced himself that the lock is broken, and has told the neighbours so, and has not tested the key for years. An announcement about its own incapacity had been made here by the people themselves, and the Book's answer is that the incapacity was chosen and maintained. This is the kind of self-description that a person adopts to protect behaviour he does not intend to change, and the Book names it because it is the mechanism that keeps a whole group in place. The remedy is uncomfortable: the claim of inability has to be tested before it can be believed, and testing it is the very act the claimant has been avoiding. The next verses show what the community did with the truth when it arrived, and the reader can compare the conduct with the excuse.

**THE LITTLE FAITH THAT REMAINS**

The verse ends with a phrase that measures rather than condemns, and the measure is the mercy hidden in the passage. A person with a little faith has something to build on, and the leaving of that remainder is itself a mercy set in the middle of an accusation. The same sūrah will later describe the people who hear the revelation and profit by it, and the contrast is not between two natures but between two sets of decisions. Anyone who has heard this excuse offered by others can set it against the last phrase here, since the door is left ajar even inside the charge. What closes it is the conduct that follows the claim, and that conduct is always still in the hands of the person choosing it.

**SELF-DESCRIPTION USED AS A DECISION**

The application of this verse is close to the reader's own speech, since most people describe themselves in ways that quietly excuse a habit. A person says that he is not good with money, or that he cannot control his temper, or that he has never been able to pray, and the description does the work of a decision while sounding like a fact. The Book's reply to the community in this verse is not an argument but a record: the hearts were small in faith, and the reason was the treatment they had received from their owners. Anyone who intends to use the passage can take one of his own self-descriptions and hold it beside the conduct that produced it, because attention is being directed away from imagined capacity and toward what a person has actually been doing. The verses that follow continue the account, and the confidence these people place in their own explanations does not survive them.
