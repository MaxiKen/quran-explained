**THE PROMISED TOUCHES**

The verse announces a policy with its schedule already printed. ***“We will certainly test you with a touch of fear”***. The testing is not an accident of the world. It is administered. The touch of fear is itemised first. Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss reads the fear here as the fear of the enemy at the door. The famine named next is the hunger of the drought years. The point is that the list is itemised because the preparation must be itemised. A generic warning equips nobody. The verse names the instruments of the test by name. Fear. Hunger. What follows is that the believer who reads the verse receives a map of the terrain, and the terrain includes the valleys. The effect of the announcing is strangely stabilising. The shock is never evidence of abandonment. The policy was filed in advance. The earlier challenge to the tested ones states the same policy as a question (2:214 — **“Do you think you will be admitted into Paradise without being tested like those before you? They were afflicted with suffering”**).

**THE LIST OF LOSSES**

The itemisation continues and grows specific enough to budget around. ***“and famine and loss of property, life”*** ***“and crops. Give good news to those”*** ***“who patiently endure”***. Property. Lives. Crops. The three columns the world counts as wealth. The verse places all three under the touch. The gloss of Ibn ʿAbbās marks the mechanics of each column. The wealth is hit through destruction. The lives are hit through killing, death, and illness. The crops are hit through the damage of the harvest. The list covers the household and the field in one sweep. The effect is that no category of loss is left unprinted on the map. The shaken scene of the tested shows the weight in historical units (2:214 — **“and adversity and were so ˹violently˺ shaken that ˹even˺ the Messenger and the believers with him cried out,”**). The Messenger himself cried out. The weight of the test is measured by that single detail. What follows is that the good news arrives inside the warning rather than after it.

**GOOD NEWS FOR THE ENDURING**

The closing phrase turns the whole list toward a named group. The patient ones. The good news is not a soft ending to a hard verse. It is the verse's purpose. The early commentators read the glad tidings here as the tidings of the mercy and the garden. The bearers of the good news are the ones who endure the listed touches without renouncing their Lord. For the modern reader the sequence is the instruction itself. Expect the touches. Prepare the itemised back. This age reads every loss as an indictment of the cause and every hardship as bad marketing. The verse reads the loss as the announced curriculum. The difference between the two readings is the difference between a shock and a scheduled lesson. The good news waits at the end of the schedule with the names of its bearers attached. The reward is prepared before the famine is finished. The policy stands. The map is in the reader's hands.

**A SCHEDULE THAT STABILISES**

The announcement of the test is the strange mercy at the heart of the passage. A world that tests without warning produces households that read every hardship as abandonment. A world whose tests are itemised in advance produces households that can budget the grief. The effect of the printing is that the shock is removed from the encounter. The famine is still a famine. The fear is still fear. But neither arrives without a label. The reason the good news is placed inside the list rather than after it is structural. The promise must be read in the same breath as the loss. The two clauses stand shoulder to shoulder so the reader cannot finish the inventory without meeting the reward. For the modern reader the schedule is the difference between panic and preparation. This age sells resilience as a product and leaves the map in the warehouse. The verse hands the map over the counter with the good news printed on the same sheet. The touches are promised. The patience is prescribed. The reward is signed at the bottom of the page.
