**THE ATTEMPT TO OUTWIT THE ONE WHO SEES**

The next verse explains what the pretenders are trying to do: ***“They seek to deceive Allah”***. The wording describes an attempt to cheat someone by showing him what he wants to see, and the early teachers took it in that sense. Ibn ʿAbbās read it as contravening God in secret while appearing to obey in public, and he added another reading in which the men were so bold that they imagined God Himself could be misled. The second reading sounds strange, and the reason the Book uses this language is that what these men actually did was try to deceive the messenger and the believers, so the attempt is traced to its true target, since an insult offered to the one who carries a message belongs to the sender of it. That is the point the verse is making, because the honour of a messenger belongs to the sender, and an insult offered to the one he sent is measured at its source.

**THE COMMUNITY THEY HOPED TO FOOL**

The verse names the community as well as God: ***“and the believers”***. The claim they were managing is not only a claim about God, who is hidden from them, but a claim about a group of people they could see every day, and the phrase shows how the pretenders understood their situation. Ibn ʿAbbās named Abū Bakr and the Companions as those they hoped to fool, and the Book records elsewhere the way the same men worked: (58:14 — **“And they swear to lies knowingly”**). The Book's answer to their scheme is that the people being deceived were protected by the One they could not deceive, and the community's life went on with a knowledge of them that they had not planned for. The reader is shown a pattern here that repeats in every age: the person who sets out to manage the perceptions of a community usually succeeds for a while, since the community cannot see what is hidden, and the same Book that exposed him at the beginning says that the accounting belongs to God. The difference between the successful plot and the one that collapses is therefore a matter of time, and the Book measures it against a scale the plotter cannot read.

**SCHEME FALLING BACK ON ITS MAKER**

The sentence turns on itself with its next words: ***“yet they only deceive themselves”***. The verb is the same verb used at the start, and the same act is traced back to the one who performed it. The explanation the early teachers gave is that the harm of the plot landed on the plotters: they were exposed in this life and punished in the next, and the gain they hoped for never came. The Book states the same law in a sentence about these very people: (4:142 — **“Surely the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but He outwits them”**). What follows that sentence in the same place describes their prayer, which they perform lazily and for the eyes of others, and the description fits the men in this passage exactly. That is why the deceit is said to fall back on the deceiver, since the plot has no effect on the One it was aimed at and leaves its mark only on the man who made it. The reader who has ever kept a lie alive to protect a position will recognise the cost, because the lie demands the whole of his attention and gives him nothing in return. A person who lies to a community is not lying to the community; he is choosing a version of himself to live in, and the verse leaves him there with the choice.

**A SHOPSMAN WHO USES A FALSE SCALE**

Think of a trader who buys a scale that shows every weight heavier than it is, and who practises with it until his hand knows the trick. He cheats his customers, and the first man harmed by the arrangement is himself, because he now buys and sells with a mind that cannot tell true weights from false ones. The verse follows the same path with the pretenders, and it finishes by naming the thing they lack: ***“but they fail to perceive it”***. They do not know that the loss is theirs, and the not-knowing is the last stage of the road. These days the same blindness appears whenever a person decides that the truth about him is a thing to be managed, since the management quickly becomes the whole of his life. The sūrah continues by describing the disease that made such a course possible.
