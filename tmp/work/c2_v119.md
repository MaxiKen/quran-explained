**THE TRUTH THE MESSENGER WAS SENT WITH**

The attention moves from the demands of the audience to the description of the one who brought the message, and his commission is stated in three parts: ***“We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺”***, ***“as a deliverer of good news”*** and ***“and a warner.”***. The truth is named as what the sending carried, which places the message rather than the messenger's person at the centre of the sentence. The two roles that follow describe what the truth does when it reaches people: it announces something to those who accept it and warns those who do not. The preceding sentence has just described an audience whose hearts all came out alike, and this sentence answers that scene by describing the work that continues regardless. What the reader receives is a picture of a mission that does not depend on the response it meets.

**GOOD NEWS AND WARNING AS ONE OFFICE**

The pairing of the two roles is not a division of labour between two messengers but a description of a single office, and the Book uses the pair together whenever it summarises a prophet's work. The good news comes first, which is worth noticing, since the warning is not the whole of what the messenger carries or even most of it. Another sūrah makes the same point when it lists what the earlier messengers brought and what they met: (3:184 — **“so too were messengers before you who came with clear proofs”**). The Book's habit of naming both roles together is a way of saying that the message is complete in what it offers and complete in what it threatens, so that no hearer can claim he was told only half. A reader who has heard the message described only as judgement will find the order of the words here worth his attention.

**THE MESSENGER NOT ANSWERABLE FOR THE OTHERS**

The sentence then relieves the messenger of a burden that the previous verses could easily have placed on him: ***“And you will not be accountable”*** and ***“for the residents of the Hellfire”***. The Book states this as a fact about the mission rather than as a comfort offered to a discouraged man, and the statement follows the description of the office directly. The same assurance appears in the history of the earlier messengers, whose audiences mocked them while the messengers delivered what they had been given: (36:30 — **“No messenger ever came to them without being mocked”**). The division of responsibility runs between the delivery and the response, and only the first belongs to the messenger. A reader who carries a message to people who will not hear it has the sentence as the limit of his obligation.

**A HERALD WHO DOES NOT OWE THE CITY ITS ANSWER**

Think of a herald who rides through a town reading the proclamation and who is not asked afterwards whether the townspeople agreed with it. The verse describes the prophet's work in the same terms, and it names the two directions the proclamation can take without making either of them the messenger's responsibility. The comparison is useful because the pressure a messenger feels is usually about results, and the sentence here removes results from the account. Anyone who has tried to make another person believe something for his own good knows the exhaustion the verse is lifting, and the lifting is done in a single clause. What remains with the messenger is the delivery, which is the part he was given to do.

**THE DELIVERY AND THE RESPONSE**

Read with the verses around it, this sentence explains why the Book can describe an audience that refused and still call the sending a sending. The signs were made clear for people of sure faith, the demand for a different kind of proof was refused, and the messenger is told that the residents of the Fire are not his to answer for. The three statements together form the answer to the objection recorded in the previous verse, and the answer is about the nature of the mission rather than about the objectors' standing. The Book's restraint here is part of its method, since it leaves the outcome with God and the delivery with the messenger. Anyone who has wondered how a prophet could continue after so much refusal has the sentence as the Book's own account of it.
