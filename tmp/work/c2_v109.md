**THE WISH HELD IN THE HEART OF THE OTHERS**

The verse names the desire behind the hostility the chapter has been describing, and it names the people it belongs to: ***“Many among the People of the Book wish they could turn you ˹believers˺ back to disbelief”***. The wish is described as directed at a whole community rather than at an individual, and it is described as a wish rather than an attempt, which shows that the thing being reported here is an inner disposition. The Book has just finished answering the objections about abrogation and the choice of messengers, and it now identifies the source of the objections it has been meeting. The same aspiration is reported in another sūrah in almost the same terms, which shows that the verse is describing a settled attitude rather than a passing mood: (3:69 — **“Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you”**). What the reader receives is a diagnosis, and the diagnosis is offered so that the community can meet the wish without being surprised by it.

**WHY THEY WANTED IT**

The motive is stated in the verse itself, and it is stated with a word that leaves no room for a generous reading: ***“because of their envy, after the truth has been made clear to them. Pardon”***. The truth had been made clear to them, and the verse places that clause immediately before the command to pardon, so that the reader measures the offence in the light of what the offenders knew. The Book's treatment of envy here is consistent with its treatment elsewhere, where it asks the community to look at what has been given to others rather than at what it wishes for itself: (4:54 — **“Or do they envy the people for Allah’s bounties?”**). The desire to see the believers return to disbelief is not presented as a response to anything the believers had done; it is presented as resentment at a gift. A reader who has felt a similar pull when someone else received what he wanted will recognise the condition the verse is describing, and the recognition is not meant to be comfortable.

**THE COMMAND TO PARDON AND TO BEAR WITH THEM**

The next words are addressed to the community that had been wronged, and they are unexpectedly restrained: ***“and bear with them”***, followed by ***“until Allah delivers His decision.”***. The Book does not tell the community to argue down the envy or to answer it in kind; it tells the community to carry on with patience while the matter remains with God. The choice of patience rather than silence is significant, since the community is not asked to abandon its position but to hold it without retaliating. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās places the verse before the permission to fight, which would explain the direction given here, and the reading also fits the sequence of the sūrah. What the sentence asks of the believer is endurance of a specific kind, the kind that keeps its conduct unchanged while it waits.

**A NEIGHBOUR WHO WAITS FOR A TENANCY TO FAIL**

Think of a man whose neighbours would like him evicted, not because he has damaged anything but because they dislike the fact that he was given the house. The verse describes a community living beside people who wanted to see it fail, and the instruction given to the community is to keep living as it had been living. The Book's method here is to name the wish without making it the centre of the believer's attention, since a person who is always watching his envious neighbour will eventually imitate the behaviour he is watching for. Someone who has been made the object of dislike for the good that came to him will find the verse's counsel more useful than a strategy, because it removes the need to settle the account. What is left for the believer is the ordinary work of the community, carried on in the meantime.

**THE DECISION THAT BELONGS TO ALLAH**

The verse closes with the attribute that has ended several sentences in this stretch: ***“Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything”***. The clause gives the community the reason it can wait, since the matter of who is favoured has already been shown to lie with the One who gives. The Book's habit of closing a command with a statement about God's capacity is a way of making the obedience reasonable rather than merely obligatory. What Ibn ʿAbbās hands down describes the warning here as one later answered by the command to fight and by the judgement that came upon the community that had chosen envy over the truth in front of it. A reader who has finished the sentence knows where the account will be settled, and the knowledge is the whole of the patience the verse asks for.
