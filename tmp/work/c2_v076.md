**TWO AUDIENCES, TWO SPEECHES**

The exposure runs on one contrast and the verse never lets it soften: ***“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.””***. The public sentence is a claim of belief, and the glosses unpack what the claim meant in the mouths that used it: an acknowledgement that Muhammad is a prophet, and the one their own book gave good tidings of — with, in some readings, the small print that he was a prophet but not a prophet for them. The private scene then opens with the leaders of the group speaking to its weaker members, and the mechanics of the hypocrisy are laid out in the glosses with the precision of a case file. The men in question were converts who had found the community's growing strength in Madinah a good investment; their disclosures of the good tidings were the tokens of a purchased sincerity; and the rebuke that greets them in private is not disbelief but a question of strategy. The attribution of the speech to the whole group follows the tongue of the language once more — some utter it and the rest hold their silence, and the whole is named for the part it permits. What the juxtaposition teaches is the shape of the disease itself, because the same mouth that sells a creed by the verse keeps the verse priced for the next bargain.

**THE KNOWLEDGE THEY TRIED TO PRICE**

The disclosure in question is identified in the glosses with a consistency that matters: ***“But in private they say ˹to each other˺, “Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you”***. What had been made known to them was the description of the awaited prophet standing in their own book — the passage the leaders now wish had stayed behind their teeth. The wider sense of the disclosure-language carries three degrees in the earlier readings: what God has commanded you, what God has decided for you, and the knowledge He has given you or eased into your hands. The identification is consistent because the whole scene turns on a document both sides can quote. Another reading takes the reference to the prophecies themselves, so that the private question becomes: whether an open affirmation of his prophethood hands him a proof to take out against them at the reckoning. The particle governing the purpose of the disclosure is itself disputed, and the dispute is worth one line: the same letters read as intent — so that they may argue with you — and as consequence — with the result that they argue with you. The question as it was spoken runs ***“so that they may use it against you before your Lord?”***, and the location clause at its end draws the same width of reading. It has been taken as the courtroom of the last day, where the two parties will be questioned face to face; as a plain concerning, the argument standing about your Lord; and, in a usage the language supports, as in the sight of your Lord, the phrase that often stands equivalent to in the book of your Lord.

**THE ARGUMENT THEY WERE GUARDING**

The economics of the secrecy are then stated by the scene's oldest account, and the account runs through named transmitters. The converts turned hypocrites used to tell the believing Arabs about the punishments their forefathers had suffered, and the community's reprimand fell on them in these terms: *"Do you tell them what God has opened against you, so that they say: we are nobler before God than you?"* — Ibn ʿAbbās and al-Suddi transmit the report, and the fear inside it is the fear of an argument lost. A second account gives the same transaction its doctrinal value: the verses of the good tidings were exactly the ammunition the believers needed to end the debate, which means every disclosure shortened the road of the argument and every concealment lengthened it. The reason the leaders reprove the disclosure rather than the faith of the men who made it is strategic rather than pious, since the market they are protecting is the market in evidence. The parallel surfaces of the same behaviour are already marked elsewhere in the revelation, where the same public sentence hides the opposite private one — (2:14 — **““We are definitely with you; we were only mocking.””**) — and where the private face is not mocking but rage.

**THE KEEPERS AND THE PROOF TAKEN OUT AGAINST THEM**

The verse ends inside the leaders' own speech, on a question that indicts the intelligence of the conspiracy: ***“Do you not understand?””***. The gloss takes the charge precisely as a rebuke of foresight: the speakers are told they have no sense of the fact that these very men will contend with them at the reckoning, holding what they were told against them. The irony the question carries is the verse's last weapon. The men pricing their knowledge are told, by their own side, that the price is a proof taken out against its keepers, and the day of the mutual questioning turns the hoarded passage into testimony for the other party. The companion exposure of secret behaviour keeps the moral in view at the level of the heart rather than the marketplace — (3:119 — **“˹O Prophet,˺ “˹May you˺ die of your rage!” Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart”**) — and the next verse of the present passage will close exactly there, on the God who knows the concealed and the shown. For communities that trade in religious knowledge today the passage is a standing audit of motive. Disclosure that serves the truth is a duty; the same disclosure withheld to keep an opponent ignorant is the exact commerce this verse prices, because its ledgers close at a door no strategy has ever locked.
