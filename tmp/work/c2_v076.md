**TWO CONVERSATIONS IN THE SAME DAY**

The verse draws a room with two doors, and the same people are heard through each of them: ***“When they meet the believers they say, “We believe.””***, ***“But in private they say ˹to each other˺, “Will you disclose to the believers the knowledge Allah has revealed to you”***, ***“so that they may use it against you before your Lord?”***, ***“Do you not understand?””*** The public sentence is short and warm, and the private one is a warning to keep quiet, which means the people speaking know exactly what they are doing. What they fear is not violence or exposure in the ordinary sense; it is that the knowledge they hold will be turned into evidence in an argument they cannot win. The chapter has already shown the same people in the same double posture earlier, where their public claim is reported word for word and their private one is a confession of mockery: (2:14 — **“But when alone with their evil associates they say, “We are definitely with you;”**). The verse in front of the reader supplies the motive that the earlier one only hinted at. Ibn ʿAbbās read the scene as a split between public recognition among the believers and private rebuke from leaders who feared that disclosed knowledge would become evidence against them.

**DISCLOSED FACTS TREATED AS LIABILITY**

The thing they are guarding is described as knowledge that has been revealed to them, and that description is the verse's sharpest point. They are not hiding a sin or a private opinion; they are hiding scriptural material, and their reason is that it bears witness to the truth the believers are preaching. A later sūrah records a similar concealment in plainer words, where a party of the People of the Book is described as distorting what they carry and passing it off as revelation: (3:78 — **“who distort the Book with their tongues to make you think this ˹distortion˺ is”**). The counsel given in private is therefore not a strategy for survival; it is a decision to suppress evidence, and the verse allows the reader to hear it being made. Their question at the end, asking whether the others understand, is ironic, since understanding is exactly what they have and are deciding to bury.

**THE BILL FOR SPEAKING IN TWO VOICES**

The passage is a study in the price of speaking in two voices, and the price is paid in the speaker's own character before it is paid anywhere else. A person who says one thing in a room and another in a corridor has to keep track of two audiences, two vocabularies, and two accounts of his own position, and the effort is spent on management rather than belief. The Book treats this condition as a sickness rather than as cleverness, and it gives its own verdict on those who practise it in the chapter's earlier portrait of the hypocrites. The community that hears both sentences is confused for a while, but the confusion does not last, because the private sentence eventually becomes the practice. The verse shows the mechanism in operation, and the reader can see where the second voice will lead before the chapter says so directly.

**COUNSEL OFFERED BEHIND A DOOR**

Think of a person in a workplace who agrees with the proposal in the meeting and then texts a colleague that the whole thing is nonsense, and who believes that the two remarks are separate events. The Book's report from this verse is that the private remark is the real position, and the public one is the price of admission. The people described here had a further motive, because the knowledge in their hands could have settled the argument, and their fear of being used against made them suppress it. A reader can identify the same trade in himself whenever he knows something that would change a discussion and keeps it back for the sake of his standing. The Book's closing question about understanding addresses that reader as much as the group in the room.

**THE UNDERSTANDING THEY DENIED TO OTHERS**

The verse ends with a question, which is the Book's way of letting the people condemn themselves out of their own mouths. They ask whether their colleagues understand that disclosure would be dangerous, and the answer the Book implies is that they have understood the wrong thing entirely: what is dangerous is not the truth but the concealment. The same pattern appears wherever a group decides that its position matters more than its evidence, and the outcome is always a community that has to keep its own members in the dark. The reader who wants a test can take the sentence he would most dislike being repeated in public and ask why, since the answer is usually the thing this verse is about. The Book does not need to accuse anyone further; it has simply recorded the two conversations and left the reader to decide which one he would rather have spoken. Present-day organisations repeat the scene when junior members admit a fact publicly and senior figures scold them for weakening the approved story.
