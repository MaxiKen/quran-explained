**CLOUDS, MEAT, AND SWEETNESS IN A BARREN PLACE**

Rescue from Pharaoh did not end the people’s dependence on their Lord, and the open wilderness offered no shelter of its own. The verse turns first to that exposure: ***“And ˹remember when˺ We shaded you with clouds”***. The plain sense of the account is that a cloud cover stood over them through the years of wandering in a land without tree or building. Ibn ʿAbbās states that God shaded the Children of Israel with clouds throughout those years (al-Nasāʾī), and the same reading is transmitted from Ibn ʿUmar, al-Rabīʿ b. Anas, al-Ḍaḥḥāk and al-Suddī. Al-Ḥasan and Qatādah place the shielding in the desert itself, where the sun’s heat was the daily hardship. The construction deserves notice, since the cloud is not the thing shaded but the shade made theirs. That is why the gift stands beside the water struck from stone in the fuller account: (7:160 — **“twelve springs gushed out. Each tribe knew its drinking place. We shaded them with clouds and sent down to them manna and”**). Water from rock and cover from sky answered two needs the people could not supply for themselves.

The provision followed them as they walked: ***“and sent down to you manna”***. Two features of it are transmitted from Qatādah. It fell in the hours before sunrise as snow falls, and it was whiter than milk and sweeter than honey. Any part kept past its day spoiled, so each morning’s collection covered only that day, and on Friday a person collected for two days because the Sabbath barred the search for food. Ibn ʿAbbās adds that the manna descended on the trees and they ate of it as they wished. The desert truffle is tied to the same provision by the Prophet’s words, *"Kam'ah (truffles) is a type of manna, and its liquid is a remedy for the eyes"* (al-Bukhārī), reported by Saʿd b. Zayd. The daily limit carried its own instruction. Because the gift could not be stored, reliance had to be renewed each morning. In other words, a harvest kept in a barn would have made the giver invisible; a fresh fall of food kept the source of every meal in view.

**DAILY BREAD MEASURED BY TRUST**

Meat reached them by the same easy path: ***“and quails, ˹saying˺, “Eat from the good things We have provided”***. What gathered around them was a bird resembling the common quail, a reading transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās and repeated by Mujāhid, al-Shaʿbī, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, al-Ḥasan and ʿIkrimah. Qatādah records that the birds gathered around them in such numbers that each person took what served him for the day, since meat kept beyond it spoiled. The permission names its object in the moral vocabulary of the Book: what is good, wholesome and lawful. A limit attached to the same provision elsewhere shows that permission is not excess: (20:81 — **“˹saying,˺ “Eat from the good things We have provided for you, but do not transgress in them,”**). What follows from the two halves of the command is a discipline of trust. Food was made easy to gather; storage was forbidden; gratitude was to govern both the taking and the eating. The provision was granted in the wilderness precisely where no field could be sown, so that the hand behind each meal stayed visible.

**NO INJURY REACHES THE ONE DISOBEYED**

The breach turned the gift into a loss. They stored the food against the command and were deprived of it, and the verse draws the line of responsibility with the words ***“for you.” The evildoers ˹certainly˺ did not wrong Us”***. The sentence marks the difference between the one wronged and the ones who suffer. Disobedience adds nothing to God’s estate and takes nothing from it; every effect of it lands on the one who commits it. The closing phrase, ***“but wronged themselves”***, states the settled rule the Book returns to in every account of a punished people: (11:101 — **“We did not wrong them, rather they wronged themselves. The gods they invoked beside Allah were of no help at all”**). The identical provision is recalled in the longer recital of Israel’s journey, where the appointment at the mountain brings the same gift: (20:80 — **“and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭûr, and sent down to you manna and quails”**). This is why the story is told at all. The people were shaded, fed, and given water in the sight of wonders, yet a refusal to trust the daily gift brought the harm home to themselves. The verse leaves no room for the thought that disobedience injures God; the injury is a person’s own, and it is named as such on the tongue of revelation.
