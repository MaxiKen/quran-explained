**THE WRITTEN GIFT THAT CONTINUES THE RESCUE**

God gave the people a rule to live by, not only an escape from danger. The reminder is ***“And ˹remember˺ when We gave Moses the Scripture”***. The Scripture given to Moses is the Torah. It is called a book because its teaching was set down, not left as a passing impression of an event. The account of the Tablets states, (7:145 — **“We wrote for him on the Tablets ˹the fundamentals˺ of everything; commandments and explanations of all things”**). The people could return to what had been given and learn what God required. Moses received it as a messenger, not as the author of a rule drawn from his own wishes. Although he is the named recipient, the gift concerns the people to whom he was sent. The same grant is described as (28:43 — **“an insight for the people, a guide, and mercy”**). This makes its place among the favours clear. Freedom without knowledge of the way to serve God would leave them vulnerable to another false claim, as the calf had shown. Because God gave them teaching as well as rescue, they were not left to build their worship around whatever sight or voice next impressed them.

The Scripture is further described as ***“the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺”***. Mujāhid identifies this standard with the Book itself: it separates truth from falsehood. These are two ways of describing the same gift, rather than proof that two different books were given. One description identifies the written revelation; the other explains what it does. The parallel account gives Moses and Aaron (21:48 — **“the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺—a light and a reminder for the righteous”**). A light makes what lies on the path visible; the standard lets competing claims be judged. It makes known the lawful and the forbidden, right worship and false worship, duties and the acts that violate them. Since the rule comes from God, the people must measure their conduct by it rather than change the rule to suit a preferred conduct. That is the difference between receiving guidance and merely possessing words. A text used only to confirm whatever its readers already desire is not being allowed to do the separating for which it was given.

The underlying sense of separation also explains other early readings. Ibn Zayd connects the standard with the victory that distinguished Moses and his people from Pharaoh’s forces. He draws a parallel with Badr, (8:41 — **“on that decisive day when the two armies met ˹at Badr˺”**). Clear signs can expose a false claim and vindicate a messenger, just as a revealed command distinguishes right action from wrong. This gives the wider reading a real connection to the story, rather than making it a different subject with no ground in the wording. Yet the Book is the nearest gift named here, so the reading adopted is that its teaching itself supplies the standard. The earlier division of the sea need not be inserted as a second event within this short reminder. Nor must an unnamed later recipient be supplied to make the standard a reference only to the Quran. The words remain about what God gave Moses. His victory displayed God’s power; the Scripture made known the rule by which the people were to live after their deliverance.

**KNOWING THE WAY AND FOLLOWING IT ARE NOT IDENTICAL**

The intended result is ***“that perhaps you would be ˹rightly˺ guided”***. The gift opens a way out of error, but possession of it does not force every recipient to follow. Guidance here includes learning what is true and being enabled to act upon it. A person can know the command and still turn from it through pride or desire. That failure is not proof that the teaching lacked a guiding purpose. It shows why the response of the heart belongs in the account alongside the gift of knowledge. The Book did bear fruit among people who remained faithful: (32:24 — **“We raised from among them leaders, guiding by Our command, when they patiently endured and firmly believed in Our signs”**). Their leadership is joined to patience and firm belief, not to a claim that inherited scripture alone placed them beyond error. Because knowledge supplies a means of guidance, refusing to use it is a serious failure rather than a neutral choice. The verse therefore praises the gift without making its recipients incapable of disobedience. The expected response is a faithful use of this gift.

Moses’ place in this gift also belongs to the wider purpose of the messengers. God speaks of sending them and says, (57:25 — **“and with them We sent down the Scripture and the balance ˹of justice˺ so that people may administer justice”**). Revelation is not detached from the lives people share. Its distinction between right and wrong governs worship, but also the duties by which people deal fairly with one another. The ruler’s wishes are not the final measure, and neither are the claims of a crowd. Those who had known Pharaoh’s rule were given something other than another powerful person’s preference. This is why the standard is itself a mercy. It makes a rightful demand knowable and gives a false demand a measure by which it can be refused. The purpose reaches beyond correcting one episode of calf worship. The people needed a faithful course for the life that continued after rescue and pardon. Remembering the Book as a gift means letting it teach that course. The honour of receiving revelation is fulfilled in following its truth, not in setting the name of its recipient above the command he brought.
