**A WARNING MEETS A CLAIM OF GOOD INTENT**

The next scene gives the hidden group a chance to hear plain advice: ***“When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!””*** The one who speaks is not named, so the stress falls on the truth of the warning and their set reply. They are not left with no word from outside their own circle. Someone names the harm, yet they turn the charge on its head. Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd read the corruption here as disbelief and acts of revolt against God. Abū al-ʿĀliyah, al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, and Qatādah also link ruin in the land to disobeying God or urging others to do so. In this setting, the harm includes a false show of faith, aid given to foes, and efforts to bar people from the path. These deeds break trust and pull the group in two ways at once. The words extend the view across the land. Sin is not sealed inside one heart when it shapes speech, ties, and public acts. It moves through the lives of other people. This is why a command found elsewhere joins the state of the land to God’s prior gift of order: (7:56 — **“Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. And call upon Him with hope”**). What God has set right must not be torn apart under a fair name.

**CORRUPTION IS KNOWN BY MORE THAN ITS LABEL**

The verse shows how moral words can be seized and turned. The people do not say that some harm is needed to gain a larger good. They deny the class of harm and claim the class of repair for themselves. Their wording makes the boast closed and strong: all we do is set things right. Yet a good name cannot cleanse a bad deed. Real reform must face facts about its means and fruit. It serves truth rather than hiding it. It keeps trust rather than using trust as a path to betrayal. It draws people toward what God made sound rather than blocking that road. A sign painted with the word peace cannot turn a road toward harm into a safe road.

The Qur’an does praise true work for peace. Secret talk, which can host plots, becomes good when it urges charity, right conduct, or peace between people: (4:114 — **“There is no good in most of their secret talks—except those encouraging charity, kindness, or reconciliation between people.”**). When two groups of believers fight, the command is first to make peace, but the peace must also stop wrong and uphold what is fair: (49:9 — **“then make peace between both ˹groups˺ in all fairness and act justly. Surely Allah loves those who uphold justice”**). These tests keep peace from becoming a soft word for leaving wrong in place. They also keep zeal for truth from becoming a plea for needless strife. Reform seeks a just peace by sound means. The hypocrites fail both sides: their hidden ties feed harm, and their public claim shields it from cure.

**WHY SELF-DECEIT MAKES CORRECTION HARD**

The warning begins with an act of speech because harm can still be named and stopped. Their answer shows why that opening may close. A person who knows an act is wrong may yet feel shame, seek pardon, and change. A person who crowns the same act as reform has built a wall around it. Advice then sounds like an attack on goodness. The next verse will state that they fail to sense what they do, but the seed is seen here: their moral map has been reversed. They count a split heart as wise care, a lie as safe policy, and aid to falsehood as public good. Because the label soothes them, the result no longer warns them or drives them to change.

This does not mean that good intent has no worth. It means that a claim of good intent cannot be the last judge of an act. A person may aim at peace and still choose a means that breeds fear, lies, or wrong. He must be willing to learn from the fruit and test the means by God’s rule. The chiefs whom Ṣāliḥ warned were described as those (26:152 — **“who spread corruption throughout the land, never setting things right.””**). Their power and speech did not earn them the name they might have liked. In the same way, a Muslim should be more ready to ask whether a plan sets things right than to insist on being called a reformer. The humble heart can bear that test. The sick heart needs the good title too much to let the deed be seen.

**A TEST FOR EVERY PUBLIC PROMISE OF REFORM**

The verse gives a lasting rule without giving readers leave to hurl its charge at each rival. Today, leaders, groups, firms, families, and lone users of a screen can all wrap a harmful choice in words such as peace, care, safety, or reform. The right response is not to distrust every such word. It is to ask for truth, just means, and sound fruit. A policy that rests on lies does not become good through a kind name. A truce that hands the weak back to an oppressor is not the fair peace urged by revelation. A harsh act is not made wise merely because its maker says that order was the aim. At the same time, a hard but just step should not be called corruption only because it causes strain. Names must follow moral facts, not replace them.

The first use of this test is near the self. When sound advice arrives, one should pause before defending a cherished aim. The adviser may be wrong, but the claim must be weighed rather than brushed aside. True reform can bear clear proof, admit loss, and change course. It joins good ends to lawful means and seeks God’s pleasure rather than the praise due to a saviour. The broad charge to Qārūn holds the two sides together: (28:77 — **“And be good ˹to others˺ as Allah has been good to you. Do not seek to spread corruption in the land,”**). Good is done, harm is refused, and both are judged before God. The hypocrites keep the name of the first while they do the second. The verse trains the believer to refuse that split in private plans as much as in public life.
