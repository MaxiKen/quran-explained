**THE MEN WHO CALL DISORDER PEACE**

The sūrah now lets the reader hear the argument the hypocrites make for themselves: ***“When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!””***. They are not accused of planning evil. They are accused of spreading it while believing they are curing it, and the answer they give is the answer of a man who has never let anyone check his work. The verse protects the reader from judging people by their vocabulary, since the word the speaker uses for himself is the least reliable part of his account. What matters is what his actions produce in the ground where they fall, and that is a question of results rather than of vocabulary. The instruction given to them is worth noticing too. It is not "do not intend corruption" but "do not spread corruption", because the command is about the effect rather than the inner motive. This is how the Book normally speaks about harm; it names the thing done and leaves the intention to God. A man may believe with all his heart that he is defending the community while he repeats a story that splits it. The verse does not argue with his sincerity. It points at the road and says that the road is going somewhere, and the destination tells the reader what the road was.

**WHY THE CLAIM IS SO CONVINCING**

The claim is convincing because communities measure themselves on intentions, and the speech of a confident man sounds like peace even when it is not. He uses the language of reform, he names the wrongs that need to be corrected, and he presents himself as the one willing to say what others avoid. The tell is not in the words but in the results, and the results take time to appear. That is why the Book has to name the pattern twice: once to catch the deceiver out loud, and once to teach the reader to recognise the harvest when it arrives. The same charge is made against the Israelites in another place, and the wording there is nearly identical: (2:42 — **“Do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly”**). The disease in both verses is the same willingness to keep a piece of the truth out of sight while the rest is spoken loudly. A speech can be true in every clause and still be corrupt in its design, because the parts that were left out were the parts that mattered. The Book treats that omission as the real act, which is why the phrase about spreading corruption is aimed at the effect rather than at the manner.

**THE QUESTION THAT TEST RESULTS ASK**

The practical lesson is short and uncomfortable: a person should test his own words by their fruits rather than by their sincerity. Has the family quarrel cooled since he began to speak about it, or has it hardened while he became more certain he was right? Did the group he advises become more united and more able to listen, or did it split into camps that no longer eat together? The answer is evidence, and it is available to anyone willing to look. The hypocrite never conducts the test because he already knows what he intends, and intention is the one thing he cannot be wrong about. A second step follows from the first. The reader can give someone the right to describe his effects back to him, since no one sees the fruit of his own words as clearly as the person standing next to him. That is what the verse assumes when it says the corrupters do not perceive it: the failure is not in their logic but in their sight. The community that practises honest reporting recovers the sight, and the man who accepts the report while it stings is the one this verse is trying to save.
