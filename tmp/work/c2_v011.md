**THE COMMAND THEY WERE GIVEN AND THE ANSWER THEY MADE**

The verse records an exchange between the pretenders and the people who corrected them: ***“When they are told”***, ***“Do not spread corruption in the land”***, ***“they reply”***, ***“We are only peace-makers!”***. The command is addressed to a group living inside a community that was being built, and the early teachers explained the corruption as the work that undermines a society from within: teaching doubt, carrying news to the enemy, and holding the believers back from the obligations they had accepted. Ibn ʿAbbās read the phrase as the attempt to turn people away from the religion, and al-Suddī and others read it as the whole of the work that spoils the life of a city, whether by disbelief, by betrayal or by stirring quarrels between neighbours. The command is repeated to every community in every age, and the Book states it as an instruction addressed to the readers of the revelation rather than to a single crowd: (7:56 — **“Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order”**).

**THE SELF-IMAGE THAT SURVIVED THE PROOF**

The answer of the pretenders is a claim about themselves: ***“We are only peace-makers!”***. The wording they chose is a claim to be the repairers rather than the spoilers, and the verse records it without a single word of comment of its own. One of the earliest teachers is reported to have said that these men had not yet appeared in his time, which shows how carefully the community watched for the type the verse describes. The Book records the same self-image in a later sūrah where the pretenders are asked to consider their end, and the question waits for them on the other side of the argument: (47:22 — **“you would then spread corruption throughout the land”**). What the reader is being taught is that a person's own account of his intentions is not evidence about his effect, and the verse puts the proof of the claim outside the claimant's hands. That is why the sūrah does not bother to argue with the sentence they produced, because the argument belongs to the damage and not to the words.

**THE MEASURE THAT IS NOT THE INTENTION**

The sūrah settles the question of measurement in the verse that follows, but the principle is present here already. The pretenders worked for a political result inside a community that had received its law, and the damage they did was measured by the damage itself and not by their intention. The Book elsewhere calls the same activity by its name and adds a verdict that removes the ambiguity: (5:64 — **“And they strive to spread corruption in the land. And Allah does not like corruptors”**). The reason this matters for the reader is that intentions are the last refuge of a person who has made a mess of his effect, and the verse removes the refuge without dismissing the possibility that the man had talked himself into believing his own account. The Book treats the self-image as a real thing in its own right, and it warns about it in a passage that begins with a question about the end of such confidence. What turns on it is the difference between a person who repairs what his work damaged and a person who defends his work because admitting the damage would cost him too much.

**A TENANT WHO CALLS HIS TAMPERING REPAIRS**

Think of a tenant who knocks out a load-bearing wall to make his room brighter and then tells the landlord that he has improved the building. The intention was comfort; the effect is a house that may fall down. The verse gives the community a way of measuring claims about who is helping and who is harming, and it gives the individual a way of measuring himself. These days the same argument is made about institutions and about reputations, where a person can call every act of self-promotion a service to the group. The reader who takes the verse seriously will ask what his behaviour does to the people around him, and he will let that answer settle a question his own account of his motives cannot settle. The correction is not meant to make a person suspicious of every good intention, since the community needs people who build, and it is meant to give him a standard he can check. A person who repairs what he has damaged is on one road, and a person who defends his work because the alternative is an admission is on another, and the difference is decided long before either man says a word about his reasons.
