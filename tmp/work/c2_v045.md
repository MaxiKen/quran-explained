**THE MEANS OF CARRYING OUT A DIFFICULT DUTY**

Rebuke is followed by help for those who must change: ***“And seek help through patience”***. In the immediate setting, they need strength to keep the covenant, tell the truth, and give up gains that stand against faith. Patience is more than waiting for pain to pass. It keeps the person doing right, holds him back from sin, and steadies him when a loss cannot be undone. In each case, the first urge must not decide the response. The command is also addressed directly to believers elsewhere, with the assurance (2:153 — **“Allah is truly with those who are patient”**). Its reach is therefore wider than the first audience’s particular struggle. In Muslim, Abū Hurayrah reports the Prophet’s command, *"Strive for what benefits you, seek God’s help, and do not give up."* Effort and reliance belong together in that instruction. Because God has appointed means of help, using them does not compete with seeking help from Him. Patience is one such means: it keeps a rightful aim from being abandoned simply because its fruit is not immediate.

Fasting makes this restraint visible, which explains Mujāhid’s reading of patience here as fasting. The person leaves food and other desires at God’s command, learning that an appetite need not rule merely because it is present. That reading identifies a strong form of patience rather than exhausting the word’s whole meaning. The surrounding demands also require truthful speech, faithful worship, and resistance to greed. A fast would not excuse failure in those duties. Nor does patience require a bereaved person to feel no sorrow. The heart may hurt without the person turning against God or leaving what he owes undone. The Quran promises, (39:10 — **“Only those who endure patiently will be given their reward without limit.””**). The reward makes sense because restraint may be costly even when no one else sees it. A person who quietly refuses an unlawful gain has exercised patience as truly as one who endures an outward misfortune. The command trains conduct, not a display of being beyond ordinary human feeling.

**TURNING TOWARD GOD WITH THE TROUBLE STILL PRESENT**

Help is sought also through ***“and prayer.”***. This is the prayer already commanded, not only a general wish that matters improve. In Abū Dāwūd and Aḥmad, Ḥudhayfah reports that the Prophet would pray when troubled by a pressing matter. The Prophet did not wait for trouble to pass before turning to God. Prayer was part of seeking help while the trial lasted. Recitation recalls God’s commands and promises, bowing checks pride, and supplication places need before the One who can answer. God’s instruction to Moses joins prayer to its purpose: (20:14 — **“and establish prayer for My remembrance”**). Prayer brings God’s word back before the mind, so that fear need not drive out every other thought. This does not promise that every earthly loss will be reversed immediately. It gives the servant a way to obey, ask, and recover steadiness without pretending the trial is unreal. Patience helps a person keep his duty; prayer renews his bond with the Lord for whose sake he keeps it. The two forms of help strengthen rather than replace one another.

**WHY A SOURCE OF HELP MAY FIRST FEEL HEAVY**

The acknowledgement ***“Indeed, it is a burden”*** prevents an easy misunderstanding. The offered help itself requires effort. Mujāhid takes the reference to be prayer, the nearest named act, and that is the most direct reading here. Another reading includes the whole instruction to seek help through patience and prayer. On either reading, the command is not pretending that change is painless. Prayer interrupts work and desire at appointed times. During it, even ordinary lawful acts such as eating and casual conversation are set aside. The person must leave the freedom to follow each new impulse. That is why prayer may feel heavy to a heart still determined to govern everything by its own wishes. The Quran describes the hypocrites standing for prayer (4:142 — **“they do it half-heartedly only to be seen by people—hardly remembering Allah at all”**). Their outward act lacks the inward purpose that would make it welcome. The contrast concerns the heart’s governing stance, not a rule that every passing struggle with tiredness or distraction proves hypocrisy. The verse directs that struggling heart toward the quality that makes obedience easier.

**HUMILITY IS NOT AN ACT PUT ON FOR ONLOOKERS**

The exception is ***“except for the humble—”***. These are people whose hearts yield to God, taking His promise and warning seriously. Ibn ʿAbbās connects their humility with belief in what God has revealed. Their quietness is not merely an outward style. Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī rejects the idea that rough clothes, coarse food, and a lowered head by themselves make a person humble. He points instead to treating high and low alike where truth is concerned, and submitting to the duties God has laid down. Since that submission reaches the will, prayer is no longer simply an interruption imposed from outside. It becomes a meeting the servant desires. The Quran describes the successful believers as (23:2 — **“those who humble themselves in prayer”**). A composed body can express that inward state, but a staged appearance cannot supply it. Someone may speak clearly and walk with purpose while remaining lowly before God. The measure is not how convincingly he looks subdued to other people, but whether God’s command finds a willing response within him.

The worth of a prayer and the ruling that it is valid are not the same thing. A strict reading, reported from Sufyān al-Thawrī and al-Ḥasan, treats prayer without humility as invalid. The other ruling does not treat a loss of focus like a failure to intend worship or to perform its required acts. This latter ruling is adopted here. A prayer begun with a true intention does not become void merely because the mind wanders, though its spiritual reward and benefit are impaired. That distinction accords with the governing limit (2:286 — **“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford.”**). It does not make carelessness desirable or excuse missing a required act. The person still seeks to pray with his whole heart, not merely to do the least that makes his prayer valid. Hope in mercy and fear of neglect both remain. The verse therefore neither demands a performance of emotion nor releases the heart from worship. Genuine surrender is the aim, and the following verse explains the belief that sustains it: the servant expects to meet his Lord.
