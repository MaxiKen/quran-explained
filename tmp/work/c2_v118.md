**THE DEMAND TO HEAR GOD SPEAK**

The verse puts a demand into the mouths of the people who had no scripture, and it quotes them directly: ***“Those who have no knowledge say, “If only Allah would speak to us”*** and ***“or a sign would come to us!”***. The demand is not for a clearer argument but for a different kind of communication, one in which the audience sets the terms. The Book has spent several verses answering claims made by communities with scriptures, and it now turns to the party that had no book at all and shows that the same demand was in its mouth. The phrasing is important because it places the condition on God rather than on the hearers, as though the message could only be accepted if it arrived by the route they had chosen. The demand on display is one that would not have settled anything, since it asks for a sign whose terms are dictated by the people who would have to accept it.

**THE OLD DEMAND IN NEW MOUTHS**

The sentence then connects the demand to an older history, and the connection is the answer: ***“The same was said by those”*** and ***“who came before. Their hearts are all alike.”***. The Book's argument is about resemblance rather than about chronology, and the clause about the hearts states the reason the same words keep appearing in different centuries. Another sūrah records the demand in the mouth of the community that received Moses, where the request for a sign was followed by the same refusal as before: (6:124 — **“We will never believe until we receive what Allah’s messengers received”**). The repetition is offered as evidence that the demand does not arise from the case being made but from a condition of the heart, and the sentence states that condition in four words. Anyone who has watched the same objection surface in every generation has the sentence to explain it.

**HEARTS THAT CAME OUT ALIKE**

The clause about the hearts is the hinge of the verse, because it moves the discussion from the evidence to the people who are examining it. The Book treats the heart as the place where a decision about a message is taken, and sameness of heart explains the repetition of speech. Another passage records the demand as it was made to Moses' community, and it shows how quickly a request for direct communication turns into a rehearsal of an old complaint: (10:20 — **“Why has no ˹other˺ sign been sent down to him from his Lord?”**). The sentence's refusal to describe the demanders as stupid is notable, since it places the problem in a disposition rather than in an intellect. Someone who recognises his own capacity for that disposition will find the clause more useful than an insult would have been.

**A WELL THAT TASTES THE SAME IN EVERY VILLAGE**

Think of a family that has moved through four different towns and found the water in each of them bitter, and that blames the towns. The verse tells the reader that the same complaint has been made in every generation, and it places the cause in the hearts that carried the complaint from place to place. The comparison also illuminates why the Book does not answer the demand with a demonstration, since a demonstration is what the demanders asked for and did not want. Anyone who has watched a person find fault with every alternative he is offered can see the pattern the sentence describes, and the pattern is not about the alternatives. The point is that the issue has never been the availability of the signs.

**THE SIGNS MADE CLEAR FOR THE SURE**

The verse closes by stating what has been provided instead, and the statement is unqualified: ***“Indeed, We have made the signs clear”*** and ***“for people of sure faith”***. The signs are described as clear without a condition, and the qualification falls on the people to whom they are clear, which places the difficulty on the receiving side. The particular phrase used here for sure faith describes a confidence that does not wait for further evidence, and the Book treats that confidence as the condition of seeing what is already there. Another sūrah makes the same link between the clarity of the revelation and the people who recognise it: (45:20 — **“a guide and mercy for people of sure faith”**). The sūrah has used this contrast from its opening verses, where the same clarity is placed against the same kind of heart, and the verse here repeats the pattern at the close of an argument. What remains with the reader is the offer of the signs and the phrase that describes those who see them.
