**THE REPLY THAT FILIBUSTERS THE SUMMONS**

The dialogue of the verse is staged as a summons and a dodge: ***“When it is said to them”***, ***““Believe in what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “We only believe in what was sent down to us,””***. The summons is general. It covers the final revelation and the books before it: (3:3 — **“He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah”**). The reply is territorial. What was sent down to us will do. The glosses read the switch as the whole confession. The believers of the reply lock their faith to the shelf that birth assigned them, and the exclusion of the rest is not argued but assumed. They treat revelation as a border rather than a message. The tradition notes the motive inside the grammar of the answer. They denied the other books for the very reason that the books were not addressed to them. That is a piece of reasoning which treats prophecy as mail rather than truth. The reply and the denial land in the same breath. The connective that joins the clauses shows one people assenting to one volume and rejecting what stands beside it. The companion indictment in the revelation's own summary of the case puts the pattern in market language again: (2:146 — **“Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly”**). Knowing, not unknowing, is the adverb that convicts.

**THE CONFIRMATION THAT BECOMES THE PROSECUTION'S EXHIBIT**

The second half of the exchange delivers the refutation in a single clause: ***“and they deny what came afterwards”***, ***“though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures!”***. The circumstantial construction is doubled in the Arabic — it is the truth, confirming what is with them — and the grammar specialists marked the doubling as deliberate. The confirming sits as a witness standing inside the defendants' own evidence. The logic the glosses draw out is prosecutorial. The new arrival conforms to the old shelf. Conformity is no threat to the earlier book; it is the earlier book's corroboration. The refusal of the confirmation therefore indicts the shelf it claims to defend. The passage's earlier recognition-metric had already fixed the standard. The descriptions in the inherited books are matched as closely as a man matches his own children: (7:157 — **““˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet, whose description they find in their Torah”**). What follows from the doubled circumstance is the reversal the whole passage has been building. The claim to believe only their own revelation becomes, under the confirming clause, a confession of disbelief in their own revelation. In other words, the shield of the reply is the spear of the prosecution.

**THE KILLINGS PUT BACK IN THE DOCKET**

The cross-examination then opens the oldest file in the room: ***“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Why”***, ***“then did you kill Allah’s prophets before”***, ***“if you are ˹truly˺ believers?””***. The glosses unpack the force of the question in two moves. First the fact. The prophets who were killed came affirming the Torah's own law, and the Torah itself forbade the killing. The deed thus contradicts the very book the reply had claimed as its exclusive faith. Then the inference. If the claim of belief were true, the prophets would have been received rather than removed. The killing stands in the file as the refutation of the claim. The reply of the defendants is preserved in the transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās, who records them saying that their forefathers were believers. His gloss of the final conditional is the flattest available: if your claims are truthful. That the question is put to the living generation about the deeds of their forefathers is itself a legal doctrine the tradition draws out carefully. Those present are addressed because they approve the deeds of the absent. The second person is earned by endorsement rather than by ancestry. The same pattern of messengers answered with violence is stated flatly in the recap of the covenant's history: (5:70 — **“Whenever a messenger came to them with what they did not desire, they denied some and killed others”**). The list of charges in the final accounting keeps the killing beside the sealed hearts: (4:155 — **“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah’s signs, killing the prophets unjustly, and for saying, “Our hearts are unreceptive!”—it”**). The sarcastic conditional at the end — if you are believers — is the last turn of the screw. It invites the defendants to measure their claim against their own exhibits.

**BELIEF TESTED BY ITS BODY COUNT**

The verse's pedagogy is the pedagogy of a courtroom demonstration, and the demonstration travels well past its first defendants. A claim of faith is tested here not by its vocabulary but by its treatment of the messengers its own scriptures promise. The test is administered with the community's own book as the yardstick. The reason the question refuses to argue theology is that the docket already holds the verdict's evidence: the graves of the prophets are the commentary on the reply. Any community today that keeps a constitution of belief while managing away every correction the belief sends will find the passage prescribing the only cross-examination that matters. Read your shelf, then read your record. The pairing of the territorial reply with the open file of the killed has one effect. It makes selective faith look exactly like what the next passage will call it: a covenant cast aside.
