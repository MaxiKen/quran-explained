**THE REPLY THAT FILIBUSTERS THE SUMMONS**

The dialogue of the verse is staged as a summons and a deflection: ***“When it is said to them”***, ***““Believe in what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “We only believe in what was sent down to us,””***. The summons is general — believe in what has been sent down, the final revelation and the books before it — and the reply is territorial: what was sent down to us will do. The glosses read the switch as the whole confession. The believers of the reply confine their faith to the shelf assigned to them by birth, and the exclusion of the rest is not argued but assumed, as though revelation were a jurisdiction rather than a message. The tradition notes the motive inside the grammar of the answer: they denied the other books for the very reason that they were not addressed to them, a piece of reasoning that treats prophecy as mail rather than truth. The simultaneity of the reply and the denial is captured in the connective that joins the clauses — the same people, in the same breath, assent to one volume and reject what stands beside it. The companion indictment in the revelation's own summary of the case puts the pattern in market language again: (2:146 — **“Yet a group of them hides the truth knowingly”**) — knowing, not unknowing, is the adverb that convicts.

**THE CONFIRMATION THAT BECOMES THE PROSECUTION'S EXHIBIT**

The second half of the exchange delivers the reply's refutation in a single clause: ***“and they deny what came afterwards”***, ***“though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures!”***. The circumstantial construction is doubled in the Arabic — it is the truth, confirming what is with them — and the emphasis was marked by the grammar specialists as deliberate: the confirming sits as a witness standing inside the defendants' own evidence. The logic the glosses draw out is prosecutorial. The new arrival conforms to the old shelf; conformity is not a threat to the earlier book but its corroboration; the refusal of the confirmation therefore indicts the shelf it claims to defend. The passage's earlier recognition-metric had already fixed the standard: the descriptions in the inherited books are matched as closely as a man matches his own children. What follows from the doubled circumstance is the reversal the whole passage has been engineering — the claim to believe only their own revelation becomes, under the confirming clause, the confession of disbelieving their own revelation. In other words, the reply's shield is the prosecution's spear.

**THE KILLINGS PUT BACK IN THE DOCKET**

The cross-examination then opens the oldest file in the room: ***“Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Why”***, ***“then did you kill Allah’s prophets before”***, ***“if you are ˹truly˺ believers?””***. The force of the question was unpacked in the glosses in two moves. First the fact: the prophets who were killed came affirming the Torah's own law, and the Torah itself forbade the killing — so the deed contradicts the very book the reply had claimed as its exclusive faith. Then the inference: if the claim of belief were true, the prophets would have been received rather than removed, and the killing stands in the file as the refutation of the claim. The reply of the defendants is preserved in the transmission from Ibn ʿAbbās, who records them saying that their forefathers were believers, and his gloss of the question's final conditional is the flattest available: if your claims are truthful. The address of the question to the living generation about the deeds of their forefathers is itself a legal doctrine the tradition draws out carefully: those present are addressed because they approve the deeds of the absent, and the second person is earned by endorsement rather than by ancestry. The same pattern of messengers-answered-with-violence is stated flatly in the recap of the covenant's history: (5:70 — **“Whenever a messenger came to them with what they did not desire, they denied some and killed others”**) — and the list of the condemned's charges in the final accounting keeps the killing beside the sealed hearts: (4:155 — **“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah’s signs, killing the prophets unjustly, and for saying, “Our hearts are unreceptive!”—it”**). The sarcastic conditional at the question's end — if you are believers — is the examination's last turn of the screw, since it invites the defendants to measure their claim against their own exhibits.

**BELIEF TESTED BY ITS BODY COUNT**

The verse's pedagogy is the pedagogy of a courtroom demonstration, and the demonstration travels well beyond its first defendants. A claim of faith is tested here not by its vocabulary but by its treatment of the messengers its own scriptures promise — and the test is administered with the community's own book as the standard of measure. The reason the question refuses to argue theology is that the docket already contains the verdict's evidence: the graves of the prophets are the commentary on the reply. For any community today that keeps a constitution of belief while managing away every correction the belief sends, the passage prescribes the only cross-examination that matters: read your shelf, then read your record. The effect of the pairing — the territorial reply and the open file of the killed — is to make selective faith look exactly like what the next passage will call it: a covenant cast aside.
