**ASKED ABOUT NEARNESS**

The exchange of the verse is best seen in its phrases from start to close: ***“When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me”***, ***“I am truly near. I respond to one’s prayer”***, ***“when they call upon Me.”***, ***“So let them respond ˹with obedience˺ to Me”***, ***“and believe in Me, perhaps they will be guided ˹to the Right Way˺”***. The order is the theology: the question, the nearness, the answer, the demand, the obedience, and the guidance, each phrase resting on the one before. The pronouns of the exchange carry the nearness home, and the prayer answers them in the same register. The occasion reported on Ibn ʿAbbās's side is answered phrase by phrase like this. The servant's half of the exchange is written in the same personal register as the nearness, which is why the verse can end in guidance rather than in doctrine alone.

When My servants ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Me frames the verse as an answer to a question, and the occasion preserved in the reports makes the question vivid: a group asked whether the Lord is near, so that they should speak to Him quietly, or far, so that they should call out to Him. The answer arrives in the first person and refuses the distance implied by both alternatives: I am truly near. The nearness is then defined by its function — I respond to one’s prayer when they call upon Me — so the theological question resolves into a liturgical one. The deity described is not an idea to be located in space but a hearer to be addressed. The closing particle — and believe in Me, perhaps they will be guided — shows that the answer is meant to change the questioner's posture. The prayer that follows the answer is not guesswork but correspondence (50:16 — **“and We are closer to them than ˹their˺ jugular vein”**).

**ANSWERED IN FIRST PERSON**

The grammar is the message: everything after the question is spoken by Allah Himself, without a narrator between. The occasion recorded on Ibn ʿAbbās's authority names the questioners and the two alternatives they proposed — whisper or shout — and the reply dissolves both by removing the distance. Because the nearness is stated in the same breath as the answering, the nearness is practical: it is the nearness of a reply. The verse then converts the doctrine into obligation: So let them respond ˹with obedience˺ to Me — the servant's response is obedience, which is the answer to the answering. The reciprocity is the structure of worship itself: He calls, the servant answers; the servant calls, He answers. The one who treats prayer as a monologue has not read the verse's symmetry.

**TWO CONDITIONS ATTACHED**

The servant's side of the correspondence carries two conditions — respond with obedience, and believe — and the pair is worth keeping together. The invocation that receives the answer is the invocation of a believer, and the obedience that follows is the proof that the invocation was heard as a conversation. The call to Me is answered with a call to the same God in return, since the verse's respond is a reversal of the direction of prayer. Because the promise of response is attached to the call, the discipline of asking is itself an act of faith in the nearness. The one who stops asking has quietly stopped believing in the reply. The two conditions are therefore one habit in two directions.

**GUIDANCE AS THE OUTCOME**

The verse ends where the Qur'an's invitations usually end: perhaps they will be guided ˹to the Right Way˺. The guidance is the product of the whole exchange — the nearness believed, the call made, the obedience returned — and the particle of hope leaves the door open. The application is to treat every prayer as the conversation the verse describes: addressed to the near, answered by the near, and closed with obedience. The listener who keeps a record of answers will find the nearness confirmed by experience as well as by text. Because the answer comes in the first person, the believer's private address is met by a personal reply. The One who proclaimed the invitation has guaranteed the channel. The guarantee is worth testing in the smallest cases as well as the largest, because the verse's promise is attached to the call and not to the size of the need. The servant who asks for help in an ordinary hour is standing inside the same nearness as the servant who asks in the worst one. Because the response is promised before the servant speaks, the silence of the moment is never evidence of the absence of the answer. The response can arrive as a gift, as a redirection, or as strength to endure, and the verse does not narrow the forms it may take. The servant's part is to keep the channel open in all three shapes of the reply. The keeping is the obedience the verse names, and it is measured in the prayer that continues after the first unanswered hour.
