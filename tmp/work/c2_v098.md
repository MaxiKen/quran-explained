**AN ENMITY THAT TAKES IN EVERY MESSENGER**

The reply widens until it has gathered everyone the objection might touch: ***“Whoever is an enemy of Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel,”*** and, in the same sentence, Michael, so that the list ends with a verdict rather than a negotiation. The verse does not argue that Gabriel deserves better treatment; it places him inside a company that includes God, the angels, and the messengers, and it makes hostility to any member of that company a single position. The complaint had been about the identity of a courier, and the answer turns that question aside: the courier was never the issue, since the message came from the same source that sent every other message. The verse closes with its own verdict, and the naming of Michael inside the same sentence leaves the community's preference with nowhere to stand: ***“and Michael, then ˹let them know that˺ Allah is certainly the enemy of the disbelievers”***. The angels are servants of the message here, and a quarrel with one of them is settled by the sentence that follows the list. The report carried from Ibn ʿAbbās places the verse over exactly this claim — one angel held to be a friend of the people, another held to be their enemy — and the answer was to put both names inside one sentence with the messengers and with God. What the community had been treating as a preference about staff is reclassified in one sentence as a stance toward the whole household.

**THE REPORTED OCCASION AND THE TWO ANGELS**

The accounts of how this passage came down agree on the shape of the objection, and one report traces it to a claim that Gabriel was the community's enemy while Michael was its friend. The two names are placed together in the verse precisely because the community had separated them, and the separation is what the Book refuses. The explanation given for the preference was that one angel was associated with severity and the other with rain and mercy, which turns an angelic office into a personal grievance about the weather. The account these people gave of themselves is visible here: they accepted the messengers whose business they liked and rejected the one who had carried the message it did not want. The verse removes the ground for that arrangement by refusing to let any of the named parties be treated as optional.

**WHY ENMITY WITH AN ANGEL IS NOT A SMALL MATTER**

Angels in the Book are described as servants who carry out what they are commanded, and hostility toward one of them is therefore hostility toward the order it serves. The same logic applies to the messengers, who are named in the list even though no one had accused them in this exchange. The verse treats a preference among servants as a decision about their master, and the closing words state the consequence in the plainest possible form. The reader who has heard someone praise a religious authority while despising the message that authority carried will recognise the pattern, and the Book's answer to it is a list rather than an argument. The list is short, and it closes the door on the idea that a person can hold the sender and reject the sending.

**ONE HOUSEHOLD AND ITS PICKED SERVANTS**

Think of a family that welcomes the gardener, tolerates the cook, and refuses to acknowledge the driver who brought the letter from the head of the house. That line had been drawn by these people, and the answer names the whole household in one sentence so that it cannot be drawn again. The point is not that every servant is equally prominent; it is that they are all sent, and the sending is what the objection was really about. A reader who has dismissed a piece of news because he disliked the person who brought it will find the argument here addressed to him. The verse does not ask for affection toward a courier; it asks the reader to stop treating his own preferences as a test of who deserves to be heard.

**LOYALTY DECIDED ALL AT ONCE**

The sentence leaves the community with no position except the one it has taken, since enmity toward the whole company is what the objection amounts to once its parts are named. The Book's habit of listing the parties in full is a way of preventing a dispute from being kept small, and the reader sees the technique at work here. A person who refuses the revelation on the ground of the messenger's identity has, by the same act, decided something about God, and the verse says so without raising its voice. What remains for a reader is the practical question of whether he has ever allowed a dislike of the carrier to settle his view of the message, since the Book treats that as the beginning of the position described here. The next verses return to the community's record of covenant-breaking, which the objection was being used to cover.
