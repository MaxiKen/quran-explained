**ASKING ABOUT THE RIGHT USE OF WEALTH**

***“They ask you ˹O Prophet in˺ what ˹way˺ they should donate. Say,”*** establishes asking about the right use of wealth. The question concerns what should be spent—mādhā yunfiqūn—and the answer directs attention not only to the amount but to the people who have claims. Charitable spending is not merely an expression of generosity; it is a social responsibility. Allah names parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, and travellers because wealth should reach those whose protection, provision, or temporary circumstances make them vulnerable. The verse does not mean that every voluntary donation must always be distributed in precisely this order regardless of circumstance. It establishes priorities and reminds believers to notice needs close to them rather than pursuing distant public praise while neglecting dependent parents or struggling relatives. Parents often deserve support because of their lifelong care and because old age, illness, or poverty can make them dependent. Relatives combine the right of need with the bond of kinship. Supporting them can be both charity and maintaining ṣilat al-raḥim, the connection of family ties. Yet family connection must not become nepotism or a reason to deny assistance to non-relatives who are in greater danger. The Prophet ﷺ taught that charity to a relative can carry the reward of charity and maintaining kinship. The giver should still avoid reminding the recipient of the favour or using money to control family members. (4:2 — **“Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺,”**) Because asking about the right use of wealth frames parents and relatives, parents and relatives returns attention to asking about the right use of wealth. In Ibn ʿAbbās’s reading, the verse orders the named recipients as parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, and the stranded guest, giving generosity a circle of concrete claims.

**PARENTS AND RELATIVES**

***““Whatever donations you give are for parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, and”*** deepens parents and relatives. Support should preserve dignity and encourage wellbeing. Orphans—yatāmā—need care because they have lost a parent or guardian and may lack someone to defend their rights. The Qur’an strongly warns against consuming their property or treating them harshly. Giving to an orphan includes food, education, shelter, emotional support, and careful guardianship of any inheritance. The needy—masākīn—may have some resources but not enough for basic life. Their poverty can be hidden, and they may avoid asking. A compassionate community learns to identify needs without humiliating people. The traveller—ibn al-sabīl—may be wealthy at home but stranded without access to his property. Help is based on present need, not on stereotypes about the person’s overall status. Whatever good you spend includes lawful money, time, food, clothing, expertise, and effort. A person may provide transport, teach a skill, pay a medical bill, or protect someone from abuse. The word khayr reminds us that spending should produce real benefit and not support harm, vanity, or corruption. The source of the wealth matters. Charity from theft does not purify the thief, and a donation that buys political silence may be a form of bribery. Allah accepts what is good and given with sincere intention. (93:9 — **“So do not oppress the orphan”**) Since orphans, the needy, and travellers follows asking about the right use of wealth, parents and relatives links purpose to orphans, the needy, and travellers.

**ORPHANS, THE NEEDY, AND TRAVELLERS**

***“˹needy˺ travellers. Whatever good you do is certainly well known to Allah.””*** clarifies orphans, the needy, and travellers. The Prophet ﷺ taught that the upper hand is better than the lower hand, while also encouraging moderation and responsibility toward dependants. Islam does not teach a person to give away what his family needs and then call the resulting hardship piety. The Prophet ﷺ advised beginning with those for whom one is responsible and leaving dependants secure. Obligatory support, debt repayment, and the rights of workers come before optional public generosity. After fulfilling immediate duties, believers look outward. A relative may need help, but so may a refugee, disaster victim, student, or neighbour of another faith. Allah’s categories establish compassion, not a narrow circle of tribal preference. The best spending reaches people with justice and wisdom. The final statement assures the giver that no act is lost. People may not know about a quiet payment, a meal delivered at night, or time spent caring for an orphan. Allah knows the amount, intention, sacrifice, and benefit. This protects charity from dependence upon human applause. It also warns the giver not to corrupt the act after giving through reminders, insults, or public humiliation. The Qur’an says that charity is cancelled when accompanied by injury and boasts. The gift belongs to Allah’s knowledge even when the recipient cannot repay it. **WHAT IS GOOD SPENDING.**

The parcel arrives, the photograph of the delivery is framed, and the brother eats at a neighbour's table. Asking about the right use of wealth reaches a reader today who gives generously to a distant cause and lets his own relatives ask a stranger for help. The verse gives him a list that begins with the two people who carried him and asks where his money goes first. Because the closing sentence names the knowledge of God rather than the thanks of the poor, the reader is taught to give for the record that matters.
