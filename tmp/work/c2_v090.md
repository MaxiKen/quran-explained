**THE SALE RECORDED AT ITS TRUE PRICE**

The verse returns to the market image and this time names the asset: ***“Miserable is the price they have sold their souls”***, ***“for denying Allah’s revelation”***. The glosses inventory the sale in three registers. Mujāhid records it as the sale of truth for falsehood together with the concealment of the truth about the awaited prophet; al-Suddī records it as the sale of the self itself, the worst purchase being what the buyers chose in place of belief, aid, and support; the inherited reading takes the sold asset as the share of reward in the world to come, so that the transaction is priced in the only currency that matters. The grammar of the exclamation was dissected as well: the indefinite noun inside the formula of blame is a specification that singles out the very thing for criticism, as though the verse were pointing the finger at the item on the counter. The denial named as the purchase's content is not ignorance but disclosure — the revelation was recognized and refused — which is why the price is stated in the language of loss rather than mistake. The market image runs through the whole passage as its accounting system: a small price, a fleeting gain, a trade of the hereafter, and now the souls themselves on the block. The book draws the same trade in its first pages and calls the profit void: (2:16 — **“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance. But this trade is profitless, and they are not ˹rightly˺ guided”**). What follows from the imagery is the verse's way of making envy look like commerce: the buyers paid with themselves and called the payment profit.

**THE ENVY THAT ARGUES WITH THE GIVER**

The second clause names the motive with a precision that no amount of theology has improved: ***“and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants!”***. The resentful particle in the Arabic is the same word for grudge and for envy, and the glosses read it as the reason-object of the disbelief itself — the denial happened because the gift was given where it was given. The target of the envy is therefore not the message but the Giver's discretion, and the tradition states the doctrinal nerve without anesthetics: prophethood is a grace bestowed, not a wage earned, so the grudge against its distribution is a grudge against the discretion of the One who gives. The word for the envy was pressed to its worst case in the transmitted commentary — there is no envy worse than this — because every other envy at least wants what another has, while this one disputes the right of the giving itself. The sentence's exclamation mark is placed exactly there: the revelation is to whomever He wills of His servants, and the will is not on trial. The effect of the clause on every later theology of merit is permanent. The grace that arrives unearned is the only kind that was ever on offer, and the grudge against it is the one vice that argues with the treasury rather than with the rival.

**ANGER RETURNING WITH ITS PREDECESSOR**

The judgment then arrives in the verse's heaviest idiom: ***“They have earned wrath upon wrath.”***. The verb for the earning is the verb of returning home, and the glosses hear the buyers coming back from the market carrying anger — an indefinite anger whose very indefiniteness magnifies its weight. The doubling was read three ways, and the readings agree on the arithmetic. One line counts the first anger as the neglect of the Torah and the second as the denial of Jesus. A second counts the denial of the Injil and its prophet as the first and the denial of the final revelation and its prophet as the second. A third, transmitted from Ibn ʿAbbās in flatter currency, reads the pair as curse upon curse. The structure is therefore cumulative by design: the passage's historians have already shown a community that lost one installment of the truth and then refused the next, and the sentence of the verse is that the installments are billed together at the end. The companion decree of the same history keeps the wording close enough to be a gloss: (3:112 — **“for rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations”**).

**THE DISGRACE THAT IS NOT PURIFICATION**

The closing line names the punishment's species, and the species is the point: ***“And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment”***. The adjective attached to the torment is the adjective of abasement, and the tradition separates this punishment in kind from the discipline of the believers. The chastisement that awaits the sinful believer is purgative — it is measured to purify — while the disgrace named here is terminal, designed to humiliate rather than to mend. The reason for the distinction is stated in the same passages: the disbelief of this class was produced by transgression and envy mastered by arrogance, and the world and the last day answer each part of that chain in its own currency. The same decree elsewhere pairs the scorn of worship with the entry into the fire in humiliation, so that the punishment fits the posture: the pride that refused prostration is sentenced to abasement. The doctrine matters pastorally as well as philosophically, since it keeps the two penitentiaries apart in the reader's mind: the fire of purification and the fire of disgrace are not the same building.

**THE SEQUENCE AS ITS OWN LESSON**

The short verse is a masterclass in sequence-reading. First the sale is priced, then the motive is tried, then the penalty is counted, then its species is named — and each stage excludes the excuse the previous stage might have supplied. The reason the motive-clause stands before the penalty-clause is that the passage is trying the heart rather than the ledger; the envy is what converts the denial from an error into a purchase. For any congregation today that treats divine generosity as a zero-sum rivalry between communities, the middle clause is the corrective in one line: grace is distributed by will, and the will has never been accountable to the envy. What turns on that corrective is the whole posture of the religious life — gratitude for the neighbor's gift instead of resentment at its address.
