**WHAT WAS SOLD, AND FOR WHAT**

The verse puts the whole matter in commercial language and then states what was sold and why: ***“Miserable is the price they have sold their souls”***, ***“for denying Allah's revelation”***, ***“and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants!”***, ***“They have earned wrath upon wrath.”***, ***“And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment”***. The deal is called a sale of the self, a phrase that makes the goods and the buyer one and the same person. Two motives are named in the same sentence, and the second is the one that explains the first: the objection was not to the content of the revelation but to the recipient of God's favour. The clause about grace being given to whom He wills is the Book's answer to the whole dispute, since the community's grievance was about a decision that was never theirs to make; the same sentence is carried in a later sūrah for this people: (3:112 — **“They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go,”**).

**ENVY AS THE MOTIVE BEHIND A THEOLOGICAL OBJECTION**

The Book names resentment of divine grace as a motive in several places, and it treats the feeling as candidly as it treats the deed. A later sūrah raises the same question and answers it with the same reference to God's freedom to give: (4:54 — **“Or do they envy the people for Allah's bounties? Indeed,”**). The point is not that the community had no arguments; the point is that the arguments were assembled after the conclusion. When a person's objection to a message is really an objection to the messenger's standing, the reasoning he produces will never be satisfied by evidence, and the Book's account here shows the mechanism at work on a national scale. Anyone who has seen a decision dismissed for the standing of the man who proposed it will know this pattern, and the passage's interest is in how such arguments are generated rather than in the arguments themselves.

**WRATH UPON WRATH**

The verse describes the consequence in a phrase that doubles what a reader would expect, and the doubling is explained by the sequence of the story. One layer of anger belongs to the rejection of the messengers and the covenant, and another belongs to this refusal of a Book the community had recognised, so that the accumulated account grows with each opportunity that is declined. The Book's habit of describing consequences as accumulated rather than instantaneous is worth noticing, since a reader often assumes that a single act is being answered. The later verses in this sūrah describe a community that resists through several stages, and the Book tracks each stage in the same sentence as its cost. What this means for a reader is that the account of his own conduct is also cumulative, and no single decision stands alone.

**THE TRADER WHO REFUSED THE CONSIGNMENT**

Think of a merchant who has waited years for a consignment, who refuses it at the dock because the shipping agent is from the wrong family, and who then explains the refusal with a story about quality. The consignment had been awaited for generations and was inspected closely enough to be recognised, and it turned it away for reasons that had nothing to do with the goods. The Book's verdict names the humiliation that follows, and the humiliation is not arbitrary; it is the natural end of a position that cannot be defended in the open. Anyone who has seen a group argue itself into a corner for the sake of standing will find the closing words of this verse already visible in the argument. The Book states the outcome plainly so that the pattern can be recognised while it is still in progress.

**THE HUMILIATION THAT CLOSES THE VERSE**

The last clause names a specific kind of punishment, and the choice of humiliation rather than pain is connected to the offence. A community that had been motivated by standing lost standing, and the Book records the correspondence instead of leaving it to the reader. The chapter arranges consequences in this way often, shaping each one after the deed that earned it: the people who traded guidance for error were traded in return, and the people who raised themselves were brought low. Read as one passage from the covenant onward, the account holds together, and its final clause is the last entry in a long ledger rather than a bolt from nowhere. The verse closes on humiliation, and humiliation is the mirror of the envy that produced the refusal.

**AN ACCOUNT OF ONE'S OWN**

The passage teaches that the account of a community's conduct is kept in the same language as its motives, and the same ledger is open to the reader's own audit. A person who resents another's success will dress his objection in principles, and the history of this people shows where that road ends. The remedy is not to suppress the feeling but to notice it, since the objection the community made was answered and the answer did not help them. A reader who wants to use the verse can begin by naming the last time he dismissed a piece of information because of who delivered it, and then checking whether the information was true. That exercise is what the passage asks of a reader, and nothing further needs to be said about it.
