**RETALIATION AND PARDON**

***“O believers! ˹The law of˺ retaliation is set”*** announces a statute in the language of prescription rather than preference, and the field is given in the second span: ***“for you in cases of murder”***. The equality of the law is then drawn in matched pairs — ***“a free man for a free man, a slave”*** and ***“for a slave, and a female”*** and ***“for a female. But”*** — each pair insisting that status does not scale the value of the life taken. Then the verse opens its second chamber: ***“if the offender is pardoned by the victim’s guardian”***, and the settlement follows through ***“then blood-money should be decided fairly”*** and ***“and payment should be made courteously. This is a concession”*** and ***“and a mercy from your Lord.”***. The statute and the mercy are one document.

**MATCHED PAIRS AND THE PARDON CHANNEL**

Retaliation is on equal terms regarding the slain in the attributes of the one slain and in the action involved, and the Sunna clarifies that a male may be killed for a female and that religious affiliation is accounted for. The pairs defend against two distortions: the aristocratic verdict that prices lives differently, and the escalation that multiplies them. The pardon channel receives equal care — the pardon may be partial, and the phrase "his brother" is placed as a conciliatory entreaty, declaring that the killing must not sever the bonds of religious brotherhood. The address softens the parties toward each other while leaving the verdict intact. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the settlement schedule with its fairness intact: three years for the full blood-money, two for the half, one for the third. The reason the verse names its own mercy is that the law is handed to hot-blooded people, and a statute of justice without a mercy valve becomes the engine of feud. (5:45 — **“We ordained for them in the Torah, “A life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose,”**)

**JUSTICE AS A COMPLETE REMEDY**

A hearer may ask whether pardoning a murderer is not an injustice to the one who was killed. The verse separates the two goods involved: the life is protected by the statute, and the family is honoured by the choice. The pardon belongs to the guardian of the blood and not to the offender's faction, so the waiver is a mercy exercised by the one who carries the loss, and the law directs that the decision be met with courtesy and a fair settlement. Justice as a fixed verdict is the statute's first half; justice as a complete remedy is its second, and the injured party stands inside both. The transgression clause guards the mercy from abuse: ***“But whoever transgresses after”*** is answered with ***“that will suffer a painful punishment”***. The one who takes the settlement and then kills again has closed the door the verse had opened.

**THE EARLIER MEASURE**

The sacredness of the life is stated in (17:33 — **“Do not take a ˹human˺ life—made sacred by Allah—except with ˹legal˺ right. If anyone is killed unjustly,”**). On the side of Ibn ʿAbbās's report, the verse was revealed regarding two Arab clans and that the Torah's statement came to settle its measure, and he records the schedule by which the payment is claimed. The application is to hold both halves of the statute in their proper order: the value of the life established by the law, and the freedom of the injured to release the claim within the law's terms. The order protects the weaker party in both directions — the victim's family cannot be pressured into the waiver, and the offender cannot escape the settlement he agreed to. Because the statute is equal and the channel is merciful, the two goods of justice and reconciliation are kept in one document. The transgressor after pardon is punished without leniency, and the pardoner is honoured without obstruction. Retaliation is set, mercy is conceded, and the seam between them is where a people becomes civilised. The seam is narrow by design, because justice without mercy becomes feud and mercy without justice becomes neglect. The statute holds both in tension on purpose, and the tension is the civilisation, because a law that keeps both goods visible trains its people to want the better one. The training works, since the law that names mercy teaches mercy before the need for mercy arrives. The painful punishment is kept ready for the one who breaks the seam.
