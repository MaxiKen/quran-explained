**WATER ASKED FOR, THEN GIVEN FROM STONE**

Thirst in the open wilderness brought the people to their prophet with a request rather than a complaint: ***“And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed”***. The form of his praying carries the sense of asking for water to be given, and the setting supplies its cause, since the wanderings were spent in a land without wells or rivers. An account of their questions in that period records them asking first who would shield them from the heat of the sun, then who would give them food, then who would give them water, and each question was answered by a separate gift. The command that answered the last of them is exact and physical: ***“for water for his people, We said, “Strike the rock with your staff.””***. The staff that strikes was itself carried as a sign through the ages before this day. The transmitted account describes it as a rod of a wood of the Garden, ten cubits long, handed down through the prophets until it reached the hand of Moses. What it had already done marks its place among the signs. Cast down, it moved as a living serpent; struck against the sea, it parted the water into standing walls; struck against the stone here, it opened the ground’s own stores. The miracle therefore attaches to the One who commands the rod, not to the wood that moves. The reason the sign hangs on the command rather than on the rod is that the rod itself is created, and every movement credited to it is movement under orders.

**A TABLE SET WITHOUT TOIL**

The result is counted and assigned: ***“Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place. ˹We then said,˺ “Eat”***. Twelve tribes descended from the twelve sons of Jacob, and the springs were divided so that no group crowded another. Ibn ʿAbbās describes the stone as square, with three springs opening on each of its sides, and he reports that each tribe kept its own spring wherever the camp moved, finding the same provision in each new stopping place (al-Nasāʾī). The Arabic verb for bursting forth marks the water at its full force, where the parallel account describes the same springs at their first flow. What gushes from stone answers the doubt that stone can hold water at all; what is assigned tribe by tribe answers the doubt that a single source could serve a whole people. Sustenance then frames the water: ***“and drink of Allah’s provisions”***. The meat, the sweetness and the drink all arrived without sowing or trade, and that is why the gift is named here as belonging to God directly. What follows from the account is the practice it leaves behind. Seeking rain by humble prayer is established practice in the law, since the essential element in it is the prayer itself, offered by a servant who owns nothing of the cloud or the spring.

**THE FAVOUR THAT MUST NOT BE ANSWERED WITH RUIN**

The permission ends where harm begins: ***“and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.””***. The clause stands beside the eating for a reason. A person fed and watered by a gift he did not produce is the person most exposed to the charge of ingratitude, and ingratitude in practice is what corruption means here. The Book states the order of that ruin elsewhere: (7:56 — **“Do not spread corruption in the land after it has been set in order. And call upon Him with hope”**). Provision sets the land in order; disobedience undoes the setting; the warning therefore precedes the harm rather than following it. The verse also turns the stone into a measure of the heart. The same people who drank as springs burst from rock were addressed later in this chapter about a hardness that exceeds stone: (2:74 — **“Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder, for some rocks gush rivers; others split, spilling water;”**). That comparison fixes the difference between the two states. A stone gave way under the command of God while living hearts held out against it, and the yielding of the one measures the defiance of the other. What follows for any community living on unearned provision is that gratitude takes the form of conduct. The land is not corrupted by drought alone; it is corrupted by hands that eat from a gift and then disobey the One who gave it.
