**A PRAYER FOR WATER ANSWERED OUT OF STONE**

The thirst of the wilderness is answered without rain, without a well, and without a cloud. The verse opens with a request made on behalf of a crowd: ***“And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed”***, ***“for water for his people, We said, “Strike the rock with your staff.””*** The man who prays is the prophet himself, and the request is for the people rather than for himself, which is the first thing the verse shows about him. The answer does not come as a supply lowered from the sky but as an instruction given to him: a command that puts the outcome in his hand and ties it to an act he can perform. The staff he is told to use is the same staff that will later be raised over the sea, and the instrument is named so that the reader sees that the power is not in the wood. What he does with it is obedience, and what comes out of the stone is not his doing.

**TWELVE STREAMS FOR TWELVE FAMILIES**

The result is described with a precision that answers a practical problem: ***“Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place. ˹We then said,˺ “Eat”***. A single spring would have served the crowd and would have turned every morning into a scramble; twelve of them, one to a tribe, removed the crowding along with the thirst. The same event is told again among the favours recounted to this people elsewhere, where the division into tribes and the water are placed in one breath: (7:160 — **“We divided them into twelve tribes—each as a community. And We revealed to Moses,”**). The detail fits the way this chapter treats its history, since the favours it lists are always shaped to the community's own arrangement, and the arrangement here is the twelve houses descended from one man. A reader who keeps that in view sees why the number matters: the gift was measured out to the people as a body with parts, and no part was left out.

**A STONE GIVING WHAT A WELL COULD NOT**

The verse leaves the shape of the stone and the size of the staff unmentioned, and the reports that fill those gaps were never meant to carry the weight of the event itself. The stone is described in one account attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās as a square block with three openings on each side, so that each tribe drank from its own mouth, while other narrations trace the staff back through the prophets to Adam. A reading that treats the wording as naming a kind of stone rather than one particular rock has been preferred by some scholars, on the ground that a command which works on any stone of that kind displays more power than one bound to a single known object. The size of the staff and the way it is said to have shone are reported too, and a reader may take such details for what they are worth without resting the verse upon them. What the Book itself insists on is the contrast that comes later in this sūrah, when hearts are described as harder than rock even though some rocks split and pour out water: (2:74 — **“Even then your hearts became hardened like a rock or even harder,”**). A stone produced streams on demand, and the people who drank from them would later prove harder than the stone they had struck.

**EATING, DRINKING, AND THE CORRUPTION THAT FOLLOWS**

The command that closes the verse carries two halves, and the second is the reason the first is mentioned at all: ***“and drink of Allah’s provisions”***, ***“and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.””*** Full stomachs are not the end of the matter, because a people who eat well and forget the giver has corrupted the thing they were given. The same pairing appears in the address to this community in another sūrah, where the provision and the warning are set side by side: (20:81 — **“˹saying,˺ “Eat from the good things We have provided for you,”**). The phrase rendered as spreading corruption carries the sense of an act that spoils what lies around the person doing it, whether by disobedience or by injustice to others, and the two are usually found together in a community that has stopped being grateful. The verse therefore ends where a reader's own life begins: water in the desert is a gift, and the gift creates an obligation to keep the ground it fell on in good order.

**THE LESSON A READER CARRIES OUT OF THE DESERT**

There is a plain comparison here for anyone who has ever been given what he asked for and then grown bored with it. A family that prays through a hard month and is brought through it will sometimes attribute the relief to its own cleverness within a year, and the change of heart is invisible while it happens. The people in this story were given water out of rock, with no work of their own in the matter, and the verse still had to end with a warning against spreading corruption. That is a measure of how quickly a gift can be separated from the memory of the giver, and the danger does not belong to one nation only. The instruction about the staff is also worth keeping, because it shows what the answer required of Moses: he was told to act, not to wait for the water to appear beside him, and the acting cost nothing but obedience. A reader who takes that seriously will not look for the provision to arrive without the strike, nor treat the strike as the source of what follows from it.
