**ONE WORD CHANGED AND A PUNISHMENT SENT**

The command of the previous verse is answered with a substitution: ***“But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say.”***, ***“So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them”***, ***“for their rebelliousness”***. The Book does not say that the people refused to enter the city. It says that they changed the words, which is a smaller act with a larger meaning. The same event is set out in another sūrah with the instruction that preceded it: (7:161 — **“And ˹remember˺ when it was said to them, “Enter this city ˹of Jerusalem˺ and eat from wherever you please. Say,”**). The sentence they had been told to say was an admission of need, and the substitution turned it into something else, so the posture of the entering changed while the entering itself continued. The punishment named in the second clause follows from that change rather than from an outright revolt. The same account is given in another sūrah with the alteration itself described, and the reports of the early teachers explain what the substitution involved: a phrase turned into its opposite, or a word twisted while the letter of the instruction was preserved. The Book's interest is not in the syllables they used. It is in the fact that a people who had been given an easy command found a way to comply with it while emptying it, and the punishment is attached to that manoeuvre. The verse is a warning about the kind of obedience that keeps the form and discards the meaning. Ibn ʿAbbās transmitted that the commanded plea was exchanged for a mocking word and that the descending punishment was a plague, so both the alteration and its consequence were public.

**A MOCKING CHANGE WITH A PUBLIC COST**

A reader may wonder why a few words would bring down a punishment from the heavens, and the answer is in the sentence that had been given to them. The words were an act of submission, and the point of submission is the surrender of the right to rephrase. A person who accepts a command and then alters it to suit himself has kept the appearance of obedience while recapturing the authority that the command was meant to take from him. That is why the Book treats the editing of the formula as rebellion, and it is why the same charge appears later in the chapter against people who distort the scripture with their own hands. The Book also shows what the substitution accomplished in the community, because a changed formula becomes a changed practice and then a changed community. The generation that rewrote the sentence would raise children who never knew the original, and the loss would look like tradition by the time anyone noticed. The Qur'an's attention to wording is therefore not pedantry; it is an early warning about a decay that begins in language and ends in worship. The punishment from the heavens is reported as the end of a process that started with a phrase.

**KEEPING THE FORM AND LOSING THE MEANING**

The practical lesson for a reader is to check where he has been substituting his own wording for what he was given. The substitutions are usually reasonable-sounding: a prayer said with attention elsewhere, a fast kept while the temper is not, a charity given for the audience, a testimony adjusted to the listeners. The Book's example is a community that was promised a reward for good-doing and changed a request for pardon into something else, and the mechanism is available to anyone who wants to use it. The verse attaches a price to the mechanism, and it attaches it without ambiguity. A reader can begin by choosing one practice he performs on most days and examining the words he uses in it, without changing anything yet. What is wanted is that he notice the gap between the form and the meaning, since that gap is where the rebellion of this verse lives. A person who restores one formula to its intended sense has done what the people in this story were told to do at the gate, and he has done it before the punishment rather than after it. Today, sacred language can be preserved on a page while its demand is edited out of contracts, policies and public conduct. It is like altering one line in a safety instruction until the sign remains polished but directs people toward danger.
