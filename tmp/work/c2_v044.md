**THE TEACHERS WHO DO NOT DO WHAT THEY TEACH**

The second half of the address turns on the men themselves, and the questions come in a pair: ***“Do you preach righteousness”***, ***“and fail to practice it yourselves”***, ***“although you read the Scripture?”***, ***“Do you not understand”***. Nothing in the sentence disputes their learning; the reading is granted in the middle of the accusation. What is being questioned is the gap between the lesson and the life of the one delivering it, and the closing question asks whether such a person has understood the first thing about the book he carries, which he has presumably read more than once. The Book names this failure without any softening. It puts the charge in the mouth of the Lord and lets it stand without a defence.

The same rebuke is given to the community in another sūrah in a single line, and the brevity there is what makes it unforgettable: (61:3 — **“How despicable it is in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do”**). The Book elsewhere shows what a teacher is supposed to be, and the alternative is stated as a role rather than a technique: (3:79 — **“Rather, he would say, “Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺”—in”**), with the rest of that verse explaining that the model of a scholar is a person whose teaching is visible in his own devotion. The contrast between the two passages is the whole moral: the Book honours the teacher and despises the performance.

**WHY THIS CHARGE IS DIRECTED AT TEACHERS FIRST**

A reader might ask why the sūrah spends its sharpest words on the people who at least know the text. The answer is in the structure of the community. A teacher's practice sets the ceiling for everyone who learns from him, and a man who instructs others in a discipline he does not keep teaches two lessons at once, the second of which is that the discipline is optional. The Book treats that as a heavier offence than private negligence, because its damage is multiplied by every student the man has. That is why the address in this sūrah reaches the scholars before it reaches anyone else in the audience. There is also a mercy inside the rebuke. The verse does not tell the teachers to stop teaching; it asks them to stop being the exception to their own instruction, which means the correction can happen without a change of office. A person in that position is not asked to give up his place but to earn it in the only way the Book recognises, by doing what he says. The question at the end of the line, with its invitation to think, is the door left open for exactly that. The Book keeps the door open even while it names the offence in the plainest possible terms.

**THE GAP A READER CAN MEASURE**

The line reaches every reader who has ever passed on advice, which is to say all of them. A parent instructs, a friend advises, a colleague corrects, and the same gap opens up wherever the instruction outruns the life. The measure the Book gives is not perfection, since nobody keeps his own advice completely. The measure is the direction of the gap: whether a person is moving toward the standard he describes or using his knowledge as a licence to remain where he is. A teacher who is visibly ashamed of his own shortfall teaches something valuable. A teacher who is untroubled by it has already been described by this verse. One practical step is to take a piece of advice given to someone in the last month and apply the same standard to himself this week. That act is the answer the verse is looking for, and it requires no audience. A person who does it quietly in one matter has put the question of this verse behind him, at least until the next time somebody asks him for guidance and he has to look in the mirror first.
