**COUNSEL THAT FAILS TO REACH THE COUNSELLOR**

The call to join the worshippers is followed by a challenge to those who direct others: ***“Do you preach righteousness”***. Righteousness covers obedience to God, not one isolated act of kindness. Ibn ʿAbbās describes teachers in Madinah who told Muslim relatives and close associates to remain firm in following Muhammad, acknowledging that his message was true. Yet they did not follow him themselves. Their advice showed that the difficulty was not simply a lack of knowledge about the message. Another early explanation, from Ibn Jurayj, includes commands to pray and fast that the speakers themselves neglected. These applications belong to the same charge: a person correctly identifies a duty, then behaves as if it belongs only to others. The question therefore does not condemn good counsel. It asks why the counsellor has left himself outside it. Nor is this warning confined to its first audience. Believers hear the corresponding challenge, (61:2 — **“O believers! Why do you say what you do not do”**). Teaching a true rule does not give the teacher an exemption from it; the very reason offered to the hearer also bears upon the speaker.

The contradiction is exposed in ***“and fail to practice it yourselves,”***. The neglect is not an innocent lapse of memory that makes the duty unknown. Their public instruction shows that they retain the knowledge. What is missing is its claim upon their own conduct. Because speaking well can earn respect, a person may begin to live on the credit of an instruction he never follows. The account carried by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from Usāmah ibn Zayd makes the danger plain: the Prophet describes a man punished in the Fire who had commanded good and forbidden evil while doing the opposite. The man circles with his entrails trailing, like a donkey turning a mill. Those who knew his counsel ask how he came to such an end, and he admits the split between his speech and his deeds. The report does not say that his hearers were wrong to follow sound advice. It shows that benefiting them did not excuse his own refusal. A message may guide others while its teacher remains in danger from the very wrong he warned them against.

**LEARNING MUST BIND THE ONE WHO POSSESSES IT**

The added circumstance, ***“although you read the Scripture?”***, removes another excuse. They are not strangers to the written command. Reading includes studying what the Book teaches, as Ibn ʿAbbās explains, and the Scripture in this address is the Torah. Its presence should bring the command closer to them. Instead, the contrast between a well-read tongue and a disobedient life makes their conduct more blameworthy. The Quran states the gravity of that divide: (61:3 — **“How despicable it is in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do”**). Since the speaker already knows what he commends, he cannot treat his instruction as a payment that clears the debt of action. Knowledge is entrusted for obedience, not only for public use. This does not mean that a person with less learning has no duty to learn or act. It means that greater knowledge takes away the claim that the demand was unseen. The warning reaches every teacher who can find the rule for a pupil, neighbour, or family member but refuses to let the same rule govern his own choices.

The closing appeal, ***“Do you not understand”***, asks for judgement that reaches conduct. It is not a claim that those addressed lack the mental ability to read, argue, or remember. Their failure lies in refusing the conclusion their own teaching requires. Understanding should hold a person back from a known harm and direct him toward a known good. The Arabic sense associated with reason includes restraint, as a tether keeps an animal from straying. The image explains what is lacking: the knowledge has not restrained its possessor. Wisdom is a great gift, (2:269 — **“And whoever is granted wisdom is certainly blessed with a great privilege.”**), but possessing information about a good path is not the same as walking it. This is why the rebuke can address learned people without contradiction. Their learning supplies the premises; their conduct rejects the conclusion. Sound judgement would turn the charge inward before using it against someone else. It would ask the teacher to place himself under the command he has already explained, rather than search for a privilege attached to the role of explaining it.

The remedy is neither silence nor a pretence of being faultless. Saʿīd ibn Jubayr warns that if complete freedom from faults were required before anyone urged good, such counsel would cease. Al-Ḥasan likewise rejects a fear that lets the duty of advising others disappear. There are two obligations: to act rightly and to call others toward what is right. Failure in one does not release a person from the other. A man who neglects prayer must correct that neglect; he does not repair it by also refusing to encourage his child to pray. Yet the permission to give counsel is no excuse to remain unchanged. The prophetic model is Shuʿayb’s declaration, (11:88 — **“I do not want to do what I am forbidding you from.”**). His stated aim joins reform of others to faithfulness in himself. The right response to the warning is therefore to keep truthful counsel, confess personal shortcomings without defending them, and work to remove the contradiction. The verse makes learning more demanding precisely because knowledge brings the command within reach.
