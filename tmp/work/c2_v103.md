**FAITH AND RESTRAINT LEFT UNCHOSEN**

The verse that closes this stretch turns from what the community did to what it might have had, and it opens with a wish: ***“If only they were faithful”***. The word for faithfulness here carries the sense of holding to something with confidence, and the Book places it first, before any mention of the reward that would have followed. The previous verse ended with the price the community paid for an art that could not profit it, and this sentence supplies the alternative it passed over. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the wish as a statement about what was available rather than a regret about what was impossible, which is the reading the grammar supports: the condition was within reach of the people being addressed. The method on display is the Book's habit of closing a section this way, describing a loss in terms of a choice that was open.

**FAITH AND MINDFULNESS NAMED TOGETHER**

The wish is completed by a second quality, and the two are joined in a single phrase: ***“and mindful ˹of Allah˺, there would have been a better reward from Allah”***. Faithfulness and mindfulness are treated as the two halves of one posture, and the Book consistently pairs them when it describes what it means to hold a religion rather than to merely inherit one. The reward is described as better rather than large, which measures it against what the community actually received in the transaction just described. Someone who has been given a great deal and who has watched the same condition appear in the sources will recognise the comparison being drawn here. The sentence does not say the reward would have been infinite or immediate; it says it would have been better than the thing the buyers took instead, because the comparison being drawn is with the bargain the community actually struck.

**THE REWARD THAT WOULD HAVE FOLLOWED**

The phrase about the reward is placed where the reader expects a description of profit, and its wording is deliberately plain: what was on offer is named as a reward from Allah rather than as a technique, a status, or a possession. The contrast with the magic described in the preceding verse is exact, since that practice brought harm to its buyers while this reward is described from the hand of the One who gives it. The point of naming the giver rather than the gift is that the terms belong to the giver, and no resentment can alter them. Another sūrah states the rule this sentence assumes, and states it as a general law rather than a promise to one community: (16:96 — **“Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting”**). The verse's wish is therefore about a durable return rather than a temporary advantage, and that contrast is the subject of the whole passage. A reader who measures his own choices by what lasts will find the comparison here easy to apply.

**A TRADER WHO KEEPS TWO SETS OF BOOKS**

Think of a trader who keeps two sets of books: the one he shows when he wants a quick return, and the one that would have grown quietly if he had credited it and left it alone. The community addressed here had a scheme beside it that cost more than it paid, and the verse names the alternative with the simplicity of a single word. The comparison is not between two equal offers; it is between something that ends and something that is described as coming from Allah, which places the two beyond any real competition. What turns on the comparison is the whole weight of the verse's regret, since the community is not accused of choosing a worse bargain by a small margin. Anyone who has watched a business kept alive for years on an arrangement everyone recognised as ruinous has seen the pattern, and the verse's regret is aimed at the recognition rather than the loss. What the sentence offers is the plain arithmetic of the choice.

**THE KNOWING THAT CHANGES NOTHING**

The verse ends where it began, with the same clause, and the repetition turns the wish into an assessment: ***“if only they knew”***. The Book has already stated, in the previous verse, that the buyers knew what they were taking on, so the closing clause is not a comment on missing information. It describes the gap between knowing a fact and letting the fact govern a decision, which is the subject the section has been building toward throughout. The clause is read by Ibn ʿAbbās as a statement about the community's knowledge of the scripture in its hands, and the reading fits the placement of the verse after the account of a covenant discarded and a Book cast away. What the reader carries away from the sentence is a question about whether his own conduct is governed by what he knows, and the question applies to the reader before it applies to anyone else. Today, a profitable skill remains a loss when its reward depends on abandoning faith, restraint and the welfare of another person.
