**ADAM’S GIFT BECOMES KNOWLEDGE SHARED WITH OTHERS**

The command turns the hidden gift into a public act: ***“Allah said, “O Adam!”*** ***“Inform them of their names.””*** In verse 31 the angels were asked to tell God, who already knew. Here Adam is told to tell the angels, who do not yet know. The change gives knowledge a social purpose. Adam is not taught merely to possess a private rank; what he received now becomes a means by which another creation learns. This is fitting for the father of a race whose members will depend on teaching. A name can pass from teacher to student, parent to child, witness to judge, and one age to the next. The knowledge may begin as a distinction, but its good appears when it is spoken truthfully for those who need it. Early readers therefore saw Adam placed in the role of a teacher before the angels in this scene. The honour does not make him the source of truth. He can inform them because God first informed him. That order protects both learning and teaching from pride. A teacher gives what was received, and a student may hold another gift the teacher lacks. Knowledge remains a trust moving among creatures, while its first source and final measure belong to God.

**ADAM SHOWS THE GIFT RATHER THAN CLAIMING IT**

The verse continues, ***“Then when Adam did, Allah said, “Did I not tell you”***. Adam names what is presented, and the difference announced in the prior verses becomes plain. This matters because a claim of knowledge and the faithful use of knowledge are not the same. The first may be made by anyone; the second answers the object, moment, and hearer with truth. Adam’s performance shows the capacity God gave him. It also answers the angels without insulting their worship or erasing their own excellence. They lacked these names because they had not been taught them, and they had already confessed that limit. Now they learn. Human beings should not turn this event into a general right to boast over angels, much less over other people. Adam’s honour rests in a gift he obeyed God by using. The same pattern governs later learning. Memory without truth can mislead, speech without duty can manipulate, and skill without obedience can serve corruption. The reason knowledge supports human authority is that it can identify what exists, distinguish duties, guide action, and be passed on. When it is used to blur names and hide reality, it undermines the very trust this scene establishes.

**THE UNSEEN EXCEEDS EVERY CREATED FIELD OF VIEW**

The reminder is ***“that I know the secrets of the heavens”*** ***“and the earth, and I know what you reveal”***. The secrets are the unseen realities hidden from created senses and inference. They include what has not yet occurred, what lies outside a creature’s place, and what no one could know unless God disclosed it. Heaven did not make the unseen open to the angels merely because they dwell in a higher realm, just as life on earth will not make it open to Adam’s children. Each creature sees from a given field; God knows heaven and earth without boundary. Another verse states, (35:38 — **“Indeed, Allah is the Knower of the unseen of the heavens”**) and then joins that knowledge to what hearts hide. This explains how God could answer the angels’ concern with certainty. He knew not only the violence that would occur but every prophet, act of justice, repentance, discovery, prayer, and mercy that no angel had yet seen. The unknown future was not unknown to Him. Therefore, the words do not tell believers to call every mystery solved. They teach where complete knowledge rests. Study can widen a human field, and revelation can disclose what senses cannot reach. Neither turns a limited knower into the Knower of all secrets.

**OPEN SPEECH AND HIDDEN MOTIVE STAND UNDER ONE KNOWLEDGE**

The final words are ***“and what you conceal?””*** God knew the angels’ spoken concern and whatever had not been spoken. Early explanations differ about the concealed matter. Some link it to an inward claim attributed to the angels about their greater knowledge or rank. Others link it to the pride Iblīs kept hidden, which the next verse will expose when he refuses the command concerning Adam. Ibn ʿAbbās also relates the words to what would be shown and withheld in the coming act of obedience. The verse itself leaves the object broad, so one disputed report should not be made its only sense. Its certain claim is wider: disclosure and concealment are equally open to God. The Quran tells every person, (3:29 — **“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah.”**). This knowledge reaches beyond polished speech to the motive beneath it. A public act of praise cannot hide pride, and an unseen sincere act cannot be lost. The effect should be both fear and comfort. Hidden sin needs repentance even when reputation remains clean; hidden faith and effort remain known even when no human being thanks them. In this scene, divine knowledge closes the gap between appearance and reality before the command to prostrate makes each inward stance visible through action.
