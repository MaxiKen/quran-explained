**THE STUDENT IS ASKED, AND THE TEACHER SPEAKS AGAIN**

The examination ends, and the reader is shown the answer being given by the one who had been taught: ***“Allah said, “O Adam!”***, ***“Inform them of their names.””***, then ***“Then when Adam did, Allah said, “Did I not tell you”***, ***“that I know the secrets of the heavens”***, ***“and the earth, and I know what you reveal”***, ***“and what you conceal?””***. The command to Adam comes before the lesson is drawn, which is how the scene stays honest. The angels did not hear a claim about Adam's knowledge; they heard him name the things in front of them. Only after the demonstration does the Lord return to the exchange that opened the scene, and the question he asks is a reminder rather than a reproach.

The final clause reaches further than the angels' doubt, and that reach is the reason the verse sits at the heart of the passage. It is not only the open question that was known; what each creature keeps hidden is known as well. The Book makes this a standing theme, and the same words appear in a later sūrah where the listener is told plainly what the heart conceals: (2:284 — **“Whether you reveal what is in your hearts or conceal it, Allah will call you to account for it.”**). The scene among the angels is therefore a small illustration of a general fact, and the reader who has followed the story is being asked to apply the fact to himself before the chapter moves on.

**A LESSON DRAWN AFTER THE DEMONSTRATION**

The order of the scene is deliberate and it repeats throughout the Book. God does not settle the matter by announcing the verdict; he lets the test run, so that nobody is asked to accept a judgement he has not seen. This is part of how the Qur'an argues. The idols are challenged to speak, the false claimants are challenged to produce, and here the critics are invited to examine what a creature has been given. Then the conclusion is drawn in the presence of the ones who raised the question. A reader can take confidence from the method itself, because it means the Book is willing to be examined while it makes its claims rather than after the fact. The other side of the method is that the answer arrives with a reminder of what people hide. The angels had one question, and the closing words of the verse widen the frame to everything that is kept back: the error covered over, the motive smoothed out of an account, the private arrangement that no audience ever sees. The Book returns to this widening move often because it is where the reader lives. He can follow a story about angels without difficulty; being told that his own concealed matters are known is a different exercise altogether. The verse puts both in one breath, and the chapter expects the reader to carry the two together from here onward.

**THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN KNOWING AND BEING KNOWN**

A reader can draw one practical distinction from the passage and carry it through the week. He knows a small amount, he can be wrong about most of it, and he is fully known. Those three facts arrange a life differently from the way most people arrange theirs. The first keeps him learning, the second keeps him humble, and the third keeps him honest when nobody is checking. The angels in the scene model the first two, and the last clause of the verse is aimed at the third, which is the one the reader controls. For a reader, the work is straightforward. Take one thing that has been kept concealed, whether from people or from an account a person gives about himself, and deal with it before the Lord who already knows it. The verse does not suggest that everything must be said publicly; the Book is not asking its reader to broadcast his faults. It is asking him to stop arranging his life as though the concealment were real, and to act on the fact that the one who taught Adam the names also knows what each of his descendants keeps back.
