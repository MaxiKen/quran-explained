**A NEW GROUP ENTERS THE SŪRAH**

The sūrah has described the guided and then the people who cover the truth, and now it names a third kind: ***“And there are some”***. The early teachers identified them as the pretenders, the people who lived inside the community and worked against it, and Ibn ʿAbbās read the opening as a description of people whose outward confession and inward state do not match. The Book gives this group more space at the head of the sūrah than it gives the deniers, and the reason is practical. A denier can be seen and counted; a pretender sits in the same gathering, speaks the same words and shares the same meals, and the harm he can do is larger because nobody knows where to stand in relation to him. The sūrah prepares the reader for that difficulty by naming the group before it describes their behaviour.

**PUBLIC CLAIM WITHOUT INNER FAITH**

The verse quotes what they say: ***“who say”***, ***“We believe in Allah”***, ***“and the Last Day”***. The claim is stated exactly, and the wording matters. The pretenders confessed faith in God and in the Day of Judgment and left the messenger out of the sentence, and the reason for the omission is easy to see, since naming him would have forced them into either a lie they could not manage or a denial that would have exposed them. The omission is a small thing in the mouth and a large thing in meaning, because the message of this Book is not a set of beliefs about God that a person can hold while standing apart from the man who brought it. The Book records their speech again in another sūrah and marks it with a witness of its own: (63:1 — **“but Allah bears witness that the hypocrites are truly liars”**). The reader is being shown a person who says the right words and is measured by something other than his words.

**CLAIM SPOKEN BACK TO ITS SPEAKERS**

The verse closes by denying the claim: ***“yet they are not ˹true˺ believers”***. The denial is placed after the quotation so that the reader hears the profession first and then hears the answer to it, and the two sit in one sentence without any argument between them. Ibn ʿAbbās read the closing as meaning that they do not believe secretly, whatever they announce openly, and al-Kalbī and others read the group as those whose inside and outside differ. The Book returns to the same people later and gives the reason their speech cannot carry them anywhere, since a confession made for an audience is measured by the audience and not by God: (4:142 — **“Surely the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but He outwits them”**). What turns on this verse for the reader is that speech is not the measure of faith in the Book, since the Book measures a person by a confession that his conduct confirms, and the next verses describe the conduct of these men in detail.

**TWO-FACED SPEECH UNDER ONE ROOF**

Think of a man who signs a contract to satisfy the people in the room while intending from the first day to break every clause that costs him anything. The signature is real and the paper is real, and none of it changes his position for a moment. The verse describes a party of such people inside the community, and it does so without any bitterness, because the reader is being taught to know what he is dealing with. These days the same type can be found in any institution, speaking the language of the group while working for something else, and the sūrah will describe them fully before it moves on to the people whose confession matches their conduct. What the reader is meant to take from the passage is caution about himself first, since the difference between the two groups is invisible from the outside and only the person himself knows which one he is. The passage is not an invitation to hunt for pretenders in a community, and the Book never asks the believers to appoint themselves as judges of what is hidden.
