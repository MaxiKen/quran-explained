**THE THIRD GROUP AT THE DOOR**

Two kinds of listener have been described, and now a third appears in the opening: ***“And there are some”***, a phrase that introduces people who are inside the community and not of it. The line then gives them their words: ***“who say, “We believe in Allah”*** and ***“and the Last Day,””***, and closes with the denial, ***“yet they are not ˹true˺ believers”***. What separates them from the group in the previous verses is that they do not attack the message; they repeat it. They use the vocabulary of belief, they attend where the believers attend, and they keep a way out on the other side. The sūrah gives them more space than either of the other two groups, because their danger is not visible from the road. Notice what they affirm and what they leave out. They name God, which costs a person nothing and offends nobody, and they name the Last Day, which is the belief that changes how time and money are spent. Nothing is said about prayer, giving, or obedience in the difficult matters, and the silence is the charge against them. The point of the verse is that a correct opinion about God and the judgement can sit beside a life that behaves as though neither had a claim on it. The line marks the exact place where words and conduct part company, and it does so with a verb that could hardly be more ordinary: they say.

**WHAT A BORROWED CONFESSION COSTS**

The judgement in this verse is delivered without adjectives. No motive is assigned beyond the fact that the words are said, and no insult is attached to the speaker. That restraint is deliberate, since the verses that follow explain the rest of the story at their own pace. A reader who wonders why anyone would do this does not have to look far: honesty about the cost of belief can be expensive in a community where belief brings enemies, and staying on both sides keeps a person's options open. The Book names the calculation for what it is instead of guessing at intentions, which leaves the reader free to recognise the same arithmetic in himself if it is there. The Qur'an returns to this group many times, and one description reads like a portrait of the habit: (63:1 — **“When the hypocrites come to you ˹O Prophet˺, they say, “We bear witness”**). The wording of the declaration and the reality behind it are separated in that verse as they are here. Another line shows what such people do when a hard duty arrives: (24:47 — **“And the hypocrites say, “We believe in Allah and the Messenger, and we obey.””**), and then, a few words later, a group of them turns away. That is the pattern wherever it appears. The words run ahead of the conduct, and the gap is discovered at the moment of cost rather than in the middle of a lecture.

**WHY THIS ONE IS HARDER THAN THE OTHERS**

The position of this group inside the community makes the harm worse for everyone, including themselves. A man who says the right words while keeping his options open acquires influence over people who trust him, and he teaches by example that the claims of the Book can be held loosely. His own case is worse than the case of the openly hostile, because he is not the victim of ignorance; he is the victim of a decision he can revisit any day and does not. The door has not been sealed, as in the earlier verse. He is standing in it, holding it half open with his foot, and the sūrah will go on to describe where that posture ends. The plain use of the verse for a reader is short and uncomfortable. It asks him to compare the sentences he uses with the life that follows them, and to do it in private rather than in front of an audience. That comparison can be done badly, in the form of self-accusation that ends in despair, and it can be done well by taking one point of the gap and closing it this week. Today the honest version of the exercise takes five minutes and costs a little pride. The sūrah does not expel this group immediately; it describes them accurately until they recognise themselves, and a reader should take that chance while it is on offer.
