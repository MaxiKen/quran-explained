**LIFE GIVEN AGAIN TO THOSE WHO HAD LOST IT**

The thunderbolt was not the last act in the account: ***“Then We brought you back to life”***. Moses had appealed to his Lord for the people. His prayer expressed concern for them and for what their loss would mean when he returned to the rest of Israel. Yet the act of restoring life is attributed to God, not to a power belonging to Moses. Qatādah explains that those struck down were raised to complete the remainder of their allotted lives. Al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas likewise distinguishes the death that came as punishment from their return to live out their remaining term. The gift was more than release from fear. Life itself, which they could not preserve by their demand or recover by their own effort, was given again. The Quran states the Lord’s power over that whole course: (22:66 — **“He is the One Who gave you life, then will cause you to die, and then will bring you back to life”**). Because the same Lord gives life and governs its end, their revival does not require another power to defeat death on His behalf. Moses’ appeal is heard through God’s mercy, and the restored people are again able to respond to the One whose message they had challenged.

The qualification ***“after your death”*** fixes the nature of the event. They had not merely fallen asleep, lost interest in worship, or received new teaching after being ignorant. The word for raising can elsewhere refer to stirring a sleeper or sending a messenger, but those senses do not cancel the plain qualification here. The Quran can describe loss of consciousness expressly, as when (7:143 — **“Moses collapsed unconscious”**). This account instead speaks of death followed by life. A figurative reading about education would therefore remove the very condition by which the gift is identified. Reading the clause as an appointment to prophethood would likewise add a role that these words do not name. The literal restoration is the reading followed here because it fits both the fatal stroke and the stated return. The verse answers the preceding danger directly. Those who could not bear what overtook them were brought back by the Lord who had power over them throughout. Their weakness was real, and so was the mercy that restored them.

**RETURNED TO LIFE, NOT RELEASED FROM DUTY**

This return belongs to their earthly history, not to the general resurrection for the final judgement. Other extraordinary restorations are also recalled in the chapter. Of those who fled in fear of death it says, (2:243 — **“Allah said to them, “Die!” then He gave them life”**). Such events show the power of the One who will raise all people; they do not mean that every person’s final resurrection has already taken place. The universal promise remains, (23:16 — **“then on the Day of Judgment you will be resurrected”**). An exceptional sign and the general course of human life can therefore be affirmed together. The people restored here returned to a life in which they could receive commands, obey, refuse, and give thanks. They had not passed into a state where earthly duty no longer applied. This distinction also prevents the miracle from becoming a demand that every dead person must now be restored whenever a doubter asks. God chooses the occasion of a sign. The future raising remains His promise, and its certainty does not depend on people being able to bring its time forward for themselves.

The purpose of their return is ***“so that perhaps you would be grateful”***. The same people who had set a condition on belief now owed thanks for a life they could not give themselves. Gratitude in this setting includes accepting the guidance they had resisted. A reading that exempts them from duty because they have witnessed such an event misses this continuing demand. Their unusual experience did not make all obedience needless; the restored life was itself an opportunity to obey. Ibn Zayd’s account records Moses again urging them to take God’s Book after their revival. That fits the purpose stated here more closely than a claim that they had moved beyond commands altogether. Since the gift restores the ability to act, the fitting response is a faithful use of that ability. It is not a boast that the experience has placed them above the ordinary servants of God. The story also speaks to the later hearers through their people’s continued life. They inherit the fruits of mercy, not a right to dismiss the message. The renewed life becomes a further reason to heed the Lord whose guidance Moses had brought.
