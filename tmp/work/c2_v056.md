**LIFE GIVEN BACK AFTER THE STROKE**

The scene of the demand has a sequel that the sūrah reports in one sentence: ***“Then We brought you back to life after your death”***, and ***“so that perhaps you would be grateful”***. Nothing is said about the interval, and the mechanism is not explained. It states that a dead people were revived, and then it attaches the usual purpose to the gift. Everything in the sentence is a repetition of the pattern the chapter has been building: a benefit given without a claim to it, and a purpose attached to the giving. The reader has seen the same ending after the crossing of the sea and after the pardon of the calf, and he is meant to recognise the refrain.

The closeness of the two verses is the point a reader should not miss. In one sentence the people demand to see God and are struck down; in the next they are given their lives back. The mercy follows the presumption immediately and without a probationary period, and the Book presents the sequence as a fact about how its Lord deals with a stubborn people. The same arrangement is repeated later in the sūrah when a shadow and a supply of food are described as favours given to the same community: (2:57 — **“And ˹remember when˺ We shaded you with clouds and sent down to you manna and quails, ˹saying˺,”**), and the sentence continues with the invitation to eat from what has been provided.

**A GIFT THAT CAME AFTER DEATH**

The Book keeps using death and revival as the plainest illustration of what it can do, and the reader is being taught to draw the conclusion rather than simply to marvel. A power that can return a person to his body after a bolt has killed him can also raise the whole of humanity at the end of time, and the argument from the smaller case to the larger runs through the Qur'an from beginning to end. The people in this scene were given a demonstration rather than a proof, which is exactly what they had demanded one verse earlier, and the Book notes the irony without comment. The second clause then names what the demonstration was for. The purpose is not that they would be convinced of a doctrine but that they would become grateful, which returns the reader to the theme the sūrah has been pressing since it described the favours of the exodus. Gratitude in the Book is the response that keeps a person in contact with reality, and a person who has been given his life back twice has more reason for it than anyone. The verse leaves the response open, as it must, and it names the response it hopes for.

**THE DEBT A SECOND CHANCE CREATES**

Every reader can find a version of this verse in his own history, since most people have been brought back from something: an illness, an accident, a decision that could have ended differently. The Book treats such recoveries as gifts and not as luck, and it asks the same thing of them that it asked of the people in this scene. The obligation is not a debt to be settled but a direction to be taken, and the sūrah will shortly spell out the direction in detail when it turns to the commands that follow. A reader can take the verse in hand by naming the second chance he has been given and the thing he intends to do with it, in one sentence, without decoration. The Book does not require a person to be afraid of the past; it requires him to be honest about the present. A life returned is a life that belongs to someone else, and the verse says so in the plainest way its wording allows.
