**THE PURPOSE OF FASTING**

For the purpose of fasting, the decisive wording is ***“O believers! Fasting is prescribed”***, ***“for you as it was”***. Allah announces the obligation of fasting with the call *O believers.* This summons honours faith while also preparing the community for a demanding act of worship. Fasting—*ṣawm* or *ṣiyām*—is abstaining from food, drink, and the other things that invalidate the fast from dawn until sunset, with an intention for Allah. The verse does not say simply that fasting makes a person hungry. It says so that you may become mindful. Fasting trains the servant to leave a lawful desire at a lawful time because Allah commanded it. That training can then restrain him from unlawful desires when no human observer is present. The hidden nature of fasting makes it an especially strong school of sincerity. Fasting was prescribed to communities before the Muslims. The details may have differed, but the spiritual principle is ancient: human beings need disciplined abstention, remembrance, and gratitude. Islam therefore does not present fasting as an alien invention. It restores and completes a practice found in the prophetic heritage. This continuity honours earlier messengers while establishing the distinct legislation given to Muhammad ﷺ. Muslims do not assume that every religious practice currently attributed to previous communities remains unchanged, but they recognize that revelation has always cultivated self-control and devotion. (19:26 — **“so I am not talking to anyone today.’””**) Because the purpose of fasting governs 2:183, mindfulness and the inner self arises from the purpose of fasting. It follows that fasting and compassion completes the purpose of fasting through mindfulness and the inner self.

**MINDFULNESS AND THE INNER SELF**

Read under taqwā and the inner self, the relevant clauses are ***“for those before you”***. The fasting person builds a protective barrier between his soul and disobedience. He experiences thirst but does not drink, hunger but does not eat, and desire but waits for Allah’s permission. The heart learns that not every urge must be obeyed immediately. This restraint is not intended to make the body an enemy. Islam recognizes bodily needs and permits nourishment after sunset and throughout the allowed hours. Fasting teaches the body its place under worship rather than declaring that physical life is evil. The best fasting produces a calmer appetite, a more grateful heart, and greater compassion for those who face hunger without choice. The Prophet ﷺ warned that whoever does not abandon false speech and acting upon it, Allah has no need for him to abandon food and drink, which al-Bukhārī records in his Ṣaḥīḥ. This does not remove the legal fast, but it warns that physical abstention without moral restraint may leave the spiritual purpose unrealized. The tongue must fast from lying, insults, backbiting, and quarrelling; the eyes from what is forbidden; the hands from harm; and the mind from nursing envy. If anger is provoked, the fasting person says, *I am fasting,* and refuses to answer evil with evil. Fasting is therefore a whole-person discipline. (33:35 — **“Surely ˹for˺ Muslim men”**) The reason fasting and compassion follows mindfulness and the inner self lies in the purpose of fasting. Therefore, fasting and compassion returns practical force to mindfulness and the inner self.

**FASTING AND COMPASSION**

Fasting and compassion begins from ***“so perhaps you will become mindful ˹of Allah˺”***. A person who experiences hunger voluntarily can better understand the vulnerable, though he must not assume that one month of fasting equals the suffering of those facing chronic poverty. The ethical response is charity, feeding people, fair trade, and reform of conditions that create hunger. The Prophet ﷺ was especially generous in Ramadan, which al-Bukhārī records in his Ṣaḥīḥ. Fasting also reduces the heart’s dependence on consumption. A culture that measures happiness by endless acquisition needs this discipline. The believer learns that contentment, prayer, family, knowledge, and service can nourish the soul in ways that material excess cannot. The plural form includes the community because fasting has a communal rhythm. Families share the pre-dawn meal, break the fast together, pray at night, and help those in need. Yet the intention and accountability remain personal. No one can fast on behalf of another or borrow another’s sincerity. The communal practice should not create hardship for the poor, the ill, or those with legitimate exemptions. The next verses explain concessions, showing that Allah’s command is paired with mercy and knowledge of human capacity. Fasting is like training a guard at a gate during peaceful hours. By refusing lawful food and intimacy for a commanded time, the self learns to stop when a far more dangerous forbidden impulse approaches. Hunger is not the goal any more than drill is the battle; alert obedience is the goal. Present-day life supplies appetite with instant service and private access, so the fast builds a form of watchfulness that remains useful after sunset and after the month has passed. (7:31 — **“Dress properly whenever you are at worship. Eat”**) In other words, fasting and compassion answers the purpose of fasting.
