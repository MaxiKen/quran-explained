**ONE COMMAND, SPOKEN TWICE**

The command is given first to the one who receives the revelation: ***“Wherever you are ˹O Prophet˺, turn your face towards the Sacred Mosque.”*** Then it is given again in the plural, so that the whole community is gathered into the same obligation: ***“And wherever you ˹believers˺ are, face towards it”***. The shift from the single address to the plural is not a slip of style but a widening, and it matches the way the passage has bound the community's identity to a direction it did not choose for itself. The repetition of a matter so important is understood to serve three purposes at once: it states the ruling, it steadies the hearts upon it, and it defends it against the objections raised from outside. What is asked of the community is therefore not a private devotion but a shared practice, held in common wherever its members happen to be.

**THE ARGUMENT TAKEN AWAY FROM THE OBJECTORS**

The purpose of the repeated command is then stated with unusual directness: ***“so that people will have no argument against you, except the wrongdoers among them”***. The change of direction had given the communities that had received earlier scriptures an opening for complaint, since they could present the older orientation as the inherited practice of the prophets. By making the Sacred Mosque the direction of the community, the ruling removes that opening and leaves the objection in the hands of those whose motive is not the truth. The treatment of the verse in al-Qurṭubī turns on this point, since a matter that had been so hard for the first believers to accept is the very matter that closes the mouths of their critics. What remains after the argument is removed is not a truce but a duty, and the sentence moves straight to the duty.

**FEAR GIVEN ITS PROPER OBJECT**

Into the place left by the objectors the verse puts a different fear: ***“Do not fear them”***, and then, in the same breath, ***“fear Me, so that I may ˹continue to˺ perfect My favour upon you”***. The pairing is exact, since the fear of people shrinks a community's practice to what will be tolerated, while the fear of God frees it to do what has been asked. Another passage gives the same instruction in the middle of a ruling about food and solemnity, where the fear of others is set aside in favour of the fear of God alone: (5:3 — **“So do not fear them; fear Me! Today I have perfected your faith for you, completed My favour upon you,”**). An earlier passage makes the same demand in fewer words: (2:40 — **“and stand in awe of Me ˹alone˺”**). The favour mentioned here is therefore not a prize added to the ruling but the ruling itself, given and then protected.

**A MARKET WITH ONE OPEN GATE**

Think of a town whose merchants argue for years about which road leads to the market, while the owner of the market has told them plainly which gate will be open and which road reaches it. The argument loses its force the moment the gate is named, and the travellers who keep debating the older roads are not defending the market but delaying their own arrival. The verse treats the objection of the communities in exactly that way: it names the direction, and it lets the naming do the work that no amount of argument would have done. Anyone who has watched a dispute fade once the person in charge gave a clear instruction will recognise the sentence's method. What the verse asks of the community afterwards is simply to walk through the gate that has been opened.

**THE FAVOUR COMPLETED IN THE GUIDANCE**

The verse closes with the purpose for which the favour was given: ***“and so you may be ˹rightly˺ guided”***. The completion of the favour and the guidance of the community are joined in one sentence, which means that the direction of prayer is not the end of the matter but the road that leads to a larger guidance. The Book describes its own clarifications in the same terms elsewhere, where the making-clear of the revelations and the guidance of the hearers stand in the same sentence: (3:103 — **“This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so that you may be ˹rightly˺ guided”**). Read with what follows, the sentence prepares the community for the account of the messenger and the blessings that accompany him. The passage on the qiblah thus ends where every passage in the sūrah ends, at the guidance of the people who are addressed.
