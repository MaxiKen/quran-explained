**THE TELLING THAT TURNS BACK TO THE CRIME**

The account now steps behind the scene it has already told: ***“˹This is˺ when a man was killed”***. The verses before this one carried the story from the command to the slaughter. This clause returns to the crime that produced the command, and the arrangement is deliberate rather than accidental. The sentence stands ahead of the tale's first scene precisely because the language permits such inversion freely, and the narrated observation about the sequence is that the telling has not been preserved in chronological order. It has been re-ordered with a subtler purpose. The section has a subject — the transgressions of a people — and the story is placed in service of that subject rather than told for its own delight. What follows from that placement is a rule for reading every narrative passage here. The order of the telling is itself commentary, and the events are arranged where the lesson needs them rather than where the clock left them.

**A KILLING ATTRIBUTED TO A PEOPLE**

The verse addresses the crime to the plural you, and the idiom has its logic. The tongue of the language attributes to a tribe what one member of it does, for blame or praise, and the attribution here carries its own point. A community of excessive greed and formidable audacity is a community from which a killing is never far. The objection that the act belongs to its own hand is true and is not being cancelled. The Book states the individual rule plainly elsewhere: (4:111 — **“And whoever commits a sin—it is only to their own loss. Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise”**). The law of the slain names the weight of the single act in the same terms: (5:32 — **“murder or mischief in the land—it will be as if they killed all of humanity; and whoever saves a life,”**). What the plural you adds is the climate of the crime rather than its fingers, and the narrations about the aftermath confirm the climate directly. The murderer had his supporters, and the dispute the verse names is not a neutral disagreement about evidence. It is a factional defence. One party cries the crime, and the other buries it under accusations aimed at innocent men. The concealment in the last clause is therefore social before it is forensic. A whole population was engaged in keeping the truth down, which means the miracle that follows is addressed to a public conspiracy and not merely to a missing witness.

**THE VERB OF PUSHING BLAME AWAY**

The second movement of the verse is the quarrel itself: ***“and you disputed who the killer was”***. The contested verb belongs to the family of deflection and pushing away, its opening sound absorbed into the letter that follows, and the picture inside that root family is the picture of the dispute: each side shoving the charge away from its own chest. The early glosses strip the word to its minimum with unusual unanimity. Al-Bukhārī's explanation of the disputed form is the single word disputed, and Mujāhid gives the same. Ata al-Khurasani and al-Ḍaḥḥāk add the plain specification that the disagreement was about this matter. Ibn Jurayj transmits the shape of the shouting across the street: one party said, you killed him, and the other answered, no, you killed him. The same account stands with the Successor Abd al-Rahman b. Zayd, whose gloss divides the crowd exactly into accusers and accused. The point is that no third posture existed in the scene. Since the killer moved among them by night and planted the body at a stranger's door, the population woke into a dispute with exactly two sides and no evidence. The verb the Book chose for them keeps the memory of how each side pushed.

**WHAT WAS CONCEALED AND WHAT BROKE IT OPEN**

The verse closes on the intervention that ends the quarrel: ***“but Allah revealed what you concealed”***. The concealment has three layers in the narrations, and the disclosure broke all three at once. The murderer hid the deed itself, moving the corpse in the dark. His partisans hid the truth beneath their accusations. The wider public hid whatever it knew behind the comfort of an unsolved case. Mujāhid takes the closing phrase as simply what you were hiding, and the gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās presses it against the crime with full force: *\"demonstrating what you hid with regard to his murder\"*. The disclosure then arrives not as testimony but as an event. The struck corpse lived, named the hand that had killed him, and died again, and the two sides of the street fell silent before a witness neither side could cross-examine. In other words, the revelation that broke the concealment was a resurrection borrowed for a day. The trial of the murder was folded inside a demonstration of the returning life. The command that will follow in the next clause is therefore the hinge between a criminal case and a creed, since the instrument of justice is also the proof of the afterlife.

**THE DEVICE NOBODY WAS ASKED TO EXPLAIN**

A standing puzzle hangs over the instrument of the disclosure, and the tradition answers it with the right measure of restraint. The question of the revived corpse, and of the cow's flesh as the instrument of reviving him, receives no catalogue of rationales. What the narrative supplies instead is a boundary for the asking. The law does not oblige the servant to discover the rationale behind each divine act, and the One whose wisdom encompasses the act needs no one's permission to choose its form. The Book itself marks the device as demonstration in the plainest terms: (2:73 — **“Allah brings the dead to life”**). The demonstration is doing double work. It closes a murder case for one audience, and it holds up a mirror to the afterlife for every audience. The present-day reader will find that this sequence calibrates the questions worth asking about the acts of God. Enquiry about reported events may run to their meaning and their moral economy. It stops at the threshold of the chooser's wisdom, because the stopping is itself a discipline the story of this murder teaches more sharply than most.
