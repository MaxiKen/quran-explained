**A PRACTICAL ALTERNATIVE WHEN TRAVELLING**

***“If you are on a journey and a scribe cannot be found, then a security can be taken.”*** establishes a practical alternative when travelling. The preceding verse established writing and witnesses for deferred debts. This verse addresses travel or circumstances in which a scribe cannot be found. A pledged security—rahn maqbūḍah—can protect the creditor until repayment. The pledge may be property held by the creditor, but it remains a security for a defined debt, not an automatic transfer of ownership. The provision reflects practical wisdom. Travellers may be far from courts, notaries, or reliable records. Allah gives a method for preserving rights without making the absence of a scribe an excuse for fraud. The pledge should be valued fairly, its condition recorded where possible, and returned when the debt is paid. If you trust one another, the verse permits the relationship to proceed without a pledge. Trust—amānah—is not foolishness; it is a moral relationship supported by faith and character. A trusted borrower must discharge what he was entrusted with, and the creditor must not abuse the trust by inventing extra claims. The verse does not condemn all unsecured lending. It recognizes that families, friends, businesses, and communities may lend on trust. But trust should be informed. A person may request documentation or collateral without insulting the borrower, and a person may offer a guarantee without promising what he cannot fulfil. The one entrusted—alladhī uʾtumina amānatahu—must fear Allah and return the trust. He records the obligation, communicates honestly when repayment is delayed, protects any pledged property, and does not treat a friend’s confidence as permission to disappear. (2:282 — **“O believers! When you contract a loan for a fixed period of time, commit it to writing.”**) Ibn ʿAbbās reads the held pledge as security when travel prevents writing and makes fear of Allah the debtor’s guard over repayment.

**TRUST CAN REPLACE SECURITY**

***“If you trust one another, then ˹there is no need for a security, but˺ the debtor should”*** deepens trust can replace security. The Prophet ﷺ taught that signs of hypocrisy include betraying a trust and breaking a promise. Repayment is a moral act even when there is no court, witness, or public pressure. A person who can repay but refuses is wronging the creditor. A person who genuinely cannot repay communicates, seeks a revised schedule, and avoids further deception. A creditor holding collateral must not use it as an opportunity to seize more than the debt. If the collateral’s value exceeds the amount owed, the surplus belongs to the owner. The creditor must care for the property and return it after settlement. Any benefit or condition attached to a loan must be examined for ribā and exploitation. Modern collateral can include vehicles, land, equipment, or financial assets. Written records, independent valuations, and lawful enforcement protect both parties. The Qur’an’s aim is security without oppression. The verse ends with the duty not to conceal testimony. A witness who knows a debt or right and hides what he knows can cause property to be stolen and trust to collapse. Concealing testimony is described as a sin of the heart because it reflects a settled willingness to hide truth, even if the person’s tongue remains silent. No witness must testify about what he did not see, expose irrelevant private matters, or participate in an unlawful proceeding. It requires truthful testimony when a legitimate right depends upon it. A person may say, I do not know, rather than inventing certainty. The verse connects testimony to the heart because financial dishonesty often begins inwardly. (4:58 — **“Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people,”**)

**THE ENTRUSTED PERSON’S DUTY**

***“honour this trust ˹by repaying the debt˺—and let them fear Allah, their Lord. And do not conceal”*** clarifies the entrusted person’s duty. A person decides that a friend’s property matters less than his own need, or that loyalty to a powerful family outweighs truth. The hidden decision then appears as silence, altered records, or evasive speech. Allah knows the inner motive and the outward act. Human beings may not discover that a witness concealed a fact, but Allah does. This protects victims and warns those who think silence is morally neutral. A healthy community needs both formal safeguards and personal trust. Documentation protects when memory fails; trust allows relationships to function without turning every interaction into litigation. Islam teaches people to use each appropriately. Wealthy institutions especially should not demand unreasonable guarantees from the poor, while vulnerable people should not be exposed to informal agreements that powerful lenders can manipulate. Charitable loan funds can combine written records, fair collateral, and mercy for hardship. Trust is strengthened by transparency, not by abandoning evidence. Someone who concealed testimony or misused a pledge must repent and restore the harmed person’s right. If a witness’s silence caused a false judgement, he should correct the record. If collateral was taken unlawfully, it must be returned or compensated. Allah’s forgiveness is sought through repair, not only words. Think of a practical alternative when travelling through a man travelling with a neighbour's goods on his camel and no paper between them. The goods are delivered, the man carries the trust home in his chest for a month, and the neighbour waits at the gate with the sum counted before either of them says a word. (4:58 — **“judge with fairness. What a noble commandment from Allah to you!”**)
