**A FIRE THAT IS KINDLED AND TAKEN**

The verse stops arguing about the men and hands the reader a picture instead: ***“Their example is that of someone”*** ***“who kindles a fire”***. The picture is drawn from a night scene that anyone in the desert would recognise, a traveller striking a light and the company drawing close for the warmth of it. The man in the parable is not described as an enemy of fire or as a fool about flint; he is described as a man who wants the light, which is what makes the next clause so sharp, since the light is taken once it has done its work. What the parable captures is a faith that was useful in a season and then removed. The traveller lit his fire when he wanted company, and the fire went out, and the story leaves him standing in the same cold with nothing gained but the memory of warmth. That is why the passage refuses to call the man a doubter outright, since a doubter does not go to the trouble of striking the match.

**THE DARKNESS AFTER THE LIGHT**

The parable's second half states the loss in full: ***“but when it lights up all around them, Allah takes away their light, leaving them in complete darkness”***. Darkness is described here as a state rather than as an accident, and the sentence ends with the man unable to see, which is the condition the earlier verses in this passage kept naming from different angles. A photograph taken in a bright room leaves the eyes worse placed than before the flash, and the memory of colour is no help in the black. Another passage uses the same contrast to describe what the Book does for a person, since the light it gives is meant to be walked in rather than admired, (6:122 — **“Can those who had been dead, to whom We gave life and a light”**), and a third speaks of a hand stretched out in perfect darkness (24:40). The picture in the verse is therefore the negative of a blessing the reader has been offered, which is why the parable is placed where it is. A light that means nothing to the man who holds it is a light he will lose, and the chill he feels afterwards is the proof that it once meant something.

**THE PART OF THE STORY LEFT OUT**

A reader may notice what the parable does not describe, and the omission carries its own lesson. No one in it is shown stealing the fire, no storm is described, and no explanation is offered for the light going out; the sentence simply says it was taken. The silence is the point, because the lesson is about who gave the light in the first place and about the difference it makes to remember the giver. A man who trusts the lamp rather than the hand that lit it has a faith with no owner, and a faith with no owner lasts exactly as long as the warmth does. This is why the Book elsewhere tells its reader that the light belongs to the Guardian of the believers, (2:257 — **“He brings them out of darkness and into light”**), and why the verse that follows this one carries the picture forward into a rainstorm. The story is unfinished on purpose, so that a reader who has lost a season of faith will read his own name in it.

**WHEN THE LIGHT GOES OUT AT HOME**

The parable explains something that puzzles people about inherited faith today, which is how a family can be religious for a generation and empty in the next. The light in such a house was often real, and the warmth was enjoyed; what was never done was the work of owning it, since the children were told to behave and seldom told why. A person who has grown up inside a religion without ever asking what it is for will feel the loss when the light goes, and the feeling will be sharp precisely because the memory of the warmth remains. The remedy is not to strike a fire of private invention but to go back to the hand that lit the first one, which is what the Book keeps recommending to people who think their case is hopeless. The fire in the parable is not lost because the man was cold; it is lost because the light was never his, and because nobody had taught him to ask who owned it.
