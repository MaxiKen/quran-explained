**A WILL IS A TRUST**

Read phrase by phrase, the passage sets out its own case in order: ***“But whoever changes the will after hearing it, the blame will only be on those”***, and ***“who made the change.”***, ***“Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing”***. The order of the claims is the order of the proof: the act first, then the blame, then the hearing that finds it. The document polices this custody precisely because the deceased can no longer supervise the record himself. Ibn ʿAbbās's reading keeps this order, so the walk follows the text rather than a commentator's scheme. The custody the passage polices is the custody of a voice that can no longer correct the record, which is why the liability travels to every living hand that touched the alteration.

But whoever changes the will after hearing it describes a crime with a specific victim: the dead cannot correct the record, and the living have edited a voice that can no longer answer. The next movement of the sentence fixes the blame on those who made the change, and the allocation of blame is absolute — the estate does not carry the sin, and neither does the deceased. The trust being discussed is the trust of the spoken word overheard and then rewritten, which places the verse in the family of laws that protect testimony. A witness is trusted precisely because he can be contradicted; the deceased cannot contradict anyone, so the changer is liable for the whole damage. The verse's closing description — Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing — is aimed at exactly this gap, because the alteration happens in rooms no officer enters. The law of bequests is completed by the next verse, but the sanctions begin here, where the temptation to revise is greatest (5:106 — **“O believers! When death approaches any of you, call upon two just Muslim men to witness as you make a bequest; otherwise,”**).

**SIGNED BY BOTH**

The plural form is decisive: the blame will only be on those who made the change attaches to every hand that touched the alteration, so the family member who dictates the new wording, the scribe who writes it, and the witness who endorses it share one liability. The occasion recorded on the authority of Ibn ʿAbbās makes the division precise: the deceased carries no responsibility for what was changed after his death, and the sentence is carried entirely by the changers. Justice protects the dead by charging the living, which is the only direction enforcement can run. The verse therefore answers the excuse of the executor who says he was only following the family's wishes — the wishes of the living never override the wish already recorded, and the record is the deceased's last standing testimony. The companion legislation on witnessing seals the same principle from the other side, since the witness swears he will not sell his testimony for any price (4:135 — **“O believers! Stand firm for justice as witnesses for Allah even if it is against yourselves, your parents,”**).

**NO EXCUSE FROM CIRCUMSTANCE**

A hearer may want to soften the ruling by imagining desperation — a son who needs the property, a widow who fears poverty. The verse makes no such concession, and its structure explains why: the will is the final transfer the deceased can perform, and an edited transfer is theft with a notary attached. Because the wording places the whole blame on those who made the change, the burden is the changers' and nobody else's, which means poverty as a motive converts nothing into a right. The same logic governs court procedure elsewhere: testimony is given under oath precisely so that circumstances cannot quietly rewrite it. The protection also runs toward the honest family, since a rule that blamed the estate would penalize the heirs the deceased actually chose. The strictness of the allocation is therefore a mercy to the intended beneficiaries, and the one who fears his own temptation should simply refuse to touch the instrument.

**A NAME FOR EVERY THEFT**

The most practical reading of the verse names the whole chain of custody: the will is heard, it is recorded, and it is executed — and sin sits on the second link whenever it diverges from the first. The application is to treat the document as frozen from the moment of hearing, because the moment of hearing is the moment the trust begins. Anyone who suspects an error has a lawful channel in the next verse and no license here to improvise one. The closing name — All-Hearing — also warns the executor that the conversation in which the change was agreed has a witness. Because the record stands as the deceased speaks through it, the living are stewards of a voice and not its editors. The stewardship ends when the estate is distributed according to what was heard. The one who keeps this order discovers that the will is the easiest document in the house to guard, because it asks for nothing except that the hands stay off it. Because the document speaks for someone who can no longer supervise the speaking, the law supplies the supervision the grave removed.
