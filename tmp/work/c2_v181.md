**PROTECTING THE LAST WILL**

The movement toward protecting the last will rests on ***“But whoever changes the will after hearing it, the blame will only be on those”***. The preceding instruction prescribed a fair bequest when death approaches. This verse protects that bequest from later manipulation. Whoever hears a valid will and changes it unjustly carries the sin of the alteration. The responsibility may fall upon a witness, executor, relative, lawyer, or any other person who knowingly replaces the deceased person’s instruction with something that serves his own interest. The verse does not forbid correcting a mistake, clarifying an ambiguity, or refusing an instruction that violates Allah’s inheritance law. It condemns deliberate alteration of what was valid and known. A will is a trust, and the people who handle it must remember that the person who wrote it may no longer be present to defend his rights. A bequest must be lawful in its subject, fair in its amount, and consistent with the fixed shares Allah later detailed in chapter al-Nisāʾ. A person cannot use a will to deprive an heir of his or her divinely assigned share, nor can an executor honour a forbidden instruction simply because it appears in writing. The Sunnah states that Allah has given each heir his right and that there is no bequest for an heir except with the consent of the other heirs. Legal systems and juristic schools differ over documentation, witnesses, and the treatment of disputed instructions. The basic moral rule is constant: preserve the truth of the deceased person’s lawful intention, and do not use technical knowledge to steal from the vulnerable. (2:180 — **“a will should be made in favour of parents”**) Because protecting the last will governs 2:181, sin belongs to the alterer arises from protecting the last will. It follows that allah hears and knows completes protecting the last will through sin belongs to the alterer.

**SIN BELONGS TO THE ALTERER**

The sequence reaches sin belongs to the alterer through ***“who made the change.”***. The statement the sin is only upon those who change it distinguishes the deceased person from the one who commits the fraud. If the original bequest was honest and lawful, the person who wrote it is not blamed for another’s betrayal. This is part of Allah’s justice: each person bears his own deed, and no one is punished for a wrong he did not commit. The principle does not excuse the testator from writing carefully. Ambiguous wording can produce conflict, and negligence may contribute to harm. A responsible person records debts, identifies assets, names trustworthy executors, and seeks qualified advice. Yet deliberate falsification remains the sin of the falsifier. Witnessing a will is not a ceremonial favour. A witness may be called to testify about what was said, the condition of the testator, and the authenticity of the document. He must not conceal relevant information, accept a bribe, or change his testimony to please a powerful heir. The Qur’an commands that testimony be upheld for Allah even against oneself and relatives. An executor, *executor*, must administer the estate with *trust*. He should settle debts, protect minors, distribute lawful shares, and keep transparent records. Taking a hidden commission, delaying distribution to pressure heirs, or favouring one branch of the family turns guardianship into betrayal. (4:11 — **“the share of the male will be twice”**) The reason allah hears and knows follows sin belongs to the alterer lies in protecting the last will. Therefore, allah hears and knows returns practical force to sin belongs to the alterer.

**ALLAH HEARS AND KNOWS**

Allah hears and knows takes shape in ***“Indeed, Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing”***. Allah is *All-Hearing* and *All-Knowing*. He hears the spoken will, the secret agreement to change it, the false oath, and the silent plea of the heir whose share is being stolen. He knows the intention behind every amendment and every claim that appears legally polished. Human courts may miss deception, but no action escapes divine knowledge. This ending is a protection for the powerless. An orphan or widow may not be able to challenge a sophisticated fraud, but Allah knows what happened. It is also a warning to those who think family pressure or social respectability can hide injustice. If a person has changed a bequest unjustly, repentance requires restoring the lawful instruction or compensating those harmed. Private regret is not enough when property has been taken from identifiable people. The wrongdoer should disclose the truth, return assets, and seek forgiveness without placing pressure on the victims. The community can prevent such harm through clear written wills, independent witnesses, accessible legal advice, and education about Islamic inheritance. These practical measures are part of protecting the trust Allah has placed in families. An executor is like a messenger carrying a sealed letter from someone who can no longer speak. Changing the address or the amount inside does not become harmless because the sender cannot object; the messenger's access creates a heavier trust. Present-day wills may pass through relatives, lawyers, officials, and digital records, yet the moral fact is unchanged. God heard the original instruction and knows the later alteration, so a successful manipulation of documents cannot transfer the blame back to the deceased. Ibn ʿAbbās explained that the deceased bears no responsibility for an alteration made after the will was heard; the burden settles upon the living person who knowingly changed the trust. (4:176 — **“so you do not go astray.”**) In other words, allah hears and knows answers protecting the last will.
