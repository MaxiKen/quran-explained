**A MURDER WITH NO WITNESS AND A COMMAND WITH NO SENSE**

The story opens on a crime that has already torn the town in two: ***“And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow.” They replied, “Are you mocking us?””***. One man had been killed and the killer was unknown, because the deed was done at night and the body was moved to a stranger's doorstep to be found at dawn. The early account transmitted by Ubaydah al-Salmani gives the crime its face: a man among the Children of Israel who was impotent and had substantial wealth, with only a nephew who would inherit from him; the nephew killed him, moved the body by night, and the next morning the cry for revenge went up. The people took up their weapons and almost fought each other, since each party held the other responsible and each deflected the charge until the matter grew between them. The feud had reached the point where each party's grief turned on its neighbour rather than on the killer, which means the crime was multiplying into a civil war while the murderer stood aside. The wise men among them stopped the feud with one question — why fight one another while a messenger of God stands among you — and Moses was asked to expose the killer. The command that answered him was deliberately plain, and Ibn ʿAbbās reads its plainness as the point: any cow would have answered. The murder is recapped at the lesson's other end as (2:72 — **“˹This is˺ when a man was killed and you disputed who the killer was, but Allah revealed what you concealed”**). What turns on their coming questions is therefore already set in the command's form, because the easy answer stood open until their dispute closed it; the next step in their own words shows the narrowing at once (2:68 — **“They said, “Call upon your Lord to clarify for us what type ˹of cow˺ it should be!” He replied, “Allah says,”**), and the sacrifice stands at once as a worldly mercy ending the feud and an otherworldly sign naming the killer.

**THE PROPHET'S REFUGE FROM THE FOLLY OF MOCKERY**

The people's reply to the command is suspicion rather than obedience: ***“Moses responded, “I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!””***. The Arabic noun in their question — are you making us a target of mockery — carries play and mockery alike in its earliest glosses, and that union explains their state of mind. To a people watching a ritual of slaughter proposed as the solvent of an unsolved murder, the command sounded like entertainment at their expense, and the fear of ridicule is the fear that sits closest to a humiliated congregation. Moses answers the suspicion at its root. The wording he flees to God from names the ignorant in the sense of the one who speaks useless words and mocks his fellows, so that the refuge is a refusal of the very character they imagined in him. The point is that mockery and messengership cannot occupy the same man, because a prophet carrying a people's fate is the one person with nothing to gain from their humiliation. The Prophet's own gloss on the line settles the reading: Ibn ʿAbbās reports the sense as *"God forbid that I ever mock the believers"*, a report that turns the grammatical subject of folly into a moral impossibility. In other words, the verse draws the character line of revelation itself: guidance does not entertain itself with its recipients. The neighbouring address to another prophet makes the same register of restraint explicit: (11:46 — **“do not ask Me about what you have no knowledge of! I warn you so you do not fall into ignorance.””**). The lesson for every later hearer follows from the exchange as a practical demand, since the one who speaks for God may never amuse himself with the people he serves, and the people in turn owe their prophet the reading that takes his words as commands rather than games. The reason the refusal matters is that a mystery invites ridicule of sacred law precisely when it is least understood, and for that situation the exchange leaves a standing instruction: the tongue that serves revelation refuses the joke before it answers the question.
