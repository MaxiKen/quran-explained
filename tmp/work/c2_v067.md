**A COMMAND OF SACRIFICE MEETS A SHRUG**

The chapter takes its name from the animal in this verse, and the exchange that follows is one of the strangest in the Book: ***“And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “Allah commands you to sacrifice a cow.” They replied, “Are you mocking us?””***, ***“Moses responded, “I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!””*** The command is delivered in the plainest words a prophet can use, and the answer he receives questions his seriousness rather than the command's content. The people had lived through the sea, the water from the rock, the shade, and the food that fell from the sky, and their first reaction to an instruction is the suspicion that their prophet is playing with them. Moses' reply is worth more attention than it usually receives, because he does not say that God does not jest; he says that he takes refuge from being counted among the foolish, which is a different and sharper answer.

**A CHARGE THAT TOUCHED THE OFFICE**

The word Moses uses of himself describes a person who does not know what he is doing or a person whose conduct has no weight in it. Accusing a prophet of mockery is a charge against his office, and the reply turns the charge back in the only direction that preserves the office: a man who invents commands on his own authority is exactly the kind of person who should not be believed. The early teachers noted that this is the first thing Moses says in the exchange, before any explanation of the command is offered, because the question he is answering is about him and not about the cow. The Book's own usage of the word elsewhere puts the same weight on it, and the reader can hear the register in a passage where the accusation of folly is what a prophet's opponents throw at each other: (7:66 — **“The disbelieving chiefs of his people responded, “We surely see you as a fool,”**). Moses' sentence, read in that light, is a refusal to be sorted into the class of people whose words can be dismissed.

**A QUESTION ASKED OUT OF HABIT**

The reader of this chapter already knows that the people's questions are not innocent. They had asked for a visible god, they had asked for the vegetables of Egypt, and each time the asking had been a step away from what they had been given. The request for detail in the verses that follow has an obvious practical cause, since the command did not name any particular animal, and a general command invites a question about which of many possible animals is meant. What turns the question into a fault is the manner of it, and the Book's guidance for a later community addresses that manner directly, since it warns against the questions that make a clarified command heavier: (2:108 — **“Or do you ˹believers˺ intend to ask of your Messenger”**). The people wanted the command made easier by being made narrower, and every clarification they obtained made the animal harder to find, which is the pattern the next three verses describe.

**THE COW, THE MURDER, AND THE REASON FOR BOTH**

The name of this sūrah comes from the animal, and the animal is connected to a death that the chapter reports a few verses later. A man had been killed, the killer was unknown, and the community disputed the matter until the Book told them to strike the body with part of the cow that had been slaughtered. The command, then, was never only a test of willingness; it was the preparation for a sign that would expose what the community was hiding. A reader who takes the two passages together sees why the sacrifice is placed first: an argument about which cow is to be killed delays an answer to a murder, and the delay belongs to the people rather than to the command. The same willingness is asked of a later community in the same book, in a passage that warns against the questions that make a clarified command heavier: (5:101 — **“may disturb you.”**). A general instruction is a mercy, and the insistence on detail is what turns it into a burden.

**TWO THINGS THE EXCHANGE LEAVES BEHIND**

Two things stand out for a person reading the passage on his own. The first concerns the tone in which a command is met, since the people's suspicion was a judgement passed on their prophet before the command was even considered, and the same instinct survives wherever a clear instruction arrives and the first question is about the messenger's motives. The second concerns the way Moses answers, because he neither defends the command nor asks for an apology; he puts himself in the position of a man who takes God's orders seriously and leaves the accusation where it belongs. A reader who has been given a plain duty and found himself asking for the details to be settled before he begins can see the shape of his own exchange in this one. The verse closes with the prophet's refuge taken from folly, and it invites the reader to take his own.
