**THE CONGREGATION TAKEN TO TASK**

The verse opens with a call to the believers, and the address establishes who is being corrected before the correction is stated: ***“O believers! Do not say, “Râ’ina.”*** ***“[Herd us!]”***. The instruction is given to the community that has accepted the message, which means the warning is not about an outsider's mockery but about a habit inside the household of faith. The word itself is an ordinary request in one language and an insult in another, and the translation supplies both meanings in brackets so that the reader can see the double edge. The Book's concern in the sentence is with what a community's speech signals about its stance, since the same request could be made with a word that carried no insult at all. What the reader is shown is a congregation being taught to weigh its words rather than to rely on its intentions.

**THE WORD THEY WERE TOLD TO DROP**

The first word is set aside in the same sentence that names its replacement, and the reason is the way it fell on the ear of the people being addressed. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās explains that the believers had taken up a form of address they had heard from the People of the Book, and that the form carried a jeer in the other community's usage. The verse does not require the believers to have intended the insult; it requires them to stop using a word that had become a mark of mockery in the mouths of others. This is the Book's consistent treatment of speech, since it judges what a phrase does in a community rather than what a speaker privately meant by it. A reader who has adopted the idiom of a hostile group without noticing what the idiom carries will find the instruction addressed to him.

**THE REPLACEMENT AND WHY IT WAS CHOSEN**

The verse supplies the substitute immediately, and the substitute is a word that asks to be attended to: ***“But say, “Unẓurna,”*** ***“[Tend to us!]”***. The change is not a matter of taste; the second term asks for attention in a way that carries no hint of diminishment, which is what the first term had come to suggest. The Book's habit of replacing a defective practice with a sound one, rather than merely forbidding it, appears throughout this sūrah and is at work here in a single sentence. Another passage states the rule the verse applies, in the same four words it takes to give the instruction: (33:70 — **“Be mindful of Allah, and say what is right”**). A community's speech is treated as part of its obedience rather than as a private matter, and the verse's replacement shows how ordinary the corrected form was meant to be.

**A GUEST WHO COPIES THE WRONG PHRASE**

Think of a guest who copies a phrase he hears at a neighbour's table, not knowing that the phrase means something dismissive in the neighbour's own family. The community in this verse had been using the form with a decent intention, and the instruction came because the form itself had been turned against the faith. The account given in another passage describes the same practice from the other side, where the phrase is listed among the things said by a group that had made a game of the language: (4:46 — **“playing with words and discrediting the faith”**). What the verse corrects, then, is not a slip of the tongue but the acceptance of a vocabulary that had been fashioned to undermine the community that used it. A reader can carry the lesson to any idiom he has absorbed without asking what it was built to do.

**LISTENING AS PART OF SPEECH**

The instruction continues with a single imperative, and it is joined to everything that came before: ***“and listen ˹attentively˺.”***. The Book places hearing beside speaking, so that the corrected form is not enough by itself; a community that has been given a better word is also required to use its ears. The verse then names the consequence of refusing the whole matter, and the punishment is stated with the same brevity as the rest of the sentence: ***“And the disbelievers will suffer a painful punishment”***. The connection between mockery and disbelief is drawn by the verse rather than inferred by the reader, since the people who turned the greeting into a joke are named in the clause that follows. The duty to hear is placed on the community in another passage as well, where the listening is joined to the mercy that follows from it: (7:204 — **“listen to it attentively and be silent”**). What remains is the doubled instruction, one for the mouth and one for the ear, and the reader is left to take up both. The next verse explains why the hostility behind the mockery exists at all.
