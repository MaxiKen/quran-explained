**THE REACTION TO ADVICE**

***“When it is said to them, “Fear Allah,” pride”*** establishes the reaction to advice. The previous verses described impressive speech followed by corruption. Here the person is confronted with a simple moral reminder: Be mindful of Allah. Instead of reflecting, he is seized by ʿizzah, a prideful sense of honour or power, and continues in sin. The verse exposes a decisive test of character: how does a person respond when someone reminds him of accountability. A sincere person may feel embarrassed or initially defensive, but he listens, checks the evidence, and corrects himself if necessary. The arrogant person treats advice as humiliation. He is more concerned with protecting his image than with stopping the harm he is causing. Pride converts a reminder into an insult and makes repentance appear beneath him. Be mindful of Allah—ittaqi Allāh—does not mean submit to my personal authority. It is an appeal to a higher Judge. The adviser and the advised are both servants of Allah. A person who uses the phrase to control people unjustly can also be wrong, but the one who hears a sincere reminder should not reject it merely because it challenges his status. Taqwā includes awareness of Allah’s knowledge, fear of His displeasure, hope in His mercy, and protection from sin. It asks the powerful person to remember the rights of the weak and the wealthy person to remember the poor. It places every human role beneath divine accountability. (2:204 — **“There are some ˹hypocrites˺ who impress you with their views regarding worldly affairs and openly call upon”**) Because the reaction to advice frames taqwā as a moral appeal, taqwā as a moral appeal returns attention to the reaction to advice.

**TAQWĀ AS A MORAL APPEAL**

***“carries them off to sin. Hell will be”*** deepens taqwā as a moral appeal. Pride drives him further into sin suggests that arrogance is not passive. It pushes the person to defend himself through more wrongdoing: he may lie to cover a lie, punish the adviser, destroy evidence, or recruit followers to praise him. What began as a moral failure becomes a system of corruption because the person refuses correction. The Prophet ﷺ described kibr as rejecting truth and looking down on people. The hadith, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, makes clear that good clothing or appearance is not pride. Pride is a disease of the heart revealed by the refusal to accept truth and the contempt for those considered inferior. The verse does not command people to confront every wrongdoer publicly in a way that creates greater harm. Advice should be sincere, informed, and proportionate. The Prophet ﷺ taught that religion is sincere counsel, and he taught methods of correcting wrong according to ability, knowledge, and likely consequence. A leader may need private counsel before public accountability; a crime may require reporting rather than informal advice; an abusive person may need firm boundaries and legal intervention. The goal is to stop sin and protect people, not to give the adviser an opportunity to display superiority. Sufficient for him is Hell is a warning that a person who refuses every reminder and persists in harming others is preparing his own destination. The expression is not approval of the punishment; it is a declaration that Hell is an adequate consequence for deliberate arrogance and corruption. (7:12 — **“Allah asked, “What prevented you from prostrating when I commanded you?” He replied,”**) Since pride produces further sin follows the reaction to advice, taqwā as a moral appeal links purpose to pride produces further sin.

**PRIDE PRODUCES FURTHER SIN**

***“their proper place. What an evil place to rest”*** clarifies pride produces further sin. The phrase evil resting place contrasts with the false security the person enjoyed in worldly power. The Qur’an repeatedly joins pride with rejection of truth. Iblīs refused Allah’s command because he considered himself superior, and Pharaoh’s arrogance led him to oppress an entire people. Pride can therefore be personal, religious, political, or intellectual. The immediate warning is to the arrogant offender, but believers must examine themselves. A person may accept advice when it is general but reject it when it names his habit. He may accuse the critic of envy, ignorance, or disloyalty without investigating. Taqwā means asking whether some portion of the reminder is true. Advisers must also avoid arrogance. Correcting another person does not prove that one is better. The Prophet ﷺ taught gentleness and concealed private faults when concealment served reform. The best counsel begins with concern for Allah and the person, not with the desire to win. The door of repentance remains open while life remains. A person who recognizes pride should say, I was wrong, restore the rights he violated, and seek forgiveness. Apology is not humiliation before people; it is strength before Allah. Communities should encourage accountability and reform rather than rewarding those who never admit fault. Think of the reaction to advice through a man who refuses to turn off the road at a sign because the sign was not written politely. (16:22 — **“Your God is ˹only˺ One God. As for those who do not believe in the Hereafter,”**) Advice, justice, and wisdom matters, which means pride produces further sin returns advice, justice, and wisdom to the reaction to advice.

**ADVICE, JUSTICE, AND WISDOM**

The road bends, the sign is behind him, and the destination is exactly the one it named. The reaction to advice reaches a reader today who takes correction as an insult and answers every reminder with the state of the other man's house. The verse gives him the picture of a man carried off by his own dignity, and the reaction to advice asks what he is defending when he refuses a word that would have saved him. Because the verse ends with the resting place rather than the argument, the reader is shown what the quarrel was worth. Because the counsel is a single short phrase, the reader is shown how little it takes to expose a man. The words that were refused here were not a ruling or a rebuke but the same mindfulness the whole passage has been asking of every pilgrim. Because the last word of the verse is a resting place, the reader is shown the bitter irony of the picture. The man who refused the counsel so that nothing would disturb his comfort is given a comfort that lasts, and the verse lets him keep it. Taqwā as a moral appeal makes the reaction to advice practical; pride produces further sin therefore matters.
