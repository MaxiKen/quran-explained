**TOLD TO FEAR**

One unit, one sentence, and the verse still walks from the warning to the fire: , ***“When it is said to them, “Fear Allah,” pride carries them off to sin. Hell will be their proper place. What an evil place to rest”***. and the last movement names the resting place. The single stretch of the verse is best read as a drama in three beats: the advice given, the pride that answers it, and the dwelling that keeps the answer. The walk through one phrase proves that a short verse can carry a full career, because the career is compressed into the pride that interrupts the advice. Ibn ʿAbbās describes the interruption as offence taken at the counsel, and the account that settles it as Hell's own settlement.

**PRIDE AS THE ARREST**

The advice given to the man is the shortest sermon in the chapter — fear Allah — and the answer it receives is the verse's whole diagnosis. The pride described does not argue with the advice; it interrupts it. The account transmitted through Ibn ʿAbbās keeps the interruption precise: the man takes offence at the counsel because the counsel implies he is somewhere he does not believe he stands. The psychology named here is the psychology of the flatterer of the previous verses at its end of the road: the voice that charmed the room cannot bear a word that reduces it. Because the pride seizes the person at the moment of the reminder, the reminder is the verse's test of every self that has been praised all week.

**HELL ENOUGH**

The figure is doing what the refusal always does, treating the gate as locked by the keeper of the gate rather than by the hand that holds his own heart. The verdict is stated in the language of sufficiency rather than of quantity: Hell will be enough, and enough is a word of accounting. The report of the settlement keeps the same economy — the destination settles the account of the pride — and the settlement answers the man who thinks he can outspend the day. The home for the arrogant is described in the legislation of the resurrection as a gate that receives them and a residence that keeps them (39:72 — **“It will be said to them, “Enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever.” What an evil home for the arrogant”**). Because the sufficiency is stated before the description, the reader learns that the fire is not excess but measure.

**A DEBT SETTLED IN FIRE**

The application is to hear the advice of the street as if it were the advice of the verse. The person who bristles at the counsel of the honest friend is rehearsing the scene this verse draws. Because the pride arrives at the moment of the reminder, the bristle is the alarm the reader should hear first. The verse's closing exclamation is one of the few raised voices in the passage, and the voice is raised for the sake of the man being described. The dwelling is chosen by the pride and paid for by the same. The arrogance of the one who is refused the book is described in the reports with the image of locks laid upon the heart and lions posted at the gate. The one who refuses the truth at the assembly walks out of the assembly with his own lock in his hand. The image of the lions is drawn from the old stories of the imprisoned heart, and the stories keep the same zoo for the same disease. Because the refusal is described as a posture rather than an event, the posture can be recognized in the mirror. The public assembly of the truth and the public assembly of the boast are held in the same square, and the two crowds cross each other without touching. The one who has been given the authority of the clear sign and the one who has been refused it stand at different doors of the same house. The lock is not placed by the tyrant, which is the passage's most disturbing detail. Reading the report of the locks beside the parable of the refusal returns the portrait to its anatomy. The zoo of the locked heart is well catalogued, and the visitor to the zoo is often the owner of the lock himself.
