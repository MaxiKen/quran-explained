**WHEN THE WAY AHEAD SEEMED CLOSED**

Moses had led his people out by God’s command, but Pharaoh’s pursuit brought them to a place where escape seemed barred. The enemy was behind them and the sea ahead. Their cry was (26:61 — **““We are overtaken for sure.””**). Moses answered, (26:62 — **““Absolutely not! My Lord is certainly with me—He will guide me.””**). The present verse recalls how that trust was vindicated: ***“And ˹remember˺ when”*** ***“We parted the sea,”***. Parting means that the waters were divided to make a way through. The narrative is about an act of God that altered their immediate danger, not merely a change in how frightened people felt. The command to Moses is explicit: (26:63 — **““Strike the sea with your staff,” and the sea was split, each part was like a huge mountain”**). His staff was used in obedience; it did not possess a power over the sea apart from God. Because the act came at the Lord’s command, the event is a sign supporting the messenger who followed that command. The people’s fear describes the danger as they saw it. Moses’ answer rests on a promise beyond what the visible road could offer, and the opened sea supplies the way he trusted God to give.

**A PASSAGE THAT REMOVED TWO DANGERS AT ONCE**

The result is ***“rescued you,”***. The Israelites were saved both from being overtaken and from drowning in the sea they crossed. The fuller instruction promises a dry passage and tells Moses, (20:77 — **“Have no fear of being overtaken, nor be concerned ˹of drowning˺.””**). Water did not merely move aside while leaving them a trap in the seabed. The way was made fit for the people who had to pass through it. Al-Suddī relates that twelve paths opened, one for each tribe. This is a transmitted account of the crossing; the verse’s own brief statement fixes the central fact of rescue. The sea is commonly identified as the Red Sea, but the verse does not name the exact crossing place. Its purpose is to make the gift clear. The trapped people were not asked to defeat the pursuing army before they could be delivered. They followed the path God opened. Their passage shows why trust in God is not the same as refusing to act: Moses obeyed, and the people crossed, while the power that made their action possible belonged to their Lord.

**THE PURSUIT ENDS WITH THE PURSUERS**

The same event has a second outcome: ***“and drowned Pharaoh’s people”***. Pharaoh is not excluded by the mention of his people. Another verse states, (17:103 — **“but We drowned him and all of those with him”**). The followers who carried his power into the pursuit shared the end of their leader. He could not make the path opened for Moses remain a path of safety for his own attack. This is why the rescue and drowning are placed together. The danger was not merely put at a greater distance, leaving the army free to renew the chase. It was ended. There is also a sharp reversal in the place of water in Pharaoh’s claims. He had boasted of Egypt and its streams, (43:51 — **“Am I not sovereign over Egypt as well as ˹all˺ these streams flowing at my feet?”**). The ruler who treated those waters as a sign of his greatness could not make water obey him here. His rank, force, and confidence did not turn the sea into his servant. The event separates God’s power from the ruler’s claim to possess it, while releasing the people whom that claim had helped to enslave.

**SIGHT CONFIRMS THE END OF THE THREAT**

The rescue took place ***“before your very eyes”***. The plain sense is direct sight, not merely knowledge gained later. The people saw the danger overcome at the place where their own escape had seemed impossible. Seeing the enemy’s end added assurance to escape itself. Fear could otherwise leave open the thought that Pharaoh still lived and would return. Qays ibn ʿUbād relates an account of their doubts about his death being answered when his body was cast upon the shore. The Quran separately states the purpose of preserving that body: (10:92 — **“Today We will preserve your corpse so that you may become an example for those who come after you.”**). The sign is not a grant of safety to the drowned ruler. It makes his defeat visible to those who might still fear or admire his power. Because the rescue was witnessed, its remembrance should carry more than a vague belief that life had somehow improved. Their people had seen the sea become a road, and had seen the pursuing power fail. The same sight that brought relief also established a duty to recognize the Lord who had delivered them.

**A FAST OF THANKS KEEPS THE DELIVERANCE IN MEMORY**

The event also belongs to the meaning of the fast of ʿĀshūrāʾ. Ibn ʿAbbās’s account, preserved in al-Bukhārī and Muslim, describes the Prophet finding Jews in Madinah fasting in remembrance of the day God saved Moses and his people. They explained that Moses had fasted in thanks. The Prophet affirmed his own closeness to Moses, fasted, and commanded the fast. The response makes the remembered deliverance an occasion for worship, not a claim that the blessing belongs to a people apart from obedience to its Giver. It also shows the bond between the messengers. Honour for Moses is expressed by following the divine guidance Muhammad brings, rather than by setting one messenger against another. Since the fast recalls rescue, its purpose is gratitude for an act that no human army could have accomplished at that place. The body that receives food by God’s provision willingly leaves it for His sake. The remembrance thus passes into an act of thanks rather than remaining only a tale about another people’s danger.

The history of the fast needs to be kept complete. Muslim and al-Bukhārī transmit ʿĀʾishah’s account that Quraysh and the Prophet had already fasted ʿĀshūrāʾ in Makkah. The encounter in Madinah was therefore not the first occasion on which he observed it. Her report also explains that, after Ramadan was prescribed, a person could keep this fast or leave it; it was no longer an obligation alongside Ramadan. The day is understood here as the tenth of Muḥarram, with the ninth added as a recommended companion day. Al-Tirmidhī’s report from Ibn ʿAbbās names the tenth, while Muslim records the Prophet’s stated intention to fast the ninth if he lived another year. Reading the latter as an addition, rather than a replacement, preserves both instructions. The virtue remains great: Abū Qatādah reports in Muslim that the Prophet hoped its fast would expiate the preceding year. These teachings give the remembrance a defined practice and prevent a claim that gratitude needs an invented rite. The sea was parted by God; the thanks offered afterward must likewise follow His guidance.
