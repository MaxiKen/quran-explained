**A ROAD THROUGH THE WATER**

The next item in the history is the escape itself: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you”***, and ***“and drowned Pharaoh's people before your very eyes”***. The sentence is short, and it holds three events that no human being could have arranged for himself: water standing where water does not stand, a people walking through it, and an army disappearing behind them. The Book reports the event elsewhere with the command that produced it, so the reader can see how the impossible was done: (26:63 — **“So We inspired Moses: “Strike the sea with your staff,” and the sea was split,”**). The rescue belongs to the one who issued the order, and the staff is credited with nothing beyond obedience. Al-Suddī carried the account of Moses striking the sea and the water opening into a road for the departing people. Reports from Ibn ʿAbbās likewise preserve the command to travel by night, which means the rescue joined revealed direction, human obedience and an opening no human power could make.

The closing phrase about the eyes is the detail that makes the verse a favour rather than a legend. The people did not hear about the drowning afterwards; they watched it happen from the far shore, which means the memory they were asked to keep was an eyewitness memory. That is why the command to remember carries such weight across the passage. The Book is not asking a later generation to believe a rumour; it is asking them to inherit a testimony from people who saw the water close. The same events are told in the sūrah of the Poets with the three stages separated into three sentences: first the sea was split, then (26:65 — **“and delivered Moses and those with him all together”**), and then (26:66 — **“Then We drowned the others”**). The reader is being shown a rescue in three movements, each of them credited to the same hand.

**A MEMORY A LATER GENERATION INHERITS**

The adult readers of this sūrah were not the ones who walked through the sea, and the Book still addresses them as the rescued. That is a pattern worth noticing, because it explains how a community carries an experience it did not have. The memory is passed on as a claim about who they are and who brought them there, and the passage expects each generation to adopt the deliverance as its own. A reader from any community can see the same mechanism at work in his own history, where the events that formed his people were mostly watched by people who are now gone. The purpose is not nostalgia, and gratitude is not treated as an emotion about the past. The exodus is placed here as the ground for the demands that follow in the chapter: the covenant, the law, and the warnings about forgetting them all rest on the fact that these people were slaves and were freed by someone who had no obligation to do it. A demand that looks arbitrary in isolation becomes proportionate once the history is remembered, which is why the Book keeps placing the history in front of the demand.

**WHAT TO BUILD IN THE SPACE A RESCUE LEAVES**

The practical side of the verse is that a person who has been brought out of something is expected to act like it. The Book does not require anyone to spend his life describing the experience, but it does require that the experience change what he obeys. A community that has been freed from a tyrant and then runs after other tyrants has learned nothing from its own history, and the sūrah will make exactly that accusation before the passage is finished. The test of gratitude in the Book is always the same: it is shown by what a person does with the freedom he was given. For a reader today the exercise is to name the thing he was delivered from and to ask what he has built in the space it left. The Book's expectation is modest and concrete, and the sūrah has already named the materials elsewhere in the chapter: prayer, charity, and the company of people who bow. A person who has been rescued and uses his freedom for those things has answered the command to remember, and the water he did not part has done its work in his life. Think of a causeway appearing where an army sees only water and a trapped people see no route. The opening becomes a road only for those who move at the command, and it closes on the power that trusted its own numbers.
