**A JEER AT THE TURN**

The jeer arrives on schedule. ***“The foolish among the people will ask, “Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?””***. The verse names the askers before it answers them. The foolish. The lightweight of the city now files a question about sacred law. Ibn ʿAbbās ties the jeer to the moment of the change. The hearts that carried no weight treated the turn as material for mockery. The point of the naming is that the question was never a question. It was a taunt with a question mark. What follows is that the answer is pitched over the heads of the askers. The verse does not argue with the jeer. It reopens the doctrine of divine ownership instead. The effect is a hard lesson in audience selection. Some questions deserve a teacher. Others deserve a sentence. This one receives the sentence first, and the teaching rides behind it for everyone still listening. The arrangement is a mercy to the sincere of the city, who get the doctrine without having to sit through the mockery.

**THE EAST AND THE WEST**

The answer begins with geography and ends with ownership. ***“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “The east”*** ***“and west belong ˹only˺ to Allah. He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.””***. The east and the west are the two doors of the horizon. The verse deeds both of them to one owner. The gloss of Ibn ʿAbbās marks the clause as a dismissal of the jeer's premise. No direction belongs to a people. The directions belong to the one who raised the sky over all of them. The same dismissal had already been filed against the claimants of exclusive guidance (2:120 — **“Never will the Jews or Christians be pleased with you, until you follow their faith. Say,”**). Their displeasure is scheduled. The verdict answers the claim to monopoly (2:120 — **““Allah’s guidance is the only ˹true˺ guidance.” And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge”**). The guidance is His to give. The turn of a prayer-direction is His to command. The difference between a shrine and a Lord is the difference between a rented room and the owner of the street.

**A QUESTION WITH TWO AUDIENCES**

The closing sentence of the answer is a policy for the Prophet's own table. He guides whoever He wills to the straight path. The clause is mercy and warning in one line. Mercy for every convert who arrives late. Warning for every family that assumes a birthright. Ibn ʿAbbās's reading exposes the two audiences of the jeer in one glance. The foolish who asked for sport. The sincere who heard an answer. For the modern reader the scene replays whenever a practice changes in public. The lightweight asks why. The weighty ask what the owner has said. This age treats every change as a scandal and every custom as a deed of ownership. The verse corrects both habits. The horizons are leased. The guidance is granted. The straight path is the only possession worth competing for, and it is distributed by the one who holds the title to both doors of the sky.

**JEERS AS AN ADDRESS PROBLEM**

The scene also grades the way a community handles its public questions. The foolish of the verse are not people who lacked information. They are people who addressed the question to the wrong department. The question of sacred law was aimed at the Prophet's back as a taunt, and the answer was mailed over their heads to the sincere. The effect of that routing is a standing policy. Public mockery receives a doctrine. Honest inquiry receives a teacher. The reason the distinction matters is that the two audiences are usually standing in the same room. The verse trains the ear to hear which one has spoken. For the modern reader the training is urgent in the square of the platforms. This age rewards the loudest question with the largest audience and calls the exchange a debate. The verse keeps the two mail-rooms separate. The jeer is answered with ownership. The sincere question is answered with guidance. The east and the west remain the property of the one who made both horizons, and the mail of the mockery never changes his address.
