**THE OBJECTION THE CHANGE WOULD PROVOKE**

The verse turns to a question that the sūrah knew was coming, and it reports it as a prediction about the shallowest element in the community: ***“The foolish among the people will ask, “Why did they turn away from the direction of prayer they used to face?””***. The objectors are named before they speak, so that the question's weight is settled in advance. The change of direction had been a public act, and the sentence shows that the Book expected it to be discussed by people whose interest was in the appearance rather than in the obedience. The account attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās identifies the questioners as those in Medina who used the change as a handle against the community, and the answer is addressed to everyone who might follow them. The objection was anticipated, which is itself part of the answer.

**A TURNING MADE AN ISSUE**

The words put in the objectors' mouths concern a turning rather than a teaching, and the sentence treats a matter of orientation as though it had to do with the ownership of God. The previous stretch of the sūrah had just finished answering claims about ancestors and inheritance, and the passage now takes up a question about ritual that had been raised against the community. The Book's practice in these disputes is to answer the premise rather than the complaint, and the answer that follows does exactly that. Anyone who has seen a change in practice become the centre of an argument can see what the question is designed to do. The objection, then, is not about the direction at all.

**OWNERSHIP STATED BEFORE GUIDANCE**

The reply opens with a statement of ownership and closes with a statement of guidance: ***“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “The east”***, ***“and west belong ˹only˺ to Allah. He guides whoever He wills to the Straight Path.””***. The two directions were named together in an earlier verse of the sūrah, and the repetition keeps the answer on the same foundation: the ground belongs to its owner. Another passage names the same principle when it tells the believers that every community has its own direction and that the competition is in good deeds: (2:148 — **“Everyone turns to their own direction ˹of prayer˺. So compete with one another in doing good”**). The closing clause then places the guidance where the Book always places it, with the One who gives it rather than with the people who argue about the form it takes. He is left with a reply that would end the dispute if the disputants were interested in the answer.

**ARGUING OVER WHICH WALL FACES THE ROAD**

Think of a family that receives instructions to move the table to the other wall and that spends the evening arguing about which of the two walls the owner of the house prefers. The sentence in the verse answers the argument by naming the owner, and it does so before anyone has a chance to settle the matter the way the objectors wanted. The comparison clarifies why the Book refuses to discuss the change on the terms offered, since the terms assume that a party other than the owner has standing in the question. Anyone who has watched a household spend its energy on the placement of furniture while the owner's instructions go elsewhere can see the situation described. The plainness of the answer and the folly of the question are what the verse leaves behind.

**WHAT IS SETTLED BY THE REPLY**

The verse settles two things in three sentences: the ground belongs to God, and the guidance of the community is His gift rather than a consequence of the direction it faces. The sūrah will go on to explain the purpose of the change in the verses that follow, but the answer given here does not wait for that explanation. The Book's manner in this passage is to place the principle before the history, so that the reader understands the reason before he hears the account. Anyone who has followed this sūrah from its opening verses will recognise the method, since the same order has been used in nearly every dispute the chapter has recorded. The difference between the objection anticipated and the answer provided is what the sentence leaves.
