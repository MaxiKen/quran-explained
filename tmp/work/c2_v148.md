**A COMPASS FOR EVERY HOUSEHOLD**

The sentence opens by granting each community a direction of its own: ***“Everyone turns to their own direction ˹of prayer˺”***. What follows can be read in two ways: a people turns itself towards what it faces, or a people is turned towards it. The second reading gives the sentence more weight, since the direction then belongs to an appointment rather than to a preference. The scope given to the phrase in the reading traced to al-Ṭabarī is every adherent of a religion. The same scope is given to the communities around the first believers by Ibn ʿAbbās. The sentence answers a quarrel, since neither side was following the other's qiblah, and each side already had what it faced; al-Qurṭubī's treatment of the verse turns on precisely that quarrel.

**A RACE WITH THE FINISH IN VIEW**

From the difference in directions the verse moves at once to the thing that counts, and what it commands is a competition in good: ***“So compete with one another in doing good”***. Striving in acts of obedience and in the acceptance of them is the sense the command bears, so that the rivalry is not over the direction but over the response. Ibn ʿAbbās gave the command the sense of an address to the community to hasten towards good works beyond every other nation. The Book uses the same words where the differences between communities are mentioned and then set aside for the same purpose: (5:48 — **“So compete with one another in doing good. To Allah you will all return,”**). The verse is not indifferent to the direction; it refuses to let the direction become the whole religion.

**GATHERED FROM LAND AND SEA**

The promise that follows turns the matter toward the end of the road: ***“Wherever you are, Allah will bring you all together ˹for judgment˺”***. Ibn ʿAbbās glosses the phrase with land and sea, since distance is no obstacle to the One who gathers, and the sentence names the assembly at which the competition and the quarrels are finally weighed. The command to compete adds promptness and completeness to the mere doing of good, so the community is asked for its best effort rather than for the minimum that satisfies the letter. That distinction is drawn out in al-Saʿdī's treatment of the verse. The verse closes on the ground of that confidence: ***“Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything”***. The power that will gather the scattered is the same power that will settle what the competitors disputed.

**TWO RUNNERS IN LANES THEY ARGUE ABOUT**

Think of two runners on separate lanes of the same track who spend the morning disputing which lane the organizer intended, while the organizer stands at the finish line and waits. The dispute is not settled by one of them proving the other's lane wrong; it is settled when the race is run and the times are read. The community is given the same instruction here: leave the quarrel about lanes to the One who set the course, and put the whole of one's strength into running well. Every community that argues about its own correctness while neglecting what it was told to do has repeated the first half of the scene and missed the second. The image also shows why the sentence leaves the others' directions alone: the question has been moved to another field.

**THE DISPUTE LEFT WHERE IT IS**

The verse neither abolishes the differences nor pretends they do not matter, and the way it handles them is instructive. The community is not asked to prove that its direction is right before it begins to act; it is asked to act, and the proof is left to the day when all are gathered. The Book makes the same point elsewhere when it denies that righteousness lies in the direction a face is turned: (2:177 — **“Righteousness is not in turning your faces towards the east or the west.”**). What follows that sentence is a list of deeds, which is exactly the order this verse establishes. The passage therefore closes its treatment of the qiblah with a command that outlasts the dispute, and the community is measured by what it does with the time it has.
