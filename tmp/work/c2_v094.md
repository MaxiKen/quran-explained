**TESTING A CLAIM OF EXCLUSIVE SALVATION**

A claim of this kind was not unique to one community, as a later passage shows when it is reported in the words of both peoples at once: (2:111 — **“The Jews and Christians each claim”**). The verse takes the claim about the next life and turns it into a wager that can be settled at once: ***“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively”***, ***“for you ˹Israelites˺ out of all humanity”***, ***“then wish for death”***, ***“if what you say is true!””*** The claim is not denied in this verse; it is accepted for the sake of the argument, and then a consequence is drawn from it. A person who is certain that he is going to a permanent home with God, and who has been told that the waiting period is a hard one, ought to want the waiting to end quickly. The Book asks for the wish rather than the death, because a wish cannot be performed as a show. The sentence is a test of sincerity that requires nothing but a person's own desire, which is why it is such an uncomfortable form of proof. Ibn ʿAbbās read the Home as Paradise claimed exclusively and the call to wish for death as a test of whether the claimants truly expected what they announced.

**EXCLUSIVE DESTINY PUT TO PROOF**

The Book could have asked for an oath or a sacrifice, and it asks for an interior act instead, and the choice is instructive. A wish for death is a statement about what a person expects to find, and it cannot be made sincerely while the expectation is absent. The community had been claiming an exclusive share of the next world, and an exclusive claim that leaves the claimant desperate to remain here is a contradiction that anyone can see. Another passage puts the claim about the Garden to the same kind of test, and the practice here is to let a claim carry its own examination. What the reader learns from the technique is that a doctrine about the future can be tested by the shape of a person's desires in the present.

**ORIGINS OF A CLAIM ABOUT THE GARDEN**

The claim was not an isolated eccentricity; it was a settled self-understanding that had grown over generations, and it appears twice in this sūrah in different forms. The Book reports the community asserting a limited punishment for itself in one place and an exclusive Garden in another, and the pattern shows a doctrine built to secure a position rather than to describe a fact. A later passage addresses the same confidence with the same invitation, so the reader meets the argument more than once: (62:6 — **“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Jews!”**). What an inherited claim of this kind protects is not usually a belief but a manner of living, and the Book's test reaches past the claim into the life that the claim was shielding. A reader who belongs to a community with a settled story about its own standing can hear the same question asked of him.

**DEATH AS A MEASURE OF WHAT A PERSON EXPECTS**

Think of a man who has been promised a house in a better country and who has spent thirty years finding reasons to postpone the voyage. The Book uses the wish for death as a lens because it measures expectation without allowing the usual evasions, and the measurement is uncomfortable for anyone who has not examined his own hope. The verse does not condemn the desire to live; the sūrah praises life and forbids its destruction elsewhere. What it exposes is the gap between a stated certainty about the next world and the actual preferences of the person stating it, and the gap is available to any reader who is honest. The Book's question in this verse is not answered by its hearers, which is an answer in itself, and the next verse explains why.

**A TEST THAT REQUIRES NOTHING BUT HONESTY**

The invitation in this verse is an unusual kind of proof, since it costs the person nothing to perform and cannot be performed falsely. Anyone who has read from the covenant up to this point can see the whole method of the sūrah in one sentence: a claim is stated, a consequence is drawn, and the conduct of the claimant is allowed to speak. The Book's interest here is not in extracting a confession but in showing a community the shape of its own position, and the demonstration is complete before anyone answers. What the reader takes away is a way of examining his own hopes, since the test can be adapted to any claim about the future that a person makes. The next verse supplies the result, and the result is given before the community has a chance to reply. Today, claims of guaranteed belonging deserve the same test of whether they produce preparation, humility and honest expectation.
