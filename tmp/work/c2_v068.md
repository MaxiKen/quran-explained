**A QUESTION THAT BENDS THE OPEN DOOR**

The suspicion of mockery died when the prophet stood by his office, and the people swung from ridicule to bargaining: ***“They said, “Call upon your Lord to clarify”***, ***“for us what type ˹of cow˺ it should be!””***. The request sounds innocent, and that is precisely its cost, because the command had arrived open. Slaughter a cow — any cow, as Ibn ʿAbbās reads the command's bare form — and the answer stood ready. What they asked for was not the essence of the thing; they knew what a cow is. The question is in truth about the attribute, the distinguishing description that would tell one animal from the rest, since a request to clarify what it is only makes sense once the named referent is already known. Their own gloss in the same dialogue confirms the reading, since Ibn ʿAbbās takes the question as asking whether the animal should be large or small. The reply then closes the first door with an instruction rather than a longer description: ***“He replied, “Allah says, ‘The cow should neither be old nor young”***, ***“but in between. So do”***, ***“as you are commanded!’””***. The closing command carries its own gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās as *"and ask no further questions"*, and the effect of their bargaining is already visible in the direction of the text. Each question they add behaves like a doorway being narrowed from both sides, which means the burden of finding the right animal grows with every condition they themselves requested. The next question in their own sequence shows the pattern continuing without pause (2:69 — **“They said, “Call upon your Lord to specify for us its colour.” He replied, “Allah says,”**).

**BETWEEN THE AGED AND THE UNTRIED**

The description given is a span rather than a point: not the aged and not the first-timer, but a middle age between the two conditions. The first designation marks the female that has passed bearing and reached the years of failure; the second marks the untried young that has never borne at all; and the language sets both in the feminine by usage, so that the terms speak of the animal as they would speak of the woman. The middle designation then does the actual work of exclusion. Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Mujāhid, Qatādah, al-Rabīʿ b. Anas, and Ibn ʿAbbās stand with al-Suddi, Ikrimah, and al-Dahhak in the wider body of opinion that takes the span as the age when breeding has begun but not yet failed — neither old nor below the age of bearing. On the tightest gloss in the list, al-Dahhak reports from Ibn ʿAbbās that the middle age means *"the age when the cow is strongest and fittest"*, and that report turns an ordinary market category into a search for the animal at the height of its value. In other words, the description is doing two jobs at once: it removes every cow at the edges of life while leaving the whole middle of the herd still in play, so that the search stays long even as the field grows smaller. What turns on this economy is the shape of the trial itself. A single instant judgment would have solved the murder; what their questions have produced instead is a catalogue of conditions that must all be satisfied at once, and the difference between those two outcomes is the lesson the story keeps in view.

**STUBBORNNESS ANSWERED WITH ITS OWN WEIGHT**

Behind the description stands the ruling principle of the whole episode, and the early commentators state it in one line: had they slaughtered any cow, that would have been enough; they imposed hardship on themselves, so hardship was imposed on them. Ibn ʿAbbās and Abū al-ʿĀliyah report the substance in those terms, and al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī relates the same balance in a prophetic report — the open command closes exactly as far as the objectors push it. The point is not that clarification is wrong; a seeker of certainty may ask. The point is the motive and the manner, since these questions come not from piety but from the obstinacy that hopes a command will dissolve under scrutiny. Their own later complaint confirms the diagnosis, because the catalogue reaches the place where all cows look alike to them and the trial they engineered has become genuinely hard (2:71 — **“wholesome and without blemish.’” They said, “Now you have come with the truth.” Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly”**). The narrated outcome follows the same balance to its end: they slaughtered the animal though they were near to not doing it, and the near-miss is the fruit of their own bargaining. The reading passes to the congregation that hears the passage today as an instruction in obedience: when a command is plain, the heart that races to fulfil it keeps the door open, and the heart that negotiates conditions forges the very narrowness it will later complain of.
