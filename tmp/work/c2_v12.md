**A SENTENCE READ BACKWARDS**
The claim of the previous verse is answered here, and the answer turns the sentence inside out: ***“Indeed, it is they”*** ***“who are the corruptors”***. The construction is a device of restriction, which means it sweeps every other candidate away at once: not the believers, not the cautious, not the men who kept their distance from trouble, but these. The Book uses the same turn of phrase at the end of the sūrah's portraits of this company, which is why the reader should hear the words as a door closing rather than as a complaint. Picture a committee that has spent a year blaming its neighbours for the state of a shared garden. It is not answered by a poll but by a photograph of the weeds growing on its own side of the wall. That is why the photograph is what the verse supplies, and it supplies it without raising its voice. The sentence is short because a reversal needs no elaboration, and a reader who has followed the portraits already knows the faces in it.
**WHY THE EYE REFUSES THE EVIDENCE**
The last clause of the verse explains how a reversal can be so invisible to the people it describes: ***“but they fail to perceive it”***. A worker who has spent his life building a wall cannot see the crack in it; that is why the Book treats the lack of perception as part of the sentence rather than as an excuse for it. The point is not that these men are unaware of the rules; it is that they have lost the habit of measuring their work against any standard other than their own comfort. The same failing closes the earlier portrait in this passage as well (2:9), and the effect of the repetition is to make blindness, rather than malice, the mark of the type. The cure therefore begins with a suspicion of oneself, which is a habit the Book keeps recommending. It grows every time a man asks a friend who has no reason to flatter him for an honest answer.
**THE COST THAT NOBODY BOOKS**
Ruining a shared thing does not feel like ruin from the inside, and that is the trap this verse sets out in the open. The man in it is not wicked in his own account; he is busy, or careful, or misunderstood. The damage he does is filed under the ordinary business of living, and any reader can catch that filing habit in himself today. A leaking tap that nobody reports wastes more water in a year than a burst pipe does in a day, and nothing about a dripping tap looks like an emergency. That is why a community needs its standards written down and its accounts open. The eye that refuses the evidence must be given something other than itself to look at, and a ledger is exactly that. The verse closes with the phrase that ended the earlier portrait. The repetition is a kindness to the reader, since it tells him that the blindness is the disease, and that a man who can still hear the sentence has not yet caught it.
