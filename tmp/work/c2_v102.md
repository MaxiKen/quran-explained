**THE CHARGE AGAINST A KING**

The next stroke of the passage restores the honor of a prophet and exposes the trade that destroyed the traders: ***“They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon”***. The charge had a shape. The claim ran that Solomon's kingdom was a kingdom of sorcery, that its first forty days were sorcery and white magic, that the throne rested on spells. The gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās records the substance of the slander plainly: the devils wrote of the collapse of Solomon's kingdom and of those forty days of magic. A rumor of that kind does two jobs at once. It degrades a prophet, and it gives magic a royal pedigree. The party in the passage reached for the second job with both hands. If magic was the currency of a prophet's rule, then magic was merchandise with a provenance. The verse's first clause names the tragedy in one word. They followed. The following is the whole engine of the loss.

**THE PARENTHESIS THAT PROTECTS A PROPHET**

The answer comes as the oldest form of defense in this surah, the declaration of separation: ***“Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved”***. The separation is made explicit in the sharpest available grammar. The prophet is cleared in the same breath that convicts the devils. The gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās turns each half into its own verdict. Solomon did not write this sorcery and this white magic. The devils disbelieved in that they did write it. The tradition behind the story keeps a second version of the same exoneration. The devils buried their books of sorcery beneath the throne, or fed fabrications to priests who compiled them, until Solomon gathered the volumes and buried them himself. After his death the devils showed people where the books lay. Either version leaves the prophet's hands clean of the trade. The books were the devils' works, kept out of the way by the very king the slander was pinned on. The freedom-of-action doctrine already carried in this surah applies with full weight here, and it is the same freedom the magic verse itself reports from the contest of the magicians: (20:69 — **“Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made,”**). What the sorcerers could never do is the verse's counter-declaration: (20:69 — **“for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go.””**). The parenthesis in the present verse says something about prophethood and something about magic at once. Prophets do not write spells. Spells do not succeed.

**THE TWO AGENTS SENT DOWN AT BABYLON**

The passage then turns from the slander to the market it fed. The teaching is named in the verse's own geography: ***“They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon”***. The gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās reads the teaching scene with two lights held up. In one reading the teachers were the devils, and the angels were never taught sorcery at all. In the other, the verse means the people also learned what the angels were inspired with. Both readings protect the same boundary. The angels are not smuggled into the trade as its craftsmen. The verse itself then says the part that governs every account of the descent: ***“The two angels never taught anyone without saying”***, and the warning follows in the angels' own quoted words: ***“We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith”***. The function of the descent was the exposure of its own buyers. Whoever bought the craft bought the means to harm. The warning in the angels' own mouths is therefore the first consumer-protection notice in the passage. The product was sold with its own hazard label attached. The buyers read the label and signed anyway. The market at Babylon was never a market of the deceived. It was a market of the informed. The reason the verse files the whole scene under testing is that the knowledge was poison in commercial form, and the buyers are exactly the party the passage is prosecuting.

**THE SEAL ON THE CRAFT**

What the craft could and could not do is then sealed with absolute clauses. First the inventory of the learning: ***“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife”***. Then the ceiling on its power: ***“although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will”***. The indictment follows in the accounting word of the passage: ***“They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them”***. The seal matters because the trade in these verses is priced twice, once by its power and once by its outcome. The power was real enough to frighten crowds and to split households. The outcome was harm to the buyer. This is the same asymmetry the trade of the wicked already carried in the surah's marketplace passages. The deal looked like a bargain at the counter and a loss in the ledger. The reason the ceiling clause matters is that it prices the craft as borrowed power, which means the buyer pays for a permission he does not control. The judgment of the passage is therefore not that magic is imaginary. The judgment is that magic is real, borrowed, capped, and expensive. The craftsman of these arts works downward. The one who refuses them loses nothing.

**THE PRICE PAID IN THE SELLING**

The close of the unit reads the loss as self-inflicted, and the verse reaches the accounting by way of what the buyers already knew: ***“although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter”***. The strangest fact of the trade is its brokerage. The gloss from Ibn ʿAbbās puts the knowing of the buyers at the center of the sale, and the knowing is what makes the brokerage so strange. The buyers traded revelation for rumor. They left the pages that named the awaited one, and they paid for the devils' pages instead. The verdict on the sale is then delivered in the passage's coldest line: ***“Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls”***. The sale had been priced twice over, once in the learning and once in the hereafter, and both invoices were in the buyers' hands before the purchase. The brokerage is therefore the verse's coldest irony. The brokers kept the revelation out of the trade and kept the ruin in it. The final wound is the wish that closes the verse: ***“if only they knew”***. What follows from the knowing clause is the passage's final refusal of the ignorance excuse. The trade of guidance for misguidance has been named in this surah before, and the naming there was clinical. For any reader who has watched a rumor priced above a document — in a family dispute, in the market, in the commentary sections under a news story — the anatomy at Babylon is instructive. The devils still publish. The throne still gets the burial, and the shelf of the two books keeps its labels. The verse keeps the two books on the table and lets the reader choose which one gets cast behind the back. The choice is the reader's own, and the verse has already priced both options.
