**THE ART THEY TOOK UP INSTEAD**

The verse begins by naming what the community had been doing while the confirming Book lay behind its back, and the first words set the alternative in place: ***“They ˹instead˺ followed the magic promoted by the devils during the reign of Solomon.”***. The ˹instead˺ in the translation marks a substitution, as though a choice had been made between two available things, and the choice was made in favour of an art that the devils themselves had promoted. The period the verse names is the reign of Solomon, which places the practice in a time the community remembered as its own golden age rather than in some foreign or forgotten era. A report carried from Ibn ʿAbbās describes the community's claim in this connection as one that traced the art back to that reign, and the verse corrects the attribution in the same breath that it records the practice. What the reader is being shown is a people that had something better in its hands and reached for something else.

**SOLOMON IS CLEARED BEFORE THE STORY IS TOLD**

Before anything is said about what was taught, an accusation that had attached itself to a prophet is removed, and the removal is emphatic: ***“Never did Solomon disbelieve, rather the devils disbelieved.”***. The order of the two clauses matters, since the denial is placed first and the true subject of the charge second, so that the reader hears the correction before the deed is described. The Book has already shown, earlier in this sūrah, how a people's own traditions can dishonour the prophets it claims to honour, and the pattern repeats here in a single sentence. The historical claim that this king had commanded the art is traced by Ibn ʿAbbās to a story the community itself circulated, which means the verse is correcting an inherited slander rather than answering an outside critic. What stands after the correction is the true account of the art's transmission, and the transmitters are not the king but the devils who claimed it.

**THE TRANSMISSION AND ITS RECIPIENTS**

The sentence continues with the activity of those devils and names the recipients: ***“They taught magic to the people, along with what had been revealed to the two angels, Hârût and Mârût, in Babylon.”***. The construction is worth a second look, because the revelation to the two angels is placed in the same clause as the teaching of magic, and the Book is careful to distinguish the two in what follows. Babylon is named as the place, and the naming gives the practice a location and a history rather than leaving it as a vague menace. The setting is the same city the community remembered as the site of its own exile, and a report from Ibn ʿAbbās places the two angels' descent there with a warning attached. A reader who has seen how quickly a practice acquires a respectable lineage will notice what this verse does with the lineage it is offered: it separates the divine revelation from the human art and refuses to let them share a name.

**THE WARNING THAT CAME WITH THE TEACHING**

The verse then reports the terms on which the knowledge was delivered, and the terms turn the whole episode into a test: ***“The two angels never taught anyone without saying,”*** and then the words they never omitted: ***“We are only a test ˹for you˺, so do not abandon ˹your˺ faith.”***. The disclaimer is spoken by the teachers themselves, which means the people who took the art up had been told plainly, at the moment of receiving it, what it was for and what it risked. There is no suggestion in the sentence that the information was unavailable or hidden, and none that the buyers were misled about the price of the transaction. The verse makes the two angels witnesses against the very use their knowledge was put to, and Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have read the clause as a warning rather than an invitation. What the reader should see is a decision made in the open, with the outcome stated in advance by the people who knew most about it.

**A PATIENT WHO READS THE WARNING LABEL**

Picture a patient who reads the leaflet that comes with a medicine, understands the risk it sets out, and takes the dose anyway because the effect is what he wants. The next words of the verse describe exactly that decision and its result: ***“Yet people learned ˹magic˺ that caused a rift ˹even˺ between husband and wife;”***, and the harm named is domestic rather than spectacular. The art's first effect is to split what the Book has spent much of this sūrah binding together, and the phrase marks the damage as reaching the smallest unit of the community. Ibn ʿAbbās describes the practice in this period as one that set spouses against each other, and the verse's own report of it is that it worked in exactly that way. Anyone who has seen a household come apart over something that promised an advantage to one of its members has seen the mechanism described here, and the Book is not finished with the subject.

**THE LIMIT ON THE HARM THEY COULD DO**

The following clause places a boundary around the whole practice: ***“although their magic could not harm anyone except by Allah’s Will.”***. The art that the community had taken up, then, was neither self-sufficient nor secure, since the harm it produced was always within the permission of the One who permitted no other power. The same boundary appears in another sūrah where the claim of the magicians is measured against what God does with it: (10:81 — **“Allah will surely make it useless”**). The Book never treats magic as a rival power; it treats it as a technique that works inside limits, and this clause is one of the clearest statements of that position. A reader who has feared such things more than he feared God has the verse's correction in the sentence, and the correction is not so much a warning as a description of where the power actually lies.

**THEY TRADED SOMETHING FOR NOTHING**

The verse then states the bargain from the buyers' side: ***“They learned what harmed them and did not benefit them—”***, and the verdict is followed by a clause about knowledge that leaves no room for ignorance: ***“although they already knew that whoever buys into magic would have no share in the Hereafter.”***. The transaction described is one in which the purchaser knew the price and the loss before he paid, which places this community in the same family as the buyers of guidance named earlier in the sūrah: (2:86 — **“These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world”**). The Book's account of the episode is therefore about a settled decision rather than a seduction, and the sentence repeats the point about knowledge to keep that fact in front of the reader. What the people acquired here was a technique with no lasting use, and the clause about the Hereafter makes the accounting explicit rather than implied.

**THE SUM PAID FOR THE ART**

The verse closes with a verdict on the terms of the sale and then with an exclamation that leaves the reader holding the loss: ***“Miserable indeed was the price for which they sold their souls,”*** and then ***“if only they knew”***. The word for the price suggests a measure that anyone could inspect, and the Book's judgement is that the sum was pitiful for what it bought. The final clause returns to knowledge, which the verse has already stated the buyers possessed, so that the exclamation reads as a lament over a knowing choice rather than a comment on information that never arrived. The passage has therefore come full circle from its opening: it began with a community following an art instead of a revelation, and it ends with the price of that decision stated in the plainest terms. The reading carried from Ibn ʿAbbās treats the closing exclamation as a lament over a knowing choice, and the verse's own words support him: the buyers had been told what the art was and what it cost. The next verse names the alternative that had been available the whole time, and the reader can compare the two outcomes for himself.
