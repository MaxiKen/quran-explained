**THE FIRST VERDICT IN THE SŪRAH**

Four verses have described a reader of the Book, and then the sentence lands: ***“It is they who are ˹truly˺ guided by their Lord”***. The word order is emphatic, and the emphasis matters. The Book does not say that such people are likely to be guided or that they are among the guided. It says that they and nobody else are the ones who have found the road from their Lord. That is a verdict pronounced early, before the sūrah has asked for anything difficult, and the reader who has been recognising himself in the description is now told plainly what he has been given. The sūrah is not holding its judgement for the end. Because the description has already been given in plain terms, the verdict is something the reader can test against his own habits instead of waiting for someone else's opinion.

The phrase is also careful about where the guidance comes from. It is guidance from their Lord, not guidance they produced, so the reader is not being congratulated on a private achievement. He followed what was shown to him, which is exactly what the earlier verse about believing the unseen and keeping the prayer described. Because the Lord is the one who gives it, the same reader can ask for more of it without insulting what he has already received. The two verses are joined at this point: the description reads like a list of qualities, and the verdict names the one who gave them, so the credit for the whole list goes to a single hand. That is why the line ends in praise rather than in self-assessment, and why the reader who finds the qualities in himself has nothing to boast about. **SUCCESS MEASURED ON A DIFFERENT SCALE**

The verse finishes with a second verdict that opens the largest question in the sūrah: ***“and it is they who will be successful”***. Everyone reading wants to succeed, and the Book immediately means something other than what the market means. The word carries the sense of reaching the thing a person was made for and being safe from the loss he should fear. It is a word about arriving whole rather than about acquiring more. A trader whose profits are rising can be failing by this measure, and a man whose business has collapsed can be succeeding. The sūrah puts the claim in front of the reader before listing a single rule, so that everything which follows is read inside the right definition of winning. A reader who keeps the market's definition in his head will find most of the sūrah puzzling, and the puzzle will be his own.

That definition is not a private opinion. The Qur'an repeats it in plain terms when it describes the same people at the end of their lives, and the phrasing there removes the ambiguity: (7:43 — **“Praise be to Allah for guiding us to this. We would have never been guided”**). In that scene the successful are the ones who have arrived and who credit their arrival to guidance they did not manufacture, and the credit is spoken aloud in front of everyone, which is why the verse is worth reading twice. So the two halves of this verse belong together: guidance comes first, and success is what guidance looks like when the road has been walked to the end. Anyone who tries to purchase the second without the first is buying a word.

**WHO IS DOING THE GUIDING AND WHO IS BEING GUIDED**

A subtle shift appears in this line and it has been prepared from the beginning. The earlier verses spoke about the believers in the third person, and here the Book turns to the reader and says that these are the ones guided by their Lord. The reader is not merely being described; he is being invited to check whether he stands in that group. Guidance is a relationship, not a certificate, and the relationship is named from the side that gives, which means it can be renewed every day instead of being filed away once. That is why the sūrah can say elsewhere that the believer asks for guidance every day without contradicting this verse. What is declared here is a state, and the state is maintained by the same God who established it. That is why the asking continues: a person does not stop needing the road because he has found it.

The word used for their Lord in this line is the one that appeared in the first sūrah as well, which ties the two chapters together: the Lord who nurtures what He made is the one doing the guiding. It means the guidance is not a single rescue but a running provision, like rain that arrives on the day it is needed. A person who has watched his own life over years will recognise the pattern more easily than one who is looking for a single decisive moment. The verse leaves room for both readings and insists only on this: the guidance is real, and it has an owner who is present in the reader's own language and circumstances. Because that owner is named as the Lord who nurtures, the line says something about care as well as about direction.

**THE PRACTICAL SIDE OF BEING CALLED GUIDED**

A reader who takes this verdict seriously has to resist two opposite errors. The first is to treat the description as a flattering label and stop examining himself, which turns guidance into a possession he can carry without using. The second is to treat the verdict as unreachable and lose heart, which turns the whole opening of the sūrah into a description of other people. The correct use is the plain one: the reader measures his week against the five marks and finds out where he stands. Today that measurement is as easy and as uncomfortable as asking whether his prayer times actually hold, whether his giving has a number attached, and whether his decisions change when nobody is watching.

The verse has one more thing to say to the reader who is honest about his failures. The people it describes have not been described as flawless; they believe, pray, give, accept the earlier books and expect the Hereafter. Nowhere does the Book say that they never slip. What it says is that this is where they are headed and this is who is guiding them, and that is a far more durable arrangement than a record of perfect behaviour, because the arrangement survives a bad week. The reader who fails today is still inside the description if he picks the habits up again tomorrow, and the sūrah will keep returning to that possibility for another two hundred and eighty verses.
