**FATHERS REPLACED**

The festival verse walks from the praise of the fathers to the half-request in its own phrases: ***“When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah”***, ***“as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺”***, ***“or even more passionately. There are some”***, ***“who say, “Our Lord!”***, ***“Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world,””***, ***“but they will have no share in the Hereafter”***. The sequence redirects the loudest custom of the year and then reviews the two prayers spoken at the monument in the festival's own voice. The direction is corrected and the loudness kept, which is the mission's method with every custom. The habit Ibn ʿAbbās describes at the monuments is what the first phrases redirect. The two prayers spoken at the monument are the festival's final exam, and the walk hands the reader both papers before the standing ends.

When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺ redirects the energy the pagans brought to their festivals. The pre-Islamic assemblies at the monuments were seasons of boasting, where the tribes recited their pedigrees and their praises of the fathers. The verse does not forbid the passion; it relocates it — the praise of the fathers becomes the praise of Allah, and the fervor is kept while the object is corrected. The report of the occasion preserves the custom in the words of Ibn ʿAbbās: the remembrance commanded is spoken aloud in the form the pagans once used for their lineage. Because the verse commands "or even more passionately," the worship receives the loudest register the culture possessed. The loudness of the festival is sanctified by its new direction.

**BOASTING AT THE STONES**

The scene at the monuments was theatrical: the pilgrims stood at the stones and proclaimed their families, and the proclamation was the rite's social currency. The verse arrives at that stage and gives the voice a new script — the extolling of Allah replaces the extolling of the bloodline. The custom is described in the reports as the naming of the fathers in the invocation, and the revelation replaces the father with the Father. Because the passion is legitimate and only its object was wrong, the correction is an elevation and not a rebuke of the emotion. The application is to review the festival's voices — including one's own — and to hear which names are being praised at the high moments of the year. The stones hear both kinds of boast; the revelation prefers one.

**TWO PRAYERS AT ONE GROUND**

The verse then gives the scene its two prayers, spoken at the same monument by two kinds of people: some ask their Lord for the bounty of this world alone and the others who ask for the world and the Hereafter together. The first prayer is described without insult — the speaker is not called a disbeliever — but its account is short: but they will have no share in the Hereafter. The one who asks for the world alone receives the world alone, which is not a punishment added but an account kept. The prayer of the good life is given in the scripture as the believer's own request: the good of this world and the Hereafter together (2:201 — **“Yet there are others who say, “Our Lord! Grant us the good of this world and the Hereafter,”**). Because both prayers are spoken on the same ground, the pilgrim hears his options aloud before he chooses.

**NO SHARE FOR HALF A REQUEST**

The closing accounting — the no-share of the one who asked only for this world — is the verse's warning to the half-prayer. The share of the Hereafter is not confiscated in anger; it was never requested, and the request determines the account. The remembrance commanded at the rites is therefore the act that teaches the full prayer, since the One remembered is the One who owns both worlds. The chosen servant's example stands behind the teaching — the one who was told to listen to what is revealed, because he had been chosen for the message (20:13 — **“I have chosen you, so listen to what is revealed”**). The listening shapes the asking: the person who listens at the rites learns to ask for the whole of the good. The application is to bring the full sentence to the monument — to praise Allah as the verse commands and to ask with both hands. The festival of the fathers has become the festival of the Father. The transformation of the assembly is the transformation of the religion in miniature: the same voices, the same passion, a new object. The verse's work is done when the monument hears the full prayer — for this world and the next — in the loudest voice the pilgrim owns. Because the praise is redirected rather than silenced, the culture keeps its poetry and loses its pride. The verse's method — redirect rather than erase — is the mission's method with every custom it meets. The reader keeps the loud voice of the festival and fills it with the full request: the good of this world and the good of the Hereafter, spoken together.
