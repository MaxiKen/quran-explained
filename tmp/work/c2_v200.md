**REMEMBRANCE AFTER THE RITES**

The verse develops remembrance after the rites with ***“When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah”***, ***“as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺”***. The pilgrimage does not end spiritually when the physical rites are completed. Allah commands the pilgrims to remember Him intensely after Hajj. Before Islam, some Arabs would remain at the sacred gathering boasting about their ancestors, recounting tribal victories and praising lineage. The Qur’an redirects that energy: remember Allah with greater seriousness than you remembered your fathers. Hence, the prayer for worldly gain is exposed as incomplete at 2:200. The command does not forbid honouring parents or preserving family history. Islam values gratitude to ancestors and maintaining kinship. It forbids turning ancestry into the centre of pride and identity while forgetting the One who created every ancestor. The best legacy of a family is obedience to Allah, not boasting about names. *Dhikr* includes magnifying God, declaring God’s oneness, praise, supplication, Qur’an recitation, and the remembrance of Allah’s gifts and promises. The pilgrim has witnessed the Kaʿbah, stood at ʿArafāt, spent the night at Muzdalifah, and performed the rites of Mina. These experiences should deepen awareness of Allah rather than become stories about personal achievement. *Or with even stronger remembrance* indicates intensity of attention and love. The heart should be more occupied with Allah than with tribal reputation, social media documentation, business conversations, or comparisons about who performed Hajj best. The greatest journey becomes a means of humility only when Allah remains its centre. (2:198 — **“When you return from ’Arafât, praise Allah near the sacred place”**) Because remembrance after the rites governs 2:200, two kinds of supplication arises from remembrance after the rites. It follows that family and the hereafter completes remembrance after the rites through two kinds of supplication.

**TWO KINDS OF SUPPLICATION**

At two kinds of supplication, attention falls on ***“or even more passionately. There are some”***. The verse contrasts two groups. Some people ask only for worldly benefit: wealth, health, success, status, or immediate security. These requests are not inherently forbidden. The problem is reducing life to the present world and having no concern for forgiveness, guidance, righteous character, or the Hereafter. Such a person may complete the physical rites while the horizon of his prayer remains worldly. The next verse gives the balanced supplication: Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire. Islam does not require abandoning lawful worldly needs. It teaches believers to ask for them within a larger desire for eternal success. A pilgrim may spend money, endure travel, and seek public honour while neglecting the purpose of worship. He may want the title of ḥājj, a business advantage, political influence, or social admiration. The verse asks whether his remembrance after the rites is directed to Allah or to the status he gained from being seen at the sanctuary. The remedy is renewing intention, hiding voluntary worship where possible, limiting unnecessary display, and remembering death and resurrection. Hajj is a rehearsal for the final gathering, where lineage and wealth will not save a person without faith and righteous action. (3:190 — **“Indeed, in the creation of the heavens”**) The reason family and the hereafter follows two kinds of supplication lies in remembrance after the rites. Therefore, family and the hereafter returns practical force to two kinds of supplication.

**FAMILY AND THE HEREAFTER**

Family and the hereafter draws together ***“who say, “Our Lord!”***, ***“Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world,””***. The reference to fathers also acknowledges that human beings naturally organize memory around family and belonging. Islam does not erase family identity; it places it under divine oneness. A person can love his parents, serve his relatives, and honour his ancestors while refusing to follow them in error. The question is whether family pride leads to gratitude or to superiority. The Prophet ﷺ warned in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī that boasting about ancestors is a remnant of the age of ignorance and taught that people are all from Ādam, while true honour comes through mindfulness. This protects communities from racism, tribal contempt, and inherited claims to religious privilege. The phrase they will have no share in the Hereafter is a warning about a person whose entire aim is the world and who meets Allah without faith or righteous concern. It is not a declaration that a believer who asks for a job, healing, marriage, or safety has no Hereafter. The distinction is between a lawful worldly request joined to faith and a worldview that excludes the Hereafter. Worldly blessings can become provision for the Hereafter when used in obedience. Wealth supports family and charity; health supports prayer and service; knowledge benefits people. The believer asks Allah to bless the world without allowing it to become his ultimate destination. (53:32 — **“as He created you from the earth”**)

**COMPLETION AND CONTINUITY**

***“but they will have no share in the Hereafter”*** mark the turn toward completion and continuity. The verse’s placement after the main Hajj rites teaches that completion is a beginning. The pilgrim returns to ordinary life carrying the habits of remembrance, prayer, patience, generosity, and equality. If the journey was accepted, its effects should appear in how he treats relatives, workers, neighbours, and the poor. The community can preserve this transformation by continuing gatherings of Qur’an and dhikr, supporting pilgrims in making changes, and discouraging competition over prestige. Remembrance should be stronger than the memory of the journey itself. Picture a traveller who finishes a demanding road may spend the evening praising the family name that funded the trip while forgetting the guide who brought him safely through. The old boasting after pilgrimage had that misplaced direction. The verse turns the tongue toward God, then exposes a prayer whose horizon ends at worldly gain. Today, a completed act of worship can likewise become family prestige, photographs, or business advantage; passionate remembrance protects the rite from being reclaimed by the ego that performed it. (49:13 — **“O humanity! Indeed, We created you from a male”**) In other words, completion and continuity answers remembrance after the rites.
