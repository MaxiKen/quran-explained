**THE LONGEST VERSE AND THE ETHICS OF DOCUMENTATION**

***“O believers! When you contract a loan for a fixed period of time, commit it to writing. Let the scribe maintain justice between the parties. The scribe should not refuse to write as Allah has taught them to write. They will write what the debtor dictates, bearing Allah in mind and not defrauding the debt. If”*** establishes the longest verse and the ethics of documentation. This is the longest āyah in the Qur’an, and its length reflects the importance of preserving rights. Debt can create gratitude, tension, suspicion, and family conflict. A written record protects the lender from denial and the borrower from exaggerated claims. It also protects heirs, witnesses, and communities when the original parties die or memories differ. The command to write—faktubūhu—is a strong instruction toward documentation. Classical jurists discussed whether it is an obligation in every debt or a recommendation reinforced by the surrounding commands. The moral principle is clear: important financial commitments should be recorded accurately, especially when repayment is delayed or the amount is significant. The scribe must write between the parties with justice—bi-l-ʿadl. He should record the actual amount, currency, goods, due date, instalments, collateral, conditions, and any lawful fees without favouring the powerful party. He must not hide a penalty, change a number, or use technical language that the other party cannot understand. The command not to refuse writing as Allah has taught him honours useful skill as a trust. A qualified accountant, lawyer, translator, notary, or digital record keeper serves the public when he documents accurately. He may charge a fair fee, but he must not use his expertise to exploit ignorance. Modern electronic contracts, bank records, signed messages, and secure digital signatures can serve the purpose of written documentation when their authenticity and terms are clear. The medium changes; the obligation of accuracy and access to the record remains. The debtor is told to dictate the terms because he is the one who owes the obligation and knows what he has undertaken. He must fear Allah and leave nothing out. He cannot conceal a condition, deny receiving the money, or exploit the creditor’s trust. Honesty is required even when the lender is powerful. Having the debtor dictate also protects against a scribe or creditor imposing terms that were not agreed. The parties should read or explain the document in a language they understand before signing. Consent is meaningful only when the material terms are known and the person is free from coercion. If the debtor is safīh, mentally deficient, weak, or unable to dictate, his guardian dictates with justice. The provision protects minors, people with impaired judgement, the illiterate, and those temporarily unable to speak or understand the transaction. A guardian is not an owner; he is a trustee whose duty is to protect the person’s rights. The guardian must not exploit disability, age, illness, or lack of education. Independent witnesses, legal oversight, accessible explanations, and supported decision-making can help preserve genuine consent. The verse’s purpose is protection, not exclusion from financial life. The verse instructs the parties to call witnesses, establishing a strong evidentiary practice for deferred debts. It describes two men, or one man and two women from those acceptable as witnesses, with the stated purpose that one may remind the other if she errs. Classical jurists discussed the conditions, scope, and legal effect of this arrangement in the specific context of documented financial transactions. The wording should not be turned into a declaration that women are universally deficient in intelligence, memory, or moral worth. The Qur’an recognizes women as moral agents, property owners, teachers, witnesses in other legal contexts according to the relevant evidence, and equal recipients of spiritual accountability. (33:35 — **“Surely ˹for˺ Muslim men and women, believing men and women, devout men and women,”**)

**A SCRIBE WITH JUSTICE**

***“the debtor is incompetent, weak, or unable to dictate, let their guardian dictate for them with justice. Call upon two of your men to witness. If two men cannot be found, then one man and two women of your choice will witness—so if one of the women forgets the other may remind her. The witnesses”*** deepens a scribe with justice. The verse gives a procedure for a particular kind of financial documentation in a particular social setting, and its application requires knowledge of the legal tradition and contemporary evidentiary systems. Witnesses must be competent, impartial, and truthful. They should not accept payment that corrupts testimony, conceal a conflict of interest, or sign a document they do not understand. Where modern courts and secure records establish reliable evidence, qualified scholars can explain how the underlying objective is fulfilled. Witnesses should not refuse when they are called means that a person who is needed to protect a right should not evade the responsibility out of inconvenience, fear, or loyalty to a powerful party. At the same time, a witness should not be summoned to harassment, public humiliation, or an unlawful investigation. The request must be legitimate, and the witness must speak only to what he knows. The Qur’an repeatedly commands truthful testimony even when it is against oneself or relatives. A witness who remains silent while a vulnerable person’s property is stolen may share in the injustice. The command applies whether the debt is small or large. Small debts can produce enormous conflict because people assume that memory is enough or that recording a modest amount is insulting. Writing protects friendship and kinship by removing uncertainty. It also prevents a lender from later adding an amount that was never agreed. The specified term must be recorded. I will pay you later is a source of avoidable dispute. A clear date, schedule, or condition gives both parties a standard for communication and lawful extension. The verse gives an exception for a transaction completed immediately—tijārah ḥāḍirah tudīrunahā baynakum. There is no blame if the parties do not write every cash exchange on the spot. Even here, receipts, invoices, and records can be wise, especially in business. The exception removes a burden; it does not forbid documentation. The next command to take witnesses when concluding a commercial contract shows that caution remains valuable. The parties choose the level of documentation according to the risk, while debts and delayed obligations deserve particular care. A scribe or witness must not be harmed. Harm includes threats, bribery, coercion, defamation, withholding payment for professional work, or punishing someone for truthful testimony. A creditor and borrower may not pressure the scribe to alter the record, and an employer or official may not retaliate against a witness. The prohibition also protects the parties from a scribe or witness who uses his position to extort them. Everyone involved remains under justice. Documentation is meant to resolve conflict, not create a new opportunity for abuse. The verse ends by saying that Allah teaches and knows all things. Financial law is therefore a field of worship. A person learns how contracts work, writes clearly, seeks expert advice, and remembers that Allah sees the hidden agreement. Taqwā prevents both careless informality and manipulative legalism. The āyah gives a model for a trustworthy economy: clear terms, informed consent, protection of vulnerable people, reliable evidence, and accountability. These principles preserve friendship and prevent disputes before they grow. The case is like two neighbours who divide a field between their houses by a line of stones that only they can remember, and whose children argue about the line twenty years after the two men have died. (4:135 — **“O believers! Stand firm for justice as witnesses for Allah even if it is against yourselves,”**)

**GUARDIANSHIP OF THE VULNERABLE**

***“is no need for you to record it, but call upon witnesses when a deal is finalized. Let no harm come to the scribe or witnesses. If you do, then you have gravely exceeded ˹your limits˺. Be mindful of Allah, for Allah ˹is the One Who˺ teaches you. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”*** closes with guardianship of the vulnerable. The closing commands prevent the procedure from becoming an idol. Writing and witnesses serve justice, but they do not replace mindfulness of Allah. A perfectly drafted contract can still conceal coercion, and a technically valid record can be used vindictively. Conversely, careless informality may destroy a right even where both parties began with goodwill. Taqwā governs the conscience that no document can fully police. The instruction that neither scribe nor witness be harmed protects the institutions of truth from retaliation and manipulation. It also warns those professionals not to exploit their necessary role. Modern equivalents include translators, notaries, accountants, digital platforms, and legal advisers: each must preserve accuracy, consent, confidentiality, and access. Think of documentation as a bridge built before a river rises. Friends may cross easily in calm weather, but clear terms keep both sides from being swept apart when illness, death, market loss, or failed memory changes the current. Allah’s perfect knowledge closes the verse because every human record is partial. He knows the spoken agreement, the hidden pressure, the omitted clause, and the intention behind compliance. That awareness turns commercial care into worship and makes correcting an error more important than winning through ambiguity. (4:58 — **“Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners; and when you judge between people,”**) Because disputes expose forgotten assumptions, careful records protect both mercy and justice. Therefore a believer treats clarity before agreement as service to every party, not as suspicion of a friend or relative. Written precision leaves more room for graciousness later, because nobody must first fight over the facts.
