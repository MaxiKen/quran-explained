**THE OPPOSITE OF ARROGANT SPEECH**

***“And there are those who would dedicate their lives to”*** establishes the opposite of arrogant speech. The previous verses described someone whose speech impresses people but whose actions spread corruption. This verse presents a contrasting servant who sells himself for Allah’s pleasure. The metaphor of sale means that he gives his life, wealth, comfort, reputation, and desires in exchange for Allah’s acceptance. Unlike the person who trades guidance for a small worldly price, this believer makes the highest possible exchange. The phrase is not a call to despise life or to seek death recklessly. It describes sincere sacrifice in obedience: enduring hardship, defending the oppressed within lawful limits, spending wealth, leaving sin, serving people, and preferring Allah’s command over the ego. The servant’s life remains a trust from Allah, not property he may destroy without right. The motive is ib'tighāʾ marḍāt Allāh, seeking the pleasure of Allah. Actions that appear heroic can be corrupted by the desire for fame, revenge, political power, or admiration. The verse directs the heart to the unseen audience whose approval matters eternally. A private act of sacrifice for Allah may be greater than a public act celebrated by people. Sincere intention does not make an unlawful act lawful. A person cannot claim to seek Allah’s pleasure through suicide, killing innocents, theft, or oppression. Allah’s pleasure is sought through the means He has permitted and the moral boundaries He has revealed. The Prophet ﷺ taught that deeds are judged by intentions, but a good intention does not cleanse a forbidden deed. (59:9 — **“As for those who had settled in the city and ˹embraced˺ the faith before ˹the arrival of˺”**) Because the opposite of arrogant speech frames seeking allah’s pleasure, seeking allah’s pleasure returns attention to the opposite of arrogant speech.

**SEEKING ALLAH’S PLEASURE**

***“Allah’s pleasure. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants”*** deepens seeking allah’s pleasure. Transmitted reports connect the verse with Companions who sacrificed wealth and homeland for faith, including Ṣuhayb al-Rūmī, while its wording also describes sincere believers generally. Historical reports differ over the immediate occasion, but the verse’s meaning is not restricted to one person. It praises every servant who makes the right exchange for Allah. The early believers left homes, status, and possessions in Makkah, endured persecution, and built a community in Madīnah. Their sacrifice was not an escape from responsibility. It created safety, worship, brotherhood, and justice. The verse therefore honours sacrifice that produces life and reform, not destruction for its own sake. Most believers will not face the dramatic circumstances of the first Muslims, but the same exchange occurs daily. A worker refuses a bribe, a student refuses cheating, a spouse apologizes, a parent sacrifices sleep for a child, a person leaves a forbidden relationship, or a wealthy Muslim gives quietly to the poor. These acts trade immediate comfort for Allah’s pleasure. The believer also sells his ego when he accepts correction. The person who lets go of the need to win an argument, forgive an insult, or share credit has not become weak. He has transferred ownership of his desires to Allah’s guidance. The closing name Raʾūf—Kind or Tenderly Compassionate—assures the servant that Allah does not waste sincere sacrifice. (2:204 — **“There are some ˹hypocrites˺ who impress you with their views regarding worldly affairs and openly call upon”**) Since the example of suhayb and others follows the opposite of arrogant speech, seeking allah’s pleasure links purpose to the example of suhayb and others.

**THE EXAMPLE OF SUHAYB AND OTHERS**

People may overlook the cost, misunderstand the motive, or exploit generosity, but Allah knows. His kindness appears in guiding the person to sacrifice, strengthening him during it, protecting him from excess, and rewarding him beyond what he gave. Allah’s kindness also means that He does not require a servant to abandon every lawful blessing. The Prophet ﷺ balanced worship with sleep, family, work, and recreation. A person who destroys his health, neglects dependants, or refuses lawful rest has not necessarily made the best exchange. Kindness includes respecting the rights Allah has placed upon the body and family. The verse belongs near passages about corruption, arrogance, and public harm. It contrasts self-giving with self-serving leadership. The righteous person gives himself to protect others; the corrupt person uses others to protect himself. This distinction is visible in how power is exercised. A leader who bears hardship for the people is closer to the verse than one who demands sacrifice while preserving his own comfort. The Qur’an praises those who give others priority even when they themselves are in need. But sacrifice must not be demanded from the vulnerable by those who refuse their own duty. Charity, service, and defence are noble when freely and lawfully undertaken, not when imposed through manipulation. Think of the opposite of arrogant speech through a merchant who is offered a partnership in a mine and sells his stall to buy the share. Selling the self through daily obedience matters, which means the example of suhayb and others returns selling the self through daily obedience to the opposite of arrogant speech.

**SELLING THE SELF THROUGH DAILY OBEDIENCE**

His neighbours think he has lost his mind, the mine opens, and the man who kept his stall spends the season selling to the miners. The opposite of arrogant speech reaches a reader today who gives generously from his money and holds his time, his comfort and his name in a closed hand. The verse gives him a man who gave the whole of what he had and asked for nothing but the pleasure of God, and it asks him which part of his own life is still in the shop. Because the aim is named rather than the reward, the reader is shown the rank of such a person. The bargain is not a calculation of profit but a preference of the One who gives over everything given, and the verse lets the reader measure his own aims against it. The Book names the purchase in a later passage, so that the opposite of arrogant speech reveals the sale of this verse as one side of a bargain the Book states in full. The warning against the other kind of man stands earlier in this chapter, where the refusal of a plain word is answered with the fire. Seeking allah’s pleasure makes the opposite of arrogant speech practical; the example of suhayb and others therefore matters.
