**PAID IN TWO CURRENCIES**

The two phrases of the verdict divide the reward and its timetable between them: ***“It is they who will receive a ˹heavenly˺ reward”***, ***“for the good they have done. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning”***. and the second phrase shortens the wait. The first phrase pays the workers in the currency they earned in, and the second phrase dates the payment so that nobody waits longer than the account requires. The pair refuses both of the failures it answers: the despair that thinks the deeds are lost and the impatience that thinks the deeds are late. Ibn ʿAbbās ties the portion to the pilgrimage and the supplication that earn it, and the speed to the reckoning that follows. Both fields are read in one breath, which is why the parable binds them together before the reader reaches the verdict.

**A SHARE OF THE WORK**

The first phrase of the verdict names the wage and its source: the portion is paid out of what the workers earned, which means the paradise of the prayerful is built from their own materials. Ibn ʿAbbās, in the gloss kept on this parable, ties the earning to the pilgrimage and the supplication of the days just commanded, so the previous verses are the works that fill the portion. The economy described is intimate: the servant's deeds are not confiscated and returned as charity but counted and repaid as wages. The scales that measure the payment are the scales of justice, and the measurement is promised in the legislation of the resurrection (21:47 — **“We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least.”**). Because the share is cut from the worker's own cloth, the reward fits the worker exactly.

**SWIFTNESS OF THE RECKONING**

The second phrase answers the anxiety the first phrase creates: if the wages are counted, how long does the counting take? The answer is a single word of speed, and the speed belongs to the Reckoner rather than to the clerk. The verdict's promise elsewhere carries the same speed of a decision that nobody reverses and a reckoning nobody delays (13:41 — **“He is swift in reckoning”**). The brevity of the phrase is the mercy's posture again: the servant who has worried about the weight of the account is answered with one adjective. Because the speed is divine, the delay the servant fears is a projection of his own ledgers and not a feature of the divine one. That honesty is deliberate.

**AN ACCOUNT ON CALL**

The application is to work while the account is still being written. The portion named in the verse is not a lottery prize but a construction project, and the project's schedule is the servant's remaining breath. The person who postpones the pilgrimage, the prayer, or the supplication has misread the swift reckoning as a distant one. Because the wage is drawn from what is earned, the empty column of the ledger is the only poverty the verse recognizes. The two phrases of the verdict are therefore also two instructions: earn, and trust the payment. The two harvests are set beside each other like two fields at the same hour: one cut and counted, the other still standing in the heat. The classical gloss presses the image with the eye of a broker, noticing the shine of the goods and the flash of the appetite. Glimpsing the standing field is what the seeker of profit does, and the moment of the cutting is what the owner of the counted sacks does. Because both fields are described in one breath, the reader is asked to stand between them and feel the wind from each. The crowd at the market of the world is loud, and the crowd at the harvest of the next life is quiet, and the ears of the heart choose which crowd to answer. The calculation is not difficult once the two fields are seen in the same light, but the seeing is the difficulty. A parable of this kind survives a thousand readings because it is drawn from the harvest rather than from the bookshelf. The study of the two fields is the study that turns the trader of the hour into the farmer of the season. Two fields, one hour, and a choice of harvest that no broker can postpone until the morning.
