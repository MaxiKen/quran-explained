**A DAY WITH NO SUBSTITUTES**

The verse completes the call to the Children of Israel with a warning that looks past their history to the day it ends, and the warning is given as an instruction to protect themselves: ***“And guard yourselves against the Day”***. The Book's pattern in these passages is to name the obligations and then to name the accounting, so that the command to remember is followed by the reason the remembering matters. The verse is very close in wording to an earlier sentence in the sūrah that made the same point with a slightly different order, which suggests that the warning was meant to be heard more than once. The whole address is framed by this sentence, since the favours described in it were given to a people who will stand before their giver. Anyone who treats a gift as a private possession has the sentence as the correction it needs. Ibn ʿAbbās understood the Day as one on which neither parent nor child can remove another's sentence, and no ransom, intercessor or helper can overturn the account.

**EVERY ACCOUNT STANDS ALONE**

The first clause of the warning removes the assistance on which a person usually relies: ***“when no soul will be of any help to another. No ransom will be taken, no intercession accepted”***. The three refusals come together and close off the three routes by which a person might hope to escape the consequences of his record. The Book states the same three refusals elsewhere in the sūrah, which shows how central the point is to its account of the day: (2:48 — **“Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another”**). Nothing here suggests that the day will be harsh in an undifferentiated way; the exact supports that will be missing are named one by one. A reader who has arranged his affairs around the influence of others has the clause as a plain description of what that arrangement is worth there.

**RANSOM THAT WILL NOT BE TAKEN**

The middle refusal concerns payment, and the Book has something to say about what a ransom would look like if it were possible. Another passage describes a rich man on that day offering everything he owns twice over in exchange for his own release, and the offer is refused at the outset: (39:47 — **“they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves from the horrible punishment on Judgment Day”**). The same image appears in a sūrah where the ransom is measured in gold sufficient to fill the earth, and the answer there is identical: (3:91 — **“it would never be accepted from them”**). The verse's brevity assumes those pictures, and the reader who has them in mind will hear the refusal as the closing of a door. The instruction then adds its own last phrase to the list, and the phrase completes the account of what will not be available: ***“and no help will be given”***.

**A DEBTOR WITH NO FRIENDS IN THE ROOM**

Think of a man brought before a court who has spent his life relying on the standing of his relatives and the goodwill of his neighbours, and who discovers that the room contains only himself and the record. The verse's three refusals strip a person of exactly those supports, and the refusals come in the order a desperate man would try them. The comparison is useful because it shows that the verse is not describing cruelty but accuracy, since each refused route was a route that only worked in the world. Anyone who has seen a person spend his last means on the hope of a favour has seen how the reasoning goes, and the answer here is that the accounting is not a negotiation. What the sentence leaves behind is the sole remaining resource, which is the record a person carries.

**THE GUARDING THE VERSE ASKS FOR**

The instruction is to guard, which means the warning is meant to be acted on now rather than feared later, and the same word the Book uses for keeping away from what is forbidden carries the instruction here. The sūrah has just described people who follow their book as it should be followed and people who reject it, and this sentence tells the reader where the difference shows itself: in the precautions he takes while the day is still ahead of him. The children of Israel had been reminded of favours that came with obligations, and the guarding named here is the form those obligations take when the favours are understood. A reader who has made a habit of the precautions the Book asks for has understood the sentence in the way it was meant. What the passage leaves with him is the timing of the matter, since the day will not offer a second appointment for those who spend this one elsewhere. Today, wealth, family standing and influential contacts can settle many earthly difficulties, which makes their complete absence from the final account especially sobering.
