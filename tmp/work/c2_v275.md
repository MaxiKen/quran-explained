**THE MORAL DISORDER OF RIBA**

***“Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to madness by Satan’s touch. That is”*** establishes the moral disorder of riba. This verse condemns ribā, an unjust increase attached to a debt or exchange, and rejects the claim that it is identical to ordinary trade. The Qur’an presents the person who consumes ribā as standing in a disordered way, like someone struck by Satan. The image conveys moral and economic confusion: the person sees exploitation as commerce and treats a vulnerable debtor as an opportunity for guaranteed gain. The phrase is not a diagnosis of mental illness or a licence to stigmatize people with psychological conditions. Mental illness is a medical reality that deserves care and compassion. The verse uses a vivid image of disorientation to describe the spiritual and social disorder produced by riba. Allah has permitted trade and forbidden interest. Trade involves an exchange of goods, services, or productive risk by mutual consent. The seller may profit because he owns an item, provides labour, accepts risk, or adds value. Ribā in its prohibited forms guarantees an increase on a debt because time passes, often placing the borrower under a growing burden regardless of whether the underlying activity succeeds. A profit is not automatically lawful merely because it is called a sale, and a loan is not automatically ribā merely because money changes hands. Contracts must be examined in their substance, risk, ownership, timing, and conditions. Modern mortgages, credit cards, business finance, inflation adjustments, and emergency loans require qualified scholars and financial experts who understand both Sharīʿah and the contract. The claim trade is only like interest ignores the difference between risk-bearing exchange and extracting increase from need. (3:130 — **“O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of Allah,”**)

**TRADE IS NOT THE SAME AS INTEREST**

***“because they say, “Trade is no different than interest.” But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest. Whoever refrains—after”*** deepens trade is not the same as interest. In a genuine sale, both parties may benefit from the asset and accept the terms. In a debt, the lender may demand more simply because the debtor needs time. The poor can become trapped, and wealth can accumulate among those already able to lend. Islam permits qarḍ ḥasan, a good loan without exploitative increase, and encourages partnership, leasing, and trade structured around real assets and risk. The goal is not to prevent investment or profit but to protect people from unjust compounding and the commodification of desperation. Ribā can produce debt spirals, family breakdown, homelessness, economic inequality, and dependence upon predatory lenders. Even when a contract is formally agreed, consent may be distorted by urgent need. Islamic law therefore looks beyond paperwork to fairness, transparency, and the harm likely to result. The prohibition also protects the lender’s heart. Guaranteed gain without productive service can cultivate greed and indifference to the borrower’s suffering. Wealth should circulate through trade, partnership, charity, and fair investment rather than becoming a permanent claim over another person’s future labour. Whoever receives an admonition and desists may keep what is past. This gives mercy to the person who stops after learning the ruling. He does not need to unravel every historical transaction in a way that creates impossible harm, while future dealings must be corrected and unlawful claims abandoned. The precise treatment of existing contracts and gains requires qualified advice and attention to other people’s rights. The phrase does not authorize a person to keep money stolen from a known victim. (2:188 — **“Do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly,”**)

**WHY THE ANALOGY FAILS**

***“having received warning from their Lord—may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to Allah. As for”*** clarifies why the analogy fails. Repentance requires returning identifiable rights, settling obligations, and ending the unlawful practice. His matter is with Allah places the unseen judgement of his past within divine mercy and knowledge. Whoever returns to it describes a person who persists after the warning and treats the prohibition as something to evade. The severe warning concerns deliberate continuation, not a person who is trapped in a complex contract, seeks advice, or is trying to exit without causing greater harm. Muslims should distinguish ignorance, compulsion, hardship, and deliberate defiance. The Prophet ﷺ warned against ribā and cursed the one who consumes it, the one who pays it, the one who records it, and the witnesses to it, according to a report Muslim collected in his Ṣaḥīḥ. The hadith shows that the prohibition reaches the network that facilitates exploitation, while legal responsibility still depends upon knowledge and circumstances. A community that wants to leave ribā must build alternatives: interest-free emergency loans, zakāh funds, cooperative savings, fair partnerships, ethical investment, debt counselling, and support for people escaping predatory contracts. Telling the poor to avoid interest without providing any lawful route can become a form of cruelty. The prohibition is joined to generosity. The next verses call for giving up remaining interest and provide relief for debtors in hardship. The Qur’anic economy protects both the borrower and the lender through justice and mercy. Think of the moral disorder of riba through a farmer who lends his neighbour a sack of seed in the spring on the condition that he takes back a sack and a half in the harvest. (30:39 — **“Whatever loans you give, ˹only˺ seeking interest at the expense of people’s wealth will not increase with”**)
