**A MESSENGER ASKED FOR FROM WITHIN THE NATION**

The prayer reaches its highest request in this verse, and the thing asked for is a person rather than a building, since the descendants will need someone to teach them what their ancestor could only ask for on their behalf: ***“Our Lord! Raise from among them a messenger”*** and ***“who will recite to them Your revelations, teach them the Book”***. The prophet who is praying asks for a prophet to come after him from among his own descendants, which is a remarkable thing for a founder to request. The Book reports the request without comment, and the answer to it shaped the history that the sūrah has been addressing. The line of Abraham was expected to produce something more than a nation with a sanctuary, and the prayer says so by asking for a messenger. Ibn ʿAbbās understood the requested messenger as one from Ishmael's descendants, wisdom as instruction in the lawful and prohibited, and purification as release from sin through divine oneness.

**FOUR TASKS IN AN ORDER**

The verse lists four tasks and they are given in an order: recitation, teaching of the Book, wisdom, and purification. The order moves from what is heard to what is understood to what is lived, the sequence the Book follows whenever it describes guidance taking hold of a community. Another passage describes the same sending in nearly the same words and calls it a favour done to the believers, which places the answer to this prayer among the gifts named in the sūrah: (62:2 — **“He is the One Who raised for the illiterate ˹people˺ a messenger from among themselves”**). The messenger is not asked for as a ruler or a conqueror but as a teacher, and the community that receives him is described by what it is taught.

**THE PURIFICATION THAT COMES LAST AND FIRST**

Purification is named at the end of the list, and the Book places it after the teaching rather than before it. The order suggests that the community is purified by what it learns rather than prepared for learning by being purified, which is the pattern the sūrah follows in its description of the believers. Another passage lists the same activities in a different order and puts purification before the teaching of wisdom, which shows that the sequence is not rigid: (2:151 — **“reciting to you Our revelations, purifying you”**). The four tasks together describe a transformation that runs through the ear, the mind and the conduct, and the prayer asks for all of them at once because none of them is sufficient alone.

**A GARDENER WHO ASKS FOR THE WATERING**

Think of a man who plants a tree and then asks that someone be sent to water it, knowing that his own lifetime will not cover the tree's growth. The prayer in this verse has that shape, since it asks for a messenger who will do among the descendants what the founder could not do himself. The request is made by a prophet rather than by an ordinary man, since only someone who knows the size of the task would ask for it in these terms. Anyone who has started something that will outlast him will understand the logic of the sentence, and the logic is that the work needs a person, not a plan. The shape of the answer that history gave to the prayer is what the verse sets before him.

**POWER AND WISDOM AT THE CLOSE**

The verse ends with an address to God under two names, and the names describe power and wisdom rather than mercy: ***“and wisdom, and purify them.”*** and ***“Indeed, You ˹alone˺ are the Almighty, All-Wise.”***. The choice is fitted to the request, since the raising of a messenger requires an authority that nothing can resist and a wisdom that places him in the right nation at the right time. The Book frequently closes a request with the names that answer it, and the practice is visible here in a single sentence. A reader who has traced the prayer from its beginning will notice that the whole sequence has been about submission, ritual, grace and teaching, and the verse ends by naming the two attributes that make all of it possible. What remains when the prayer closes is the account of how the community that inherited it behaved. The prayer's own two names answer the request it makes, and the account of the covenant and the calf has already shown what happens when a community inherits a message without the teaching that belongs with it. Another passage calls the answer to this prayer a favour done to the believers, which places the messenger's work among the gifts the sūrah has been counting: (3:164 — **“Indeed, Allah has done the believers a ˹great˺ favour”**). Today, communities need more than information: they need truthful recitation, sound judgement and the purification of habits that knowledge alone may leave intact.
