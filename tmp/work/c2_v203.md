**THE APPOINTED DAYS OF HAJJ**

***“And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second”*** establishes the appointed days of hajj. The appointed days refer to the days of tashrīq in the Hajj rites, especially the days when pilgrims remain at Minā, remember Allah, and stone the designated pillars. The exact legal details of the days and times are explained by the Sunnah and juristic tradition. The central command is clear: the pilgrim’s movement through the rites must be filled with dhikr rather than distraction, pride, or quarrelling. The days are called appointed because Allah has designated their time. Sacred worship has a rhythm that the servant receives rather than invents. Pilgrims follow the established sequence, remain patient in crowds, and understand that a delay or early departure can be lawful when performed within the prescribed limits. The pilgrim may hasten after completing the appointed portion and leave Minā after two days, or delay and remain for an additional day. There is no sin upon him does not make the options meaningless; it removes the fear that one lawful choice is inherently blameworthy. The choice depends upon the pilgrim’s circumstances, the prophetic practice, and the guidance of qualified scholars. Some people assume that staying longer is automatically more pious, while others rush in order to finish without care. The verse teaches that obedience is measured by following Allah’s allowance, not by creating a competition in visible hardship. A person should choose the sound option without belittling those who choose the other. The pilgrim remembers Allah through takbīr, tahlīl, supplication, Qur’an, and awareness of the meaning of the rites. (2:196 — **“Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah. But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺,”**) Because the appointed days of hajj frames hasting or delaying, hasting or delaying returns attention to the appointed days of hajj.

**HASTING OR DELAYING**

***“day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third—seeking”*** deepens hasting or delaying. The stoning of the pillars recalls opposition to Satan and rejection of his commands; it is not an act of attacking a physical devil inside the pillars. The sacrifice, prayer, and movement through Minā connect the pilgrim to Ibrāhīm’s submission and the community’s shared covenant. The Prophet ﷺ made abundant remembrance during Hajj and taught the Companions the words and times of the rites. The pilgrim should avoid inventing formulas that claim guaranteed effects or turning the rite into a loud contest that disturbs others. Dhikr is worship and should be accompanied by reverence. The verse qualifies both hastening and delaying with for whoever is mindful of Allah. Taqwā is what transforms a legal option into worship. A person who leaves after two days with gratitude and care may be more sincere than one who stays longer to impress people. A person who delays to complete the rite calmly and assist the weak may be acting with greater taqwā than one who rushes from impatience. Taqwā also controls treatment of other pilgrims. The person who pushes, insults, steals, or harms people cannot claim that physical completion alone proves acceptance. Hajj is a training in obedience, patience, and mercy. Know that you will be gathered to Him returns the pilgrim’s mind from Minā to resurrection. The enormous gathering of Hajj is a visible reminder of the final assembly, where every human being will stand before Allah without tribal privilege. (22:28 — **“so they may obtain the benefits ˹in store˺ for them,”**) Since dhikr in the rites follows the appointed days of hajj, hasting or delaying links purpose to dhikr in the rites.

**DHIKR IN THE RITES**

***“additional reward˺, so long as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺. And be”*** clarifies dhikr in the rites. The rites end, but the account continues. This reminder gives meaning to the earlier commands about provision, promises, charity, and restraint. The pilgrim prepares for the road home, and every person prepares for the road to Allah. No delay in worldly life can prevent the final gathering, and no hidden deed will be absent from the account. The verse’s allowance demonstrates the flexibility of Islamic law within a clear framework. People differ in health, age, crowd conditions, transport, and responsibilities. Allah does not require every pilgrim to follow one personal timetable when the revealed options are broader. Unity comes from sharing the lawful rite, not from forcing identical preferences in every detail. Pilgrims should therefore learn before travelling, follow reliable instructions on the ground, and avoid arguing over minor differences. The one who knows the law has a duty to teach gently rather than shame others. Think of the appointed days of hajj through a wedding at which the guests stay on for the dancing and forget the couple. The couple are married either way, and the guests go home praising themselves for the food. The appointed days of hajj reaches a reader today who performs the obligations of a season and treats the days that follow them as a holiday from God. The verse gives him days named for remembrance in the middle of the rest, and the appointed days of hajj asks what he is resting for. Because the choice of departure is left open, the appointed days of hajj shows that the law is concerned with the state of the heart rather than with the timetable of the road. (13:28 — **“whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort”**) Taqwā makes both options good matters, which means dhikr in the rites returns taqwā makes both options good to the appointed days of hajj.

**TAQWĀ MAKES BOTH OPTIONS GOOD**

***“mindful of Allah, and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered”*** closes with taqwā makes both options good. Because the days are named as days of remembrance, the appointed days of hajj shows that a festivity can be worship when the heart is awake in it. The community is not told to leave the cooking and the visiting; it is told what the cooking and the visiting are for. The Book keeps the same easing of the rites in a later passage, so that the appointed days of hajj reveals the two departures as one instance of a rule that runs through the religion. The verse of the season before this one ends in the same command, and the two closes together show the pilgrim what he is meant to carry home. The two options are kept by Ibn ʿAbbās as they are stated, so that each pilgrim has a choice and neither choice carries a fault. Because the sentence links both choices to mindfulness of the Lord, the appointed days of hajj shows that the permission is not an invitation to carelessness, and the reason for the choice stays with the one who makes it. (3:97 — **“In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it should be safe.”**) Hasting or delaying makes the appointed days of hajj practical; dhikr in the rites therefore matters.
