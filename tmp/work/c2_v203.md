**THE DAYS WITH NAMES**

The phrases of the day-count walk from the remembrance to the departure and back to the mindful: ***“And remember Allah during ˹these˺ appointed days. Whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, neither are those who stay behind ˹till the third”***, ***“seeking additional reward˺, so long”***, ***“as they are mindful ˹of their Lord˺.”***, ***“And be mindful of Allah”***, ***“and know that to Him you will ˹all˺ be gathered”***. and the closing phrase spells out the quality the days build. The walk moves through the stones, the calendar, the traveller's permission, and the stay-behind's reward, which is the whole jurisprudence of the days in one line. Nothing in the sequence is ornamental: the remembrance is commanded first because the counting without the remembrance is only a calendar. Ibn ʿAbbās enumerates the takbirs of the days — the greatness, the unity, and the glory — spoken over the same stones.

**CASTING AND REMEMBERING**

The remembrance commanded at the stones is spoken in the phrases the reports preserve: the greatness of Allah proclaimed over the days, together with the words of unity and glory. The transmission that Ibn ʿAbbās kept enumerates the takbirs themselves, and the enumeration turns the stone-throwing into a liturgy of speech. The days are counted as five in that enumeration — the day of ʿArafāt, the day of the sacrifice, and the days of the tashrīq — so the counting is a ritual calendar rather than a stopwatch. The rites just legislated in the pilgrimage passage end in this remembrance, and the pairing of stone and tongue is the pilgrim's final equipment (22:36 — **“when they are lined up ˹for sacrifice˺. Once they have fallen ˹lifeless˺ on their sides, you may eat from their meat,”**). Because the remembrance is commanded during the days, the days themselves are the mosque. Because the demand is dated, the fairness of the measure stays visible to the trader of the market.

**LEAVING ON THE SECOND DAY**

The traveller's permission is stated without blame: whoever departs swiftly on the second day is not sinful, and the permission travels with the pilgrim whose road is long. The ruling shows the law reading the bodies of its people once again, since the traveller who leaves early keeps the remembrance he can keep. The permission is a release and not a rebuke, and its phrasing protects the traveller from the pious comparisons of the crowd. Because the days are appointed, the early departure is inside the appointment rather than outside it. The one who stays and the one who travels are governed by the same calendar in two different postures. The counting keeps the traveller honest on both halves of the road.

**STAYING FOR MORE**

The other half of the ruling praises the one who stays until the third day seeking the additional reward, and the reward is named as the reason for the staying. The law therefore blesses both doors — the road and the stay — and the blessing is attached to the intention behind each. The application is to choose between the two by the verse's own criteria: the length of the road and the size of the hope. Because the reward of the staying is additional, the extra day is a purchase rather than a delay. The pilgrim with the short road learns from this verse that his extra day is worth more than the camel he saves. The praise closes the ledger kindly.

**WHOEVER IS MINDFUL**

The closing phrase collects the rulings into one character: the mindful are the ones the days are designed to produce. The remembrance at the stones, the traveller's permission, and the stay-behind's reward all feed the same quality, and the quality is the measure of the rites. Because the phrase names mindfulness as the product, the pilgrim can grade his own tashrīq by the temper he carries home. The days end and the takbirs end, and what remains is the character the takbirs built. The shout of the pilgrims is the sound of a request already granted: the tongue keeps saying the answer while the feet keep walking the road. The tradition of the call at the valley is preserved with the calm of a catalogue, and the catalogue keeps the dates and the stations attached to the shout. Repeating the formula is the discipline that keeps the journey from collapsing into tourism. Because the days of the pilgrimage are numbered and named, the counting is part of the worship rather than a piece of administration. The one who shouts at the valley and the one who shouts at the plain are answering one summons from the same house. The answer that the tongue carries at the summons is the answer of the presence, and the whole pilgrimage is the grammar of that answer. Nothing in the ritual is left to improvisation, and nothing in the improvisation is left out of the ritual. The counting of the days and the counting of the stones are both acts of attention.
