**THE WORK THE COMMUNITY IS TOLD TO DO**

After the envy and the command to wait, the verse gives the community something to occupy it, and the instruction is about practice rather than controversy: ***“Establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. Whatever good you send forth”***. The order of the sentence matters, since the Book answers the wish of the envious with a command about the believers' own conduct rather than with a rebuttal of the wish. Prayer and almsgiving are named together throughout this sūrah, which presents the two as one posture: worship directed upward and expenditure directed outward. The verse's placement after the report of hostility suggests that the community's best defence was never an argument but a pattern of life that could be seen. What the reader is given here is a way to spend the interval the previous verse described.

**THE FIGURE OF SENDING GOOD AHEAD**

The phrase about sending good ahead is a figure drawn from travel, and it describes goods dispatched before the traveller arrives: ***“for yourselves, you will ˹certainly˺ find ˹its reward˺ with Allah. Surely Allah is All-Seeing of what you do”***. The Book uses the figure to teach that the reward for a deed is not collected in the present but stored where the deed was sent, which is why the verse says the community will find it rather than receive it now. The plural form is used throughout the sentence, which places the responsibility on the community rather than on individuals alone, since prayer and almsgiving in the Book are gathered into the description of the believers as a body. Another sūrah joins the same practices to the same promise, which shows how stable the pairing is: (2:277 — **“Indeed, those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax will receive their reward from their Lord”**). The verse therefore turns a report about other people's envy into an occasion for the believers' own accounting.

**THE EYES THAT SEE WHAT IS SENT**

The sentence ends with an attribute that answers a question the reader might not have asked aloud: if the reward is stored where the sender cannot see it, who keeps the record? The clause answers by naming God as All-Seeing of what the community does, which places the smallest deed inside a supervision that never lapses. The attribute also closes off the possibility that a good act might be lost through being unnoticed, since the Book treats the accounting as a matter of sight rather than of memory. The point of the clause is not that God is aware in general but that He is aware of this community's conduct in particular, and the verse places the two statements side by side without a connective. A reader who has given something quietly and wondered whether it counted has the sentence as the answer to his question, and the answer is stated before he asks it.

**A TRAVELLER WHO SENDS HIS LUGGAGE AHEAD**

Think of a traveller who sends his bags ahead to the city he is going to, so that he arrives without them and finds them waiting when he gets there. The verse uses the same logic for good deeds, and the travellers in the comparison are the believers who will one day arrive at the place where what they sent has been kept. The comparison also explains why the Book can call a deed an investment rather than a payment, since the reward is not presented as something received in exchange but as something restored to its owner. A reader who has experienced the ordinary truth that what is given away lasts longer than what is held will find the figure easy to carry, and the verse's instruction stands on that plain experience. What the sentence asks for is not a large gesture but a habit of sending, performed while the interval lasts.

**THE CLOSING ATTRIBUTE IN THIS VERSE**

The clause about sight has one more function in the passage, and it is aimed at the community's morale. The believers had been told to wait while others worked to turn them back, and a community in that position can easily conclude that its conduct is invisible and its patience unrewarded. The verse answers that conclusion by naming the One whose observation settles the account, and it places the answer at the end of a command rather than at the end of an argument. The Book's method in these verses is to move from what others wish to what the community does, and the movement is the whole of the answer. A reader who has kept to a practice he was told to keep and seen no return on it has the sentence as the statement he needs, and it is short enough to hold on to through the interval the verse has in view.
