**MENSTRUATION IS NOT A STIGMA**

***“They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about menstruation. Say, “Beware of its harm! So keep away, and”*** establishes menstruation is not a stigma. The question about menstruation—ḥayḍ—is answered with practical guidance and moral dignity. It is described as adhā, a condition involving discomfort, bleeding, and physical vulnerability. The verse does not call menstruating women impure as people, cursed, or unfit for companionship. It gives a temporary sexual boundary while preserving their honour and ordinary human relationships. The phrase keep away from women refers to avoiding sexual intercourse during menstruation, not abandoning, isolating, or refusing to touch women. The Prophet ﷺ ate with his menstruating wives, drank from the same place they drank, rested with them, and instructed them in worship. ʿĀʾishah رضي الله عنها reported that he would ask her to hand him something while she was menstruating, demonstrating that her person was not contaminated. The prohibition concerns intercourse through the vaginal passage during menstruation. It protects the woman from increased discomfort and possible harm, and it gives the body a period of rest. The restriction is not a judgement of moral fault. Menstruation is a natural function created by Allah, and a woman is not blamed for missing prayer or fasting during it; she makes up missed Ramadan fasts but not missed prayers, according to the established Sunnah. The husband must not use the verse to punish or humiliate his wife. Affection, conversation, sleeping together, eating together, and emotional support remain lawful. **A TEMPORARY PHYSICAL BOUNDARY**

***“do not have intercourse with your wives during their monthly cycles until they are purified.”*** deepens a temporary physical boundary. The marriage continues; only a particular act is paused until the condition ends and purification is completed. Until they are pure and when they have purified themselves refer to the end of the menstrual period and the ghusl required before intercourse and the return to ritual prayer. The exact signs of the end of menstruation, irregular bleeding, and the rules for postpartum bleeding require study from qualified jurists. Women should not be forced into worship based on guesswork or shamed for asking detailed questions. Ṭahārah is both physical and spiritual. Ghusl cleanses the body for ritual worship, while repentance cleanses the heart. The verse’s closing praise joins the two without treating the menstruating woman as sinful. She is excused from certain acts by Allah’s mercy, not excluded from remembering Him, making supplication, learning, giving charity, or remaining part of the community. **PURITY AND GHUSL**

***“When they purify themselves, then you may approach them in the manner specified by Allah.”*** clarifies purity and ghusl. The sequence shows that sexuality is part of a wider covenant. Desire is recognized, but it is regulated by mercy, responsibility, and reverence for Allah’s limits. Allah loves al-tawwābīn, those who repeatedly return to Him, and al-mutaṭahhirīn, those who purify themselves. Repentance is not only for dramatic sins. It includes returning after carelessness, repairing harsh speech, and renewing obedience. Physical cleanliness, wudūʾ, ghusl, clean clothing, and a clean environment also matter in Islamic worship. The pairing prevents two errors. A person may maintain outward cleanliness while refusing to repent from arrogance or injustice. Another may feel spiritually ashamed because of a natural bodily process. Allah’s love is for those who pursue both inward repentance and outward purification according to His guidance. The husband and family should understand the practical realities of menstruation. Pain, fatigue, mood changes, and heavy bleeding may require medical care and rest. Religious law should be taught in a way that helps women worship confidently rather than making them feel dirty or burdensome. The Prophet ﷺ showed affection and patience, and his household was a school in humane conduct. If bleeding is abnormal, persistent, or accompanied by severe symptoms, a woman should seek qualified medical care. Spiritual guidance and medical expertise cooperate; one does not replace the other.
