**DESCRIBING THE LORD**

***“Your God is ˹only˺ One God. There is no”*** strips the answer to its skeleton: one object of worship, without division of essence or rank. The confession completes itself with ***“god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him”***, stated both positively and negatively, because nothing else holds a position that is singular by definition. I take the question behind the verse to be the demand to describe the Lord — the answer arrives as description rather than as argument. From Ibn ʿAbbās comes the report that the unbelievers denied this very oneness, so the verse answers a live dispute. What remains is the character of the One, and the verse adds it in two words of mercy. The creed is completed in the same breath in which it is introduced.

**THE SHORTEST CREED**

The answer refuses both errors floating in Mecca: the division of the divine into parts, and the shrinking of the divine into an idol's share of power. Ibn ʿAbbās gives the confession in these terms — one God without son or partner, the Beneficent as the Tender and the Merciful as the Compassionate. The reason the description stops at mercy rather than continuing into majesty is instructive, because the questioners wanted a handle they could grasp; the text gives them a relation they must enter. The creed is short enough for a child and deep enough for a theologian, and the verse intends both readers at once. Because the description is revealed rather than negotiated, the mercy named at the end stands on the same footing as the oneness named at the start. (112:1 — **“Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “He is Allah—One ˹and Indivisible˺”**)

**ONE STANDARD, ONE ADMINISTRATION**

A hearer may ask whether a God described only as One and Merciful governs anything as serious as the warnings before this verse. The objection dissolves once mercy and judgment are read as one policy, because the One who seals the condemned at death is the same One who holds the door open for the whole of life. Severity in the earlier verses is mercy's property boundary, not a rival to it. The oneness of God includes the oneness of His administration: one law of return, one hour of closing, one standard applied to every soul. The tender description is therefore not decoration placed between warnings; it is the ground of both the warnings and the offers. Fear and hope share one root. The objection can also be turned around: it is precisely because the One is merciful that the warnings are terrible, since a universe governed by indifference would have no need of them. The breath spent on the warning is itself a form of the mercy named at the close. Because the same names govern both passages — the warnings before this verse and the tenderness inside it — the reader is invited to hold them together rather than to choose between them. A creed that keeps only the mercy becomes sentiment, and a creed that keeps only the severity becomes despair; the verse supplies the proportion in ten words.

**RECEIVED, NOT COMPOSED**

The exaltedness of the One is named again in (39:4 — **“He is Allah—the One, the Supreme”**), and the two statements together fix the confession's register. Ibn ʿAbbās narrates that the unbelievers denied the divine oneness because it excluded son and partner, which means the verse answers the objection at its root. The application is simple and permanent: whoever worships one God lives under one standard, and the mercy at the verse's end is the mercy to which every reader of the warnings is invited while there is still breath to answer with. The invitation is dated by the same grammar that dates the warnings. The hearer is not asked to compose a theology but to receive one. The creed's brevity is itself an argument against the alternatives on offer, because the divided divine of Mecca required a priesthood to manage its contradictions while the One requires only a servant to answer. Because the answer is short enough to carry into every prostration, the verse gives the worshipper a creed that survives travel, fear, and the hour of death. The theology is received in the same posture the prayer demands. The confession closes on the character of the One: ***“the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful”***.
