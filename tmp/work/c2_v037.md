**THE WAY BACK IS ITSELF A GIFT**

Forgiveness begins here with help given to the one who has fallen: ***“Then Adam was inspired with words”*** ***“˹of prayer˺ by his Lord,”***. Adam does not find a path back by denying the command or defending what he did. His Lord teaches him how to turn. The source of the words is therefore part of their meaning. The Lord who judged the lapse has not ceased to care for the servant. Al-Ḥasan explains the receiving of these words as learning them and acting upon them. They are not sounds placed on the tongue while the heart keeps its former stance. Adam takes them as the truth about himself and his need. Another recitation makes the words the subject, as words coming to meet Adam. The two readings draw attention to both sides of the event: help reaches him, and he receives it. This is why repentance cannot become a ground for self-praise. Even the resolve to return is a mercy. The same order appears in a later account: (9:118 — **“Then He turned to them in mercy so that they might repent.”**). Divine kindness opens the way; the servant must actually walk it. Adam’s shame is thus met with teaching, not with a command to invent a cure beyond his reach.

The prayer identified by Mujāhid and Saʿīd ibn Jubayr gives the words their clearest Quranic form: (7:23 — **““Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us, we will certainly be losers.””**). Its parts belong together. The pair address God as their Lord, own the wrong, seek pardon and mercy, and admit what loss would mean without them. They do not offer the serpent, the tempter, or the tree as an excuse that clears their own act. Nor do they claim that their earlier honour puts God under a debt to forgive them. The prayer speaks in the plural, which also prevents the mention of Adam alone here from excluding his wife from repentance. Both had faced the command; both confess their need. The same truthful approach appears in Moses’ plea: (28:16 — **““My Lord! I have definitely wronged my soul, so forgive me.””**). Jonah likewise joins praise of God to an admission of wrong: (21:87 — **“Glory be to You! I have certainly done wrong.””**). The situations differ, but the prayers share a refusal to blame God for the servant’s fault. These are words of worship because they place greatness and need where they belong. Their force lies in a true return of heart and conduct, not in a claim that uttering a formula makes continued wrongdoing safe.

**PARDON FROM THE LORD WHO INVITES RETURN**

The answer is not left uncertain: ***“so He accepted his repentance.”***. God accepted the return that He had enabled. The prayer did not merely bring Adam relief from shame while leaving his standing with God unchanged. It brought pardon. In an account from Ibn ʿAbbās carried by al-Ḥākim, Adam asks whether repenting and putting matters right will lead to his return to Paradise. The answer is yes. That account carries hope within repentance, not a right to go on sinning because a return has been promised. The turn has to be real. Regret for the wrong must lead to leaving it and resolving not to resume it. The Quran links the two: (25:71 — **“And whoever repents and does good has truly turned to Allah properly”**). This also explains why pardon need not erase every worldly effect of an act. Adam’s earthly life will still follow, but it does not mean his prayer was refused. The present verse states the acceptance plainly. The general promise extends beyond the first human being: (4:110 — **“Whoever commits evil or wrongs themselves then seeks Allah’s forgiveness will certainly find Allah All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”**). A past fault need not remain a person’s final state. The door opens through seeking God’s forgiveness, not through declaring the fault harmless or calling despair a form of piety.

The closing names explain the answer: ***“Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance,”***, ***“Most Merciful”***. The Arabic expression behind repentance concerns a return. For the servant it is a turn away from sin toward obedience. When used of God here, it means His gracious turn toward the servant, not repentance from any wrong of His own. The name expresses His repeated acceptance of those who return and the great number of people He pardons. Mercy appears at both ends of Adam’s change: God supplies the words, then accepts the prayer made with them. A later verse joins these truths to His knowledge: (42:25 — **“He is the One Who accepts repentance from His servants and pardons ˹their˺ sins. And He knows whatever you do”**). Since He knows what people do, no outward show can force an acceptance that the heart and conduct deny. Yet His knowledge is not a reason for the sincere sinner to hide. Adam’s fault was already known when the words were given. The right response to that knowledge is the honest confession the verse teaches. No human being owns this pardon or can sell access to it. A teacher may show the prayer and call a person to repent; the Lord alone forgives the sin. His mercy makes return possible without making wrongdoing right.
