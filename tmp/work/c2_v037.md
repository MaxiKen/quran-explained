**WORDS GIVEN TO A MAN WHO HAD JUST FALLEN**

The story does not end with the descent, because the next verse says what happened between the fall and the sentence: ***“Then Adam was inspired with words ˹of prayer˺ by his Lord,”***, and ***“so He accepted his repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful”***. The words were not invented by the man; they were placed in him, which is the difference between a creature left to improvise his way back and a creature who is handed the door. The Book reports the prayer itself in another sūrah, and the wording there shows what the inspired words were: (7:23 — **“They replied, “Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves. If You do not forgive us and have mercy on us,”**). The confession is complete, and it contains no excuse and no attempt to share the blame. Two things are joined in that sentence: the admission that the wrong was their own, and the appeal for mercy rather than for justice. A person who has understood the story of the fall will notice how much those two clauses cover. Adam does not blame the serpent, and he does not blame his wife, and he does not blame the intensity of the desire that was stirred in him. He names himself as the one who did the wrong and then asks for something he has no claim on. That is what the verse calls a return, and the Book treats it as the model for everyone who comes after him.

**HOW THE RETURN IS ANSWERED**

The answer arrives immediately and it is described in two names, and both of them matter to a reader who is afraid that his own record disqualifies him. The one who accepts the return is called the Accepter of Repentance and the Most Merciful, and the Book repeats these descriptions wherever it wants a person to stop despairing of himself. The same acceptance is reported in another sūrah with a third element joined to the other two: (20:122 — **“Then his Lord chose him ˹for His grace˺, accepted his repentance, and guided him ˹rightly˺”**). Chosen, accepted, and guided: the verse supplies more than forgiveness, because it supplies the road afterwards. The contrast with the previous scene is the sharpest thing in the passage. One creature refused a command, and another creature broke one, and the difference in what happened to them is not explained by the size of the offense. It is explained by what each did when the moment came. One argued about his own worth, and the other admitted a fault and asked for mercy, and the Book lets the reader draw the conclusion without a sentence of commentary. A person who reads both scenes on the same page has received one of the plainest lessons the Qur'an offers about how to fail.

**WHAT RETURNING REQUIRES OF A READER**

The practical content of the verse is that a person can always come back and that he cannot come back on his own terms. The words were given, which means the returning has a shape the Lord has set rather than one the returning person prefers. The Book elsewhere describes the same road with more detail: (20:82 — **“But I am truly Most Forgiving to whoever repents, believes, and does good, then persists on ˹true˺ guidance.””**). Repentance, belief, good conduct, and persistence: the four items are not four separate tasks so much as four faces of one direction, and a person who takes the direction will find every one of them changing the others. The exercise for a reader is to say the sentence the inspired words contain, in the plainest way available, about the thing a reader has been excusing to himself for years. The excuse is the part that has to go, because the excuse is what keeps the fault in place and the person comfortable. The Book holds out an immediate acceptance in the same verse as the fall, and a reader who delays his return while waiting to feel worthy has misunderstood both halves of the sentence.
