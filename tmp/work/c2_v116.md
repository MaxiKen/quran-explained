**THE SON-CLAIM IN THREE MOUTHS**

The passage now answers the theology that the scripture parties had carried into the argument: ***“They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! ”***. Ibn ʿAbbās names the claim in each of its historical mouths. The Jews said it of Ezra. The Christians said it of the Messiah. The polytheists of the city said it of the angels, the daughters they assigned. The claim therefore arrives in the passage wearing three sets of clothes at once, and the verse answers the wardrobe rather than the outfit. The exclamation in the middle of the sentence is the verdict before the argument. Glory be to him. The distance between the claim and the majesty is measured in the exclamation itself, and the measurement is infinite.

**GLORY AS THE VERDICT**

The argument then lands in one line of estate law: ***“In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Wi”***. The companion's reading turns the ownership into the servitude of the whole estate. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth are his servants, linked to him by the bond of servitude. The second tradition notes the grammar of the inclusion. The little word that gathers all things is chosen precisely to leave nothing outside the estate. The reasoning is then plain enough to state as a syllogism. The owner of the whole estate is served by the whole estate. A son of the owner would stand inside the estate as one of the served. The claim arriving from all three mouths therefore fails on its own paperwork. The sovereignty and the sonship cannot both be registered at the same desk. The verse of the oneness that the whole book keeps in view is the verse the claim collides with: (2:255 — **“Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him, the Ever-Living,”**). The living that needs no successor is the living that takes no child.

**THE SUBJECTED UNIVERSE**

The closing clause of the verse is the survey of the whole estate under one will: ***“—all are subject to His Wil”***. The subjection is total and it is personal. The heavens are subject. The earth is subject. The claim of the three mouths is answered by the register of the three realms. The passage has used this form of answer before, against the slander of the prophet and the envy of the wishers. Locate the ownership. The slander runs out of standing. For the doctrine of the son-claim the answer is therefore not a quarrel with any one sect's wording. The answer is the estate. The register lists the whole creation on one side of the ledger. The claim of the offspring asks the ledger to add a line that the ownership cannot sign. The verse closes the registry and the exclamation closes the verse. Glory be to him. The registrar's office belongs to the owner alone, and no committee of the three mouths holds a pen at that desk.

**THE COUNCIL AND THE REGISTRY**

The three claims of the three mouths are documented at the level of the book's own report as well. The scripture party's version names Ezra in one chair and the Messiah in the other, and the sentence the passage reports is the sentence the council of the claim has always passed: (9:30 — **“The Jews say, “Ezra is the son of Allah,” while the Christians say,”**). The second half of the report names the other chair: (9:30 — **““The Messiah is the son of Allah.” Such are their baseless assertions,”**). The passage of the estate answers the council before the council rises. Also within the heavens and earth's ownership, the registry was opened and the servants were counted, and the seat of the sonship was found empty. What the three mouths call a household, the register calls an estate. The difference is not a matter of taste. The household assumes an owner with a successor. The estate assumes an owner with servants. The register in this verse lists servants alone, and the counting of the servants is the argument. The council of the claim never adjourns, because the claim is cheap to reprint. The registry closes after one reading. That asymmetry is the passage's final mercy to the argument.
