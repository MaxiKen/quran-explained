**CREATOR AND CREATURE CONFUSED BY DESCENT**

A claim about God is reported here that the Book treats as a category error rather than a difference of opinion, and it is stated in two words: ***“They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him!”***. The claim is quoted without its authors being named, which places the sentence beside the disputes of the earlier verses as one more assertion made about God without warrant. The Book's answer begins immediately with the words of exaltation rather than with an argument, and the order tells the reader that the claim is being refused at its root. The communities that made such claims had scriptures and traditions, and the verse shows the reader what can be said about God by people who have both. What follows in the sentence is the reasoning that the exaltation only begins. Ibn ʿAbbās connected the claim of a son with what was said about Ezra and Jesus, then read the answer as universal servitude that leaves no creature outside God's ownership.

**EXALTATION BEFORE ARGUMENT**

The phrase of exaltation is the Book's standard reply when a claim about God is too small for its subject, and its placement here is a judgement on the claim before any evidence is offered. The words describe God as free of every deficiency, and among the deficiencies is the suggestion that He shares His nature with anything. Other passages answer the reported claim of offspring with identical exaltation, and in one of them the exaltation is followed by the same kind of reasoning used here: (19:35 — **“It is not for Allah to take a son!”**). The reader is therefore shown a pattern the Book follows consistently, where the claim is refused by a statement about what God is rather than by a comparison between God and the claimants. A person who has heard a doctrine about God that struck him as unworthy of the subject will recognise the reply.

**UNIVERSAL OWNERSHIP ANSWERS THE CLAIM**

The verse then gives the reason the claim collapses, and the reason is about ownership: ***“In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens”***, ***“and the earth”*** and ***“all are subject to His Will”***. A person who owns everything cannot have derived a part of himself from it, since ownership and derivation are different relations and the verse keeps them apart. The same claim about ownership appears in a passage that also reports the claim of a son, and the order of the two statements there matches the order here: (6:101 — **“How could He have children when He has no mate?”**). The Book's argument is not that the claim is unlikely but that it is incoherent, since the one who owns all things has nothing beside Him from which anything could be begotten. The sentence therefore rests on a fact the reader has already met many times in the sūrah.

**A CRAFTSMAN WHO OWNS HIS WORK**

Think of a craftsman whose work fills a workshop and who is said to have fathered the furniture he made, so that the relation between him and his work is confused with the relation between him and a child. The verse's reasoning separates the two, and it does so by naming the whole created order as his possession. The comparison makes plain why the Book treats the claim as an error about God's nature rather than as an insult, since the mistake lies in the kind of relation that is being asserted. Anyone who has heard a person speak of the divine in terms drawn from human families can see how easily the confusion arises, and how simply the Book dissolves it. What the sentence leaves with the reader is the ownership of everything and the subordination of everything, which together rule out the claim.

**SUBJECTION AS THE STATE OF EVERYTHING**

The verse ends with a clause that goes beyond ownership to describe the condition of the created order: all are subject to His Will. The word describes a readiness to be directed, and it is applied here to everything in the heavens and the earth without exception. Closing a refutation with a statement about the created order shows the reader the scale of what is being discussed when a claim about God is made. The sentence declares God free of the deficiency, states that everything belongs to Him, and describes everything as under His command. The next verse names Him as the one who originated the heavens and the earth, which places the same reasoning in a sentence about creation itself. In the present day, admiration becomes corruption when a servant of God is lifted from honoured service into a share of divine identity.
