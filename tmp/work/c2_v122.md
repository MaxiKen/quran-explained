**AN ADDRESS THAT REOPENS THE HISTORY**

The verse turns back to the community whose story has run through the sūrah, addressing it by the name that carries its ancestry: ***“O Children of Israel!”***. The address is not a new topic but a return, since the same call has already opened a passage earlier in the chapter where a covenant and its terms were named. The Book's habit of returning to a community after long stretches of other material is part of its method, and the return here comes immediately after a sentence about those who follow their book and those who reject it. The reader is therefore invited to hear the call as the beginning of an argument rather than as a fresh introduction. What the verse asks of the community is stated in the two clauses that follow, and the two clauses are about memory and about a favour.

**REMEMBERING A FAVOUR**

The first request is that the community recall what it had been given: ***“Remember My favours upon you”***. The Book's instruction to remember is not an appeal to nostalgia but a summons to account for the obligations that came with the favours, since the two clauses that follow in the sūrah's earlier passage join memory to a covenant. The sūrah has already named several of these favours, from the deliverance at the sea to the provision in the desert, and the verse's brevity assumes the reader has them in view. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with the view that the remembering here means acting on what the favours require rather than reciting them. What the reader should see is the Book's insistence that a gift received and forgotten is a gift that will be accounted for, since memory is placed first and treated as a duty.

**HONOUR ABOVE THE OTHERS**

The second clause states the particular favour that the community was most likely to remember with pride: ***“and how I honoured you above the others”***. The honouring is stated as a fact here, and the fact is placed beside the command to remember, which keeps it attached to its obligations. The favoured status was real, and the Book nowhere denies it; what it denies is the conclusion the community drew from it in later generations. The comparison with the earlier passage makes the intention clear, since the same words there are followed by the warning of a day on which no soul will be of use to another: (2:47 — **“how I honoured you above the others”**). Someone who has received an advantage and treated it as a possession will find the pairing of favour with duty familiar from his own experience.

**A FAMILY HEIRLOOM WITH A CONDITION ATTACHED**

Think of a family that was given land by a benefactor and that still speaks of the gift while ignoring the terms on which it was made. The verse calls the community back to the memory of the gift, and the Book's method is to name the gift and the terms together so that the second cannot be separated from the first. The comparison explains the Book's frequent returns to this history, since a community that remembers only the favour misunderstands both the favour and itself. Anyone who has inherited an advantage and treated it as an entitlement can see what the sentence is guarding against, and the guard is set at the point of memory. The obligation attached to what he has been given is what the reader is left holding.

**THE RETURN TO AN OLDER HISTORY**

The return to the Children of Israel comes at a point where the sūrah has been discussing the People of the Book, and the two communities are not the same, since the earlier history belongs to a nation rather than to a pair of religious bodies. The Book's argument with this community is about a covenant and a favour, while its argument with the others has been about a scripture and a claim. What the return establishes is that the sūrah's case rests on a history that its first audience knew in detail, and the call by name is a way of placing that history in front of them. Another passage states the same favour in the language of giving and provision, which shows how the Book frames the whole account: (45:16 — **“We gave the Children of Israel the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood”**). The reader who follows the address to its conclusion will find that the favours named are the ground of everything the chapter has been arguing about. Present-day privilege is remembered properly only when gratitude becomes responsibility rather than a claim of permanent superiority.
