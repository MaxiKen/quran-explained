**PROOFS ARRIVING WITH THE HANDS FULL**

The recap opens with the inventory of a whole prophetic career compressed into one clause: ***“Indeed, Moses came to you with clear proofs”***. The proofs named here span a catalog the tradition keeps whole — the staff that became a serpent, the hand that shone white, the sea that parted into dry road, the locusts and lice and frogs and flood-years, the manna and quails of the wilderness — and one line of analysis takes the word for the proofs as naming the Torah itself with everything it demonstrates. Ibn ʿAbbās glosses the plural in the plainest register he has: commands, prohibitions, and signs. The naming of the signs as clarifiers is itself the lexicographers' point: they are called by the word for making-plain because they clarify to every onlooker that the claim is a miracle rather than a maneuver. The analysts of the sentence's opening stack count three reinforcements in a row — an oath-letter, an affirmation particle, and the emphatic form — a triple emphasis the passage spends on the fact nobody disputed. The readings over the scope of the proofs split productively: some take them as the evidences of the prophet's truthfulness, others as the evidences of the divine unity he came to teach, and the wider reading gathers both, since the fuller the light was, the darker the next clause becomes. The reason the inventory stands first is procedural: the verse is building the delivery receipt before it opens the charge. The verse's own logic is the logic of an exhibit list: the more signatures on the receipt, the worse the receiving.

**THE INTERVAL THE TURNING USED**

The fall is then dated by its own grammar: ***“then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully”***. The particle of sequence here is heavier than a plain and — the tradition notes — it implies a respite: the calf was taken up after the signs had been pondered and the prophet had gone to his appointment, which converts the lapse into a decision. The manufacture is remembered in the glosses with its material detail: the image was cast from their own ornaments and jewelry, so that the idol was minted out of the wealth God had given, the first monument in the record to be financed by its own congregation. The phrase for the absence is the phrase of the appointment itself — the one whose duration the people of the calf later refused to respect — and their words at the height of the episode were preserved verbatim in the account of the return: (20:91 — **“They replied, “We will not cease to worship it until Moses returns to us.””**). The final word of the verse names the wrong in the language of injustice rather than error, and the earliest glosses press it to its doctrinal floor: the worship of the calf denied the prophet who had parted the sea and the Lord who had parted it through him. The sequence of the accusation is worth reading twice: proofs delivered, prophet absent, image financed, worship performed, injustice named — and no stage of the file is missing its signature.

**THE APPROVAL THAT SHARES THE GUILT**

The verse's legal afterlife concerns the second generation, and the tradition states the transfer of liability without embarrassment. The contemporaries of the last prophet did not themselves bow to the calf; what they did was honor the forefathers who had, and take their side — and the passage counts the honoring as the sharing. The same approval-mechanism runs the question of the killed prophets in the verse before this one: those addressed are addressed for what they endorse, not only for what their hands did. The pattern-prediction in the tradition follows the same mechanics: a people whose forefathers denied Moses while the sea was still drying under their feet should not be surprised to find their grandchildren denying the final messenger while the descriptions sit unrolled in their laps. Moses himself opened the confrontation with his own people in the words the passage recalls — (2:54 — **“And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf,”**) — note the reflexive form: the wronging of themselves is the wrong's first address.

**THE EXHIBITS THAT CANNOT BE DISOWNED**

The practical demand the verse makes on later readers is evidentiary rather than sentimental. A community is not tried on its ancestry but on its docket of endorsements — the causes it funds, the forefathers it crowns, the silences it keeps — and the calf's financing from the people's own jewelry remains the passage's emblem of how worship is underwritten. What turns on the emblem is the whole sociology of the fall: the image was not imported from a foreign cult but minted at home in the prophet's week away. For any congregation today that keeps its devotion warm in the intervals between instructions, the verse dates the danger to the calendar of the absences. The proofs do not expire because the prophet steps out of the room; they become the exhibits the return will open with.
