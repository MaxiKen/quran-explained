**THE QUESTION THAT NOBODY CAN ANSWER**

The sūrah turns now to the plainest argument in the chapter: ***“How can you deny Allah?”***, ***“You were lifeless and He gave you life”***, ***“then He will cause you to die”***, ***“and again bring you to life”***, ***“and then to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned”***. The question is not a request for information, and it is not addressed to a stranger. It is the sort of question a teacher puts to a student who already knows the answer, and the answer is supplied immediately in the four stages of a human life. Nothingness, life, death, resurrection, return. The whole of a person's story is sketched in one line, and every stage of it is in someone else's hands, including the last one.

The stages are chosen so that the reader cannot claim any of them. He did not arrange his own arrival, he cannot postpone his own death, and he has no vote in what happens after it. The one stage he might have wanted to control, the stretch of years he is living, is where the denial in the question actually happens, since a person does not usually deny his origin so much as live as though it had no consequences. The Book elsewhere repeats the same sequence as a reminder of ingratitude: (22:66 — **“And He is the One Who gave you life, then will cause you to die,”**), and the clause that follows in that verse names the human response as ever ungrateful, which is the same diagnosis this sūrah makes in a question.

**THE LAST STAGE THAT SETTLES THE FIRST THREE**

Of the four stages, the last one carries the weight. Death is not the end of the account but the end of the period in which a person can do anything about it, and the return means that everything kept back will be produced. That is why the verse puts the resurrection and the return in the same breath: they are one movement. A reader who accepts the first three stages easily and stumbles at the fourth has not fully believed the first three either, because the one who gave life from nothing is not limited by death. The verse is not adding a hard doctrine to an easy one. It is stating what the earlier stages already imply. The Book repeats the same argument in several places in a form a reader can carry, and the wording shifts with the audience. Verses like this one are sometimes read as though they were aimed at an atheist, but the audience here had no theory to defend. They lived around a sanctuary, they acknowledged a creator, and they still arranged their loyalties as though the matter ended with their deaths. Their denial was practical rather than philosophical, and the question is aimed at conduct, not at a creed.

**WHAT TO DO WITH A QUESTION THIS DIRECT**

A question of this kind is best answered in the present tense. A reader can look at the way he spends his money, the hours he keeps, and the promises he makes, and ask whether those things belong to a person who expects to be returned to someone. The Book does not ask anybody to produce a feeling of certainty; it asks him to live as though those four stages are true, and the living is what makes the belief real. That is why the verse does not argue with the denier. It simply puts the facts of his own biography in front of him and asks how he accounts for them. Today the practical use is to take one part of the day and hand it back to the one whose gift the day itself is. The Book has already named in this sūrah what that looks like: worship of the one who created you, the habits that grow out of it, and the ties that are kept rather than cut. A person who does the small version of this finds that the question loses its sting, since the answer is no longer a sentence he recites but the shape his hours are taking.
