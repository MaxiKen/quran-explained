**LIFE ITSELF TESTIFIES AGAINST DENIAL**

A question addressed to the human being interrupts the description of the losers: ***“How can you deny Allah?”***. The question is not looking for information; it is pointing at a contradiction, and the contradiction is stated in the words that follow it. The early teachers read the sentence as addressed to everyone who hears it, and the sūrah has already spoken once to the whole of humanity in the same way, which means the question is meant for the reader and not only for the people of the first century. The Book asks a question of this kind in several places where a person's own existence is used as the proof against him, and the sūrah here does the same with the shortest possible account of a human life. A man who cannot explain how he came to be is in no position to explain away the One who brought him.

**THE LIFE THAT WAS GIVEN BEFORE IT WAS ASKED FOR**

The answer begins with the state the hearer was in: ***“You were lifeless and He gave you”***, ***“life”***. The wording puts the reader in the position of a thing that had no claim on existence and received it anyway. For Ibn ʿAbbās, the phrase described the human being before the breath was placed in him, and other early teachers took it as a description of the dust from which he was formed. The Book states the same act in another sūrah with the sentence that follows the gift to its next stage: (22:66 — **“And He is the One Who gave you life, then will cause you to die,”**). The reason the argument starts with the first gift rather than with the last is that the reader has already received the first one, and he is standing in the middle of an arrangement whose beginning he did not negotiate. What turns on the phrase is that gratitude for life itself is not a feeling the verse asks for; it is a fact the verse records.

**THE DEATH AND THE RETURN THAT HAVE NOT HAPPENED YET**

The verse continues through the two events that are still ahead of the reader: ***“then He will cause you to die”***, ***“and again bring you to life”***, ***“and then to Him you will ˹all˺”***, ***“be returned”***. The sentence names the whole course of a human being in one line, and it places the return to God at the end of it as the point where the account is settled. The Book repeats the same sequence in a passage that ends with a warning about ingratitude: (30:19 — **“And He gives life to the earth after its death. And so will you be brought forth ˹from the grave˺”**). It was noted among the earliest teachers that the dying and the raising are stated with the same certainty as the giving of life in the past, which means the future belongs to the same hand that has already acted. The reason the sentence reads as a list rather than as a threat is that every item in it has happened or will happen to the reader himself, and the list is simply his own life stated from the outside. A reader who has stood at the grave of someone he loved knows that the third event in this list is the one his whole life is arranged around, whether he admits it or not. The verse puts the return at the end of the sentence without a description of what waits there, because the fact of the return is enough to change how a person spends the interval.

**BORROWED LIFE POINTS BACK TO ITS GIVER**

Think of a passenger on a ship who is told that his passage was paid before he was born and that the same hand will settle the account at the end of the voyage. His only contribution is the way he spends the journey, and the question in the verse asks how such a man could deny the owner of the ship. These days a person can live with the sense that his existence is simply the ground under his feet, and the verse makes the ground itself an argument. The next verses continue the same account with the earth, the sky and the means of life, and they lead the reader to the place where the argument of the whole sūrah has been heading from its first page.
