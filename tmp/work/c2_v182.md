**FEAR THAT CATCHES ERRORS**

Walking the verse's phrases in sequence shows the ruling taking shape: ***“Whoever suspects an error”***, ***“or an injustice in the will”***, ***“and brings about a ˹fair˺ settlement among the parties will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. Each step of the walk narrows the field of the sinning party and widens the field of the repair, which is the movement of the whole ruling. The repair is therefore legal work in the strict sense, and the ruling names each act the work requires. The occasion preserved through Ibn ʿAbbās sits inside this same order. The ruling's movement from suspicion to settlement to immunity is one continuous act of repair, and the walk shows the act assembled piece by piece from the verse's own words.

Whoever suspects an error or an injustice in the will opens the door that the previous verse kept shut: suspicion is not permission to rewrite, but it is permission to act. The second phrase deepens the trigger — or an injustice in the will — so the reformer is someone who has read the instrument carefully enough to see where it departs from right. The response is defined with equal care: and brings about a ˹fair˺ settlement among the parties will not be sinful, and the settlement is between people rather than between the document and the family's preferences. The reformer's immunity is the verse's engine, because without it the honest executor would stay silent to avoid liability. A society where correcting a wrong exposes the corrector to blame is a society where wrongs are allowed to stand. The clause that grants immunity is itself protected by the divine names at the close, since the settlement required is one Allah forgives and rewards (4:128 — **“there is no blame on either of them if they seek ˹fair˺ settlement, which is best. Humans are ever inclined to selfishness.”**).

**SETTLEMENT IS THE REPAIR**

The wording is procedural rather than emotional: the verse asks for a settlement brought about among the parties, which means conversation, evidence, and an agreed correction. The report transmitted through Ibn ʿAbbās traces the passage to its occasion, where a dispute over a testament was resolved once the fair settlement was made — the reformer emerged from the process without sin because the process was repair and not revenge. This is the difference between the two possible responses to a bad will: alteration rewrites the record, while settlement preserves the record and fixes its effects. The second is slower and less flattering to the person who wants the estate arranged his way, and that is exactly why the verse protects it with an explicit immunity. Because humans are inclined to selfishness, the difficulty of the repair is predictable, and the immunity is written for the difficult case. The parties must be gathered, the error named, and the shares put right.

**ONE EXCEPTION FOR TWO SINS**

The two triggers — error and injustice — cover the innocent mistake and the deliberate distortion, which is why the verse needs both words. A drafter who misheard needs the error clause; a family that engineered the distortion needs the injustice clause; the reformer who confronts either one stands inside the immunity. The legislation that follows shows how the suspicion is adjudicated when the parties cannot agree, since the disputed testimony is replaced by heirs who swear an oath that truer than the altered version (5:107 — **“If they are found guilty ˹of false testimony˺, let the deceased’s two closest heirs affected by the bequest replace the witnesses”**). The oath itself is the court's answer to a world where documents can be edited, and the oath-takers know the penalty named in the same passage. Because the settlement is preferred over the contest, the verse's person acts first and litigates only when the repair fails.

**MERCY THAT FOLLOWS FAIRNESS**

The closing names are placed where the reformer needs them most: after the work of correcting is done, and before the family's gratitude or resentment arrives. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving toward the reformer's mistakes in judgment and Most Merciful toward the effort that repairs what others corrupted. The application is to prefer the repair openly — to call the family together, to name the error, and to propose the fair division — because the verse rewards the settlement and not the clever redraft. The person who waits for the dead to be beyond correction before acting has misunderstood the deadline: the trust is repaired while the heirs can still be gathered. Because the immunity is granted to whoever brings about the settlement, the last word of the passage belongs to the peacemaker. The peacemaker's immunity is worth dwelling on, because every other verse of this passage warns and this one protects. The warning and the protection arrive together so that the conscience has both its fear and its work. The person who repairs an instrument of the dead is doing the work of justice on behalf of a party who cannot appear in court.
