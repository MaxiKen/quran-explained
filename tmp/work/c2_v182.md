**REFORMING A HARMFUL BEQUEST**

Reforming a harmful bequest draws together ***“Whoever suspects an error”***. This verse makes an important exception to the prohibition against changing a bequest. If someone fears that the testator has made an error or acted unjustly, and he brings reconciliation between the concerned parties, he is not sinful for correcting the matter. The goal is not to honour a document mechanically when doing so would violate Allah’s law or cause clear harm. The goal is to preserve justice and the real purpose of the bequest. The person who intervenes must be motivated by *reconciliation*, repair, not by desire to divert the estate toward himself. He should establish what the testator intended, identify the rights of heirs and creditors, and seek a lawful solution. Reconciliation does not mean secretly replacing a will; it means correcting injustice transparently and with proper evidence. An error may be a mistaken name, an impossible condition, a misunderstanding of the law, or an instruction that becomes impossible to carry out. Injustice may include depriving an heir, favouring one child because of anger, assigning more than the lawful charitable limit, or directing property toward something forbidden. A will can be validly written yet contain an element that must be corrected. The verse covers both. People may make mistakes through illness, pressure, ignorance, or confused speech, and others may need to protect the estate from the effects. Allah knows the difference between a genuine mistake and calculated fraud. (2:180 — **“a will should be made in favour of parents”**) Because reforming a harmful bequest governs 2:182, reconciliation requires knowledge arises from reforming a harmful bequest. It follows that forgiveness and mercy completes reforming a harmful bequest through reconciliation requires knowledge.

**RECONCILIATION REQUIRES KNOWLEDGE**

***“or an injustice in the will”*** mark the turn toward reconciliation requires knowledge. Not every person who suspects unfairness is qualified to alter a bequest. The mediator should consult the Qur’an, authentic Sunnah, competent scholars, legal experts, and all affected parties. He should hear both sides and avoid relying on gossip. A sincere intention does not make an uninformed intervention just. The process should be as transparent as possible. The family can document the disagreement, preserve evidence, protect minors, and seek a trustworthy adjudicator. If a dispute concerns a fixed inheritance share, no private preference may cancel what Allah has prescribed. If it concerns a voluntary bequest to a non-heir, the parties can negotiate within the lawful limits. A person who changes the will to obtain a larger share cannot claim this verse as cover. The correction must serve justice between the parties, not the mediator’s appetite. If the mediator is himself an heir, an independent adviser or court is especially important. Hidden alteration creates the very sin condemned in the previous āyah. The Qur’an praises reconciliation in many contexts, but it never calls injustice reconciliation. A settlement that forces the weak to surrender their rights, protects an abuser, or hides a crime may produce outward peace while deepening the wrong. True *reconciliation* restores rights and reduces harm through truth. (4:35 — **“If you anticipate a split between them, appoint a mediator from his family”**) The reason forgiveness and mercy follows reconciliation requires knowledge lies in reforming a harmful bequest. Therefore, forgiveness and mercy returns practical force to reconciliation requires knowledge.

**FORGIVENESS AND MERCY**

The verse develops forgiveness and mercy with ***“and brings about a ˹fair˺ settlement among the parties will not be sinful. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful”***. Allah closes with His forgiveness and mercy. This gives hope to the testator who made an error and to the person who intervenes imperfectly while sincerely seeking repair. It also calls families to show mercy. Not every disagreement requires litigation; relatives may voluntarily accommodate one another after each person’s right is known and protected. Mercy does not erase the rules of inheritance. It makes people willing to implement them without spite and to give additional gifts or relinquish a claim voluntarily after receiving it. A person cannot call coercion generosity. Consent must be real, informed, and free from threat. The verse teaches a general principle: preserving the purpose of a command can require correcting a harmful application of its outward form. Documents, institutions, and customs are means; justice and obedience to Allah are the ends. At the same time, people must not invoke *higher purpose* whenever a clear rule frustrates their preference. Reform is guided by revelation, evidence, and trustworthiness. The Muslim community should train mediators who combine legal knowledge with compassion. They can prevent family disputes from becoming permanent hostility and protect the rights of children, widows, the elderly, and relatives who lack social power. Correcting an unjust will is like resetting a scale whose pointer was bent before the goods were weighed. The repair changes the reading, but it does so to recover accuracy rather than to steal weight for either side. Mediation after a flawed bequest has that character when it seeks fair agreement instead of private advantage. In families today, the difference can be seen in transparency: the peacemaker brings affected parties into the repair, while the self-interested changer works through secrecy and calls the result a settlement. (49:9 — **“then make peace between both ˹groups˺ in all fairness”**) In other words, forgiveness and mercy answers reforming a harmful bequest.
