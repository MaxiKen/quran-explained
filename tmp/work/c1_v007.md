**THE ROAD IS DESCRIBED BY THE PEOPLE WHO WALKED IT**

The request for guidance was made in the previous line, and here the road is shown: ***“the Path of those You have blessed”***. Rather than defining the way by rules, the sūrah points to the travellers, which is a kindness to a reader who has already heard that the path is straight but has not been told where it goes. The Book answers the question in one verse that names four ranks at once: (4:69 — **“And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be in the company of those blessed by Allah: the prophets,”**) and it continues with the people of truth, the martyrs and the upright. Companionship is on offer there, not merely a route. A person walking a hard road with the right company survives what he could not survive alone, and that is part of what the line asks for.

The blessed ones, then, are not a crowd of the impressive. They are the truthful, the ones who died for what they believed, the ordinary upright, each at his own level, and the sūrah prays to be put on their road rather than to be made like them in every respect. Ibn ʿAbbās read the phrase as asking for the way of those God has favoured with the religion, and he named the prophets among them. The point is that guidance has visible results in ordinary lives: a person prays, pays what he owes, tells the truth when it costs him, and the shape of his days becomes evidence. Because the reader can see such lives around him, the prayer is not asking for something invisible. It is asking to be placed where they are.

**TWO WRONG ROADS, AND THEY GO WRONG DIFFERENTLY**

The next words rule out two alternatives, and the rule is specific: ***“not those You are displeased with”***, and then ***“or those who are astray”***. One group knew what was right and set it aside, and the other meant well and lost the knowledge. The first broke its covenant knowing what it was doing, so anger follows; the second wandered without a reliable map, so being lost describes it better. That difference matters, because it means failure is not one thing. A person can be ruined by pride in what he knows, and he can be ruined by effort spent in the wrong direction. The sūrah prays against both, which is why the verse carries two negations rather than one.

Both roads are worth understanding, because most people who take one believe they are on the other. A rule-keeper who has stopped asking why can end up in the first group without noticing, since habit and obedience look identical from outside. A searcher who reads widely and prays rarely can drift into the second, since interest in truth is not the same as surrender to it. The Qur'an describes the two conditions in its own language in both cases, and classical commentary applies them to the communities that received earlier scriptures and then parted from their purpose. Read that way, the ending of the sūrah is not an insult aimed at anyone's neighbours. It is a map of two failure modes every believer can recognise in himself once the day has gone wrong in a way he did not plan. The point of the map is to keep the traveller watching both hands: what he knows and what he does with it.

**THE ENDING THE SŪRAH CHOSE**

These two groups were named by the Prophet ﷺ himself, in an answer that removes the ambiguity. He said, *"The Jews earned the wrath, and the Christians went astray,"* as Ahmad carries it. The point of the report is not the names but the diagnosis: knowledge without practice and practice without knowledge, and every community since has produced both kinds. Notice also what this ending does to the reader's self-image. Seven lines that opened with mercy close with a warning about how a life can go wrong in either direction, and the warning is placed in the mouth of the person praying, not thrown at anyone else. The reader says it about himself, in company with everyone else praying, many times a day, which is why the warning does its work on the speaker before it ever reaches his neighbour.

The final word of the sūrah is the one sound everyone makes after it. The prayer concludes with the word for the plea to be answered, and the Companions used it behind the Prophet ﷺ openly in prayer. Its meaning is simply the request for what has just been asked, which is why it does not belong to the sūrah's letters but to the reader's own voice. A person does not merely finish a recitation at that point; he signs the request and asks for it to be granted, which is a different act from reading. A person who has said all seven lines has asked for guidance, named the company he wants and named the roads he refuses, and the word settles the matter as a request rather than a report. Today the whole sūrah can be said in the time it takes to walk to a bus stop. It leaves the reader with one line on his lips and the day's own choices waiting in front of him.

**CERTAINTY IS NOT IN THE PACKAGE**

A last observation belongs to the shape of the prayer rather than to its words. Guidance is asked for, not assumed, and the request is placed on the lips of people who have already been guided. That combination tells the reader that certainty about his own direction is not part of the package. A person who cannot imagine being wrong has already stopped asking, and the sūrah gives such a person nothing to lean on. The better posture is the one the words themselves describe: walking with the company of the blessed, using the knowledge that has reached him, and asking every day for the next stretch of the road. That is the whole religion in seven lines, and it is left open for every reader who takes them up.
