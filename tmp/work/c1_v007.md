**A PATH MARKED BY THE PEOPLE ON IT**

The sūrah has asked for the straight path, and this verse tells the reader where that path runs by naming the people who are on it: ***“the Path of those You have blessed”***. The change of method is deliberate. A road can be described by its surface and its direction, and a person may still fail to find it; a road can also be described by the company travelling on it, and then the seeker knows both the way and the destination. The Book identifies the blessed company in a verse that gathers four ranks in one sentence: (4:69 — **“the prophets, the people of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous”**), and it adds its own approval of the company: *"what honourable company"*. Ibn ʿAbbās read the blessed as the followers of the prophets who held to the religion while the favour lasted, and he also read them as the prophets themselves. Both readings point in the same direction, because the prophets are the leaders of the road and those who follow them are on it behind them. The request therefore becomes a request for company as much as for direction. A person who asks to be among the blessed has asked to be placed with people whose steps he can see, and the request is answered in the community before it is answered anywhere else.

**KNOWLEDGE THAT TURNED AWAY, STRIVING THAT MISSED**

The verse then states what the path is not, and it names two kinds of people rather than one: ***“not those You are displeased with”***, and ***“or those who are astray”***. The Prophet explained the two in a report carried by al-Tirmidhī. ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim asked him about the two descriptions, and he answered that the first are those who knew the truth and turned away from it, and the second are those who sought a way and missed it. The two failures are therefore not the same failure, and the reports draw the line carefully. One is the failure of a person whose knowledge did not reach his conduct, and the other is the failure of a person whose effort did not reach the knowledge. The Book names both in other places, and it warns against each. It warns against the second by pointing back to those who went before: (5:77 — **“nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before ˹you˺”**). It warns against the first by describing a people whose hearts were left unprotected: (2:90 — **“They have earned wrath upon wrath”**). The two failures also explain why the prayer asks for a road rather than for a prize, since both are failures of travelling and neither is a failure of receiving. The two are also joined in the reading of the verse as a single warning: the prayer asks for a road on which a person knows and acts, since the two failures are knowledge without action and action without knowledge, and the straight road is the one on which the two are held together.

**WHY A PRAYER ABOUT GUIDANCE ENDS IN REFUSAL**

The last words of the sūrah are words of denial, and their place there teaches something about the nature of the request. A person who asks for a road asks by implication to be kept from every other road, and the sūrah states the implication rather than leaving it to be worked out. A prayer that named only the destination would be a wish; a prayer that also names the ways it refuses is a commitment. The recitation ends here, and the reports teach what follows the recitation. A report from Abū Hurayrah, carried by al-Bukhārī and Muslim, gives the instruction of the Prophet in these words: *"When the imam says Āmīn, say Āmīn"*. The word is a request that the prayer be answered, and it comes at the end of a sentence that has refused two failures by name. Nothing more is added after the refusal. The refusal does the work of a fence set beside a cliff, and the fence is part of the road and not an extra beside it. The absence of a closing clause is itself a lesson in the sūrah's method, because the reader is left holding a request rather than a conclusion. It also explains why the sūrah can be recited again a few minutes later without losing its point: a road prayer is spoken while the road is being walked. The sūrah does not close with a description of reward or a promise of rest. It closes with a person standing at the head of a road, having said whom he follows, whom he refuses to follow, and where he asks to be taken.

**THE TRAIL MARKED BY THOSE WHO REACHED THE TOP**

Picture a mountain trail that is marked at intervals by the people who have walked it before. A stone is set upright at a turn, and a strip of cloth is tied to a branch at the crossing where the path is easy to miss. A climber who follows the marks arrives, and a climber who trusts his own sense of direction may not. The verse does the work of the marks. It tells the climber that the road has been walked, that the ones who walked it are known, and that their footprints are still visible in the Book and in the lives of those who carried it. The two failures at the end of the verse are the two cliffs beside the trail: a person who knows where the path goes and will not climb it, and a person who climbs with great energy in the wrong direction. Both are dangerous, and the prayer refuses both. The verse is recited at the end of every prayer, and a person who says it thoughtfully has already checked his position on the road. It leaves him with a request on his lips and a direction in his hands. These days a person is offered more routes than any generation before him and less certainty about which one leads anywhere, and the verse answers the confusion with a company rather than a map. The company is the one the reader has just named, and the request is the one he has just made. A person who says the sentence honestly will find that it has told him what kind of traveller he intends to be, and the telling is the beginning of the walking.
