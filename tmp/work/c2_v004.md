**THE EARLIER SCRIPTURES, ACCEPTED AS THEY CAME**

The description of the guided reader continues, and it widens: ***“and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺”*** and ***“and what was revealed before you”***. The first half of the line is an act of faith in what the messenger brought: ***“and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺”***. The second half then reaches backwards through history, ***“and what was revealed before you”***, and the second act is not optional. The Muslim is asked to accept the scriptures that came before his own, in the form the Qur'an itself gives them, which means the Book does not present itself as a replacement that erases what went before. It presents itself as the thing those earlier books pointed at. That is why the man who had been waiting for a prophet did not have to become a different person in order to believe; he had to finish the journey he was already on.

The phrase also settles a question that troubles new Muslims in every generation. A person who has left another faith often wonders what he must now repudiate. The answer in this line is that he keeps what was true in it and receives the rest: the messengers are all accepted, and no line is drawn between one and another. The Prophet ﷺ said, *"Whoever has faith in what was revealed to you and what was revealed before you, and in the Hereafter they are certain,"* as the descriptions of the believers were carried by the Companions, and the reward for such a person is described as twofold for the sake of what he had already held and what he then accepted. Because the reader is not asked to despise his own history, the Book can arrive as a completion, which is a gentler thing than a replacement.

**CERTAINTY ABOUT A WORLD NOBODY HAS SEEN**

The verse then names a third mark, and it is a strange one to place beside the other two: ***“and have sure faith in the Hereafter”***. It does not say that they hope, or that they consider it likely. It says that they are certain of a life after this one, and the certainty is described as a settled conviction rather than a guess. Such a conviction changes a person's arithmetic without his having to announce it. He will not sell his whole future for one hour of this world, and he will not treat an unfair gain as free money. Everything he does with his time is priced according to a longer calendar than the one he can see, and that is why the Book puts the Hereafter so early in its description.

The Book describes the same certainty elsewhere as the mark that keeps a person from despair and from carelessness at once: (2:286 — **“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford. All good will be for its own benefit,”**) sets the same horizon, where every soul is answered for what it could carry. Nothing in the verse pretends that the Hereafter can be inspected, and that is precisely its role in the list. The reader who accepts a world he cannot see completes the same movement he made with the unseen in the previous verse, only this time the stake is not belief in general but the future in particular. Two men can look identical on a Tuesday: the one who expects to be raised and judged, and the one who expects nothing at all. They differ on the Thursday when the first of them is offered a profit he should refuse. Because the difference between them appears in moments like that, the sūrah treats certainty about the Hereafter as a practical quality rather than a mood.

**THE ORDER THE FIVE MARKS ARE GIVEN IN**

Read as a single sentence, the two verses describe a person in an order that can be tested: he accepts the unseen, he prays, he gives, he believes in the prophets before him, and he is sure of the world to come. Each item is harder than the one before it in a way that quietly builds. Accepting the unseen costs nothing at the start, prayer claims a part of his day, and giving claims part of his income. Believing in the earlier scriptures costs him his sense of having arrived first. Certainty about the Hereafter costs him the freedom to spend his life as though it were the only one. A reader who finds one of the items hard can tell from the list which item he is resisting.

The Companions who narrated these descriptions said that the two groups in view were those who came to Islam from a life of idols and those who came to it from the earlier scriptures, and it is worth noticing that the sūrah does not rank them. Both are called guided in the next verse. A reader who came in from one door and a reader who came in from the other finish the sentence at the same place, holding the same five things. That equality is not a small claim in a city where the two groups lived side by side and each was certain of its own standing. The Book settles it by describing a single kind of believer and letting both groups find themselves in it.

**WHAT CERTAINTY DOES TO A WORKING WEEK**

A person who lives with the Hereafter in view behaves differently in ways that are small enough to be missed and large enough to matter. He pays a debt before it is demanded because he expects to be asked about the delay. He takes less than he could, accepts a lower price, forgives a small cruelty, because the profit he is protecting is elsewhere. Today these choices look irrational from the outside, and they are usually made quietly by people who would not describe them as religious decisions at all. That is how a conviction about the next life shows up in this one: not in speeches, but in what a man refuses to do, and the refusals are usually invisible to anyone who is not watching closely.

The verse has one more use, and it is for the reader who finds himself doubting rather than believing. He is not asked to manufacture a feeling on demand; he is asked to keep the habits that carry it. Certainty is not a talent given to a few; it grows out of living as if the thing were true and watching what the living does to the doubt. The person who prays, gives and keeps his word in the ordinary week is the same person who finds the next world easier to hold as real. This is why the list in the sūrah is a list of habits rather than experiences, and why the reader is told these people are certain only after the habits that carry the certainty have been named.
