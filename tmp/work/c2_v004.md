**A FAITH THAT TAKES IN EVERY BOOK SENT DOWN**

The fourth mark of the mindful is stated in three parts, and the first concerns the revelation given to the Prophet: ***“and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺”***. Ibn ʿAbbās understood the phrase as belief in the Qur'an as the word of God, and with the further point that what is believed is not the paper and the ink but the message those carry. The address to the Prophet in the middle of a verse about his followers is worth noticing, because it keeps the messenger in view while the community is being described. A believer accepts what came to him through a man he never met, and he accepts it because of the One who sent it rather than because of any evidence he gathered first. The same faith is asked of the community in a verse addressed to believers as a command: (4:136 — **“the Book He has revealed to His Messenger, and the Scriptures He revealed before”**). The revelation is one act of God with more than one delivery, and the verse begins by naming the latest of them.

**THE EARLIER REVELATION AND THE ONE PEOPLE WHO RECEIVED IT**

The second part widens the same belief to everything sent before: ***“and what was revealed before you”***. The early authorities read this as the Scriptures given to the prophets of earlier communities, and the Book itself names what was given to Moses and to Jesus and to the prophets who came between them. Ibn ʿAbbās understood this as the believer’s affirmation that all of it came from the same Lord, and Abū al-ʿĀliyah and others described the believers here as those who accept every book that God sent down. The wording does not ask the reader to follow the earlier Scriptures in their present form, a point the sūrah returns to later when it speaks about their preservation and their readers. What it does ask is that he refuse to divide the messengers, and the Book states that refusal in the words of the believers themselves: (3:84 — **“we make no distinction between any of them”**). A community that thinks of itself as the first people God ever addressed will not carry the humility the verse is teaching, because the point of the phrase is that the road it walks was marked before it arrived. The difference between a newcomer and an heir shows itself in how each one treats the people who came earlier, and the verse is asking the reader to behave like an heir.

**THE CERTAINTY ABOUT THE LIFE TO COME**

The third part closes the verse: ***“and have sure faith in the Hereafter”***. The wording here is not the same as the word for belief used earlier in the same sentence; it carries assurance and settled conviction, as though the matter had been checked and put beyond dispute. Al-Suddī and Qatādah are credited with explaining it as certainty without doubt, and Ibn ʿAbbās with the reading that the Hereafter is certain in the heart of such a person and not merely repeated by his tongue. The Book uses the same phrase for the same people at the head of other sūrahs, where the description of the guided is repeated in almost the same order: (27:3 — **“have sure faith in the Hereafter”**). Placing it last among the marks is fitting, because a person's certainty about the return is what makes his prayer steady and his giving cheerful. Without that certainty the two are carried out as duties that may or may not matter in the end, and the reason the sūrah ends the description with it is that everything before it depends on it.

**A FAMILY THAT KEEPS LETTERS FROM SEVERAL SENDERS**

Think of a household that receives letters from a father who has written at different times and through different carriers. The family does not treat the earlier letters as belonging to someone else, and it does not obey the letter that suits them while shelving the rest. The verse forms the reader into a person who accepts the whole correspondence, the newest included, and who lives with his eyes on the appointment his father promised. These days it is easy to meet a person who is devoted to the newest thing and dismissive of everything old, and just as easy to meet the opposite, and the verse refuses both habits in a single line. It asks the reader to hold the revelation as one thing, delivered at different times, and to be as certain of the meeting it announces as he is of the day he can already count in his calendar.
