**THE ARTICLE THEY KEPT AND THE TWO THEY BROKE**

The second article of the pledge is civil, and its terms read like a statute: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant”***, ***“that you would neither shed each other’s blood”***. The glosses fix the range of the clause at once. The forbidden shedding is the spilling of one another's blood, kinsman and ally alike. The first named witnesses of the tradition point to the two clans whose houses the later fighting emptied as the clause's standing examples. The figure in the wording was itself dissected. Killing another member of the community is called killing oneself, since the blood is joined to the shedder by kin or by religion or by the retaliation it unleashes. A second reading hears a warning about consequence: do not spill others' blood so that your own is spilled, as though you had shed it yourself. A third hears a plea against the deeds that make blood lawful at all. The scale of the crime is set elsewhere in the book in the terms the covenant only implies: (5:32 — **“That is why We ordained for the Children of Israel that whoever takes a life—unless as a punishment for”**). The prohibition is not a courtesy of the sanctuary. It is the first law of the commonwealth.

**THE WAR THE COVENANT WAS WRITTEN AGAINST**

The historical setting is given with unusual care, and it makes the covenant's teeth visible. The two clans of the city had fought their old wars under the alliance system of the age of ignorance. Three Jewish factions had each allied with one of the warring parties. When the battle lines met, a man would fight beside his ally against fighters who included members of his own people. When the war ended, the victors ransomed the captives of their own side. The tradition marks that whole arrangement as the background of the charge, and the ethicists of the revelation put the arithmetic in plain terms. Three obligations stood together in the covenant: do not shed one another's blood, do not expel one another from your homes, and ransom your captive. The community acted on the third while breaking the first two. The perversity of that selective observance is the whole accusation. The article they kept was the article that cost them least after the fighting was already over. The expulsion clause widened the civil protection. It forbade the harassment that drives a household out without any decree, and the line from Ibn ʿAbbās names the houses the practice emptied before the sentence arrived.

**THE SIGNATURE THE SIGNATORIES GAVE AWAY**

The covenant closes on a clause about testimony, and the clause is the passage's quiet masterpiece: ***“nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge”***, ***“and bore witness”***. The agreement is described in the glosses as explicit as the statement of a witness in court. To confirm the pledge is to accept it as true and binding. To bear witness is to attest it against oneself in the very act of acceptance. One line of reading hears the clause as addressed to the community in the present tense: you confess the acceptance even now, as the sentence is read over you. Another line takes it as the plain fact of the first assent. They ratified it while knowing exactly what they were doing, and the tradition keeps that reading from Ibn ʿAbbās in its bluntest form. The parallel summons of the scripture-bearers elsewhere draws the same portrait from life: (3:187 — **“and not hide it, yet they cast it behind their backs and traded it for a fleeting gain. What a miserable profit”**). Together the two passages define the crime as a knowing breach and not a lapse of memory. What turns on the witness-language is the legal position of the house at the bar. The covenant is not being alleged. It is being produced with the defendant's own signature on it.

**THE SELF THE VERSE CALLS THE VICTIM**

The deepest reading of the clause is the one the wording invites, and it survives every historical setting the passage has been read through. The members are joined into one self by confession and kin, so the shedding of the brother is the wounding of the body and the expulsion of the neighbor is the flight of the limb from its own trunk. The doctrine that the faithful are like a single body, each member answering for the rest, is the anthropology this grammar assumes. It is why the verse can address a whole nation as one witness who perjured himself. The reason the grammar matters is that it turns a moral appeal into a legal fact. A body cannot un-own its limb, and a nation cannot un-sign its charter. The practical consequence for a modern community that reads its charter in the mirror is exact. A covenant signed in public is enforced in public, and the testimony that once blessed the signature will also testify to the breach. The verse ends where every court ends, with the party's own words.
