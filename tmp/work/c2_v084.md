**A SECOND PLEDGE ABOUT BLOOD AND HOMES**

The covenant in this verse is more specific than the list before it, and it deals with the two things a community does to itself: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant”***, ***“that you would neither shed each other's blood”***, ***“nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge”***, ***“and bore witness”***. The terms forbid killing inside the community and the driving out of neighbours from their houses, and the verse then records that the people accepted the terms and testified to them. That final clause is the reason the next verse is so severe, because a pledge that has been openly witnessed cannot be broken by mistake. The Book has moved from the duties owed to the weak to the duties owed to one another, and it is building a record in which the community's own agreement is the evidence against it. The pledge taken from this people is recalled again where the messengers sent to them are named beside it: (5:70 — **“Indeed, We took a covenant from the Children of Israel and sent them messengers.”**).

**LIFE AND HOME UNDER THE SAME PLEDGE**

The verse protects life and it protects the home, which are the two things a person cannot replace by his own effort, and the Book names them together because they are usually threatened together. A community at war with itself does not simply kill; it drives families out, and the exile that follows is often slower than the violence it avoids. The terms of this covenant are therefore a set of walls around the whole group, and the Book treats the agreement as a settled matter that the people had already accepted. The same charges are laid out in another passage among the reasons a people was condemned, where covenant-breaking stands at the head of the list: (4:155 — **“˹They were condemned˺ for breaking their covenant, rejecting Allah's signs,”**). A reader can see the Book building its case with the community's own words, since the pledge and the testimony are recorded in the same sentence as the offence that follows.

**THE WEIGHT OF A WITNESSED PLEDGE**

The closing words of this verse are easy to read past, and they carry the whole argument of the passage. Bearing witness is a voluntary act, and a person who has testified to an agreement has placed his own credibility inside it, so that the breaking of the terms becomes a matter of his honesty as well as his conduct. The Book's law later requires witnesses for contracts precisely because a witnessed agreement can be enforced, and this verse supplies the most solemn possible witness. The people had given their word twice over, once in speech and once in testimony, which is why the next verse can ask them how they came to be doing the very things they had promised to avoid. The reader who follows the Book's argument here will notice that the exposure depends on the community's own record rather than on an outside accusation.

**A STREET THAT SIGNED AND THEN BUILT GATES**

Think of a street whose residents have signed an agreement that nobody will build a wall across the common path, and who then, one by one, put up gates and shut the newer families out of the square. The Book's account of this people uses the same structure: a written pledge, a public witness, and then a slow reversal in practice that everyone can see and nobody will name. The offence in the verse is not an isolated act of violence but a condition that had come to be accepted, since the next verse describes people who kill and expel while continuing to ransom their captives. The Book's charge is that the community kept the ceremonies of the covenant while dismantling its substance, and the reader who has watched a group treat a promise as a formality knows the pattern. A person can test himself by listing the agreements he has signed and asking which of them he no longer treats as binding.

**THE PLEDGE AS THE BASIS OF JUDGEMENT**

The verse establishes that this community will be judged by its own covenant and not by an external standard, and the Book keeps to that principle throughout the passage. The terms were known, the pledge was given, and the testimony was recorded, so the argument that follows needs no further evidence and the reader meets it as a matter of the community's own word. A reader who belongs to a community that carries a scripture is in the same position, since the terms he has accepted are also available in writing and his own profession of them is a form of testimony. The Book's use of this material is not a history lesson aimed at another people; it is a demonstration of how a record of obligations is kept and used. The next verse opens the account of how this particular record was treated, and the question it raises can be heard by any reader who has a pledge of his own.
