**NO TRUE LESSON IS TOO SMALL FOR DIVINE SPEECH**

The next words answer an objection about what God chooses to mention: ***“Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito”***. Reports differ about the first objectors. Some connect their scorn to the fire and storm used for hypocrites. Others connect it to people who mocked the Qur’an for speaking of a fly or a spider. The shared fault is clear. They decide that a small creature is beneath divine speech, then treat their taste as a measure of God’s wisdom. Yet size does not decide whether an example is true. A fly can expose the weakness of an idol because all false gods together cannot make one: (22:73 — **“those ˹idols˺ you invoke besides Allah can never create ˹so much as˺ a fly, even if they ˹all˺ were to come together”**). A spider’s home can expose trust placed in powerless protectors: (29:41 — **“And the flimsiest of all shelters is certainly that of a spider, if only they knew”**). Qatādah says that God does not hold back from truth when He gives a parable, whether its subject seems great or small. The point is that divine majesty is not guarded by hiding the facts of creation. The smallest life may display power that human beings cannot give, and a plain image may uncover pride that grand speech leaves untouched.

**THE MOSQUITO MEASURES THE HEARER, NOT THE CREATOR**

The range remains open with ***“or what is even smaller.”*** The underlying expression was also read as what is above the mosquito in size, while another reading follows the sense used here: what goes still further in smallness. Neither makes the lesson depend on a fixed scale. If the truth needs a creature larger than a mosquito, that creature serves. If it needs one still less impressive to human eyes, smallness gives no ground for shame. This is why the mosquito tests judgment. A person may praise vast skies as signs, yet refuse to learn from a wing, a bite, or a fragile life near the skin. That refusal mistakes human notice for true worth. The mosquito is easy to dismiss but hard to create, complete with the parts and powers needed for its brief life. Ibn ʿAbbās presses this point: all created beings could join forces and still fail to create what the objector calls low. The difference between a useful parable and a poor one therefore lies in truth and fit, not in the social rank people give its subject. A teacher may use a grain, shadow, thread, or insect if it makes the matter clear. God knows the full reality of both the example and the thing shown by it. His choice lowers no truth; it lowers the pride that will only listen when the image flatters it.

**ONE PARABLE REVEALS TWO WAYS OF HEARING**

The first response is calm: ***“As for the believers, they know”*** ***“that it is the truth from their Lord.”*** They may not yet grasp every detail, but they know that the Lord does not speak without wisdom. That trust is not a ban on thought. It gives thought a sound start, so they attend to the point instead of inventing a flaw in the object used. People grounded in knowledge answer difficult revelation with, (3:7 — **“our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason”**), after confessing that it all comes from Him. The second response exposes a different aim: ***“And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?””*** The same words could form an honest question, but here the speakers use them to reject before learning. Their issue is not lack of an answer. It is scorn for the form in which the answer came. Therefore, the parable does more than carry an idea; it reveals whether the hearer seeks guidance or an excuse. The Quran later records almost the same taunt over the number of Hell’s keepers, then says the test causes believers to grow in faith. A reader should not fear a sincere question, since careful inquiry can serve belief. The danger begins when a question is made impossible to answer because its hidden rule is that God must first speak according to the hearer’s pride.

**GUIDANCE AND ERROR PART AT A MORAL FAULT LINE**

The result is stated as divine action: ***“Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray”*** ***“and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious”***. One revealed parable becomes guidance to some and a further step into error for others. This does not mean that the words are empty and each reader may make them mean anything. The truth remains one, but the moral response differs. Believers receive, think, and gain light. The rebellious resist, mock, and make their refusal harder. Thus the Quran can be (17:82 — **“a healing and mercy for the believers, but it only increases the wrongdoers in loss”**). The reason is not a defect in the medicine. A cure refused can become one more witness that the sick person chose against help. Nor does the verse portray error as a random trap laid for innocent seekers. It limits this leaving astray to the rebellious, and the next verse will name their breach, severing, and harm. A similar order appears when a people first choose a crooked course: (61:5 — **“when you already know I am Allah’s messenger to you?” So when they ˹persistently˺ deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate.”**). God’s judgment is real, and so is the revolt it judges. The safe response is not to demand a sign shaped by pride, but to ask what truth the given sign makes plain and then submit to the Lord who gave it.
