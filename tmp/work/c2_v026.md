**THE OBJECTION TO THE PARABLES AND THE ANSWER TO IT**

The sūrah now answers a complaint that had been raised against its own method: ***“Surely Allah does not shy away from”***, ***“using the parable of a mosquito”***, ***“or what is even smaller.”***. The objection came from people who found the small creatures used in the Book's comparisons unworthy of a divine text, and the verse meets the objection by naming the smallest of them and then going below it. A report from Ibn ʿAbbās explains that God does not refrain from striking a comparison even with a gnat, and al-Suddī reported that the objection was raised when the parables about the pretenders in the earlier verses were recited. The early teachers also placed the objection in the mouths of the Jews who scoffed at the mention of a fly or a spider, and the Book answers all of them in one sentence. The reason the matter is worth a verse of its own is that the objection was not about a creature but about who decides what is fitting for revelation.

**WHAT A PARABLE IS FOR AND WHY SIZE IS IRRELEVANT**

The Book explains the purpose of its comparisons in a verse that makes the answer plain: (29:43 — **“These are the parables We set forth for humanity, but none will understand them except the people of knowledge”**). A parable is a tool for carrying a meaning into a mind that would not hold it in its abstract form, and the tool is chosen for the work rather than for its dignity. The Book uses the lowliest things in creation when they carry the point best, and it uses the loftiest, such as the light of the heavens, when the subject calls for that. The reasoning behind the answer is that the objection confused the size of the example with the weight of the meaning, which is like refusing a key because it is smaller than the door. What turns on the sentence for the reader is that he should meet a comparison in the Book by asking what it teaches rather than by asking whether the subject of it was respectable.

**HOW THE SAME WORDS ARE RECEIVED**

The verse then divides its hearers by what they do with the same words: ***“As for the believers, they know”***, ***“that it is the truth from their”***, ***“Lord.”***, ***“And as for the disbelievers, they argue”***, ***“What does Allah mean by such a”***, ***“parable?”***. The believers are described as knowing, and the disbelievers as arguing, and the two reactions come from the same sentence read by two different hearts. The Book describes the same split in another place where the comparisons are said to be set out for people who may reflect on them: (14:25 — **“This is how Allah sets forth parables for the people, so perhaps they will be mindful”**). The reason a parable can guide one person and unsettle another is that it is a test of what a person wants from the text, and the verse says as much in its next words. The reader who finds himself annoyed by a comparison has been given a small window into his own state, and the sūrah treats that reaction as information rather than as an accident.

**THE STRAYING THAT IS MENTIONED HERE**

The verse closes with a statement that has disturbed some readers: ***“Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many”***, ***“to stray”***, ***“and guides many. And He leaves none”***, ***“to stray except the rebellious”***. The sentence ties the leaving to the test and the rebellion to the people who take the road, and the last words name the character of the people being described. The Book explains the same leaving in another sūrah when it says that the comparison is understood by the people of knowledge and missed by the rest. The reason the verse can speak this way is that a person is not left without a route; he is left to the route he has chosen, and the words that follow in the next verse describe the men who break their covenants. The Book sets out the same test in another sūrah where the comparison is said to separate the people who reflect from the rest: (13:17 — **“This is how Allah sets forth parables”**). Both readings of the words agree on where the decision sits, since the text is the same for the two groups and the difference is in what each one brings to it. Think of a signpost that sends one traveller home and another into a ditch: the sign is the same and the difference is in the traveller, and the Book is content to let the reader decide which of the two he has been so far.  Today, a reader can still dismiss a small example while avoiding the large truth it exposes, because scale does not decide whether a comparison is sound.
