**A MOSQUITO AND THE ARGUMENT ABOUT SMALL THINGS**

The objection came before the answer did, and the sūrah reports both: ***“Surely Allah does not shy away from using the parable of a mosquito”***, ***“or what is even smaller.”*** The people who raised it were not offended by parables in general; they were offended by the choice of a small creature, as though the size of the example measured the dignity of the speaker. The verse meets that objection without conceding an inch. Nothing in creation is beneath mention by the one who made it, and the humblest comparison is worth using if it makes a point clear. The reading attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās treats the line as a statement that God does not refrain from coining comparisons even about a gnat, and the earliest teachers treated the objection as the small thing in the story rather than the mosquito.

Then the verse shows how the same parable lands on two different audiences. ***“As for the believers, they know”***, and ***“that it is the truth from their Lord.”***; while ***“And as for the disbelievers, they argue, “What does Allah mean by such a parable?””***. The division is not caused by the parable; it is revealed by it. A person whose mind is set on learning will ask what the comparison teaches, and a person whose mind is set on resisting will ask why it was used at all. The same words produce the two reactions on the same day, and the sūrah draws the conclusion with an even hand in the next sentence. The Book says as much when it explains why the numbers and the images are given at all: (74:31 — **“We have appointed only ˹stern˺ angels as wardens of the Fire.”**), and the verse continues with the two effects, the stumbling of some and the increase of faith in others.

**THE SORT OF TEST THAT SEPARATES PEOPLE**

The remaining lines state the effect plainly: ***“Through this ˹test˺, He leaves many to stray”***, ***“and guides many. And He leaves none to stray except the rebellious”***. The Book is describing truth functioning as a separator, and it does not apologise for the fact. A message addressed to a free creature will always have two possible outcomes, and a reader who wants the outcomes merged into one is asking for a world in which nobody decides anything. What the verse refuses is the complaint that the tool is unfair because it produced a reaction the complainer did not choose. The reaction was his; the tool simply made it visible. The word for rebellion here is the same word the Book uses for a person who has stepped outside a boundary he knows is there. It is not ignorance, and it is not weakness. That is why the verse can say that only the rebellious are left to stray: the others are either guided by the message or left unaffected because they never came near it, and the ones who take the light and use it to look for a way out are a category of their own. The earlier descriptions in this sūrah have been building the same portrait from different angles, and the closing line of the verse completes it.

**WHAT A SMALL PARABLE ASKS OF A READER**

The practical lesson is about the size of things. A reader who will not change his conduct over a matter he considers minor has already answered the question the objectors raised, because the mosquito in the story stands for every small instruction. Prayer at its proper time, a coin given quietly, a word kept back from a conversation: these are small creatures in the moral landscape until a person refuses to hand them over. That refusal is the moment the mosquito becomes the test. The Book uses small things because a person's real loyalty is less often revealed in the large decisions than in the ones he never expected to matter. Today the exercise is to choose the smallest command a reader has been setting aside and hand it over this week, not because it is heavy but because it is light. The person who does that has understood the verse from the inside, and he will find that the objection about the choice of example loses its force once the example has been obeyed. Nothing in the passage asks anyone to admire the creature. It asks him to take the lesson, which is smaller than the creature and harder to swallow.
