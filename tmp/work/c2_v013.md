**AN INSTRUCTION THEY COULD NOT ACCEPT**

The next exchange between the same people and their neighbours begins with an instruction: ***“And when they are told”***, ***“Believe”***, ***“as others believe,”***. The people held up as the standard are the Companions of the Prophet, and the early authorities agree on this: Ibn ʿAbbās named ʿAbdullāh ibn Salām and his companions from the Jewish community of Madinah who had accepted the message, and other teachers named the emigrants and the helpers as a body. The command is not to invent a faith of one's own but to take the shape the community had taken, and the reason is that faith is not a private theory about God but a way of living that a person enters alongside others. The Book settles the same point when it describes the people who answered the call and left the most of what they had: they became the standard by which the next generation was measured, and the verse here makes that standard an instruction.

**THE ANSWER THAT TURNED THE WORD AROUND**

The reply of the pretenders is recorded in their own words: ***“Will we believe”***, ***“as the fools believe?”***. They took the word for foolishness and fixed it to the people who had submitted, and the early teachers explained the answer as the defence of a position rather than a question of fact. The Book uses the same accusation in the story of another prophet, where the chiefs of his people answered him in exactly that tone: (7:66 — **“We surely see you as a fool”**). The pattern is worth noticing for the reason that it repeats: when a person cannot answer the substance of a call, he turns to the character of the people who answered it. That is why the insult is met with a verdict instead of an explanation, since the explanation had already been given and refused. The pretenders could see that the believers had given up comfortable positions and old alliances, and they measured that sacrifice by the standards of the market and found it a loss, which means the insult was really an argument about what a person should count as gain.

**THE REVERSAL THAT THE VERSE PRONOUNCES**

The sūrah takes the word back and gives it to the men who used it: ***“Indeed, it is they”***, ***“who are fools, but they do not know”***. The Book pronounces the verdict in two parts, the second of which explains why the first was needed. A fool in this usage is not a person with a weak mind; he is a person who has the wrong measure of what is worth having, and the Book applies the word on that basis. It states the same judgment about the man who turns away from the way of Abraham: (2:130 — **“And who would reject the faith of Abraham except a fool!”**). The early teachers said that the men knew very well who the people of understanding were, and that they used the word because it served their position, which means the verdict here is about a preference and not about an intellect. What turns on the reversal is that the reader learns where the judgment of worth sits, and it does not sit with the loudest voice in the room.

**A CROWD THAT LAUGHS AT THE FIRST PEOPLE TO BOARD**

Think of a ship being loaded while a crowd on the quay mocks the passengers for giving up their places in the town. The laughter is loud until the water rises, and then the word for foolishness moves to the ones still standing on the shore. The verse uses the same reversal with the community that had accepted the message while the pretenders kept their options open, and the reversal is stated in the language the mockers themselves had used. These days the same scene repeats whenever a person decides that the standards of the people around him are the measure of what is sensible, and the passage gives him the freedom to be counted a fool by that measure while keeping his place among the people who submitted. The ending of the verse settles which of the two groups knows what it is doing, and it leaves the matter there. The reason the passage does not soften the word is that the choice between the two measures is a choice about who a person is, and a softened word would leave the reader free to hold both measures at once.
