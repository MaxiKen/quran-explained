**WHEN THE ANSWER IS A MOCKING QUESTION**

The sūrah offers a third sample of the same habit: ***“And when they are told, “Believe”***, ***“as others believe,” they reply, “Will we believe”***, ***“as the fools believe?””***. The invitation is plain and the reply turns it into an insult. Notice what the reply does not do: it never examines the claim of the invitation, and it never asks what the belief involves. It moves straight to the character of the people who already believe, because a question about them is easier than a question about God. The verse is showing the reader a technique rather than merely recording an incident, and the technique is still in wide use. The technique is old and effective. If a person can be made to feel that the believing position is held by fools, the argument about evidence never has to happen, since nobody wants to join a group he has just been taught to despise. The Book answers the technique by naming its user. The next words in the verse are ***“Indeed, it is they”***, ***“who are fools, but they do not know”***, and the reversal lands precisely because the word was already out of the speaker's mouth. The reader is meant to notice how quickly an insult about others can be returned with interest.

**THE FLATTERY INSIDE THE INSULT**

Behind the mockery there is a flattering premise, and it is worth stating because it survives in every generation. The premise is that the speaker belongs to a small clear-sighted minority who have seen through the credulity of the crowd. He is not one of the simple people who accept what they are told; he has standards, and the standards cost him something. The verse takes the flattery seriously enough to answer it. What the speaker has actually done is to protect a position he never tested, while borrowing the vocabulary of a man who tests everything. His scepticism stops exactly at the point where it would have to look at his own arrangement. The Qur'an treats this posture as a form of folly in a technical sense, not as an insult thrown back. Foolishness in the Book's vocabulary means putting a thing where it does not belong: distrust where the evidence is strong, confidence where nothing has been verified. A man who distrusts the plain report of a trustworthy messenger and trusts his own untested guess has mislaid his judgment in both directions at once. That is why the verse can say the fools are the ones laughing, and why the phrase ***“but they do not know”*** is not a dismissal but a description of the position they are standing in.

**THE COST OF BEING TOO CLEVER TO JOIN**

A third point is hidden in the phrase "as others believe". The invitation is not to invent a private faith but to believe with a community, which means that accepting it requires a person to be one of a crowd. That is precisely what a proud man cannot do, and the verse shows him refusing not on the merits but on the company. The reader should notice that the Book does not therefore make the community the standard. It simply refuses to let pride, which is about the company, dress itself as rigour, which claims to be about the facts. For a reader today the verse is a check on the jokes he tells. Ridicule aimed at believers is one of the most available amusements there is, and it is cheap, and it is now distributed at scale to people who never meet the believers they laugh at. The serious question is not whether the jokes are funny but whether he has ever examined the thing being laughed at, in its strongest form rather than in its weakest. Today that examination can be done: take one claim, find the best version of it, and look at it. The fool in the verse never got as far as that, and the difference is the whole distance between him and the reader.
