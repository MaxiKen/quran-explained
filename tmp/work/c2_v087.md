**A CHAIN OF MESSENGERS AND A PATTERN OF REFUSAL**

Favours granted to this people are counted again here, and the greatest of them is named: ***“Indeed, We gave Moses the Book”***, ***“and sent after him successive messengers.”***, ***“And We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs”***, ***“and supported him with the holy spirit. Why is it”***, ***“that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some”***, ***“and killing others”***. The verse lists the gifts and the response in one sentence each, and the contrast is the whole of its argument. Messengers were sent in sequence, each confirming the one before, and the community's reaction was calibrated by its preferences rather than by the credentials of the messengers. The question at the end is addressed to the people who received all of that, and it names the fault precisely: not doubt, but arrogance. The Book names one of those announcements in particular, where a messenger speaks of another who will come after him: (61:6 — **“And ˹remember˺ when Jesus, son of Mary, said, “O children of Israel!”**).

**THE PROOFS GIVEN AND THE SPIRIT THAT SUPPORTED HIM**

The mention of Jesus in this verse comes with two gifts attached to him, and both are named because the community he was sent to had a particular difficulty with him. He was given clear proofs, and he was supported by a spirit that came from God, and the Book says elsewhere that he spoke to people from the cradle and announced a messenger to come after him. The passage places him among the successive messengers rather than outside the sequence, and that placement is the argument against those who either rejected him or made him into something the Book does not say. The same catalogue of prophetic favours appears in a later sūrah with the same charge against the recipients: (5:70 — **“Whenever a messenger came to them with what they did not desire,”**). A reader who follows the Book's own account of this chain will notice that it never separates the prophets into competing camps.

**ARROGANCE AS THE ROOT OF REJECTION**

The verse does not describe the community's response as a failure of evidence, and that choice of words is important for the reader. The proofs were clear and the messengers came in sequence, so the Book names the actual cause, which is a refusal to accept what does not please a person. The same word is used in the Book for those who elevate themselves above a command, and the sentences that follow in this sūrah continue the charge of selective acceptance. A reader who has ever declined a piece of advice because of who gave it, and later found it correct, has the beginning of the condition described here. The Book's diagnosis is that arrogance grows in a community that has grown used to being addressed by God, which is a warning to anyone who has received much.

**THE PATIENT WHO REFUSES HIS OWN DOCTOR**

Think of a man who has chosen a specialist, paid for the consultation, and then refuses the prescription because the specialist's tone was not to his liking. The people addressed here had asked for their messengers and were given the proofs, and it then measured each messenger against its comfort. The Book's account of the killing of prophets follows from the same habit, since a community that has learned to reject inconvenient messages will eventually have no patience with the men who carry them. The reader should not read this as a charge against a distant people only; the chapter begins with three descriptions of readers, one of which is the person who hears and does not accept. The arrogance named here is the very fault that the earlier passage called a sickness in the heart, and the community's own account of it arrives in the next verse.

**WHY THEY CALLED THEIR HEARTS UNRECEPTIVE**

The verse prepares the ground for the community's own explanation of itself, which arrives in the next verse, and the Book's structure here is worth noting. The people are shown dismissing what they do not like through their prophets, and then explaining the pattern as a feature of their own nature rather than as a matter of choice. That is the last move in the sequence the Book is describing, and it is the one the reader should watch for in himself: a habit of refusal dressed up as a description of temperament. The chapter's answer comes in a single word about who sealed the hearts, and it leaves no room for the excuse the community had prepared. What the reader takes from the passage is the alternative it offers, which is the simple willingness to accept what has been sent whether or not it suits him.
