**KINDNESS CAN BE BETTER THAN MONEY**

***“Kind words and forgiveness are better than charity”*** establishes kindness can be better than money. A kind word—qawl maʿrūf—and forgiveness are better than charity that is followed by hurt. The verse does not diminish material assistance. A hungry person needs food and a debtor may need money. It teaches that the spiritual and moral manner of giving matters so much that an apparently generous gift can become worse than a respectful refusal if the gift humiliates and injures the recipient. Sometimes a person cannot give financially. A gentle explanation, sincere apology, useful information, prayer, or referral to someone able to help can preserve dignity. The one who asks should not be mocked or treated as a burden. The one who cannot give should say so kindly rather than making promises he cannot keep. Forgiveness—maghfirah—can mean overlooking an offensive request, declining to shame someone who cannot repay, or forgiving a person’s manner while addressing the underlying need. It does not require surrendering a legitimate right or remaining in an unsafe situation. A victim can seek justice and still refuse the desire to humiliate the wrongdoer. The pairing of kindness and forgiveness shows that charity is relational. Wealth can solve an immediate problem, but mercy can protect the person’s heart and future. The recipient should leave with hope rather than a new wound. A person who gives may unconsciously assume superiority. The verse corrects this by reminding him that the recipient is not less valuable before Allah. (2:261 — **“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a”**) A reading attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās makes the kind word include prayer and praise for another person, while forgiveness overlooks an offence without using generosity to injure.

**FORGIVENESS AS A GIFT**

***“followed by injury. And Allah is Self-Sufficient, Most Forbearing”*** deepens forgiveness as a gift. The donor is being tested through wealth, while the recipient may be tested through need. The roles can reverse quickly, and both are servants of the same Lord. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah does not look at bodies or wealth but at hearts and deeds. This should shape charitable institutions: no degrading photographs, no public queues designed for spectacle, no insulting language, and no conditions that force people to praise the donor. Allah is Ghaniyy, Self-Sufficient. He does not need the charity, praise, or obedience of His servants. This name removes the donor’s illusion that he has done Allah a favour. Allah commands charity for the benefit of people and the purification of the giver; His dominion is not increased by a donation. Because Allah is free of need, He can reward a small gift enormously. The giver is not competing with Allah or reducing divine wealth. He is using a provision that Allah entrusted to him. Allah is Ḥalīm, Forbearing. He delays punishment for those who give arrogantly, injure recipients, or use His name for reputation. This forbearance is an opportunity to repent, not evidence that hurtful charity is acceptable. A person should correct his intention and conduct before the hidden damage becomes a pattern. The name also teaches the giver to be patient with those he helps. A recipient may be slow to repay, anxious, disorganized, or unable to express gratitude. (17:53 — **“Tell My ˹believing˺ servants to say only what is best.”**)

**CHARITY AND HUMAN DIGNITY**

Patience does not excuse fraud, but it prevents a donor from turning assistance into constant complaint. There are times when giving money would enable addiction, corruption, or dependence. A person can refuse while offering a safer alternative. A parent may pay a bill directly instead of handing over cash; a charity may provide treatment rather than fund destructive conduct. The refusal should still be kind and free of humiliation. The verse’s ideal is not indiscriminate giving. It is beneficial assistance administered with wisdom, honesty, and mercy. A community guided by this āyah makes help accessible without forcing people to expose their poverty. It trains volunteers to speak respectfully, funds professional services, and ensures that those who ask are not punished for their need. It also teaches recipients that accepting help does not remove their agency or right to make lawful decisions. Charity becomes iḥsān when the giver seeks Allah, the recipient is honoured, and the assistance produces genuine benefit. Think of kindness can be better than money through a woman at a well who has no bucket of her own and greets every traveller as he fills his; she carries nothing home, and the whole village remembers the morning with her in it. Kindness can be better than money confronts forgiveness as a gift in a reader who has nothing to give and thinks that he has therefore nothing to offer at all. The verse gives him a kind word, a forgiveness and a place above the coin, and it asks him who told him that a poor man's hands are the only part of him that counts.

**ALLAH IS SELF-SUFFICIENT**

Because the close names the Self-Sufficient, showing through kindness can be better than money that the Lord of the worlds asks for the kind answer of a person because that answer is what the person is. Since the coin never reaches the Lord of the worlds, the reader sees that a gift is measured by the manner it is wrapped in. The kind word is joined to the same patience of the Lord in the verse about the giving that is spoiled. And the good word is asked of the believers in another chapter, where the Book sets the manner of a person beside his faith.
