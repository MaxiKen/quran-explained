**ENVY AT THE BLESSING OF OTHERS**

The verse turns from the corrected greeting to the resentment behind the mockery, and it names the parties without softening the description: ***“The disbelievers from the People of the Book”***. The Book has been speaking to the community that received the message, and here it explains that the objection to the message was never only about the wording used in the street. The party named first is the one that possessed scriptures of its own, which makes its resentment of a new blessing a matter of a different order from the ignorance of a people who had never received anything. Ibn ʿAbbās describes the verse as coming down over the leaders of that community and their refusal to see the Messenger's people favoured at all. What the reader is being prepared for is the reason behind the mockery, and the reason is named as a posture rather than an argument.

**DISBELIEVERS AND POLYTHEISTS MADE ONE PARTY**

The sentence joins a second party to the first, and the joining is itself a statement: ***“and the polytheists would not want you to receive any blessing from your Lord”***. The two groups had little in common in their beliefs, and the Book places them together at the point where their attitude converges, which is the refusal to see the believers favoured. The blessing in question is from the Lord of the believers, so the refusal is directed at His choice rather than at their merit, and that is the subject of the sentence. The verse does not engage the objections of either group in detail here; it states the desire that stands behind them, and the statement is short because the desire is simple. A reader who has met hostility that could not be reasoned with has met the thing this clause describes, and the Book names it without pretending it is an argument.

**THE CHOICE THAT BELONGS TO ALLAH**

The next clause supplies the answer to the resentment, and it is an answer about authority rather than about merit: ***“but Allah selects whoever He wills”***. The verse does not argue that the believers deserved what they received; it places the giving where the Book always places it, in a choice that belongs to the giver. The same sentence reappears in another sūrah in nearly the same words, which shows how central the point is to the Book's treatment of favour: (3:74 — **“He chooses whoever He wills to receive His mercy”**). The resentment described in the earlier clauses is therefore a quarrel with a decision that was never submitted to the objectors in the first place. What the reader takes from the sentence is the ground of his own confidence, since a gift that depends on the giver's will is not diminished by anyone's disapproval.

**A DOOR NO ONE CAN SHUT**

Think of a house whose owner opens the door to a guest that the neighbours had decided to keep out, and who does not ask the neighbours for permission before doing it. The verse describes a favour that no amount of resentment could withhold, because the giving is placed in the hands of the One who owns the favour. The Book states the same position elsewhere when it discusses the bounty that passes between communities, and the verse here is one of its plainest statements of the principle. A reader who has been made to feel that his share of guidance was a matter for other people to approve has the sentence as his answer, and the answer is not a claim about his own worth. What is being refused in the earlier clauses is not the blessing but the freedom of the giver to bestow it.

**BOUNTY DESCRIBED AS A LORDLY ATTRIBUTE**

The verse closes by describing the One who gives, and the description is a statement about abundance rather than merely about power: ***“for His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty”***. The word for bounty carries the sense of a giving that does not run down, which is why it is used to close a sentence about resentment over a gift. The sending of the Book is therefore presented as one instance of a generosity that has no narrow channel, and the verse intends the reader to see the objection as small beside the description that ends it. A community that reads the sūrah in order has already met this attribute in its opening verses, and the repetition at this point in the argument is deliberate. The same open-handedness is stated where the Book describes the reward of those who work: (24:38 — **“And Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit”**). The next verses answer a related objection, this time about what happens to earlier revelations when a new one arrives.
