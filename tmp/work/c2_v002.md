**A DECLARATION THAT POINTS AT SOMETHING ALREADY KNOWN**

The sūrah leaves the letters and speaks plainly: ***“This is the Book!”*** The sentence is a declaration, and the particle it opens with points at something honoured and known rather than at something near at hand. The generality of the earliest commentators read the two words as meaning that this is the Book, the one promised and expected, now standing in front of its readers. The distance in the wording carries its own point, because the demonstrative looks back over the whole of the opening sūrah and answers the request made there: the servant asked to be guided, and the answer is a Book that will do the guiding. The reader who finished al-Fātiḥah with a plea for the straight path meets the reply in the first clear sentence of al-Baqarah, and the arrangement is not a coincidence of editing but the shape of the revelation.

**THE CLAIM THAT NO DOUBT BELONGS TO IT**

The next phrase is a claim about the Book itself: ***“There is no doubt about it”***. Ibn ʿAbbās read it as meaning that the Book has come from God, and Mujāhid, Saʿīd ibn Jubayr, Abū al-ʿĀliyah, Qatādah and al-Suddī are credited with the same reading. It is recorded that no disagreement on the point was known among the early teachers. The wording does not say that everyone will be free of doubt; it says that no doubt belongs to the Book, and the difference matters. A doubt is something the doubter brings, and the sūrah goes on in the following verses to describe people who carry it and are not moved by any argument. The same claim is made in another sūrah about the revelation as a whole: (32:2 — **“The revelation of this Book is—beyond doubt—from the Lord of all worlds”**). What the reader is being handed at the start of the longest sūrah is therefore a firm surface, and the firmness is claimed before any of the contents have been shown.

**THE QUALIFICATION THAT THE BOOK SETS ON ITSELF**

The verse does not stop at certainty, and its last phrase names the people the Book will guide: ***“a guide for those mindful ˹of Allah˺”***. The word carries the sense of guarding oneself from harm, and the sūrah describes its people in that language again at the end of a passage that lists what they do and what they give: (2:177 — **“it is they who are mindful ˹of Allah˺”**). The guidance is stated as a fact about the Book, and the qualification is stated as a condition of receiving it. The early authorities put the point plainly, reading the phrase as meaning that the Book guides the one who comes to it with the intention of guarding himself from what God has forbidden. A reader who opens the Book to win an argument will find material for an argument; the one who opens it to be corrected will find the correction. The difference is not in the Book, which is the same for both, but in what they brought to it.

**TWO TRAVELLERS AND A WELL-MARKED ROAD**

Think of two men driving the same road at night. Both pass the same signs, and one arrives while the other takes a wrong turning and blames the signs. The signs did not change between them; the driver who arrived read them with the intention of obeying them. The verse says the Book is guidance and then says it is guidance for the mindful, and the two statements together tell the reader where the difference lies. These days a person can have the whole text on his phone and never once open it with the intention of being corrected, and the verse quietly denies him the excuse of the sign. What the passage asks of the reader is simple to state and hard to keep: come to the Book to be told what to do, and the guidance that is already in it will reach him. The verse also protects the reader from a common mistake, which is to measure the Book by the behaviour of the people who claim it and then walk away when the behaviour disappoints him, because the claim of this sentence is about the Book and not about its readers.
