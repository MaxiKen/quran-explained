**A PRAISE WIDER THAN THANKSGIVING**

***“All praise is for Allah”*** opens the sūrah's second sentence, and the wording carries more than the English word suggests. Praise is the extolling of one who is worthy of it, and it is spoken of qualities before it is spoken of gifts. The Book uses both words, and the difference between them teaches the reader something about the God it names. The verse itself is built on that difference, and the reports explain it: Ibn ʿAbbās said that the sentence is the statement of appreciation, and that when the servant says it God answers that His servant has praised Him. The point is stated in the plainest form by the teachers of the language. Praise is given for what the one praised is in himself and for what he does; thanks are given for what he has given. Because praise reaches further than thanks, the Book does not say *all thanks is for Allah* in this place, and it does not leave the sentence to a time of good fortune alone. It is reported from Qatādah that praise is the finest of what a person says, since it is spoken in plenty and in want alike, while thanks comes most easily when the hand is full. That is why a person is told to say that all praise is for Allah, and not that some of it is. The definite article in the Arabic does the same work as the word *all* in the translation. It gathers every kind of praise, from every voice, at every time.

**THE LORD OF EVERY WORLD**

The second half of the verse gives God the title ***“Lord of all worlds”***, and each part of that title deserves its own explanation. The word *rabb* in Arabic carries three ideas at once: a master who is obeyed, an owner who possesses what he holds, and one who cultivates what he owns until it reaches its proper state. The last of the three is the most surprising in this place, because it describes the care of a farmer for a field and the care of a parent for a child, and the verse applies it to God without any other qualification. It is for this reason that some said the title points to the sustaining of every creature through every stage of its life, and others said it points to the authority of the one who orders all affairs. Ibn ʿAbbās explained the phrase as the Lord of everything that has a spirit and walks the earth, and also of the dwellers of the heavens, and he added that it means the Creator, the Provider, and the one who changes all things from one state into another. The Book sets the title on the same pattern in more than forty places, and it also puts it in the mouths of the favoured: (26:192 — **“This is certainly a revelation from the Lord of all worlds”**). Its widest use in the Qur'an comes in a sentence reporting what the people of Paradise say, where the title is joined to praise and rises above every particular creature: (10:10 — **“their closing prayer will be, “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds”**).

**THE SENTENCE AS GOD HEARS IT**

The reports add a layer to this sentence that no private reading of it can reach. Abū Hurayrah reports that the Prophet said that God says, *"I have divided the prayer between Myself and My servant into two halves, and My servant shall have what he asks"*, and that when the servant says *"All praise is for Allah, Lord of all worlds"*, God says, *"My servant has praised Me"* (Muslim). The report makes the recitation a conversation rather than a declaration. A person says the words in the direction of his own mouth, and the answer is already spoken before the sentence is finished. The same report explains why the sūrah is called the prayer in the first place, since its words are what God and the servant exchange in every unit. ʿAlī, the fourth of the caliphs, described the sentence as a statement God loved for Himself, was pleased with for Himself, and loves to have repeated. It is also reported from Anas ibn Mālik that the Prophet said, *"God is pleased with a servant who eats a morsel and praises Him for it, or drinks a drink and praises Him for it"* (Muslim). The sentence is therefore not reserved for the prayer mat. It is the language of a person who has understood where his food came from.

**THE ROOM THAT KEEPS BEING SWEPT**

Picture a large room that receives visitors all day. The floor is swept after every one of them, and the sweeping is not an event that anybody photographs. It is what keeps the room usable. Praise does that work in a person's inner life. The believer says this sentence over the meal, over the found object, over the difficult day, and each saying clears a little of the dust that a day of taking things for granted leaves behind. A person who never says it slowly begins to receive everything as a debt owed to him, and that is the beginning of misery as surely as hunger is the beginning of illness. These days the accounts of what a person has are fully displayed and rarely counted, and the sentence in this verse forces the count. It teaches the servant to say what God is before he says what he needs, and it puts the two halves of the sūrah in their right order: praise first, then request.
