**ONE FAITH, TWO ARRIVALS**
The portrait continues, and the next mark of the guided widens the room considerably: ***“and who believe in what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺”***. The people described here accept the message sent to Muhammad while keeping their place among those who had received earlier messages, and the Qur'an treats that double loyalty as a single faith rather than a compromise. Early readers sorted the two groups of believers: Ibn Masʿūd and Ibn ʿAbbās are reported to have said that some of the mindful had come from among the idol-worshippers and some from among the People of the Book, and that the verses name one group after the other so that neither is left outside the description. The wording places the two revelations side by side, and a side-by-side arrangement is how a community is taught that it is the continuation of a story rather than the start of a new one.
**WHAT WAS SENT BEFORE, AND WHY IT STILL COUNTS**
The verse then names the earlier revelation without naming a book: ***“and what was revealed before you”***. The omission is deliberate, because the sentence is about a pattern rather than a title, and other passages fill in the details. One says plainly what the earliest scriptures were, (3:3 — **“as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel”**), and another puts the Book sent to Muhammad in the position of judge over what came before it, (5:48 — **“a confirmation of previous Scriptures”**). It is like a household that keeps the deeds to its land in one envelope, where each paper is part of one title. The Qur'an treats revelation the same way: the earlier scriptures are not discarded paper, and the final Book is not a stranger to them. That is why belief in the earlier books is a duty of the same faith; denying them would leave a believer holding half of a chain, since a chain is only useful while both ends hold.
**CERTAINTY ABOUT A DAY NO ONE HAS SEEN**
The sentence closes with the mark that holds the whole portrait together: ***“and have sure faith in the Hereafter”***. The word for certainty is the opposite of guessing, and the Qur'an gives it to the man who orders his whole week around a day he will never observe from the outside. The arrangement itself carries the emphasis, because the verse before this one spoke of the hidden in general terms and this one singles out the meeting with God, so the two together cover what a believer accepts without seeing. Certainty about the meeting with God is what makes a costly obedience rational. A man who sells a field he loves to pay a debt he owes is not losing a field; he is buying a clear conscience in a market where the price is fixed and the seller cannot cheat him.
**WHERE THE TWO ARRIVALS MEET**
What the verse pictures is a single house with two doors. A person can enter from the world of idols and find that the prophets he never knew were always part of the family. Another can enter from the People of the Book and find that the teacher he waited for has come, with the earlier scriptures intact behind him and the final Book in front. The Qur'an states the duty of the second group in a single command, (4:136 — **“the Scriptures He revealed before”**), and it keeps the two revelations in one sentence rather than two, so that the believer's creed is a line of light running from the first prophet to the last. That line is what allows a Muslim to honour Moses and Jesus without confusion, and to read the Torah and the Gospel as the story his own Book completes.
**A FAITH THAT CHANGES THE WEEK**
What follows from the verse is a difference that shows up in a week rather than in a debate. A person who believes that revelation ended with the closing of one Book will look for nothing else and will read the earlier scriptures with contempt or fear; the Qur'an asks for the opposite, since a well-grounded believer can sit with a Jewish or Christian neighbour and honour what their books contain even while he holds his own as the final word. Certainty about the Hereafter then does the rest, because a man who knows he will be questioned about his dealings will not settle for a profitable lie. Both halves of the verse, the earlier books and the coming day, work on the same nerve: they take a believer's eyes off the month he is living and put them on the length of the road he is travelling, and both are tested in ordinary decisions this week, at a desk, a market or a dinner table.
