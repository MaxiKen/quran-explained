**THE UNLETTERED AND THEIR WISHFUL STORIES**

The chapter turns from the people who altered the text to the people who never had it: ***“And among them are the illiterate”***, ***“who know nothing about the Scripture except lies”***, ***“and ˹so˺ they ˹wishfully˺ speculate”***. The word translated here as the illiterate describes people who could not read a book at all, and what they possess instead of a text is a set of claims they hope are true. The Book distinguishes them from the group described in the previous verse, because the learned who changed the words and the unlettered who repeat the changes are not the same offenders. What the reader is shown is a two-storey arrangement: a written tradition being edited above and a spoken one being invented below, with the second depending on the first for its authority. The verse's last word is about guessing, and the whole tragedy of the passage is that people with no access to the Book are confident about what it says. The same kind of untested hope is named later in this sūrah, where two communities at once claim the future for themselves: (2:111 — **“The Jews and Christians each claim”**).

**THE TALES THAT WERE EASY TO BELIEVE**

The claims held by the unlettered are described as wishes, which tells the reader how they survived: they were pleasant, they flattered the community, and nobody in a position to check them had an interest in doing so. A later sūrah reports the same community in the same posture, generations after the covenant, still working from inherited claims that had never been tested against the text: (7:169 — **“Then they were succeeded by other generations”**). The Book's charge against the educated is that they knowingly corrupted the words; its charge against the ordinary people is that they were content with comfortable guesses. Neither charge is about a lack of intelligence, and neither is excused by the other. A community in which knowledge is handled by a small group and never verified by the rest will produce exactly this division, and the Book records the result without surprise.

**A FAMILY STORY THAT NOBODY CHECKED**

Think of a family that has repeated for three generations that a particular piece of property was a gift and never a loan, and that discovers after a death that the document says otherwise. The people repeating the claim are honest, and the claim is still wrong, and the person who benefits from the error is not necessarily the one who invented it. The illiterate of this verse were the last link in that chain, and the Book places them in a chapter about covenant-breaking because a community's belief is carried by its unexamined commonplaces as much as by its scholars. A reader who wants to apply the verse does not need to find an illiterate man; he needs to find the beliefs he holds about his own religion that he has never once checked against a text, because the wishful claim survives precisely as long as nobody opens the book.

**THE DUTY OF THE UNLEARNED AND THE GUILT OF THE LEARNED**

The passage distributes responsibility with a precision that is worth copying in any community. The people who cannot read are not blamed for failing to read; they are blamed for speculating, which is something they could have declined to do. The people who can read are blamed for altering what they had, which is a different offence with a heavier penalty named in the next verse. This distribution protects the weak and indicts the strong, and it leaves no one with the excuse that his education was limited. The same Book tells its own community that the believers must ask and learn rather than speculate, and it places the burden of teaching on those who hold the knowledge. Where a community has stopped teaching, the Book's account of this people shows what grows in the space.

**A SPECULATION THAT BECAME A CREED**

The verse is short, and it describes a process that takes decades. A claim invented to explain an awkward text, repeated by people who trust the teller, becomes the settled belief of a village, and then the belief is defended as tradition by people who have never seen the text. By the time anyone checks, the correction sounds like an attack on the community, which is why the Book records the sequence at the point where the blame is still traceable. The reader's defence against the process is the same in every era, and it is unglamorous: check the claim against the source before it becomes part of you. The unlettered in this verse were not condemned for what they could not do, and the reader who has the means to check is in a stronger position than they were, which raises the standard rather than lowering it.
