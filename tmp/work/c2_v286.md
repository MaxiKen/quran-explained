**CAPACITY, DUTY, AND MERCY**

***“Allah does not require of any soul more than what it can afford. All good will be for its own benefit, and all evil”*** establishes capacity, duty, and mercy. The sūrah closes by limiting every burden to the soul’s capacity—lā yukallifu Allāhu nafsan illā wusʿahā. This is a foundational principle of Islamic law and spiritual life. Allah knows the body, mind, resources, circumstances, knowledge, and genuine ability of every servant. He does not demand the impossible or punish a person for what lies completely beyond control. The principle does not mean that every obligation will feel easy. Prayer, fasting, charity, patience, truthfulness, and resisting sin can require effort. Capacity includes the ability to persevere through reasonable difficulty, while genuine illness, disability, coercion, danger, and incapacity bring concessions explained throughout the Sharīʿah. The verse immediately joins capacity to personal accountability. Each soul receives the benefit of what it earns—lahā mā kasabat—and bears the consequence of what it earns—ʿalayhā mā iktasabat. No one can claim another person’s reward as his own, and no one is punished for another person’s sin. The principle of mercy does not erase responsibility. Good deeds are attributed as a gift and benefit to the servant, while wrongdoing is described as something he acquires against himself. Allah’s justice recognizes intention, knowledge, coercion, and ability. A person who makes an honest mistake is not judged like one who deliberately chooses betrayal after repeated clarification. The supplication asks Allah not to take believers to task for forgetfulness or error. The Prophet ﷺ reported that Allah answered this request, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. This does not make negligence harmless. A person who forgets a prayer makes it up when he remembers; a person who makes a financial mistake corrects the record; a person who harms another apologizes and repairs what he can. The distinction is between an unintentional error and deliberate neglect disguised as forgetfulness. (7:157 — **““˹They are˺ the ones who follow the Messenger, the unlettered Prophet,”**) The balance between capacity and earning means mercy cannot be turned into moral indifference. Precisely because Allah measures each soul justly, every available ability and every genuine limitation matters in the account.

**EACH SOUL EARNS AND BEARS**

***“will be to its own loss. ˹The believers pray,˺ “Our Lord! Do not punish us if we forget or make a mistake. Our Lord!”*** deepens each soul earns and bears. Allah knows whether a person truly forgot, failed despite reasonable care, or avoided responsibility and then claimed an excuse. The servant takes precautions while trusting Allah’s mercy for what remains beyond him. The believers ask not to be placed under a burden like those placed upon earlier communities. This can refer to severe legal burdens, difficult tests, or the consequences that followed earlier peoples’ disobedience. The prayer is not a complaint against Allah’s justice; it is a request for mercy and ease in the final community’s law. The Qur’an describes the Prophet ﷺ as lifting burdens and shackles from people through the final revelation. The law contains clear obligations but also purification, repentance, concessions, and the principle of capacity. Muslims should not invent extra burdens and then call them greater piety. The believers ask not to be burdened with what they cannot bear—mā lā ṭāqata lanā bih. This prayer is relevant to illness, poverty, persecution, family conflict, debt, trauma, and spiritual weakness. It invites people to ask Allah for relief while using lawful means: medical care, counselling, legal protection, financial assistance, and supportive community. A person should not be shamed for using a concession Allah granted. A sick person may break a fast and make it up or give fidyah according to the ruling; a traveller may shorten prayer; someone in danger may pray according to ability. The form changes, but the servant remains connected to Allah. The supplication then asks for ʿafw, pardon; maghfirah, forgiveness; and raḥmah, mercy. Pardon can mean Allah overlooking a fault, forgiveness can mean covering the sin and its consequence, and mercy can mean granting guidance and good throughout earthly life and at the eternal return. The sequence shows how completely the servant depends upon Allah. The believer should also embody these qualities. He pardons where justice and safety allow, forgives sincere repentance, and shows mercy to those facing weakness. (2:190 — **“Fight in the cause of Allah ˹only˺ against those who wage war against you,”**)

**FORGETTING AND MISTAKE**

***“Do not place a burden on us like the one you placed on those before us. Our Lord! Do not burden us with what we”*** clarifies forgetting and mistake. He does not use the capacity principle to excuse oppressors, nor does he use accountability to crush people who are genuinely unable. You are our Protector—anta mawlānā. Mawlā can mean guardian, master, supporter, and intimate ally. The servant belongs to Allah and seeks His protection from sin, fear, despair, and hostile forces. This allegiance does not require hatred of every person outside the faith. It places ultimate loyalty with Allah while commanding justice toward peaceful people. A believer asks Allah for help against those who disbelieve and actively oppose the truth or attack the community, in the context of the earlier verses. He must not use the prayer as permission to harm innocent people or to turn religious difference into private violence. The final request for help against disbelieving people completes the sūrah’s discussions of persecution, defence, leadership, charity, and patience. Help may come through victory, protection, truthful evidence, reconciliation, or the strength to remain faithful. It is sought within the limits of 2:190–193: no transgression, acceptance of cessation, and justice toward peaceful people who take no part in fighting. The Prophet ﷺ taught believers to seek Allah’s help and not become helpless, as Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records. The final victory is not only defeating an external opponent; it is preserving faith and justice without becoming oppressive. Sūrah al-Baqarah closes with faith, obedience, forgiveness, capacity, and supplication. It has moved through worship, family law, charity, contracts, ribā, leadership, conflict, and revelation. The final prayer gathers all these subjects. People need Allah’s guidance in public law and private weakness, in wealth and poverty, in victory and fear. The Prophet ﷺ said that the last two verses of this sūrah suffice the one who recites them at night, as al-Bukhārī and Muslim record. Their sufficiency is not a substitute for action; it is a source of protection, remembrance, and faith. (2:284 — **“To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.”**)

**THE BURDENS OF EARLIER COMMUNITIES**

***“cannot bear. Pardon us, forgive us, and have mercy on us. You are our ˹only˺ Guardian. So grant us victory over the disbelieving people.””*** closes with the burdens of earlier communities. The servant recites them, understands them, and lives by their submission and mercy. Think of capacity, duty, and mercy through a man who is asked to carry a load up a hill and is met at the bottom by the owner of the load, who measures the sack against his frame and takes a third of it out before the climb. The hill is the same hill, the load is the same load, and the man reaches the top on his own feet. Capacity, duty, and mercy confronts each soul earns and bears in one present-day reader; forgetting and mistake meets, under the demand of capacity, duty, and mercy, someone who has been carrying the fear that the commands of the Book were made for someone stronger than himself. The verse gives him a measure, a deed that belongs to its doer and three prayers that he may say out loud, and forgetting and mistake asks what he would ask for first if the three were put in his hand. Because the close of the chapter is a prayer for pardon, mercy and victory, forgetting and mistake teaches that a servant of the Lord is meant to finish his reading on his knees and not on his feet. Because capacity, duty, and mercy opens into each soul earns and bears, forgetting and mistake joins capacity, duty, and mercy to each soul earns and bears. Since forgetting and mistake develops capacity, duty, and mercy, each soul earns and bears carries the transition. The burdens of earlier communities matters, which means forgetting and mistake answers each soul earns and bears. Each soul earns and bears gives practical form to capacity, duty, and mercy; the burdens of earlier communities sets its horizon. Today, capacity, duty, and mercy shapes each soul earns and bears in present decisions. (2:185 — **“Ramaḍân is the month in which the Quran was revealed as a guide for humanity with clear”**)
