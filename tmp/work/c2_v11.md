**A COMMAND ADDRESSED TO THE WHOLE STREET**
The verse arrives as a scene with two voices in it: ***“When they are told, “Do not spread corruption in the land,” they reply, “We are only peace-makers!””***. It is worth noticing who is being addressed, because the command is not aimed at the hypocrites alone. The land in question belongs to everyone who lives there, so the order not to ruin it is the first thing a settled community owes itself. That is also why the warning takes the shape of a prohibition rather than a piece of advice, since a prohibition assumes a shared standard and an authority behind it. A neighbourhood that agrees to keep the street clear has already decided what the street is for, and a man who dumps his rubbish at night is not renegotiating that decision, only hiding from it. The same charge returns in another sūrah with the ruin attached (47:22). The answer that follows in the scene tells the reader that the order had been given many times and that the reply had been waiting for it.
**WHAT COUNTS AS CORRUPTION**
The earliest teachers filled the term with more than broken windows. Disbelief and acts of disobedience are the reading reported from Ibn ʿAbbās and from Ibn Masʿūd, and that reading keeps the verse from shrinking into a lesson about property. The damage reaches past property into a society's moral weather: the powerful taking what is not theirs, the weak priced out of fairness, a public trust sold in private, the community's secrets carried to its enemies. Another passage sets the same command beside the memory of a world already set in order, (7:56 — **“Do not spread corruption in the land”**), and a third reports what happens when the command is ignored, (30:41 — **“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people's hands have done”**). Ruin rarely announces itself in such scenes; it arrives as a series of small permissions that nobody called by its name, which is why the command had to keep being repeated.
**THE ANSWER THAT CLAIMS THE HIGH GROUND**
The second voice does not deny the charge, and that is the interesting part. The reply takes the form of a claim about identity rather than a defence of conduct, and the little word that carries the claim is a word of restriction: we are nothing but peace-makers, and nobody else in the room is included in the description. That is why the sentence quietly removes the label from the believers who had been doing the work all along. Picture a man drilling a hole in the hull of a boat while assuring the passengers that he is letting in fresh air. He is not making a mistake about his intentions; he is asking to be judged by the label he has chosen rather than by the water rising in the hold. A claim of that kind cannot be argued away, since the man has already appointed himself the judge of his own work. The scene is left waiting for a verdict from somewhere higher.
**THE HOLE IN THE REPLY**
The verdict arrives, and the reader has only to turn the page: the next verse answers the claim by naming the men in it, (2:12 — **“who are the corruptors”**). Results decide, and intentions do not, which is why the whole passage turns on what a man's work actually produces. A hospital administrator who says his only aim is the recovery of his patients is nevertheless judged by the infection rates in his wards, and no amount of good will repairs a ward that has been left unclean. The Book applies that standard to the man who claims to be a reformer while his work produces the opposite, and it does not accept his self-description as evidence. Real repair leaves marks that a neighbour can see. That is why the Book defines the good deed among people in terms of reconciliation rather than reputation, (4:114 — **“or reconciliation between people”**), and asks the community to mend what has broken between its members, (49:10 — **“so make peace between your brothers”**).
**REPAIR THAT OUTLIVES THE CLAIM**
A reader in the middle of a quarrel can use this test today, and the test is merciful because it moves the argument away from names and back to results. Two men can both call themselves peace-makers, and the Book's rule makes that claim easier to check than to say. Which of them do the weaker parties trust, whose arrival lowers the temperature of a room, and what remains standing after both have gone. Peace-making in this sense is often dull work: returning a borrowed item, keeping an appointment, refusing to repeat a story that would worsen a division, and paying a small price in pride to keep a family whole. None of that can be claimed in a speech, and all of it can be observed by anyone watching. That is why the verse puts the claim and the charge so close together, since the claim is cheap and the work that proves it is not.
