**ATTRACTIVE SPEECH IS NOT PROOF OF SINCERITY**

***“There are some ˹hypocrites˺ who impress you with their views”*** establishes attractive speech is not proof of sincerity. The verse describes a person whose words appear convincing in public. His speech about worldly life may be polished, emotional, and persuasive, and he may invoke Allah as a witness to his sincerity. Yet his inner reality is different: he is the most intense of opponents in dispute—aladd al-khiṣām. The warning is against judging character solely by eloquence, religious vocabulary, or public claims. The Qur’an does not condemn beautiful speech. A truthful person may speak well, and calling Allah to witness can be genuine when it arises from reverence. The danger is using sacred language as a shield against examination. A person who repeatedly says Allah knows my heart while cheating, intimidating, or refusing correction turns a true statement into a cover for false conduct. The phrase yuʿjibuka qawluhu fī al-ḥayāti al-dunyā can indicate speech that impresses people concerning worldly matters or speech that presents a pleasing image of this life. The individual may promise prosperity, protection, reform, or success. He knows how to identify people’s hopes and fears, and he presents himself as their advocate. The Qur’an teaches that claims must be tested against conduct. Does the speaker fulfil promises, protect the weak, admit mistakes, and accept lawful accountability. Or does his pleasant speech disappear when he gains power. The believer listens carefully but does not surrender judgement to performance. (16:91 — **“Honour Allah’s covenant when you make a pledge, and do not break your oaths after confirming them,”**) Because attractive speech is not proof of sincerity frames speech about worldly life, speech about worldly life returns attention to attractive speech is not proof of sincerity.

**SPEECH ABOUT WORLDLY LIFE**

***“regarding worldly affairs and openly call upon Allah to witness what”*** deepens speech about worldly life. Calling Allah to witness increases the seriousness of speech. If the claim is true, it should make the person more truthful and cautious. If it is false, the invocation becomes an act of impiety and manipulation. The Qur’an warns against using oaths to conceal betrayal and commands believers to speak words that are straight and responsible. A person should therefore avoid casual religious oaths. He should not say By Allah to win a dispute, pressure a buyer, silence a victim, or make an uncertain claim appear absolute. Truth does not need theatrical sanctification. Aladd al-khiṣām describes someone who is highly skilled and relentless in disputation. He may change the subject, attack the person rather than the evidence, exploit technicalities, and continue arguing after the truth is clear. The problem is not firmness in defending a right. The problem is using argument to defeat truth and preserve ego. The Prophet ﷺ warned against the most hated person to Allah being the quarrelsome disputatious one, in the authentic reports of reports collected by Muslim as well as al-Bukhārī. The hadith does not forbid asking questions or seeking justice. It condemns debate that is fueled by hostility and has no sincere desire to reach what is right. The following verse reveals the person’s conduct: when he turns away, he strives through the land to spread corruption and destroy crops and livestock. (33:70 — **“O believers! Be mindful of Allah, and say what is right”**) Since invoking allah as a witness follows attractive speech is not proof of sincerity, speech about worldly life links purpose to invoking allah as a witness.

**INVOKING ALLAH AS A WITNESS**

***“is in their hearts, yet they are your worst adversaries”*** clarifies invoking allah as a witness. The sequence exposes the gap between speech and action. A person’s true priorities appear when he gains authority or leaves the audience that admired him. This principle helps evaluate public leaders, teachers, activists, businesspeople, and even ourselves. Beautiful words should be supported by transparent records, consistent treatment of people, and willingness to accept scrutiny. A Muslim does not invent accusations merely because he dislikes a speaker, but he also does not ignore clear evidence because the speaker is charming. Manipulators often present themselves as devoted to the people while creating division, exploiting resources, or demanding personal loyalty. They may use religious language to resist accountability and call criticism an attack on faith. The Qur’an exposes this pattern so that communities will value integrity over charisma. Leaders should welcome consultation, disclose conflicts of interest, keep promises, and make decisions through justice. Followers should avoid treating a popular speaker as infallible. The best speech is that which leads people to Allah and remains consistent with mercy and truth. The verse is not only about famous hypocrites. Ordinary believers can fall into the same habit in family arguments, online discussions, and business disputes. A person may use tears, oaths, or religious quotations to appear innocent while refusing to acknowledge the harm he caused. Self-examination asks whether our speech invites truth or merely seeks victory. The cure is ikhlāṣ, sincerity, and ṣidq, truthfulness. (49:6 — **“O believers, if an evildoer brings you any news,”**) The fiercest opponent matters, which means invoking allah as a witness returns the fiercest opponent to attractive speech is not proof of sincerity.

**THE FIERCEST OPPONENT**

Because the verse ends with the hidden purpose rather than the pleasant speech, the reader is shown where the danger lies in his own circle. The man who agrees with everyone has no quarrel to settle and no position to defend, and the passage says that his friendship is the one to fear most. Today, polished campaign promises, fundraising appeals, and religious claims must be tested against conduct after the audience has gone.
