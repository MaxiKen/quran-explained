**A VOICE THAT PLEASES**

The description runs phrase by phrase from the pleasing speech to the sworn witness: ***“There are some ˹hypocrites˺”***, ***“who impress you with their views regarding worldly affairs”***, ***“and openly call upon Allah to witness what is in their hearts”***, ***“yet they are your worst adversaries”***. and the last phrase names the enmity the speech hides. The sequence builds a portrait whose last stroke redefines every stroke before it, and the redefinition is the purpose of the description. The reader who walks the phrases learns to hear the oath as evidence of the plot rather than as proof of the sincerity. The account of Ibn ʿAbbās fixes the portrait on the outward appearance and the oath that covers what the appearance hides.

**OATHS COVERING THE PLOT**

The portrait's second stroke is the oath, and the oath is placed in the description to show what the pleasing speech is for. The speaker calls Allah to witness what is in his heart, and the call is answered by the verse with a portrait of the heart instead. The reports on the occasion fix the picture on a man of eloquence and appearance whose words satisfied the room while his intentions served the opposite side. The courtesy of the speech is therefore not a small virtue with a small flaw attached; the speech is the instrument of the plot. The warning carried in the legislation of the voice keeps the measure of speech in the public square (31:19 — **“Be moderate in your pace. And lower your voice, for the ugliest of all voices is certainly the braying of donkeys.””**). Because the oath is sworn in the same breath as the flattery, the listener is trained to hear both together. The point of the portrait is practical: the one who recognizes the gait in himself can change his walk before the road ends.

**THE EXIT THAT TELLS**

The exit is the verse's evidence: when the flatterer leaves your presence, he moves through the land to corrupt it, and the contrast between the room and the road is the whole diagnosis. The application is to weigh people by the record of their exits rather than the warmth of their entrances. The community that judges by the meeting's applause will be governed by its worst adversaries. Because the exit is watched by the One who saw the oath, the exposure is a matter of record and not of suspicion. The portrait is given so that the room can stop rewarding the performance. The exit is dated by the passage itself, and the dating keeps the portrait attached to a real morning in the city.

**ADVERSARY BY EVIDENCE**

The closing phrase of the scene delivers the legal verdict: the flatterer is the worst adversary, and the verdict is evidence-based rather than personal. The self enthroned behind the speech is described in the general legislation of the desires, where the person who takes his own desire as a god is named in a question to the Prophet (45:23 — **“Have you seen ˹O Prophet˺ those who have taken their own desires as their god?”**). Because the desire is the deity behind the pleasing words, the flattery is worship directed at the wrong altar. The believer who hears the verse is equipped with a filter for every assembly he enters. The speech that pleases the room and corrupts the land has now been named, dated, and priced.

The portrait of the worldly man is drawn with the vocabulary of the market, and the inherited commentary even keeps the old market slang for the types of the argument: the one who trades in praise, the one who debates in the lanes, and the one who boasts at the assembly. Each type is a dealer of a different commodity, and the commodities trade well in the season of the world. Because the portrait is sociological rather than insulting, it survives its century and arrives at the next bazaar intact. The scholars of the language note that the root of the insult is the ignorance of the season, and the season here is the short season of the trading floor. Hearing the tale at the doorstep and repeating it in the assembly is the ancient pipeline of the reputation economy. The one who is ruined by the sound of a tale should examine what he listens with. The portrait teaches that the ear can be a market too. The lanes of the argument are older than the city that hosts them, and the types return at every opening of the market.
