**THE CRITICAL END OF THE WAITING PERIOD**

***“When you divorce women and they have ˹almost˺ reached the end of their waiting period, either retain them honourably or let them”*** establishes the critical end of the waiting period. The verse addresses the moment when a revocable divorce’s waiting period is nearly complete. The husband must choose: return to the marriage honourably or allow the separation to finish honourably. This time can be emotionally vulnerable because a return may still be legally possible, but the relationship is also close to ending. Allah prevents the husband from using the final days to create uncertainty and pain. A return—imsāk—must be based on genuine reconciliation, restored rights, and a willingness to live with kindness. Release—tasrīḥ—means allowing the woman to leave without harassment, withholding property, slander, or manipulation. Neither option permits keeping a spouse suspended while refusing the responsibilities of marriage. The prohibition do not retain them to harm them addresses a practice in which a husband would take a wife back shortly before the waiting period ended, then divorce her again, repeatedly extending the ordeal. This was a form of control. The Qur’an calls it transgression because it uses a lawful mechanism for an unlawful purpose. Harm can take many forms: threatening the wife, preventing her remarriage, withholding maintenance, exposing private matters, manipulating children, refusing documentation, or using financial dependence to force obedience. The general Islamic legal principle is that harm must not be inflicted or returned. (2:228 — **“Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can re-marry˺.”**) Because the critical end of the waiting period frames retaining to cause harm, retaining to cause harm returns attention to the critical end of the waiting period. A transmitted explanation from Ibn ʿAbbās makes kind retention include companionship and restored rights, while kind release ends the waiting period without calculated injury.

**RETAINING TO CAUSE HARM**

***“go honourably. But do not retain them ˹only˺ to harm them ˹or˺ to take advantage ˹of them˺. Whoever does that surely”*** deepens retaining to cause harm. A person cannot defend cruelty by saying that a technical form was available to him. Whoever does that has wronged himself. The oppressor may think he has power over the woman, but he is actually placing himself under divine accountability. Every act of humiliation, every withheld right, and every calculated delay is recorded. The person also damages his own soul by turning marriage into a tool of revenge. The phrase reminds believers that injustice against another eventually returns to the wrongdoer. A person may escape family criticism or manipulate a court, but he cannot escape Allah’s knowledge. Repentance requires ending the harm, restoring rights, and allowing the former spouse to live safely. Taking Allah’s revelations as a joke—huzuwan—includes treating legal commands as toys that can be activated and deactivated for amusement or control. It includes mocking the limits, using pious words to hide abuse, and invoking divorce repeatedly without seriousness. Revelation is not a collection of loopholes for emotional games. The command does not forbid light-heartedness in ordinary life. It forbids turning sacred obligations into a means of harm. A person who is unsure about a divorce should pause and consult qualified scholarship before speaking, rather than experimenting with words whose legal consequences may be irreversible. (65:1 — **“O Prophet! ˹Instruct the believers:˺ When you ˹intend to˺ divorce women,”**) Since wronging oneself follows the critical end of the waiting period, retaining to cause harm links purpose to wronging oneself.

**WRONGING ONESELF**

***“wrongs his own soul. Do not take Allah’s revelations lightly. Remember Allah’s favours upon you as well as the Book and”*** clarifies wronging oneself. Allah reminds the believers of His favour and of the Book and wisdom He sent down. The favour includes guidance that replaced tribal customs with clear rights and limits. The Book is the Qur’an, and wisdom is explained by many scholars as the prophetic Sunnah and sound judgement. Together they teach how to preserve families while protecting people from abuse. Remembering this favour means studying the law, applying it with mercy, and recognizing that a spouse is a servant of Allah before being a person under one’s authority. The Prophet ﷺ was the best model of family conduct: he listened, consulted, forgave, and never used revelation to excuse cruelty. The command to be mindful of Allah is directed especially to the person who has procedural power. Taqwā restrains a husband, judge, guardian, family elder, and community leader from using a legal role for private revenge. It also protects the wife from responding to harm with false accusation or unlawful retaliation. Divorce should be documented, witnessed, and handled with qualified advice. Modern legal protections can help enforce maintenance, custody, and property rights. Using civil law to preserve justice is not opposed to religion; it can be part of fulfilling the Qur’anic trust. Think of the critical end of the waiting period through a man who parks his car across a neighbour's gate for the night and tells himself he is only keeping the space. (2:223 — **“Your wives are like farmland for you, so approach them ˹consensually˺ as you please. And”**) Revelation is not a tool of harm matters, which means wronging oneself returns revelation is not a tool of harm to the critical end of the waiting period.

**REVELATION IS NOT A TOOL OF HARM**

***“wisdom He has sent down for your guidance. Be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of all things”*** closes with revelation is not a tool of harm. The gate opens once in the morning, the neighbour's day begins late, and the space he kept was never the one he needed. The critical end of the waiting period confronts retaining to cause harm today in anyone who keeps his wife in the house so that no other man may have her, and who calls that keeping a family. The verse gives him two honourable doors and forbids the third way, and the critical end of the waiting period asks what he is holding and why. Because the close of the verse speaks of the favours and the Book, the critical end of the waiting period teaches that a marriage is one of the places where a person shows what he has done with what he was given. The two roads of the verse are set out in the earlier ruling. And the rights of the woman in a house are guarded again in the chapter of the women. (4:35 — **“If you anticipate a split between them, appoint a mediator from his family and another from hers.”**) Retaining to cause harm makes the critical end of the waiting period practical; wronging oneself therefore matters.
