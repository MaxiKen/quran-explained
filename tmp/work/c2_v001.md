**THE LETTERS THAT OPEN TWENTY-NINE SŪRAHS**

The sūrah begins with three letters: ***“Alif-Lãm-Mĩm”***. They are read by their names and not by their sounds, so the reciter says alif, lām, mīm, and he does not join them into a word. Twenty-nine sūrahs of the Book open in this way, which means that a reader meets the device again and again and cannot dismiss it as an accident of one page. Some of these openings are single letters and some are longer combinations, and the letters are drawn from the ordinary alphabet of the Arabic tongue. The reader who has just finished asking God for guidance in the opening sūrah arrives here and is given a sentence he cannot work out for himself, and that is the first lesson of the chapter: the Book he is about to read contains matters that are settled by receiving them, not by solving them.

**WHAT HAS BEEN HANDED DOWN ABOUT THEM**

What the letters mean has been discussed since the earliest generations, and the answers that have come down differ. Ibn ʿAbbās is credited with one reading in which the three letters stand for the three names that carry the message: alif for Allah, lām for the angel Gabriel who brought the revelation, and mīm for Muhammad who received it. He is also credited with other readings, one making the letters stand for God's blessings, kindness and dominion, and another making them the beginnings of three of God's names. The weight of the early community fell on leaving the meaning to God, and al-Shaʿbī and a group with him took that position: the letters are among the verses whose meaning is left to God, and the community believes them as they were sent down. That reading was held to be the secret of the Book, and a saying attributed to Abū Bakr puts it in a form worth remembering: every book has a secret, and the secret of this Book lies in the openings of its sūrahs.

**THE SAME OPENING IN OTHER SŪRAHS**

The Book uses the same opening elsewhere, and the combinations there help the reader see the pattern without pretending to know more than he has been given. Another sūrah of the same kind begins with the identical three letters: (3:1 — **“Alif-Lãm-Mĩm”**). A third adds a fourth letter to them: (7:1 — **“Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ṣãd”**). The Book also announces, at the head of one of these sūrahs, that the revelation is from the Lord of all worlds and that no doubt belongs to it, which tells the reader what to expect after the letters are spoken. What the openings do is to catch the attention of a people who were proud of their language, and to remind them that the very sounds they use for poetry and trade stand at the door of a text they cannot match. The letters are common; the sentence built from them is not.

**WHAT THE READER CAN AND CANNOT SETTLE**

Think of a sealed envelope handed to a man who can read every word on the outside but cannot open it. He knows who sent it, because the seal says so, and he keeps it with the respect its sender deserves. The verse places the reader in that position on the first line of the chapter, and the position is not a humiliation but a discipline. These days a person can look up any fact within seconds and come to expect an answer for every question he meets, and the opening letters quietly deny him that expectation where God has chosen to withhold the answer. The reader is being taught that his knowledge has a boundary, and that the boundary is part of the message rather than a failure of it.

**THE CHALLENGE THAT FOLLOWS THE LETTERS**

The letters also stand at the head of a text that dares its readers to match it, and the sūrah makes the challenge a few lines later. The Book invites anyone who doubts the revelation to bring a single sūrah of its kind: (2:23 — **“then produce a sûrah like it”**), and it adds that the helpers they call on besides God will not make the difference. A reader who has followed the argument sees why the letters come first, because the challenge is made in the material the Arabs handled every day. They knew every letter; they had never produced a sentence like the one the letters introduce. The claim of the sūrah is not that the vocabulary is beyond them but that the arrangement is, and the argument holds today in a world that has more readers of Arabic than ever and still no rival to this text.
