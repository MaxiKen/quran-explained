**THE SAME ADDRESS GIVEN A SECOND TIME**

The favours are recounted again, and the wording is almost identical to the address that opened the earlier passage: ***“O Children of Israel!”***, ***“Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you”***, ***“and how I honoured you above the others”***. The Book repeats an address on purpose, and a reader who notices the repetition learns something about how the sūrah is built. Between the two addresses the chapter has placed a block of warnings, an account of the covenant, and the reminder about the Day when no help will be available. The repetition is a hinge: the same community is brought back to the same starting point with more information than it had the first time. The phrase about honouring them above the others has been read in more than one way, and the reading that fits the sūrah best is the one the Book states elsewhere in the same terms: (44:32 — **“And indeed, We chose the Israelites knowingly above the others”**). The choosing is explained in other passages as a matter of prophets, scripture, and a period of leadership, and the reader is not being asked to conclude that the honour was permanent or unconditional, since the very next verses list what happened when the terms were neglected. The verse names a favour that was given, and it does so in a community's own history, which is why the next sentence will set out the terms that came with it.

**GIFTS NAMED BEFORE ANYTHING IS ASKED**

Every time the Book addresses this community, the favours come first. The pattern is deliberate, and it is the pattern of the whole sūrah: the gift is named, and then the obligation that fits the gift is named. A reader who skips to the commands will find them arbitrary, and a reader who reads the reminder first will find them proportionate. The Book is asking a people to remember what they were carried through, and it treats that memory as the motive strong enough to make obedience reasonable. The demand never arrives without a history attached to it. The Book keeps this order even where the tone hardens later in the chapter, which is a sign that the order is not a rhetorical device. Moses addresses his people in the same way when he reminds them of what they received, and the reminder there is also followed by the command: (5:20 — **“And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Remember Allah's favours upon you”**). The pattern holds across the sūrahs, and a reader who learns to expect it can read any of these passages with a better sense of what the Book thinks a person needs before he is asked for anything.

**GRATITUDE AS THE ENGINE OF OBEDIENCE**

For a reader from another community, the plainest lesson in the sūrah is this: obligation follows memory, and the memory has to be kept alive deliberately. Every person carries a list of favours he did not arrange, and the list is usually invisible because it is the ground he stands on. The verse does not ask the children of Israel to feel grateful; it asks them to remember, which is a discipline rather than a sentiment, and the remembering is done by naming the favours rather than by waiting for an emotion. The exercise is to write the list of favours in his own case and then read the obligations in this chapter against it, one at a time. The Book is asking, in every one of these addresses, whether the response matches the gift. A reader who finds that his obligations look small against his list has understood the point the sūrah keeps making, and he has understood it without needing to be a member of the community that received the original address.
