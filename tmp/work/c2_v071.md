**NEITHER YOKED NOR TURNING THE SOIL**

The last description removes the working class of cattle in one stroke: ***“He replied, “Allah says, ‘It should have been used neither to till the soil”***, ***“nor water the fields”***. The first exclusion is the animal broken to the plough, the kind that churns the earth with its hooves season after season until the work is written into its body. The second is the draught animal kept for carrying water to the crops, the one the language marks with its own designation because watering was its trade. Ibn ʿAbbās gives the pair in its plainest terms: the cow is unyoked and unfettered, not subjugated for the ploughing of the soil, nor for carrying water to irrigate it. The closing seal then disqualifies her by appearance rather than by employment. She is whole, free of any fault and of the effects of toil, with no mark of a colour other than her own on her, so that neither work nor wear may have touched her. Their own question had asked which occupation would disqualify the animal. The answer disqualified both occupations at once and added a purity of hide that no amount of bargaining had put on the list. The specification had escaped their control entirely.

**THE VOWELS THAT SPLIT ONE SHAPE IN TWO**

The first adjective of the description is a small monument of the language, because one written shape divides by its vowels into two different lives. In the reading of the animal the designation means the tamed one, the creature whose wildness has been taken by riding or by labour. Spoken with a different vowelling the same shape describes the abject person whose dignity is gone. The verse takes the first sense entirely, and the narrated gloss on the line makes the causation explicit. The cow is called untamed because work never tamed her. She never churned the soil with her hooves, and no water-course was laid on her for the crops. The grammar of the negation then drew the grammarians into a clean division, and the division is worth recording as evidence of how carefully the line was read. The majority, including al-Akhfash in his insistence on the point, take the negated adjective as an attribute standing to the cow, her own described state. Abu Abd al-Rahman al-Sulami is transmitted with the accusative instead, on the reading that makes the negation its own clause with the predicate left understood. The two readings agree on the sense while dividing on the casing. In other words, what turns on the dispute is small in doctrine and large in attention. The cow's freedom from labour was treated by the readers of the Book as a fact stated in the cow's own description, not as an incidental clause.

**THE MOMENT THE OBJECTIONS RAN OUT**

The people's reply marks the first time their speech carries no objection: ***“wholesome and without blemish.’” They said, “Now you have come with the truth.””***. The admission is glossed from two directions, and both keep the sense of completion. Ibn ʿAbbās renders their words as now we know the cow's right description. The short sense circulating in the glosses is that the Prophet has now explained the matter clearly. The search that follows is one of the tenderest scenes in the episode. The narrations place the animal with a boy known for devotion to his mother, so that the cow marked out by divine description was kept by the hands of filial duty. The two details arrive in the same frame as though the story wanted the reader to see the character of the household that still owned a whole cow. The purchase is reported in two forms that agree on the weight of the price. One transmission has them buying her for her weight in gold. Another has the owner demanding the skin's fill of gold and receiving it. The difference between a weight and a measure does not touch the lesson, since both say the same thing about what the catalogue has done to the market.

**THE PRICE OF THE LAST QUESTION**

The verse ends on the tone of the deed rather than its fact: ***“Yet they still slaughtered it hesitantly”***. The hesitation is glossed twice from one authority, and the two glosses are really one cause seen from two sides. Ibn ʿAbbās takes the hesitation as their first impulse, the drawing back before the act. As it is also said, the hesitation was the price, because the cow had become hugely expensive. The arithmetic of the whole episode is now visible. An open command once carried no price tag at all. The catalogue their questions assembled turned a slaughter into a purchase, and the dearest animal in the market is what obstinacy left in their hands. The proverb against that exact bargain is spoken in this surah by Moses himself: (2:61 — **““Do you exchange what is better for what is worse? ˹You can˺ go down to any village”**). His own line in the earlier exchange keeps its shape against the outcome, (2:67 — **“Moses responded, “I seek refuge in Allah from acting foolishly!””**) — the man who refused mockery now waits while the mockers haggle over their own penance. The instrument of the answer then does its work in a single gesture: (2:73 — **““Strike the dead body with a piece of the cow.” This is how ˹easily˺ Allah brings the dead to life,”**). The extravagance the people feared to spend turns out to have been the solvent of the crime they could not solve. For the reader today the sequence keeps its ordinary shape. Hesitation at the altar of a plain duty is the receipt for the bargaining that came before it, and the price is quoted in the currency the bargaining itself invented.
