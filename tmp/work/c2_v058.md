**A CITY NAMED BY ITS MEANING RATHER THAN ITS STREETS**

The order is brief and its object is left unlabelled: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We said, “Enter this city”***. The wording does not print the city’s name on the page, and the transmitted readings divide on which town stands behind it. Qatādah, al-Suddī and al-Rabīʿ b. Anas take it as the Holy House of Jerusalem, while Ibn ʿAbbās names Jericho; al-Ḍaḥḥāk and Muqātil name other towns of the same region. The parallel account settles the family of readings by supplying what this sentence withholds: (7:161 — **“And ˹remember˺ when it was said to them, “Enter this city ˹of Jerusalem˺ and eat from wherever you please. Say, ‘Absolve us,’”**). Since the same command reappears with the place identified, the reading followed here is Jerusalem, and the alternative that names Jericho stands as a transmitted report rather than a competing certainty. The command also closes the journey an earlier order began: (5:21 — **“O my people! Enter the Holy Land which Allah has destined for you ˹to enter˺. And do not turn back”**). What was refused at the border of the land is now permitted at its centre, because the wandering had run its appointed length and a generation raised in the desert had come of age to enter.

**EATING FREELY WHERE THE YEARS OF WANT ENDED**

Provision inside the city is promised without the measuring of the wilderness: ***“and eat freely from wherever you please”***. The expression carries the sense of ease and expansion after straitness, and it answers the years in which every meal had been limited to that day’s need. The same formula was spoken to Adam in the garden before any hardship entered human life: (2:35 — **“We cautioned, “O Adam! Live with your wife in Paradise and eat as freely as you please,”**). In both places the eating is permission and gift, and in both it stands beside a boundary, since a gift without a limit is not the test that either the garden or the city was made to be. That is why abundance here is paired at once with an act of humility. Bread without command would have suited a people already trained to gather only what the day required; the entry is ordered so that remembrance of the Giver enters along with the gift. The effect of the pairing is a mercy that does not dissolve obligation. Food and land arrive together, and both are held on terms.

**THE PROMISE SPOKEN IN THE SAME BREATH AS THE COMMAND**

The manner of the entry is fixed as carefully as its place: ***“enter the gate with humility, saying, ‘Absolve us.’ We will forgive your sins”***. What the people were told to say is rendered *"Absolve us"* — a single expression carrying the sense of unburdening and pardon. Ibn ʿAbbās glosses it as *"forgive our sins"*, and another early gloss takes it as an acknowledgement that there is no god but God. The posture is named in a word whose reach covers prostration while its use here also covers deep bowing, so the reports of the entry describe people entering bent at the door rather than striding through it. The Prophet relates that God said to the Children of Israel, *"Enter the gate prostrating and say, 'Absolve us,' and We will forgive your sins"* (Muslim). Pardon is therefore offered to a people whose refusal at the border had cost them forty years, and it is offered in the same breath as the command. The reason forgiveness is tied to speech here is that the commanded word is itself an admission of fault, and an admission put on the lips is the first act of the returning heart.

The closing promise divides its gifts by their receivers: ***“and multiply the reward”*** ***“for the good-doers.””***. The increase belongs to those whose conduct answers the gift, and the transmitted gloss explains it as God’s adding to their good deeds. The clause therefore does more than repeat the pardon. Pardon closes the old account because the fault has been admitted; increase belongs to the one who lives afterwards in a way that answers what he has received. A preserved reading recites the pardon in the passive voice, so that the announcement stands settled on its own terms as *"they will be forgiven"*. What turns on the division is plain: entry is open to the whole people, pardon is offered to everyone who says the word, and increase is reserved for the one who then does good. The verse leaves three stations behind it — the gift received, the fault admitted, and the life rebuilt on both.
