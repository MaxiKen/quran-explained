**A CITY, A GATE, AND ONE SENTENCE TO SAY**

The history continues with an instruction that combines a place, a manner, and a formula: ***“And ˹remember˺ when We said, “Enter this city”***, ***“and eat freely from wherever you please”***, ***“enter the gate with humility, saying, ‘Absolve us.' We will forgive your sins”***, ***“and multiply the reward”***, ***“for the good-doers.””***. The command has three parts and each of them is a test in miniature. A place was given to them, the food in it was declared free, and the manner of entry was specified. What the verse is describing is not a military conquest but an admission, and the terms of admission are humility, a spoken request for pardon, and the promise of a reward for those who do good. The Book returns to the same scene elsewhere and the wording there is close enough to show that the two accounts describe one event: the same city, the same freedom to eat, and the same requirement to lower themselves as they pass through the gate. The comparison matters because the sūrah is teaching the reader to recognise a pattern in the way God deals with people. A gift is placed in front of them, a small act of humility is asked for, and the response to the two together determines what happens next. Everything in the passage is arranged so that the test is easy to pass and impossible to mistake.

**THE MANNER MATTERED MORE THAN THE CITY**

The manner of entry is the part of the command that carries the lesson, since the city and the food were gifts and the bowing was the response. A people who had been rescued from slavery and fed in the desert were now being given a home, and the instruction was to walk in asking for pardon rather than as conquerors. The Book is drawing a line under the whole story: everything they had was received, and the manner of the receiving was expected to show it. The same principle appears in the sūrah's description of the believers, who give from what they have been given and are certain about the return. A reader should also notice the wording of the request. The sentence they were told to say is short, and it asks for pardon rather than for the city, the food, or the victory. The Book puts the request for forgiveness at the head of the entry because the thing being repaired is the relationship, and the rest follows from it. The reward mentioned in the closing words of the promise is then described as multiplication, which means the arrangement does not operate on a strict exchange rate. Both halves of the sentence come from the same generosity.

**ENTERING WITH THE RIGHT WORDS**

How a person receives what he has been given is the question this line puts to him. A promotion, a house, a recovery, a new year: each of them can be entered either as a right one has earned or as something received from the one who arranges matters, and the difference shows up immediately in how the person speaks. The Book places the two possible postures side by side in the story, since the next verse records a community that changed the words it had been given. The instruction in this verse was small, which is exactly what makes the following failure so instructive. A reader can take the verse as a template for the things he enters this week: a job, a room, a conversation, a meal. The formula is not complicated, and the verse gives it in plain language. One sentence acknowledging who gave the thing, and then the use of it as a gift rather than as a possession. A person who does that at one threshold has kept the command of this verse, and he has done it in a manner the Book promises to reward.
