**A DEMAND TO SEE WHAT CANNOT BE SEEN**

The next scene opens with a demand that a reader has heard before in other mouths: ***“And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses!”***, ***“We will never believe you”***, ***“until we see Allah with our own eyes,””***, and then ***“so a thunderbolt struck you”***, ***“while you were looking on”***. The request is not for evidence; the sea had parted and the mountain had shaken in front of these people. What they demanded was a change in the terms of the relationship, since seeing God with their own eyes would have removed the element of trust that the whole arrangement rests on. The verse reports the answer without a word of commentary, and the answer is that the demand was met with a stroke that left them watching their own end.

The Book tells the same story in another sūrah with more of the exchange preserved, and the request there is repeated in a form that makes its nature plain: the people ask Moses to bring down a written revelation, and the Book records the escalation in their demands. The Qur'an treats this appetite as a recurring disease rather than a single event, and it names the condition in a passage about the same community: (4:153 — **“The People of the Book demand that you ˹O Prophet˺ bring down”**), with the rest of the verse listing the requests that followed. The reader is being shown what happens when a person decides that belief should be made easy on his own terms.

**A DEMAND THAT CONTRADICTS TRUST**

The request in this verse is often read as an excess of honesty, and the Book is clear that it is the opposite. A person who insists on seeing before he believes has already decided that the evidence available to him is not enough, and in this case the available evidence had been overwhelming. What the demand really asks for is a God who is available on command and a relationship without the risk of trust. That is a different religion from the one being offered, and it is the religion the sūrah has been describing since it named the people who say they believe while keeping their options open. The Book elsewhere states the principle that this scene illustrates, and it puts the sight of God at a distance no living person can cross: no eye can reach him in this world, and the request to try has been refused to everyone who asked. The verse does not explain this, and it does not need to. A reader who has understood that trust is the whole content of the relationship will see at once why the demand in the sentence had to be answered with a bolt rather than a demonstration.

**WANTING PROOF ON ONE'S OWN TERMS**

A reader can find this demand in his own week easily enough, because it takes the form of conditions. A person says that he would commit if he were shown a sign, that he would change if the evidence were stronger, that he would pray if he felt something. The Book has already given the evidence it intends to give, and the demand in this verse is the same demand in modern clothes. The lesson of the scene is not that questions are forbidden, since the sūrah itself has been answering them for fifty verses. The lesson is that a condition attached to obedience is a refusal in disguise. The practical step is to name one condition that is standing between a person and a decision, and to state honestly whether it is a genuine question or a way of postponing something already known. The people in this verse had an answer available to them all along, and the Book records that they were looking on when it arrived. A reader who notices the same posture in himself has learned the lesson of the passage without having to receive the answer in their form.
