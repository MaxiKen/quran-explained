**ASSENT MADE DEPENDENT ON A PRIVATE CONDITION**

The remembered exchange begins with the people’s own words: ***“And ˹remember˺ when you said”***. They address the messenger, ***“O Moses!”***, and declare, ***“We will never believe you”***. Ibn Zayd places this refusal after Moses brought the Tablets and presented their commands as God’s revelation. Al-Suddī relates it to the company who went with Moses to the appointment; another passage records, (7:155 — **“Moses chose seventy men from among his people for Our appointment”**). The precise relation between the accounts need not be forced into this verse, which does not itself number the speakers. Here the demand is read as a challenge to the revelation Moses brought. They are not simply people who have never heard of his mission or seen a sign. They have been confronted with the grounds for trusting him, yet refuse the message unless a further condition is met. Because their words concern what they will accept from him, the difficulty lies in their response to a known messenger, not merely in a lack of information about an unknown claim. The next words expose the condition by which they seek to withhold that acceptance.

They require it to wait ***“until we see Allah with our own eyes”***. Ibn ʿAbbās explains the demand as open, direct sight. They are not asking only for inward understanding or for a dream. The addition about their own eyes makes the intended kind of seeing plain. Their refusal is also bounded by that demand: the force of never believing here is until the claimed condition has been fulfilled, not a separate statement about every possible future. The point is that they make their acceptance of God’s message depend on a form of encounter they can demand for themselves. Yet the chapter has already praised those (2:3 — **“who believe in the unseen”**). Belief in the unseen is not belief with no grounds; Moses had brought signs and revelation. It means that the truth disclosed by God is not confined to what every hearer has personally seen. The speakers reject that relation between messenger and hearer. They seek to make their own sight a condition above the evidence already granted, as though the Lord must be made visible before His word can bind them.

**LONGING TO SEE IS NOT THE SAME AS WITHHOLDING BELIEF**

The fault is not that every desire for greater assurance is wrong. Moses himself asks, (7:143 — **“My Lord! Reveal Yourself to me so I may see You”**). His request arises within faith and longing, not a threat to reject his Lord unless the request is granted. Abraham’s question about the raising of the dead is likewise joined to the affirmation, (2:260 — **“Yes I do, but just so my heart can be reassured”**). The difference concerns the stance from which the request is made. Since the present speakers use their demand to withhold belief from the messenger, their words are not treated as that faithful search for reassurance. Nor does their punishment establish that God can never be seen in the life to come. The blessed are described as (75:23 — **“looking at their Lord”**). That gift belongs to God’s promise and permission, not to a human power to summon it on demand. The account therefore neither condemns honest inquiry as such nor makes sight the only valid ground of faith. It exposes a refusal that tries to dictate the terms on which a known revelation will be accepted.

**THE SIGHT THEY RECEIVED SHOWED THEIR OWN POWERLESSNESS**

The consequence is ***“so a thunderbolt struck you”***. The stroke is overwhelming and fatal. Al-Suddī describes fire, while the report from al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas emphasizes a sound that struck them down. These descriptions bring out the terror of the event; the decisive fact for its outcome is made plain by the following verse, (2:56 — **“Then We brought you back to life after your death”**). Because that statement speaks of death and restoration to life, the event here cannot be reduced to a passing scare. The term for this overwhelming stroke can elsewhere describe a swoon, but the surrounding account determines the sense in this case. They had demanded control over the form of evidence they would receive, yet could not secure even the continuance of their own lives. The power belongs to the Lord whom they sought to place under a condition. The punishment is not the proof that Moses had failed. It follows the refusal of those who challenged him, and leaves him pleading rather than claiming a power to reverse it himself.

The words ***“while you were looking on”*** make the warning immediate. ʿUrwah ibn Ruwaym explains that members of the group saw others being struck. The sight belongs to the event as it overtook them; it does not require the claim that a dead person continued watching after death. They were confronted with what they could neither prevent nor turn aside. Another account makes the link to their offence explicit: (4:153 — **“So a thunderbolt struck them for their wrongdoing”**). The final clause also recalls the earlier rescue, when the Israelites watched Pharaoh’s people drown. Sight had then confirmed deliverance; now those making this demand witnessed judgement among themselves. Having seen an enemy’s fall did not place them outside the duty to trust and obey. Their past privilege could not convert this refusal into a right. Yet the sequence does not end with the thunderbolt. Moses appeals to his Lord for the people, and the next reminder concerns life restored. The severe consequence must be read with that ensuing mercy, not turned into a claim that every path back was closed.
