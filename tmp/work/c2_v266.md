**A TERRIFYING PICTURE OF LOST PROVISION**

***“Would any of you wish to have a garden with palm trees, grapevines, and”*** establishes a terrifying picture of lost provision. This verse presents a vivid parable about a person who possesses a flourishing garden and depends upon it in old age while children are weak and unable to support him. A fiery whirlwind destroys it at the moment he needs it most. The image follows the discussion of charity and the loss of reward through showing off, reminders, and injury. A deed may appear valuable for years, then be destroyed by a final act of hypocrisy or harm. The parable is emotionally powerful because the garden represents security, family, livelihood, and accumulated effort. Its destruction is not a minor reduction in comfort. It leaves the elderly person and dependent children exposed. The Qur’an asks whether anyone would wish for such a fate, inviting the heart to recognize the horror of losing the Hereafter’s harvest after believing it was secure. Old age and weak children represent vulnerability. A person may need the fruits of his past work when his physical strength declines and his dependants cannot replace him. In the same way, a believer needs the reward of his deeds when he stands before Allah unable to return and act again. The image also teaches responsibility toward parents and children. Wealth should not be accumulated only for personal prestige while dependants are left insecure. Charity and family support must be balanced. A person who gives publicly to impress people but neglects those under his care has misunderstood both generosity and worship. (2:261 — **“The example of those who spend their wealth in the cause of Allah is that of a”**) A transmitted account from Ibn ʿAbbās describes the vulnerable owner as elderly with dependants unable to support themselves when the burning wind destroys the garden.

**AGE AND DEPENDANTS**

***“all kinds of fruits with rivers flowing underneath and as they grow very old”*** deepens age and dependants. The destructive wind contains fire—iʿṣār fīhi nār—a sudden force that combines movement and burning. It evokes the way a hidden spiritual disease can destroy the reward of an outwardly impressive life. The person may have cultivated charity, prayer, or service, but the fire of showing off, arrogance, and injury can consume the harvest. The image does not mean that every mistake erases every good deed. It warns about deliberate corruption and about the danger of assuming that a deed is safe merely because it was large or long ago. Sincere repentance can repair what was damaged while life remains. The garden contains every kind of fruit, suggesting abundant opportunity. A person may have been given wealth, health, education, time, influence, and access to many forms of good. These are gardens planted by Allah. The question is how they are cultivated and whether the harvest is protected from destructive conduct. A wealthy person can use assets to support family, create employment, fund education, relieve debt, and care for the poor. If he uses them for vanity, corruption, or harm, the apparent garden becomes a source of loss. The fruit is judged by its moral use. The whirlwind is sudden. A person cannot assume that he will have years to correct his intention or repair a wrong. Death, illness, economic collapse, public scandal, and the loss of opportunity can arrive without warning. The wise servant reviews his deeds regularly and does not postpone repentance. (2:180 — **“It is prescribed that when death approaches any of you—if they leave something of value—a will should”**)

**THE FIERY WHIRLWIND**

***“with dependent children, a fiery whirlwind hits the garden, burning it all up? This”*** clarifies the fiery whirlwind. This urgency should not produce panic or obsessive fear. It should produce practical habits: hidden charity, honest apologies, clear wills, lawful earnings, and consistent worship. The believer asks Allah to accept deeds and to protect them from corruption. Allah makes the revelation clear so that people may reflect—yatafakkarūn. Reflection connects the image with personal life. What garden has Allah entrusted to me. Who depends upon its fruit. What kind of fire could destroy it. Is my charity nourishing people or feeding my ego. The mind that reflects does not stop at the image. It changes behaviour. It protects the rights of recipients, controls speech, and seeks a sincere ending. The parable also demands communal compassion. Elderly people, orphans, single parents, and children should not be left at the mercy of sudden loss. Zakah, social support, fair inheritance, and responsible institutions can preserve the garden of a family when its provider is gone. The person who helps protect another family’s provision may find that Allah protects his own harvest. Charity is a network of mercy, not only a private transaction. Think of a terrifying picture of lost provision through a man who plants a grove in the year his first child is born and waters it every dry season until his hair is white. The fire comes on the evening after his youngest son's wedding, and the only thing that survives is the line of the ditch he dug with his own hands.
