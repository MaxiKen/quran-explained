**THE VERDICT THE VERSE RETURNS**

The answer to the claim comes immediately, and it is delivered without argument: ***“Indeed, it is they”***, ***“who are the corruptors”***, ***“but they fail to perceive it”***. The verse overturns the label and hands it back to the people who used it about themselves in the opposite direction. It does not say that they are mistaken about one matter or that they need better information. It says that the persons answering this way are the very corrupters, and that the reason they cannot see it is the condition described two verses earlier. The sentence is short because the diagnosis is already complete. Notice where the verse places the blindness. It is not in the intellect, as though these people lacked the ability to reason. It is in perception, which means the facts are available and only the instrument is faulty, and a faulty instrument cannot repair itself by looking harder. That is why more argument will not help them, and why the Book does not bother to argue with the claim it has just quoted. It states the truth and moves on, leaving the reader with a warning about the sort of condition that can hide a person's own condition from him. Nothing is more dangerous than a fault that removes the ability to detect itself, and that is the reason this verse refuses to argue the point at length.

**WHAT BLINDNESS OF THIS KIND LOOKS LIKE**

The blindness is not a drama; it is a habit of skipping. A man in this state does not deny that his words have consequences, he simply never traces them. Ask him about the last quarrel and he will tell you what was said to him and by whom, and the account will be accurate as far as it goes. The part that goes missing is the sentence he himself added and the timing he chose. The verse's phrase, ***“but they fail to perceive it”***, describes exactly that hole in the record, and it explains why the sūrah keeps returning to the theme of seeing rather than merely hearing. The Book reports the same condition at the end of a long argument about signs in an earlier sūrah, and the wording there is a diagnosis as well as a warning: (2:175 — **“They are the ones who trade guidance for misguidance”**). Laid beside this verse, the two statements describe one transaction. A person gives away the light that would have shown him what he was doing, and receives in exchange a confidence that prevents him from noticing the loss. The Qur'an almost always ties the loss of sight to a prior choice, which is why the blindness here is not a misfortune but a consequence.

**HOW THE MIRROR TURNS TOWARD THE READER**

The reader is invited to ask a direct question: what do the people closest to me think my words have done in the last year? Not what he intended, and not what he announced, but what those who live with the effects believe happened. The question is easy to avoid and hard to answer, which is exactly why the Book puts it in front of a person before the account is closed. A man who can ask it out loud and listen to the answer without defending himself has the use of his sight, and the use of that faculty is the whole point of the verse. The remaining error is to apply the verse to someone else and feel settled. The sūrah gives the reader no permission for that, and the reason is that every reader carries a piece of this condition. Today the honest form of the exercise is small: choose one relationship, ask the person in it what his words have been doing, and write the answer down without editing it. The ability to be corrected is the one thing the corrupters in this verse have lost, and it can be kept only by using it while it is still uncomfortable.
