**GOD OVERTURNS THE NAME THEY GAVE THEMSELVES**

The reply is brief, firm, and framed to wake the hearer: ***“Indeed, it is they”***. The speakers in the last verse had shut the debate with a grand claim that they alone set things right. God now rejects both the claim and the scale by which they made it. The opening calls for close notice, while the pointed pronoun sends the charge back to its true owners. The believers who warn them are not the source of the ruin. The people who hide denial, break trust, and aid falsehood are. Their view of themselves cannot change God’s verdict. This matters because a good title is one of the first shields a bad course seeks. If the actor can fix the name, he hopes to silence all thought about the deed. Revelation tears away that shield. Good and evil are not made equal by a crowd’s praise, a leader’s claim, or a fine aim stated after the fact. The same rule appears in a broad command: (5:100 — **“Good and evil are not equal, though you may be dazzled by the abundance of evil.”**). Moral fact stands even when the eye is drawn elsewhere. The words here restore that fact before they explain why the guilty group misses it.

**THE CORRUPTORS ARE KNOWN BY WHAT THEIR COURSE BREAKS**

The verdict names those ***“who are the corruptors”***. Ibn ʿAbbās and Ibn Masʿūd point to disbelief and revolt against God. Abū al-ʿĀliyah, al-Rabīʿ ibn Anas, and Qatādah add the harm of urging such revolt. Ibn ʿAbbās also reads their work as a hindrance: leaders lead followers away while the followers fail to grasp where they are being led. These strands show why the charge is not about a lone fault with no reach beyond the sinner. The group joins false faith to acts that block the path, disclose the believers’ affairs to foes, and loosen the bonds of truth. Their course makes harm spread. Each new follower can give the false path one more voice and a wider field.

The Qur’an can use corruption for clear loss in both life and land. A later figure speaks well in public, then goes out to (2:205 — **“strive throughout the land to spread mischief in it and destroy crops and cattle.”**). Another passage states, (30:41 — **“Corruption has spread on land and sea as a result of what people’s hands have done,”**), so that some results may be tasted and people may return. Here the first wreckage is faith and trust, but it does not stay unseen. A false ally can twist plans, make truth seem weak, and draw others into the same split. Those who mislead bear more than their own load: (16:25 — **“as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Evil indeed is what they will bear”**). This is why hidden denial may become public corruption even before streets or fields show its mark. It breaks the moral roots from which just life grows.

**A FAILURE TO SENSE WHAT THEIR OWN DEEDS HAVE BECOME**

The final clause, ***“but they fail to perceive it”***, returns to the blind spot named in verse 9. Their lack of sense does not make the harm unreal or free them from blame. They have been warned. They refuse the warning because their false account feels safer than the truth. At first, a person may know that he is using a fair name to mask a wrong deed. With time, the name can dull his sense of the wrong. Praise from friends, gain from the plan, and anger at each critic then look like proof that he is right. The heart no longer uses pain caused, trust broken, or truth barred as signs to stop. This is why self-praise is so grave here.

One early reading draws a further line within the group: the followers do not sense that their chiefs lead them astray. This does not erase the followers’ duty, but it explains how ruin can pass through ranks. The chief calls harm reform; the close circle repeats the claim; and those farther out inherit both the name and the deed. Each layer makes the next less likely to see. The Qur’an warns of efforts that are lost while their makers still think they do good: (18:104 — **“˹They are˺ those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good!””**). Mere force of intent is therefore not a safe guide. A sound conscience needs truth from God, honest counsel from others, and the will to review results without first protecting pride.

**THE VERDICT DOES NOT GIVE US GOD’S PLACE**

This verse gives a sure ruling about the people God exposes. It does not give each reader a free right to name every rival a corrupter. That misuse would copy the same pride the verse rebukes. God warns, (53:32 — **“So do not ˹falsely˺ elevate yourselves. He knows best who is ˹truly˺ righteous”**). Today, acts and their known results can be judged by revealed and just standards. Hidden creeds and final ends belong to God. The line lets a community oppose lies and harm without turning suspicion into knowledge of hearts. The verse is strongest when it trains the self before it arms the tongue. A person should ask what trusted bond his plan keeps, what truth it serves, whom it may lead, and whether he can still hear sound blame. Those checks do not make moral judgment weak. They keep it clean. Past peoples lacked enough people who would bar such ruin: (11:116 — **“If only there had been among the ˹destroyed˺ peoples before you, ˹O believers,˺ virtuous individuals who forbade corruption in the land”**). So faithful life needs clear warning as well as humble self-review. The hypocrites fail at both points: they spread harm, then strip the warning of its power by calling themselves healers. The believer seeks the reverse. He names harm by God’s scale, welcomes proof even when it cuts pride, repairs what he has broken, and never treats a fine name as proof of a sound deed.
