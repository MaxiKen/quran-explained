**THE ANSWER GIVEN TO THE CLAIM OF REPAIR**

The sūrah answers the pretenders in a single short verse: ***“Indeed, it is they”***, ***“who are the corruptors”***. The opening word is a particle of alerting, and its force is to make the reader stop and look, since the sentence that follows overturns what the men had just said about themselves. The Book does not argue with them and does not describe their reasons; it states what they are, and the early teachers read the sentence as a verdict on the result of their work rather than on the tone of their speech. Ibn ʿAbbās said that the corruption in question was the blocking of the road to the religion, and Ibn Masʿūd is credited with the same reading, which means the harm was measured by the number of people who were kept away and not by any damage the men intended to do.

**THE WORDS THEY COULD NOT HEAR**

The verse adds why the sentence had to be spoken at all: ***“but they fail to perceive it”***. The phrase repeats almost word for word from the earlier description of the same group, and the repetition is the point, because the men had now been told twice that they were damaging the community. The Book describes their blindness again in another sūrah where their whole way of life is named in a list: (5:64 — **“And they strive to spread corruption in the land. And Allah does not like corruptors”**). The reason the verse returns to the same phrase is that the punishment of the pretender is not only the loss of the reward but the loss of the ability to see what he has done, and the two losses belong together. What turns on the phrase for the reader is that the failure to see is described as part of the verdict rather than as an excuse for it.

**THE REPAIR THAT IS NOT REPAIR**

The work named in this sentence is worth pinning down for a reader who meets the word often and rarely in a plain sense. The Book uses it for whatever breaks the order God has set in a society: the taking of life, the breaking of agreements, the spreading of falsehood and the poisoning of trust between neighbours. The pretenders of this passage did their damage in the coin of trust, since they spoke as members of the community while carrying the community's affairs to its enemies, and one betrayal of that kind can undo the work of years. The early teachers described the same group as the one whose inside and outside differ, and the corruption follows from the difference itself, because the community cannot protect itself from a man it believes to be its own. That is why the verse does not describe a single act of the men but names their standing among the people, and why the reader is meant to fear the description for himself before he looks for it in anyone else. The Book is careful here in a way that should be noticed, since it never accuses the pretenders of a crime that a court could punish; what it names is the state of a man whose presence in a community is itself the harm.

**A TERMITE THAT LOOKS LIKE A GRAIN OF WOOD**

Think of a beam in a roof that has been eaten hollow from the inside and still wears a sound brown surface to the eye. Nothing on the outside of the timber announces the damage, and the whole roof depends on that beam. The pretender is the beam in the community, and the sūrah tells the reader what he is before it tells him what to do about it. These days the damage of the same kind travels through messages and reputations rather than through arms, and the description holds without any change, because the harm is done by a person the group still counts as one of its own. The verse is short because there is nothing to discuss once the matter has been stated, and the reader is left to decide what he will do with the sentence now that he has heard it. The reason the sentence is placed immediately after the claim of repair is that a claim and its refutation belong together, and the reader who meets them in that order cannot mistake the verdict for a later opinion. The two verses therefore form one movement, and the next exchange between the same people and their neighbours begins on the same footing, with a command and an answer that shows what they really think of the people they live among.
