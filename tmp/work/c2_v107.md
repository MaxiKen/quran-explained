**A SECOND APPEAL TO SETTLED KNOWLEDGE**

A second question turns from divine power toward knowledge already held by the audience: ***“Do you not know”***. The repetition of the address is deliberate, since the Book is not asking for information but for attention to something the audience already holds. The question is placed before the content it introduces, so that the reader arrives at the statement that follows with the answer already half given. In the preceding verse the same phrasing introduced the claim about capability, and here it introduces a claim about ownership, which is the foundation the rest of the sentence rests on. Ibn ʿAbbās reads the two questions as one argument, the first about power over the revelation and the second about power over the order it addresses. What he is being asked to acknowledge is not a subtlety but the plainest fact the Book states about the world.

**THE KINGDOM THAT TAKES IN HEAVEN AND EARTH**

The question is followed by the statement it was preparing, and the statement moves from the heavens downward: ***“that the kingdom of the heavens”*** and then ***“and the earth belongs ˹only˺ to Allah, and you have no guardian”***. The claim is one of ownership rather than of rule alone, and it covers both realms in a single sentence, so that nothing in the created order falls outside it. Another sūrah states the same fact in language close enough to serve as its witness, and it draws the same conclusion about protection: (9:116 — **“Indeed, to Allah ˹alone˺ belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth”**). The Book's arguments about dependence repeatedly begin from this premise, and the verse here gives the reader the premise without ornament. A person who has accepted the opening of the sūrah has already accepted the sentence that follows from it.

**NO GUARDIAN AND NO HELPER BESIDE HIM**

The verse completes its claim with the human consequence, added to the ownership without a pause: ***“or helper besides Allah”***. The two terms name the two things a person in difficulty looks for, someone to take charge and someone to assist, and the sentence denies both outside the One who owns the kingdom. The clause is not a threat but a description, since a kingdom that is entirely owned leaves nothing and no one outside its owner's authority. The Book returns to this pair of words when it describes the condition of those who have no protector on the day the ownership becomes visible, and the vocabulary is consistent across the sūrah. A reader who has placed his confidence in a person or an institution has the sentence to reckon with, and the verse states the matter as something already known rather than as news.

**TRUST PLACED WHERE THE SENTENCE PLACES IT**

The sentence leaves the reader with a choice about where to put his reliance, and it does so by removing all the alternatives in one clause. The Book's treatment of trust follows the same pattern as its treatment of mercy in the earlier verse, where the giving is placed in the hands of the One who gives freely. The sentence never argues for reliance on God; it states the ownership, and the reliance follows from it as a conclusion. The Book's confidence in this method is visible throughout the sūrah, where statements about God's prerogative are used to settle disputes rather than to win them. A reader who has tried to hold the ownership of the heavens and the earth and the loyalty of his own heart at the same time will find the sentence's logic unavoidable. Another passage puts the conclusion in the mouth of the Prophet himself, and the words are the answer to the absence of any helper: (9:129 — **“Allah is sufficient for me”**).

**WHAT FOLLOWS FROM OWNERSHIP**

The verse closes the sequence that began with the objection to the believers' greeting, and it does so by returning the argument to its foundation. If the kingdom belongs to Allah, then the selection of whom to favour is His, the replacement of a verse is His, and the protection of a community is His. The question the verse asks is therefore a test of the reader's own position rather than a request for agreement, and the Book leaves it there. The next verses turn to what this community was told to do with the guidance it had been given, and the reader can follow the argument from ownership to obligation without a break in the reasoning. What the passage has established by this point is that the objections raised against the revelation were quarrels about a prerogative that was never delegated to the objectors at all. Today, dependence on a powerful sponsor can feel secure until the sponsor reaches the boundary beyond which neither friendship nor force can help. The claim to another protector is like carrying a paper umbrella into a flood after refusing the only high ground.
