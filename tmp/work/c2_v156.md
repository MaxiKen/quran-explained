**THE WORDS SAID AT THE DOOR**

Two sentences land at the door of every disaster. ***“who, when faced with a disaster, say”*** ***““Surely to Allah we belong”*** ***“and to Him we will ˹all˺ return.””***. Ownership and return. One breath. One creed. Ibn ʿAbbās's gloss unpacks both clauses at their full weight. We are the slaves of Allah. He does with us as He pleases. We are returning to Him after we die. The transmission adds the condition that governs the acceptance of the sentence. If we are not pleased with His decree, He will not be pleased with our works. The point is that the sentence is spoken before the grief is finished. It is a door-sentence. The disaster knocks. The believer answers with the creed. What follows is that the ownership clause reframes the loss itself. What was owned has gone home to the owner. The return clause reframes the mourner. The mourner is also on the road. The effect is a theology of property that covers the whole crisis.

**THE OWNERSHIP CONFESSED**

The first clause is the constitution of the believer's world. To Allah we belong. The belongings travel back to the owner without injustice. The provision-promise states what the return includes on its receiving-side (22:58 — **“indeed grant them a good provision. Surely Allah is the Best Provider”**). The return is not a repossession. It is a reception by the best of providers. al-Bukhārī and Muslim both record the report attached to this phrase. Its promise covers the moment of the saying. The report gives the sentence its terms: *"when an affliction befalls him God will reward him and compensate him with what is better"*. The grief is real. The transaction is also real. The difference between the two is the difference between the world's arithmetic and the verse's. The world counts the loss. The verse counts the saying. For the modern reader the creed is portable past every border of sorrow. The sentence fits the hospital door and the graveside in any city.

**A RETURN THAT ORGANISES**

The second clause does the future's work. And to Him we will return. The return organises the rest of the life. The early commentators read the patience here as the child of that single sentence. The disaster is not the end of the road. It is a bend on the road. The reward of the doers waits at the bend's far side (39:10 — **“who do good in this world will have a good reward.”**). The patient ones are the doers in the season of the disaster. What follows is that the creed works as a compass in six words. The first three set the ownership. The last three set the destination. The middle of the life is walked between the two. This age sells a thousand sentences for the hard hour and ships them in bright packaging. The verse keeps one. The sentence has survived every century because the two facts inside it have never changed. The belonging is real. The returning is real. The door opens onto both.

**A CREED BUILT FOR CRISIS**

The placement of the creed at the door of the disaster is deliberate psychology and deliberate theology. The sentence is short enough to survive the shock. The two facts it carries are large enough to organise the shock. Ownership re-labels the loss. The return re-labels the mourner. The effect of the re-labelling is that the grief is given a frame that does not collapse under its own weight. The point of the acceptance-clause in the transmitted gloss is that the creed is a submission as well as a speech. The heart is asked to agree with the sentence it speaks. The reason the report of the reward is attached to the formula is that the sentence is heard at the other end. The one who receives the owned is also the one who records the saying. For the modern reader the six words are the most portable equipment in the passage. This age offers a thousand therapies for the hard hour and forgets the address of the owner. The creed supplies the address and the destination in one breath. The crisis arrives. The sentence is spoken. The door holds.
