**THE WORDS OF THE PATIENT AT THE MOMENT OF LOSS**

The previous verse gave good news to those who endure, and this verse shows what endurance looks like when it is spoken aloud: ***“who, when faced with a disaster, say,”***. The definition is deliberately tied to the moment of the blow rather than to the months that follow, since endurance at the first shock is the endurance that carries a person through the rest. The word used for the disaster covers every kind of harm that reaches a human being, whether it touches the body, the property, the family, or the peace of the heart, and the phrase has been read as extending even to a snuffed-out lamp. The explanation traced to Ibn ʿAbbās names the patient as those who acknowledge the ownership of God over what He has taken and accept His decree without complaint. The verse therefore does not ask for a feeling but for a saying, and the saying is placed in their mouths at the exact moment when it costs the most.

**BELONGING FIRST, THEN THE RETURN**

The sentence the patient speak consists of two halves, and the order between them matters: ***“Surely to Allah we belong and to Him we will ˹all˺ return”***. The first half establishes that what has been lost was never the possession of the one who lost it, which removes the ground of resentment before it can take root, and the second half fixes the direction of the journey, so the loss is understood as a stage on a road rather than as a wall. Another passage makes the same return the ground of all accountability, and it places the matter beyond any dispute: (2:210 — **“And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺”**). The two statements together give the grieving person a fact about ownership and a fact about destination, and the Book treats both as medicine for the heart. What the formula does not do is deny the pain; it gives the pain a place to stand.

**WHY THESE WORDS EASE WHAT THEY TOUCH**

The words work on the sorrow from two directions at once, since a person who admits that the lost thing belonged to God cannot accuse God of taking what was his, and a person who expects to return cannot treat the present separation as permanent. This is why the practice of reciting the formula at the news of a death has spread among the community, and why the sentence is taken as its origin. The teaching that accompanies it is that the reward belongs to the one who says it when the blow falls, so that the saying is the act of patience itself rather than a comment upon it. Nothing in the sentence requires the mourner to feel nothing; it requires him to speak the truth about what has happened while he feels it. The Book trains the tongue because the tongue is what carries the heart in the first hours of a loss.

**A TOOL LENT BY A FRIEND**

Think of a borrower today who borrows a valuable tool from a friend, uses it for a season, and then has it taken back. He may grieve the loss of the convenience, but he cannot accuse the owner of theft, since the tool was never his, and the return of it belongs to the arrangement by which he received it. The verse gives the grieving the same standing: the words confess the loan and the destination together, and the confession disarms the resentment that otherwise follows every serious loss. Whoever has seen a person carry a crushing loss calmly will usually find this formula, or its meaning, working quietly underneath. What the sentence adds is the promise that the owner of the loan has not forgotten the borrower.

**THE DEFINITION THAT THE NEXT VERSE COMPLETES**

Taken with what follows, the verse supplies the description that the previous verse promised to explain, so that patience is no longer an abstraction but a way of speaking at a specific hour. The community is told what to say when the loss arrives, and the reward is then named in the verse immediately after, so that the words and their fruit stand side by side. The return to God is stated again in another chapter as the end of every creature's path, which shows that the formula is not a private consolation but a statement of the way things are: (3:83 — **“and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned”**). The passage on testing thus ends its description of the patient with the words they use, and the words are within the reach of anyone who is willing to say them.
