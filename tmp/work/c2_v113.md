**THE CROSS-DISQUALIFICATION**

The passage then widens the lens on the exclusive claims to show the parties disqualifying each other: ***“The Jews say, “The Christians have nothing to stand on” and the Christians say, “The Jews have nothing to stand on,” ”***. The old gloss gives each verdict its venue. The Jews of Medina say the Christians follow nothing as a religion from God, and there is no true religion except Judaism. The Christians of Najrān say the same of the Jews, and there is no true religion except Christianity. The two verdicts land in the same grave. Each party buries the other's claim while standing on its own. The reason the geometry matters is that the two claims cannot both stand. The geometry of the passage is worth naming. Two exclusive claims that annihilate each other are not two truths in tension. The reason the third voice matters is that it shows the claim traveling downhill, which means the verse is diagnosing a temperament rather than a doctrine. They are two unproven notes failing at the same counter.

**THE BOOKS THEY SHARE**

The verse's next clause is the quietest and the deadliest: ***“ although both recite the Scriptures”***. Ibn ʿAbbās gives the clause its weight. Both are readers of the book. The reading is the indictment. The transmitted gloss traces the mutual recognition inside the two volumes themselves. In the scripture of the Jews there is the confirmation of the prophet who came after Moses. In the scripture of the Christians there is the confirmation of Moses before. The shelves each party stands on therefore carry the refutation of the other's exclusivity and the admission of the shared line. The passage of the shared recognition has already been quoted in this surah's family image: (2:146 — **“Those We have given the Scripture recognize this ˹Prophet˺ as they recognize their own children.”**). The knowing is in both rooms. The cross-disqualification is performed anyway. Reading and reciting are not the same verb. Reading lets the book address the reader. Reciting lets the reader parade the book.

**THE THIRD PARTY OF IGNORANCE**

The verse then folds a third voice into the exchange: ***“And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺.”***. The claim of the knowing has become the claim of the knowing-nothing. The unlettered party of the city picked up the same exclusive grammar without even the books to recite. The development is the verse's warning about how the claim travels. It starts as doctrine in a committee. It ends as a slogan in the street. The judgment reserved for the whole exchange is then attached to the ownership of the judgment: the arbiter is the one whose freedom of decision has been the passage's standing survey: (2:253 — **“if Allah had willed, they would not have fought one another. But Allah does what He wills”**). The verdict on the reciprocal disqualifications is therefore booked above all three parties. The arguments are filed here below. The finding arrives from the desk of the owner.

**THE ARGUMENT THAT PREEMPTS THE JUDGMENT**

For the modern reader, the scene is a complete map of every trialogue in which the communities recite and nobody reads. The verdicts cancel. The books confirm. The street amplifies. The verse's answer is to defer the finding to the day of the finding and to mark the pre-judgment as the error it is. A party that disqualifies the other's book while its own book confirms the other's lineage has tried to sit in the chair reserved for the ownership. The passage keeps the chair empty on purpose. For any household or forum where the same cross-verdicts are pronounced every year, the discipline of the empty chair is the medicine. Recite less. Read more. Leave the finding to the finder. The books have carried their confirmations for centuries across the same shelves. The reciprocal silencing has never once moved a syllable of either page. What travels forward is the claim that one committee can clear the docket of the day of the finding. The verse answers that travel with its own geometry. Two cancelled verdicts leave one chair, and the chair is not on the road between the two houses. It is at the end of the road where the owner of both houses receives the case whole. The books will speak at that hearing. The verdicts of the street will not. The docket is therefore kept by the owner of the shelves, and the shelves have outlasted every committee that ever tried to move them.
