**TWO CLAIMS MADE IN THE SAME BREATH**

A dispute between two communities that each held a scripture is reported here, with both positions set out side by side: ***“The Jews say, “The Christians have nothing to stand on”*** and ***“and the Christians say, “The Jews have nothing to stand on,”***. The symmetry is the point of the construction, since the Book puts the accusation and its mirror image side by side and lets the reader hear how they cancel each other. Neither claim is answered here with an argument about who is right, because the sentence is doing something else: it is showing that the two parties had made the same move against one another. The dispute had reached the believers as an invitation to take sides, and the Book's first response is to display the dispute for what it is. Anyone who has been drawn into a quarrel between two confident parties will recognise the pattern on display here. Ibn ʿAbbās located the dispute among the Jews of Madinah and the Christians of Najran, each denying the other's religion despite reading a revealed Scripture.

**EACH SIDE ACCUSED OF HAVING NO GROUND**

The phrase translated as having nothing to stand on describes a position without a foundation, and each community applied it to the other while claiming the ground for itself. The Book has already described this community's habit of treating its own inheritance as the measure of everyone else, and the verse shows the same measure being used in the opposite direction. Another sūrah names the same dispute and gives its cause as envy rather than conviction: (3:19 — **“Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them”**). The accusation in this verse is therefore not presented as the fruit of study, since each side was reading the same claim in the other's mouth. What the reader learns from the symmetry is that a quarrel conducted in these terms cannot produce a winner.

**SCRIBES WHO READ THE SAME BOOKS**

The verse then states the fact that makes the quarrel remarkable, and it states it briefly: ***“although both recite the Scriptures.”***. Both communities possessed and recited texts that they took to be from God, and the verse's clause places that shared practice beside the mutual accusation without comment. The contradiction is left to speak for itself here, since a person who recites a scripture and denies another community any ground occupies a position that the recitation will not support. The sentence does not say that the two scriptures were identical; it says that both parties were reciting, which is enough to show that the argument was not about information. A reader who has watched two people argue from the same document while each denies the other's reading of it has seen the condition the verse describes.

**THE PAGANS WHO REPEATED THE FORMULA**

The sentence widens to take in a third party, and the widening is a judgement on the first two: ***“And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺.”***. The people who had no scripture at all used the same formula as the communities who did, which means the formula belonged to a habit of mind rather than to a tradition. The Book's comparison is severe because it places the learned and the ignorant side by side on the strength of a shared sentence. What the verse establishes is that the denial of another person's ground is not produced by study, since the same denial appears where no study exists. Anyone who has heard a slogan repeated across very different groups will find the observation familiar.

**THE DAY THE DISPUTE IS SETTLED**

The verse closes by moving the dispute out of the community's hands and into a court that no party controls: ***“Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute”***. The same sentence is used elsewhere for the whole range of communities that disagreed about religion, and it is deliberately general: (22:17 — **“Allah will judge between them ˹all˺ on Judgment Day”**). The Book's answer to the quarrel is therefore not an adjudication in the present but a postponement to the day when the judgement is made by the One who knows what each community was given. The final picture holds two claims ringing against one another beside a single sentence about the day of settlement, a refusal to take the quarrel up on its own terms. What the passage requires of the believer is patience rather than a verdict, since the verdict has been reserved. Today, rival communities may each dismiss the other with absolute slogans while reading sources that should have taught greater care. The exchange is like two students holding copies of the same syllabus while each insists the other was assigned no course at all.
