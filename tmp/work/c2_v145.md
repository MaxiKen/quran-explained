**NO PROOF THAT WILL MOVE THEM**

The verse states what no amount of evidence could accomplish with the communities that had received scriptures, and it states it with an opening that concedes the maximum: ***“Even if you were to bring every proof to the People of the Book, they would not accept your direction ˹of prayer˺”***. The proof is granted in full and refused in the same sentence, which removes the possibility that the matter was a question of evidence. The sūrah has come back to this point again and again in its account of these communities, and the verse states it without qualification. Someone who has tried to settle a dispute by producing the strongest argument available will find the sentence describing the limit of that method. The refusal, then, belongs to the will rather than to the understanding.

**A REFUSAL THAT RUNS IN BOTH DIRECTIONS**

The verse then adds two more refusals, and they are symmetrical with the first: ***“nor would you accept theirs”*** and ***“nor would any of them accept the direction ˹of prayer˺ of another.”***. The sentence leaves no party holding a position the others will adopt, which means the disagreement is settled by the Book rather than by agreement among the parties. The same outlook is stated in another passage where the Messenger is told that the two communities will never be satisfied with him short of conversion: (2:120 — **“Never will the Jews or Christians be pleased with you”**). The symmetry in the sentence is a kind of mercy, since it lifts a burden that argument could never have carried away. What the reader is released from is the hope that the right evidence would finally produce agreement.

**A CONDITION AIMED AT THE BELIEVERS**

The matter is then turned toward the community itself, in a warning about following what the others desire: ***“And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge”***, ***“that has come to you”*** and ***“then you would certainly be one of the wrongdoers”***. The condition is hypothetical, and the Book uses the form to name a danger without predicting that it will happen, which is how it addressed the same community in an earlier verse about the desire for the neighbours' approval. The warning is placed after the knowledge has been named, so that the fault described would be a decision against information rather than a failure of information. Another passage states that following the majority of people on earth leads away from the path, which places this sentence in a pattern the sūrah has already established: (6:116 — **“If you were to obey most of those on earth, they would lead you away from Allah’s Way”**). Where the severity is directed is worth noticing, since the warning falls on the believers rather than on the others.

**TWO SHOPS THAT WILL NOT TRADE**

Think of two shops on the same street, each of which refuses to stock what the other sells, and neither of which will be persuaded by the other's catalogue. The verse describes that standoff in the matter of the direction of prayer, and it ends the discussion by naming the one thing that would make the believer a wrongdoer. The standoff explains why no further argument is invited here, since the parties are not in a state of enquiry that more information could advance. Watching a community argue itself into the position of its opponents by small accommodations shows what the warning is for. The boundary drawn around the community's own position is what the verse leaves in place. A boundary crossed in small steps is still crossed, and the warning is aimed at the steps rather than at the final position.

**THE GROUND UNDER THE WARNING**

The final clause's force depends on the phrase before it, since the knowledge said to have come to the community is the measure of what following the others' desires would cost. The Book's use of this phrase in this sūrah is consistent: the knowledge is what the community received, and it is also what makes its conduct accountable. Another passage states the same truth in a form addressed to the Prophet's own heart, where doubt is forbidden on the ground of what has already arrived: (3:60 — **“This is the truth from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt”**). The warning's weight comes entirely from what the community already knows, and not from any fresh evidence that could be brought forward, which is why the matter can be stated as settled rather than as a question still open to debate. The next verse turns to that knowledge again and to what a group among the others does with it.
