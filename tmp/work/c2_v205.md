**STRIVING IN THE LAND**

From departure to destruction, the two phrases trace the route the hidden man takes: ***“And when they leave ˹you˺, they strive throughout the land to spread mischief in it”***, ***“and destroy crops and cattle. Allah does not like mischief”***. and the route ends in a dislike the text states plainly. The walk compresses a whole career of mischief into two phrases, and the compression is the exposure: the same energy that charmed the room scorches the field. The crops and the cattle are named because they are the visible measure of what corruption costs the street. Ibn ʿAbbās reports the burning of the harvest and the stacks of grain as the deed behind the phrase. The description of the shape is the first half of the judgment, and the judgment itself is delivered in the second half.

**BURNING THE HARVEST**

The portrait's second phase begins at the door of the meeting: once the flatterer is gone, the striving begins. Ibn ʿAbbās names the deeds behind the phrase — the burning of the harvest and the stacks of grain, the ruin of the tillage and the livestock — and the naming turns the abstract corruption into a list of properties destroyed. The crops and the cattle are the food of the street, so the mischief described is famine planned by policy. The corruption condemned in the general legislation of the land carries the same prohibition and the same victims (11:85 — **“nor go about spreading corruption in the land”**). Because the striving is effort, the corruption of the powerful is a career rather than an accident, and the career is what the verse prosecutes.

**MISCHIEF WITH A FACE**

The closing phrase refuses to personalize the mischief and condemns the mischief itself: the dislike is aimed at the deed rather than at the doer's reputation. The naming of the corruption in the earliest warnings of this chapter carries the same verdict on the people who spread it while denying it (2:12 — **“Indeed, it is they who are the corruptors, but they fail to perceive it”**). Because the dislike is stated without a name, the verse can be applied to every age's version of the burning harvest. The ruler who ruins the economy, the merchant who cheats the scale, and the official who sells the verdict all stand inside the same phrase. The face changes and the mischief does not.

**THE COSTS THE EARTH PAYS**

The application is to price every policy by its harvest. The portrait invites the reader to follow any man from his public speech to his private road and to count the crops along the way. Because the mischief is measured in cattle and grain, the accounting is concrete enough for any generation to perform. The one who leaves the meeting and heads for the fields with fire in his hands has been drawn here before. The chapter gives the community its eyes back: the pleasing words and the burning harvest belong to one portrait, and the portrait is now complete. The spreading of the corruption appears in the other passages of the book with the same economy of images: the seal upon the heart, the veil upon the hearing, and the dust upon the eyes. The dust is the last covering laid upon the land that has been ruined from within, and the covering is what makes the ruin invisible to its owner. The limbs of the one who walks in the corruption are described elsewhere as witnesses at the trial, and the witness list includes the skin and the hearing and the sight. Because the limbs testify, the corruption is recorded in the body of the one who spreads it. The seal placed upon the heart is the same seal named in the earlier passages of this chapter, and the naming keeps the anatomy consistent. The one who wants to see the corruption of a land should read its markets and its courts at the hour of the closing. The dust is patient, and the eyes under it are patient, and the reckoning is patient too. The land keeps the receipt of every ruin inside its own soil, and the receipt is legible to the one who reads the dust. The receipt waits under the dust for the day of the reading.
