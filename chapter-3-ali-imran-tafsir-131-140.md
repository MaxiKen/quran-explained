# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 131–140

The last batch opened Uḥud: dawn posting, near-collapse steadied by God's *walī*-ship, Badr remembered as gift, angels under the condition of *ṣabr* and *taqwā*, help from God alone, the wounded Prophet told he owns none of the final *amr*, and a community ordered not to devour *ribā* doubled and redoubled. These ten verses do not yet give the full autopsy of the day. They give the moral climate in which the autopsy will be bearable.

What follows 130 is deliberate adjacency. Fear the Fire prepared for the disbelievers (131). Obey God and the Messenger, that you may receive mercy (132). Then race — *sāriʿū* — toward forgiveness and a Garden as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the *muttaqīn* (133), whose portrait fills 134–136: they spend in ease and hardship; they restrain anger; they pardon people; God loves the *muḥsinīn*; when they commit a shameful deed or wrong themselves, they remember God and seek forgiveness, and they do not knowingly persist. Their reward is forgiveness and Gardens forever. Then the camera widens again: ways of life have passed before you; travel the land and see the end of the deniers (137); this is an insight for humankind, guidance and admonition for the God-fearing (138). Only then does the surah speak the wound directly: do not weaken or grieve; you will be uppermost if you are believers (139); if a wound has touched you, a similar wound has touched them; these are days We rotate among people, so that God may make the believers evident and take martyrs from you — and God does not love the wrongdoers (140).

Five cruxes govern the reading. The first is why Fire and Paradise sit immediately after *ribā* and before the battle's grief — the moral frame is not a digression from Uḥud but the condition for surviving it. The second is the *muttaqīn* portrait at 134: spending, *kaẓm al-ghayẓ*, *ʿafw*, and *iḥsān* as one ladder, not three slogans. The third is 135's open door — *fahishah* or self-wrong, then remembrance, *istighfār*, and the refusal to persist knowingly — without turning the door into cheap licence. The fourth is 139's *al-aʿlawn* after a military wound: uppermost in what sense, under what condition. The fifth is 140's theodicy: days rotated (*nudāwiluhā*), God "knowing" believers without ignorance, and *shuhadāʾ* taken from among you — held as honour and as cost, not as propaganda.

The batch is the hinge between the opening frame of Uḥud and the longer argument that will name causes, ask "how did this happen to us?", and sort the living. It teaches a wounded community how to be *muttaqīn* before it finishes teaching them why the day went as it did.

## Verse 131

**"Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers."**

In simple terms: protect yourselves from the Fire that has been prepared for those who reject faith.

**The verse that makes 130 eschatological, not only economic.**

**"Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers."** *Wa-ttaqū al-nāra allatī uʿiddat li-l-kāfirīn.*

*Ittaqū* — the same root as *taqwā*, here as a verb of guarding, shielding, taking cover. *Al-nār* — the Fire. *Uʿiddat* — has been prepared, made ready. The passive keeps the preparation as a settled fact, not a threat invented in the moment. *Li-l-kāfirīn* — for the disbelievers, those who cover and reject.

**Why here, one verse after ribā.** Classical readers noticed the adjacency and did not treat it as random. Verse 130 banned devouring *ribā* doubled and redoubled and ordered *taqwā* so that the community might prosper. Verse 131 names what *taqwā* is also guarding against: a Fire already prepared. The market-sin and the end are not in different moral universes. A community that funds its life by eating the distressed is walking toward a Fire that was not built for the *muttaqīn*. Ibn Kathīr and others pair the prepared Fire here with the prepared Garden at 133 — two *uʿiddat* sentences facing each other across the race to forgiveness.

**"Prepared for the disbelievers"** is not a licence for believers to feel safe while they practise what the disbelievers' economy taught them. The address is still *yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū* from 130. Believers are told to fear a Fire whose primary occupants are named as *kāfirīn* — which means the path that leads there is the path of rejection, and *ribā* has just been placed on the map of paths that must not be walked. The verse does not say every usurer is a *kāfir*. It says the Fire is real, prepared, and the posture toward it is *ittaqū*.

**Connection backward.** *Taqwā* has been the batch-keyword since 120's shield, 123's gratitude-route, 125's angel-condition, 130's prosperity-condition. Here *ittaqū* takes the concrete object *al-nār*. Mindfulness is not mood. It is taking cover from a real end.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The modern habit is to keep finance in one drawer and last things in another. Verse 131 closes the drawers into one. If a contract, a product, a "growth" model systematically multiplies itself on distress, the question is not only regulatory. It is whether you are walking toward a Fire that was prepared for people who refused God's measure of the world. Guarding yourself may mean exiting a product, restructuring a debt, refusing a clause, or accepting a slower return. *Ittaqū al-nār* is practical before it is poetic.

**And the spiritual centre.** Preparation (*uʿiddat*) means the end is not improvisation. The Fire is not God's mood on a bad day. It is a reality the revelation has already named. Fear of it is not neurosis. It is sanity inside a moral universe that has exits and ends.

**The double preparation.** Fire *uʿiddat* for the disbelievers (131); Garden *uʿiddat* for the *muttaqīn* (133). The Qur'an does not leave the end as fog. Both ends are prepared. The human act in between is *ittaqū* and *sāriʿū* — guard and race. A theology that only threatens without a prepared Garden produces terror. A theology that only promises without a prepared Fire produces sentiment. This stretch of the surah refuses both reductions.

**Connection to 3:12 and the earlier Fire-warnings.** The surah has already told those who reject that they will be overcome and gathered to Hell. Verse 131 turns the warning into an address to believers: you also guard yourselves. The Fire is not "their problem only" in the sense that believers may walk toward it by paths of predation and then claim the label of faith as cover.

## Verse 132

**"Obey Allah and the Messenger, so you may be shown mercy."**

In simple terms: obey God and the Messenger, so that you may be shown mercy.

**The short hinge between Fire and the race.**

**"Obey Allah and the Messenger, so you may be shown mercy."** *Wa-aṭīʿū Allāha wa-al-rasūla laʿallakum turḥamūn.*

*Aṭīʿū* — obey, give ear and compliance. God and the Messenger are paired, as they are paired across the Madinan discourse: obedience to the Messenger is not a second religion beside God; it is the form God's guidance takes in a living community. *Laʿallakum turḥamūn* — so that you may be shown mercy, receive *raḥmah*. The *laʿalla* keeps hope without turning obedience into a vending machine.

**Why this verse is not filler.** After *ribā* and Fire, before the long portrait of the *muttaqīn*, the community is told the basic posture again: obey. Uḥud's coming autopsy will name disobedience to clear orders as a cause of the wound. Verse 132 plants the remedy before the diagnosis finishes. Mercy is attached to obedience — not to the feeling of having been wronged, not to the rawness of grief alone.

**"And the Messenger."** At Uḥud the Messenger ﷺ is the man whose face was cut and whose tooth was broken, the man told at 128 that he has no share in the final *amr*. Obedience to him is not obedience to a man seizing godhood. It is obedience to the one God sent, whose limits God Himself just announced. The pair *Allāh wa-al-rasūl* after 128 is theologically clean: God is obeyed as God; the Messenger is obeyed as Messenger; neither confusion nor reduction.

**Connection to 3:31–32 and 3:132's wider family.** Love of God is tied to following the Prophet; those who turn away are told God does not love the disbelievers. Here the positive face: obey, that you may be shown mercy. The same structure of attachment — God, then the Messenger as the living path.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** After a communal failure, the temptation is to invent a new spirituality of grievance: process the pain, rewrite the rules, sideline the authority that "let this happen." Verse 132 is almost blunt. The way back through Fire-fear toward Garden-race is obedience to what God and His Messenger already said — including the orders that were left, the posts that were abandoned, the spoils that were loved too early. Mercy is on the far side of that return, not on the far side of a better narrative.

**Obedience as the bridge into the portrait.** Without 132, the *muttaqīn* portrait at 134–136 could be misread as a free-floating virtue list anyone might adopt as self-help. With 132, the virtues sit under obedience to God and the Messenger. Spending, restraining anger, pardoning, seeking forgiveness — these are not a parallel religion. They are what obedience looks like in character when a community has been cut.

**Laʿallakum turḥamūn and the wounded.** Mercy is what a wounded community most wants and least believes it still deserves when it can see its own failures in the day. The verse attaches mercy to obedience, which is severe and kind. Severe: mercy is not the automatic product of having suffered. Kind: the path to mercy is still open and named — obey.

## Verse 133

**"And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺."**

In simple terms: race toward forgiveness from your Lord and toward a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those who are mindful of God.

**The command that turns the wounded from staring at the wound to running toward a width.**

**"And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth."** *Wa-sāriʿū ilā maghfiratin min rabbikum wa-jannatin ʿarḍuhā al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍ.*

*Sāriʿū* — race, hasten, outstrip one another. Not stroll. Not eventually. The verb is competitive without being ugly: a race toward mercy, not a race toward status. *Maghfirah min rabbikum* — forgiveness from your Lord. The Lord-name keeps the race intimate; you run toward the One who already owns you. *Jannah* whose *ʿarḍ* — width, breadth — is the heavens and the earth. The image is not a small private garden. It is a width that makes Uḥud's hill look like what it is: a local field inside a much larger map.

**"Prepared for those mindful of Allah."** *Uʿiddat li-l-muttaqīn.*

The same passive *uʿiddat* as the Fire at 131. Fire prepared for the disbelievers; Garden prepared for the *muttaqīn*. The half-bracketed "of Allah" in the translation correctly supplies what *muttaqīn* always implies. Preparation means the Garden is not a last-minute reward invented to cheer the defeated. It was ready. The race is into something already made.

**Why race after a wound.** A wounded community can freeze — replay the day, count the dead, nurse the grievance. *Sāriʿū* breaks the freeze. The first object of the race is not victory-revision. It is *maghfirah*. Even the community that stood at Uḥud needs forgiveness — for the posts left, for the early spoils, for the near-cowering, for whatever private sins the public day did not cancel. The Garden is the second object, and it is sized to heal smallness of hope.

**Hadith and classical colour.** The tradition carries reports that when this width was mentioned, a Jew or a questioner asked about its size relative to the heavens and earth, and the answer turned on the folding and the surplus of God's creation — details that vary in the routes and should not be over-weighted. What is solid in the verse itself is the command to race and the preparation for the *muttaqīn*. Ibn Kathīr and others stress the parallel with 57:21's similar race-language and the pairing of prepared Fire and prepared Garden.

**Connection to 134–136.** Verse 133 names the destination and the people it was prepared for. Verses 134–136 will describe those people without letting the description become a closed club. The *muttaqīn* are known by habits: spending, restraining anger, pardoning, seeking forgiveness after failure, refusing to persist knowingly.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** After any personal or collective failure, two races compete. One is the race to self-justification. The other is the race to *maghfirah*. Verse 133 orders the second. Practically: the apology made before the defence is finished; the debt restructured before the reputation is managed; the prayer of *istighfār* before the post-mortem meeting. The width of the Garden is given so that the race does not feel like running toward a narrow door that only the already-perfect can enter.

**ʿArḍuhā al-samāwātu wa-al-arḍ — width as medicine.** After a hill that felt like the whole world, the verse sizes hope to the heavens and the earth. Some classical discussion asks how a Garden can be as wide as the heavens and the earth if both already occupy that width; answers range from "as wide as both together" to "the width of the creation you know, and more beyond." The literary force does not depend on settling the cosmology. The force is: stop measuring the future by the ridge of Uḥud.

**Sāriʿū as communal verb.** The plural command makes the race a shared motion. A wounded community can race together toward forgiveness — public *istighfār*, repaired rights, restored posts of duty — rather than only private guilt in separate rooms.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 134

**"˹They are˺ those who donate in prosperity and adversity, control their anger, and pardon others. And Allah loves the good-doers."**

In simple terms: they are the ones who give in times of ease and in times of hardship, who restrain their anger, and who pardon other people. And God loves those who do good.

**The first half of the *muttaqīn* portrait — three habits and a love.**

**"Those who donate in prosperity and adversity."** *Alladhīna yunfiqūna fī al-sarrā'i wa-al-ḍarrā'.*

*Yunfiqūna* — they spend, they channel out. The supplied "donate" is fair dynamic equivalence; the Arabic is the ordinary Qur'anic verb of *infāq*, not a narrow modern charity-term. *Al-sarrā'* — ease, prosperity, the open time. *Al-ḍarrā'* — adversity, hardship, the closed time. They spend in both. Classical readers drew the practical points carefully: the poor are not exempt from the *habit* of spending within capacity; spending is not only money but time, labour, presence; ease and hardship are named because both are the usual doors of forgetting God — ease through distraction, hardship through panic.

**Why this belongs after Uḥud's opening.** Battles produce need and produce the temptation to hoard. *Ribā* at 130 was one predation. Refusing to spend in *ḍarrā'* is another. The *muttaqīn* answer both by the open hand in both seasons. The community that will bury seventy and walk home wounded is being told what its best people look like: still spending.

**"Control their anger."** *Wa-al-kāẓimīna al-ghayẓ.*

*Kaẓm* is to restrain, to hold back, as one holds back a throat or a waterskin's mouth — the image is suppression of what wants to burst. *Al-ghayẓ* — rage, the hot anger that seeks outlet. The supplied "control their anger" is accurate. They do not satisfy rage on people. Authentic reports belong here without invention of chains: the strong person is not the one who throws others down in wrestling, but the one who controls himself when angry (Bukhārī, Muslim, and the routes through Abū Hurayrah). Another report praises a swallow of anger restrained for God's sake as among the best swallows a servant takes. The verse's own claim is enough: *kāẓimīn al-ghayẓ* are part of the *muttaqīn* definition.

**"And pardon others."** *Wa-al-ʿāfīna ʿan al-nās.*

*ʿAfw* — to wipe away, to pardon, to leave the claim. Not only "feel less angry" but release the debt of the offence. The ladder is deliberate: restrain the burst (*kaẓm*), then pardon (*ʿafw*). Some classical pastoral readings add a third step visible in the *muḥsinīn* close: after pardon, *iḥsān* — actual good to the one who wronged you. The maid who recited this verse to a member of the Prophet's household in a moment of rising anger, and saw the anger fall, is a famous later story in the *adab* literature; it illustrates reception, not asbāb of revelation.

**"And Allah loves the good-doers."** *Wa-Allāhu yuḥibbu al-muḥsinīn.*

*Al-muḥsinīn* — those who do *iḥsān*, excellence, beauty of conduct that goes past the minimum of justice. God loves them. After a batch that stripped the Prophet of the final *amr* (128) and named God as not loving wrongdoers in the verse that will close this batch (140), this love is a positive pole: there is a human posture God loves, and it is this open-handed, anger-restraining, pardoning excellence.

**Uḥud's special pressure on this verse.** A wounded community is a community full of *ghayẓ* — at the enemy, at the archers who left the post, at the rumour that the Prophet had been killed, at oneself. Verse 134 does not ban the moral clarity that wrong occurred. It bans the unrestrained satisfaction of rage and the refusal of pardon where pardon is the higher path. The *muttaqīn* are not people who never feel anger. They are people who *kaẓm* it and then *ʿafw*.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The three habits are testable this week. Spending in *sarrā'* is easy to perform and easy to publicise; spending in *ḍarrā'* is the test — the transfer sent when the account is tight, the time given when the calendar is cruel. Anger: the email not sent, the voice not raised, the night slept on. Pardon: the claim dropped, the coldness ended, the person who hurt you not made into a permanent category. *Iḥsān* beyond that — the help offered to the one you could have crushed — is where God's love is named. None of this requires a battlefield. All of it is how a battlefield's survivors stay human.

**The ladder in one verse.** Spend → restrain anger → pardon → (implied in *muḥsinīn*) excellence beyond justice. Each step is harder than the one before for a wounded ego. Spending costs goods. Restraining anger costs the pleasure of explosion. Pardoning costs the claim. *Iḥsān* costs the last superiority of having been the one who could have crushed and did not. God loves that last posture.

**Sarrā' and ḍarrā' after ribā.** Verse 130 forbade a form of increase built on distress. Verse 134 commands a form of decrease — spending — that continues in distress. The economic ethic of the *muttaqīn* is not only "do not eat people." It is "keep feeding the path of God when you feel empty."

## Verse 135

**"˹They are˺ those who, upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness and do not knowingly persist in sin—and who forgives sins except Allah?"**

In simple terms: they are the ones who, when they do something shameful or wrong themselves, remember God and ask His forgiveness — and who forgives sins except God? — and who do not knowingly keep doing what they did.

**The second half of the portrait — the *muttaqīn* are not sinless; they are returners.**

**"Those who, upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness."** *Wa-alladhīna idhā faʿalū fāḥishatan aw ẓalamū anfusahum dhakarū Allāha fa-staghfarū li-dhunūbihim.*

*Fahishah* — a shameful deed, an obscenity, a grave indecency; the word covers major moral breaches, including sexual transgression in many classical readings, without being locked to only that. *Aw ẓalamū anfusahum* — or wronged themselves: the wider category of sin that may not be *fahishah* in the narrow sense but is still self-injury before God. *Idhā* — when, whenever. The *muttaqīn* are defined not as people to whom this never happens, but as people for whom, when it happens, a sequence begins.

**The sequence: remember, then seek forgiveness.** *Dhakarū Allāha* — they remembered God. Sin's first damage is forgetting. The return begins with remembrance — not with self-hatred as an end in itself, not with public performance, but with God brought back into the centre of the moment. *Fa-staghfarū* — then they sought forgiveness for their sins. The *fa* marks sequence and consequence. Remembrance that does not become *istighfār* is incomplete. *Istighfār* that has no remembrance under it can become technique.

**"And who forgives sins except Allah?"** *Wa-man yaghfiru al-dhunūba illā Allāh.*

A rhetorical question that is also a doctrinal wall. No priestly class, no communal vote, no personal self-absolution owns the forgiveness of sins. God alone. The question comforts and terrifies at once. Comforts: the door is not held by the person you wronged, nor by your reputation. Terrifies: you cannot forgive yourself into purity by private narrative. You ask the One who forgives.

**"And do not knowingly persist in sin."** *Wa-lam yuṣirrū ʿalā mā faʿalū wa-hum yaʿlamūn.*

*Lam yuṣirrū* — they do not persist, do not dig in, do not make the sin a settled residence. *Wa-hum yaʿlamūn* — while they know. The knowledge clause matters. Persistence in ignorance is one moral shape; persistence while knowing is another, and it is the one the verse refuses to let the *muttaqīn* carry. The door of 135 is wide open for the one who falls and returns. It is not a revolving door for the one who falls, nods at *istighfār*, and keeps the same room furnished for the same sin without intention to leave.

**What this verse does inside the Uḥud climate.** Some of Uḥud's failures were public and tactical. Some were private. Verse 135 refuses a spirituality in which only the public failures count, and refuses a despair in which any failure ends the story. The *muttaqīn* include people who have done *fahishah* or wronged themselves — and then remembered, asked, and refused to persist knowingly. That is one of the most pastorally important sentences in the surah.

**Connection to 3:89 and 3:128–129.** The repentance gate after the wall (86–88 → 89) and the open door of *tawbah* even for those who bloodied the Prophet (128) sit in the same moral world as 135. God forgives. Humans return. Persistence while knowing is the refusal that changes the moral shape.

**A necessary caution.** This verse must not be preached as cheap grace. *Istighfār* without any turn, "I ask forgiveness" as a slogan over an unaltered life, is not what *lam yuṣirrū wa-hum yaʿlamūn* describes. Nor must it be preached as despair for anyone who has fallen badly. The verse's own grammar includes the fall inside the definition of the people the Garden was prepared for.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** The practical test is the forty-eight hours after a clear wrong: a lie, a betrayal, a private indecency, a cruelty. Do you remember God, or only manage the consequences? Do you ask forgiveness of Him — and, where a right of a person is involved, address that right — or only wait for the feeling to pass? Do you change the conditions that made the sin easy, or leave the door propped open while promising yourself you will not walk through it again? Verse 135 is not abstract ethics. It is a sequence you can audit.

**Dhakarū Allāh as the pivot.** Many moral systems know remorse. The Qur'an's sequence names God at the pivot. Remorse that never becomes remembrance of God can still be self-centred — pain about the self's image, not about the right of God and the right of persons. *Dhakarū Allāha fa-staghfarū* keeps the vertical line intact.

**Wa-man yaghfiru al-dhunūba illā Allāh in a wounded community.** After Uḥud there will be blame enough to go around. This clause takes final forgiveness out of the blame-market. Humans may need to settle rights with humans. They may not set up as the owners of whether God forgives a brother who left a post and then broke in tears. God alone forgives sins.

## Verse 136

**"Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever. How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness˺!"**

In simple terms: their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens with rivers flowing under them, where they will remain forever. How excellent is the reward of those who work.

**The payoff of the portrait — forgiveness first, then Gardens.**

**"Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever."** *Ulā'ika jazā'uhum maghfiratun min rabbihim wa-jannātun tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru khālidīna fīhā.*

*Ulā'ika* — those: the ones just described across 134–135. *Jazā'uhum* — their repayment, their reward. The first item in the reward is *maghfirah* — forgiveness. Not first the scenery. Forgiveness. The race at 133 was toward forgiveness and a Garden; the reward matches the race. *Min rabbihim* — from their Lord: intimate again. *Jannāt* with rivers running beneath — the Qur'an's standing image of Paradise's life and continuity. *Khālidīna fīhā* — abiding there always. The wound of Uḥud is temporary. This stay is not.

**"How excellent is the reward for those who work righteousness!"** *Wa-niʿma ajru al-ʿāmilīn.*

*Niʿma* — how excellent, what a fine. *Ajru al-ʿāmilīn* — the wage of those who work. The half-bracketed "righteousness" correctly names the kind of work in view. The *muttaqīn* portrait is not a personality type. It is work: spending, restraining, pardoning, remembering, seeking forgiveness, refusing persistence. The reward is called a wage so that no one imagines the Garden as random favour disconnected from a life. And the wage begins with forgiveness so that no one imagines the work as purchasing God.

**Connection to 133.** Race toward *maghfirah* and *jannah* prepared for the *muttaqīn*; reward of *maghfirah* and *jannāt* for those who worked. The circle closes without tautology: 133 commands and sizes the hope; 134–135 define the people; 136 pays.

**Why this lands inside Uḥud.** A community counting its dead needs to know that the moral life that produces martyrs and survivors is not a life God leaves unpaid. Forgiveness and Gardens are not a consolation prize for people who failed to hold a post. They are the stated end of the people who spend, restrain, pardon, and return after falling — including people who will have to return after Uḥud's own failures.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** Keep the order of the reward when you encourage yourself or others. Forgiveness first. Then the width. A spirituality that only sells scenery without *maghfirah* produces consumers of paradise-imagery. A spirituality that only sells guilt without the Garden produces despair. Verse 136 holds both, and calls the whole thing the excellent wage of workers.

**Forgiveness as wage-first.** Ranking *maghfirah* before the Gardens trains desire. People can want paradise-imagery for comfort while still refusing the forgiveness that requires facing what they have done. The verse will not let the scenery outrun the pardon. First you are forgiven. Then you abide where rivers run.

**Khālidīna fīhā against Uḥud's temporality.** Everything about a battle-day screams temporary: the wound, the rumour, the flight, the return. *Khālidīna fīhā* answers that scream with a different duration. The point is not to despise the day's real pain. The point is to refuse to let the day's duration become the duration of hope.

**Al-ʿāmilīn — workers.** The Qur'an is willing to call the moral life work and its outcome a wage. That vocabulary protects against two opposite errors: the error that effort purchases God, and the error that God is indifferent to effort. *Niʿma ajru al-ʿāmilīn* praises the wage and names the workers without turning the workers into creditors of the divine.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 137

**"Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers."**

In simple terms: patterns like this have already run their course before you. So travel the land and see what became of those who denied.

**The camera lifts from the private portrait to the public map of history.**

**"Similar situations came to pass before you."** *Qad khalat min qablikum sunanun.*

*Khalat* — have gone, have passed. *Sunan* — ways, well-trodden patterns, courses of God's dealing with nations. Not "coincidences." Patterns. What happened at Uḥud is not the first time a community of faith met a day that cut it. The verse does not flatten Uḥud into "just another battle." It places the community inside a longer pedagogy: God has ways with those who deny and with those who believe, and those ways can be read.

**"So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers."** *Fa-sīrū fī al-arḍi fa-unẓurū kayfa kāna ʿāqibatu al-mukadhdhibīn.*

*Sīrū* — travel, walk. *Unẓurū* — look, consider. *ʿĀqibat al-mukadhdhibīn* — the end-state of those who cried lies, who denied. The command is empirical in the Qur'an's sense: go and look at ruins, at overturned peoples, at the ends that rhetoric did not prevent. Āl 'Imrān has already used the language of signs for people of insight (3:13). Here the sign is historical and geographical.

**What this does for a wounded community.** Two errors after a wound: thinking we are the first to suffer, and thinking suffering means the deniers were right. Verse 137 blocks both. You are not the first; the *sunan* have passed before you. And the deniers' present swagger is not their *ʿāqibah*. Travel and see how their story ends when the full scroll is read.

**Connection to 3:11 and 3:137's family.** Pharaoh's people and those before them were already named as a pattern earlier in the surah. The *sunan* language will return in the Qur'an as one of its major theodicies: God does not leave history without grain. Uḥud is inside that grain, not outside it.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** After a personal or communal reverse, the habit of doom-scrolling the present is strong. Verse 137 orders a different scroll: history. Read how denial ends. Read how communities that held *taqwā* under pressure were remembered. The "travel" may be literal pilgrimage through ruins, or serious reading, or listening to elders who watched a cycle complete. The point is the same: your day is not the only day, and the deniers' noon is not their sunset.

**Sunan as God's ways, not bare cycles.** In later theological and historical Muslim discourse *sunan Allāh* becomes a major category: God's consistent ways with nations who deny and with communities who are tested. Verse 137 is one of the root-texts of that category. Reading it as mere "history repeats" without God as actor misses the theistic grain. Reading it as bare determinism without human responsibility misses the command to travel and look — a command that assumes moral learning is possible.

**The fate of the deniers is a mercy to the wounded.** It is not gloating. It is medicine against the lie that the deniers' present strength is the last word. Uḥud's survivors needed that medicine. So do later readers watching injustice look permanent.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 138

**"This is an insight to humanity —a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing."**

In simple terms: this is a clear statement for all people — guidance and a lesson for those who fear God.

**The verse that names what the revelation is doing in this stretch.**

**"This is an insight to humanity — a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing."** *Hādhā bayānun li-l-nāsi wa-hudan wa-mawʿiẓatun li-l-muttaqīn.*

*Bayān* — a clear statement, a making-plain, an insight laid open. *Li-l-nās* — for people, for humankind. The address widens past the immediate fighters. What is being said about Fire and Garden, about spending and anger and *istighfār*, about *sunan* in the land, is public speech, not a private military memo. *Hudan* — guidance. *Mawʿiẓah* — admonition, a lesson that wants to change the hearer. *Li-l-muttaqīn* — for the God-fearing. The same people the Garden was prepared for at 133.

**The double audience.** For humankind: *bayān* — clarity available. For the *muttaqīn*: *hudā* and *mawʿiẓah* — guidance and admonition that take. The distinction is not that the *muttaqīn* hear different words. It is that the same clear speech becomes guidance and lesson in a heart that fears God, while remaining mere information — or mere accusation — in a heart that does not. This pairs with the surah's long interest in who has *albāb* and *abṣār*.

**"This" — what does hādhā point to?** Classical readings take it as the Qur'an itself, or this passage, or the clarification just given about the *sunan* and the ends of deniers. All three sit inside one claim: what you are hearing is not fog. It is *bayān*. After a confusing day on a hill, the revelation insists it is still in the business of making things plain.

**Connection forward to 139–140.** Clarity is given before consolation is given. First: this is *bayān*, *hudā*, *mawʿiẓah*. Then: do not weaken; the days are rotated. The order matters. Consolation without clarity becomes anaesthetic. Clarity without the coming consolation becomes crushing.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** When you read a hard passage after a hard week, ask which audience you are in. Are you collecting *bayān* as information to deploy against someone else? Or are you letting it become *hudā* and *mawʿiẓah* — guidance that reroutes you, a lesson that stings and heals? The verse does not despise the public clarity. It names the further gift that only *taqwā* unlocks.

**Bayān, hudā, mawʿiẓah — three words, three functions.** *Bayān* makes plain. *Hudā* routes the plain toward the goal. *Mawʿiẓah* presses the routed heart to change. A community can receive *bayān* and still refuse *hudā*. It can nod at *hudā* and still refuse the sting of *mawʿiẓah*. Verse 138 stacks all three so that no one mistakes information for transformation.

**For the muttaqīn specifically.** The Garden was prepared for them (133); the admonition is for them (138). *Taqwā* is both destination-criterion and reception-condition. That is not circular in a vicious sense. It is pedagogical: the fear of God is how clear speech becomes a life, and the life is how one remains among those the Garden was prepared for.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 139

**"Do not falter or grieve, for you will have the upper hand, if you are ˹true˺ believers."**

In simple terms: do not lose heart and do not grieve. You will come out on top, if you are true believers.

**The direct address to a community that has just been cut.**

**"Do not falter or grieve."** *Wa-lā tahinū wa-lā taḥzanū.*

*Lā tahinū* — do not weaken, do not grow feeble, do not go soft in resolve. *Lā taḥzanū* — do not grieve in the way that sinks the mission. The verse does not ban the human tear. The Prophet ﷺ himself wept over the dead. It bans the collapse of will and the grief that becomes identity — the grief that says the story is over because the day hurt.

**"For you will have the upper hand, if you are true believers."** *Wa-antum al-aʿlawna in kuntum mu'minīn.*

*Al-aʿlawn* — the higher ones, the uppermost, those who end above. The half-bracketed "true" before "believers" marks that *in kuntum mu'minīn* is conditional and qualitative — if you are believers, if belief is what you are. The supplied translation's "upper hand" is dynamic and fair. Classical readers did not reduce this to a guarantee of the next skirmish's scoreboard. The uppermost-ness of a believing community is finally measured by God's criteria: faith held, *taqwā* kept, the end with God — even when a particular afternoon is a wound. At the same time the verse is not pure metaphor; the community did survive Uḥud, Madinah was not taken, and the longer arc of the sīrah does not end with Quraysh on top. Both the moral and the historical readings have classical weight; forcing only one flattens the text.

**Why this verse must not be weaponised.** "You will be uppermost" said over a community that is currently wronging people becomes triumphalism. The condition is *in kuntum mu'minīn*. Belief as the Qur'an means it includes the *muttaqīn* portrait just given — spending, restraining anger, pardoning, returning after sin. Uppermost without that portrait is a slogan. Uppermost with it is a promise under condition.

**Connection to 3:110 and 3:139.** *Khayra ummah* was a title tied to conditions. *Al-aʿlawn* is a position tied to belief. The surah does not hand permanent supremacy to a census. It ties height to faith.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** After a failure — a project collapsed, a public shame, a family rupture — two voices arrive. One says: weaken, grieve as identity, rewrite yourself as the permanent loser. The other says: do not falter; do not let grief become the whole name of the story; height is still the trajectory if faith is still the ground. Verse 139 authorises the second voice without mocking the pain that makes the first voice loud.

**And the spiritual centre.** The prohibition on weakening is itself a mercy. God does not say "feel nothing." He says "do not become less." The difference is the whole of moral recovery.

**Lā tahinū and the body.** Weakening after Uḥud was not only spiritual metaphor. Men had fled; some had climbed the mountain; the Prophet ﷺ was calling them back; exhaustion and shock were physical. The prohibition lands on real bodies. Do not let the body's shock become the community's new constitution.

**Lā taḥzanū and legitimate grief.** The Qur'an elsewhere honours tears and the pain of loss. Yaʿqūb's eyes whiten with grief; the Prophet ﷺ weeps over his son Ibrāhīm and over the dead of the community. Verse 139 targets the grief that disables obedience and dissolves trust — grief as collapse — not the grief that loves the dead and still stands.

**In kuntum mu'minīn as ongoing test.** The condition is not a one-time certificate issued at Badr. It is present-tense identity under pressure. Uḥud is exactly where that identity is tested. The promise of being uppermost is not withdrawn; it is tied to remaining believers in the full sense the surah has been teaching.


===VERSE-END===

## Verse 140

**"If you have suffered injuries ˹at Uḥud˺, they suffered similarly ˹at Badr˺. We alternate these days ˹of victory and defeat˺ among people so that Allah may reveal the ˹true˺ believers, choose martyrs from among you—and Allah does not like the wrongdoers—"**

In simple terms: if you have been wounded, they have been wounded in a similar way. We rotate these days among people so that God may make the believers evident and take martyrs from you. And God does not love those who do wrong.

**The theodicy-verse of the batch — wound for wound, days rotated, martyrs taken, wrongdoers unloved.**

**"If you have suffered injuries at Uḥud, they suffered similarly at Badr."** *In yamsaskum qarḥun fa-qad massa al-qawma qarḥun mithluhu.*

*Qarḥ* — a wound, an ulcer, a sore; here the injury of battle — killing and wounding. *Yamsaskum* — if it touches you. *Massa al-qawma* — it has already touched the people, the opposing host. The half-brackets in the supplied translation ("at Uḥud" / "at Badr") are interpretive aids naming the occasions the classical tafsir agrees on: Muslim losses at Uḥud, Quraysh losses at Badr (and, in some readings, the shared bleeding inside Uḥud itself). The verse's own claim is the symmetry of *qarḥ*: you are not uniquely wounded in the history of this conflict. They have bled too.

**Why the symmetry matters.** Grief after Uḥud could feel like proof that God had switched sides. The verse answers with reciprocity of pain before it answers with theology of days. Empirically first: they have a wound like yours. Then: the days are rotated.

**"We alternate these days of victory and defeat among people."** *Wa-tilka al-ayyāmu nudāwiluhā bayna al-nās.*

*Tilka al-ayyām* — these days, those days: not merely twenty-four-hour units but the fortunes days carry — victory, defeat, ease, pressure. *Nudāwiluhā* — We rotate them, We cause them to turn, We hand them around in turns. *Bayna al-nās* — among people. No community in this world is awarded permanent noon. The rotation is God's act (*nu-* — We), not chaos and not the enemy's final authorship. This is one of the Qur'an's plainest statements of historical theodicy under *tawḥīd*.

**"So that Allah may reveal the true believers."** *Wa-li-yaʿlama Allāhu alladhīna āmanū.*

*Li-yaʿlama* — that He may know. The classical theological consensus, continuous with how such phrases are read across the Qur'an, is that this is knowledge as manifestation, evidence, sorting in the open — not God acquiring information He lacked. God already knows. The day makes known in history what faith holds under pressure. The half-bracketed "true" and "reveal" in the translation lean into that manifesting sense and are fair. Uḥud sorted: who held the post, who fled, who stood around the Prophet ﷺ, who only loved the spoils. The rotation of days is partly for that sorting.

**"And choose martyrs from among you."** *Wa-yattakhidha minkum shuhadā'.*

*Yattakhidha* — that He may take to Himself, adopt, choose. *Shuhadā'* — witnesses, martyrs. Both senses live in the root *sh-h-d*: those who bear witness with their blood, and those whose death becomes a testimony. God takes martyrs from you — the phrasing is honour, not waste. The seventy at Uḥud are not a divine accident. They are, in the verse's vocabulary, taken. The cost is not denied by the honour. The honour is not cancelled by the cost.

**"And Allah does not like the wrongdoers."** *Wa-Allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-ẓālimīn.*

The batch that named God's love for the *muḥsinīn* (134) closes this verse on God's not loving the *ẓālimīn*. The wrongdoers in view include those whose aggression and rejection produced the war, and — by the surah's wider usage — anyone who puts things out of place, including believers when they wrong. The rotation of days is not moral indifference. God is not a neutral wheel of fortune. He does not love wrongdoing even when a day of defeat falls on the community of faith as discipline and sorting.

**Connection to 126–127 and 139.** Help is from God alone; a flank may be cut; the enemy may still go home *khā'ibīn* in the strategic sense. You will be uppermost if you are believers. Days are rotated so that faith becomes evident and martyrs are taken. The theology is one piece: God is not absent from the wound; He is not the author of wrong; He is the rotator of days and the taker of *shuhadā'* and the One who loves *iḥsān* and does not love *ẓulm*.

**A pastoral caution on "so that He may know."** Do not let a crude reading make God a student of events. Do not let a crude reverse reading make human suffering a puppet-show with no real stakes. The verse holds real wound, real martyrdom, real sorting, and real divine knowledge that is eternal — the day makes the known visible.

**Now bring this into ordinary life.** When a season turns against you — health, work, reputation, a community's standing — verse 140 offers a frame that is neither fatalism nor self-blame alone. Days rotate among people; your noon was not a personal property; your night is not proof of abandonment. Ask what the day is making evident about faith. Honour the costs that are real losses and, where someone has died on a path of witness, refuse to call that death meaningless. And do not use the rotation of days as cover for *ẓulm* — God does not love it, whether it is winning or losing this afternoon.

**And the spiritual centre of the ten verses.** Fire to guard against. Obedience for mercy. A race to forgiveness and a Garden of impossible width. *Muttaqīn* who spend in both seasons, restrain rage, pardon, fall and remember and ask and refuse to persist knowingly. Forgiveness and Gardens as their wage. *Sunan* already run; travel and see the deniers' end. Clarity for humankind; guidance and admonition for the God-fearing. Do not weaken; do not grieve as collapse; you are uppermost if you believe. Wound for wound; days rotated; believers made evident; martyrs taken; wrongdoers unloved.

Uḥud is no longer only a morning of posts. It is a school. The next batches will keep the community inside that school — causes, questions, the living and the dead.

**Qarḥ mithluhu — like it, not identical theatre.** The verse does not claim the two wounds are cinematographically the same. It claims a likeness sufficient to break the illusion of unique victimhood. Badr cost Quraysh dead and prestige. Uḥud cost the Muslims dead and shock. The likeness is moral and historical enough to carry the argument.

**Nudāwiluhā and the refusal of permanent worldly coronation.** If days are rotated by God among people, then no empire, no movement, no generation may read its afternoon as proof of eternal election in this world. The final uppermost-ness of 139 sits above this rotation, not inside a fantasy that believers never lose a day.

**Shuhadā' taken — the grammar of honour.** *Yattakhidha minkum* — take from you, take to Himself. Martyrdom in this vocabulary is not the community throwing lives away for narrative. It is God taking witnesses. That still leaves human causes of death to be examined in the autopsy-verses to come — disobedience, love of spoils, abandonment of posts. Divine taking and human failure can both be true of the same afternoon without contradiction, because God's wisdom can take martyrs even from a day that also exposes sin.

**Wa-Allāhu lā yuḥibbu al-ẓālimīn as the seal.** The verse will not end on rotation alone, as if history were an amoral wheel. It ends on love refused to wrongdoers. Whatever day you are having, *ẓulm* is still hated by God. That seals the theodicy against two misuses: "we are winning, so we may wrong," and "we are losing, so wrong has become the nature of things."


===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses are the moral and theological climate of Uḥud before the long autopsy finishes. They take a community that has been framed by dawn, near-collapse, Badr's memory, conditional angels, help from God alone, a stripped Prophet, and a ban on *ribā*, and they teach that community how to be *muttaqīn* under a wound.

**131–132 set the stakes and the posture.** Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers — *ribā*'s neighbour-verse, making the economic ban eschatological. Obey God and the Messenger, that you may be shown mercy. Fire-fear and obedience are not delays before the battle-talk. They are what make the battle-talk survivable without becoming either despair or vengeance-religion.

**133–136 set the race and the portrait.** Race to forgiveness from your Lord and to a Garden as wide as the heavens and the earth, prepared for the *muttaqīn*. Who are they? Those who spend in ease and hardship; who restrain anger; who pardon people; God loves the *muḥsinīn*. Those who, when they commit *fahishah* or wrong themselves, remember God and seek forgiveness — and who forgives sins except God? — and do not knowingly persist. Their reward is forgiveness and Gardens forever; how excellent the wage of those who work. The portrait is the surah's answer to a community full of *ghayẓ* and failure: not sinlessness, but open hands, restrained rage, pardon, return, refusal to dig in.

**137–138 set the historical and revelatory frame.** *Sunan* have passed before you; travel the land; see the end of the deniers. This is *bayān* for humankind, *hudā* and *mawʿiẓah* for the God-fearing. Uḥud is inside a longer pattern; the revelation is still in the business of making plain.

**139–140 set the direct consolation and the theodicy.** Do not weaken or grieve; you are uppermost if you are believers. If a *qarḥ* has touched you, a like *qarḥ* has touched them; We rotate these days among people so that God may make the believers evident and take martyrs from you; God does not love the wrongdoers. Symmetry of wound, rotation of fortunes, manifestation of faith, honour of *shuhadā'*, refusal of moral indifference.

**Threads carried and extended.**

- ***Taqwā / muttaqīn*** — from 120's shield and 130's prosperity through 131's *ittaqū al-nār*, 133's prepared Garden, 134–136's portrait, 138's audience of admonition.
- ***Ṣabr and anger*** — *ṣabr* as battle-condition (125) meets *kaẓm al-ghayẓ* as character (134); both are forms of held force.
- ***Maghfirah*** — race-object (133), reward-first-item (136), open after *fahishah* (135); continuous with 128–129's open door of *tawbah*.
- ***Naṣr and height*** — help from God alone (126) and *al-aʿlawn* if you believe (139): height is not self-awarded supremacy.
- ***Love and its opposite*** — God loves the *muḥsinīn* (134); God does not love the *ẓālimīn* (140).
- ***Wealth's moral life*** — *ribā* banned (130); spending in *sarrā'* and *ḍarrā'* required (134); continuous with 3:92 and 3:117.
- **Theodicy under *tawḥīd*** — days rotated by "We" (140); *sunan* already run (137); not chaos, not divine absence, not divine love of wrong.

**Where the supplied translation has chosen, it has been flagged.** Half-bracketed additions ("of Allah," "They are," "true," "at Uḥud," "at Badr," "of victory and defeat," "reveal," "righteousness") are interpretive aids, mostly exact to classical sense. "Donate" for *yunfiqūna* is modern-charity colour over a wider *infāq*. "Control their anger" for *kāẓimīna al-ghayẓ* is accurate. "Insight" for *bayān* is fair. "Upper hand" for *al-aʿlawn* is dynamic. "Injuries" for *qarḥ* is fair. "Choose martyrs" for *yattakhidha… shuhadā'* carries both witness and martyrdom. None is distortion; each is a choice the reader is entitled to see.

**The verses to leave with are 134–135 read under 140.** The people God prepares Gardens for are not people who never bleed and never fall. They are people who spend when it hurts, hold rage when it burns, pardon when they could collect, remember God when they have done ugly things, ask forgiveness from the only One who forgives, and refuse to make sin a home while knowing better. Days will rotate. Wounds will touch both sides. Martyrs will be taken. Wrongdoing remains unloved. Do not weaken. Race.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them.

Five places in this batch required particular care.

**The first is 131 after 130.** The Fire-verse is not a random eschatological insert. Classical pairing with the prepared Garden at 133 and the moral adjacency to *ribā* are real. The verse does not declare every usurer a *kāfir*; it orders believers to guard against a Fire prepared for disbelievers, with *ribā* still in the immediate rear-view.

**The second is the *muttaqīn* portrait at 134–136.** Spending in both seasons, *kaẓm al-ghayẓ*, *ʿafw*, *iḥsān*, then the fall-and-return sequence of 135, then the wage of 136. Hadith on anger is widely attested and used as colour, not as a second scripture. The maid-and-anger reception story is *adab*, not asbāb. *Fahishah* is graded as major shameful deed without being locked by this commentary to one act alone.

**The third is 135's door.** Open for the one who falls, remembers, asks, and does not persist knowingly. Not cheap licence for slogan-*istighfār* over an unaltered life. Not despair for anyone who has fallen badly. Both misreadings are refused.

**The fourth is 139's *al-aʿlawn*.** Conditional on belief; not a blank cheque for communal swagger; both moral-final and historical-arc readings have weight; the Prophet's own weeping shows *lā taḥzanū* is not a ban on tears.

**The fifth is 140.** *Qarḥ* symmetry (Uḥud / Badr as dominant classical pairing; shared bleeding at Uḥud as secondary reading). *Nudāwiluhā* as God's rotation of fortunes. *Li-yaʿlama* as manifestation, not divine ignorance. *Shuhadā'* as honour and cost together. God does not love *ẓālimīn* — the rotation is not moral neutrality.

**On the translation.** Bracketed occasion-markers at 140, "donate," "upper hand," "insight," "true believers," and "choose martyrs" are choices noted above. The translation is not wrong; it has chosen, and the reader is entitled to know what was chosen.

Two boundaries observed throughout. No reading of the *muttaqīn* portrait has been allowed to become a closed club for the already-pure — 135 puts the fall inside the definition. And no reading of days rotated has been allowed to become fatalism or to erase the real dead at Uḥud — *yattakhidha minkum shuhadā'* both honours and costs. The batch stops at the threshold of the next movement's continued sorting. Waiting for authorisation to continue.
