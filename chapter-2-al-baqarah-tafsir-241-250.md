# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 241–250

This batch closes the long legislative passage on marriage and then turns, abruptly and deliberately, to a different subject.

Two verses finish the divorce law (241–242). Then the surah gives three short, hard verses about death, war, and money (243–245) — linked by a logic that is easy to miss and that I will draw out. Then it opens a sustained narrative: the Children of Israel demanding a king, the appointment of Ṭālūt, the Ark, the river, and the prayer uttered in front of Goliath (246–250).

**One caution that must be stated before the narrative begins, because it governs how 246–250 should be read.** This is the most *Isrā'īliyyāt*-exposed passage in al-Baqarah so far. The names, the numbers, the contents of the Ark, the identity of the prophet, and the location of the river are all supplied by transmitted reports that vary between themselves, and many of them reach us through Wahb ibn Munabbih and similar conduits that the hadith critics have always treated with caution. **The Qur'anic text is deliberately spare.** It names a prophet without naming him, gives a sign without describing it in detail, and says "a few" where the reports say a number. **A reader should hold the text firmly and the reports loosely**, and I will flag every reported detail as reported.

And one structural note to watch: the word *qalīl* — "a few" — appears twice in four verses (246, 249). The surah is counting, and the count is not flattering.

## Verse 241

**"Reasonable provisions must be made for divorced women—a duty on those mindful ˹of Allah˺."**

In simple terms: and for divorced women is a provision according to what is recognised as good — an obligation upon the God-fearing.

*Wa li-al-muṭallaqāti matā'un bi-al-ma'rūfi ḥaqqan 'alā al-muttaqīn.*

**This is a short verse doing two large things: it generalises, and it re-describes.**

*Wa li-**al-muṭallaqāti*** — "and for **divorced women**."

**Note the definite article and the generality.** 2:236 spoke of one specific case — a woman divorced before consummation with no bridal gift fixed. **2:241 speaks of divorced women as a class.** So the provision (*mut'ah*) that was stated for the narrowest case is here stated for all of them.

**The jurists built on exactly this move, and disagreed about how far it reaches.** The Schools differ over whether the *mut'ah* is obligatory or recommended, and over which divorced women are owed it — with the differences turning on how each weighs 2:236 and 2:241 against 2:237 (which settles the case of a specified gift and no consummation at half). The rough shape of the disagreement: some hold it is owed to every divorced woman except the one whose gift was specified and whose marriage was consummated (she has her full gift already); others hold it is recommended in general and obligatory only in the cases the text singles out; others interpolate further cases. **The disagreement is real and should be consulted rather than collapsed.** What is not disputed is the direction: 2:241 asserts a claim for divorced women as such.

*Matā'un bi-al-ma'rūf* — "a provision according to what is recognised as good."

Seventh appearance of the formula in this passage. **Still not a number.** The measure is what the community recognises as decent, scaled to means — as 2:236 made explicit and as this verse assumes.

*Ḥaqqan 'alā **al-muttaqīn*** — "an obligation upon the **God-fearing**."

**And here is the verse's second move, and it is the one worth sitting with.**

At 2:236, the same obligation was stated as *ḥaqqan 'alā al-**muḥsinīn*** — upon the **doers of good** (*iḥsān*). Here it is *ḥaqqan 'alā **al-muttaqīn*** — upon the **God-fearing** (*taqwā*).

**Two verses, two different nouns, sixteen lines apart.** The commentators read the variation as meaningful rather than as stylistic. *Muḥsinīn* names those who have gone beyond the minimum into excellence. *Muttaqīn* names those who are guarding themselves from God. **And there is a reason to use different words:**

- *Iḥsān* is the word for doing the beautiful thing when no one can compel you.
- *Taqwā* is the word for the barrier you put between yourself and God's displeasure.

**So 2:236 says: the excellent give this. And 2:241 says: the God-fearing owe this.** One is a description of the good; the other is a statement of obligation. Together they say the *mut'ah* is both the mark of excellence and a debt.

**And there is a bookend here that is easy to miss.** *Al-muttaqīn* is the very first word the surah used to describe its audience: *hudan li-**al-muttaqīn*** — "a guidance for the God-fearing" (2:2). **The surah opened by identifying who it is for, and it now tells those people what they owe a divorced woman.** The definition of the God-fearing is not only in what they believe. It is in what they pay.

**Note also the placement: this is the last ruling of the marriage passage.** The surah has legislated, since 2:221, whom to marry, how to conduct the marriage, and how to end it — and it closes the whole thing on one sentence about a woman who has been sent away and is owed something decent. **That is the note the passage chooses to end on.**

## Verse 242

**"This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so perhaps you will understand."**

In simple terms: thus does God make clear to you His signs, so that you may use your reason.

*Kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakum āyātihi la'allakum ta'qilūn.*

**This verse closes the block that began at 2:221, and the verb it closes on is the whole point.**

*Kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakum āyātihi* — "thus does God make clear to you His signs."

Note the verb: ***yubayyinu*** — He makes **clear**, He separates and distinguishes. The same claim was made at the end of 2:219 (*kadhālika yubayyinu Allāhu lakumu al-āyāt*), which closed the passage on intoxicants, spending and orphans. **So the surah twice says, after two long legislative blocks: this is how I explain things to you.**

**And that is a claim about method, not merely about content.** God is not saying "these are my rulings." He is saying: **notice that I have been explaining them.** Every ruling in 221–241 came with a reason — the pull toward the Fire (221), the harm (222), the fixed place and the free manner (223), the barrier to good (224), the heart (225), the limit on suspension (226), the knowing of what is heard (227), the reciprocity (228), the two doors (229), the deterrence of the third (230), the harm and the jest (231), the need of the couple (232), the capacity and the child (233), the fixed term (234), the thought and the knot (235), the scale (236), the grace (237), the year (240), the claim of the divorced woman (241).

**A passage this thoroughly reasoned then says: I did this so you would think.** That is the verse.

*La'allakum **ta'qilūn*** — "so that you may **use your reason**."

**And here is the observation I most want to leave with a reader, because it is invisible unless you are watching for it.** The surah closes its three great legislative blocks by naming three different faculties:

- **2:219** — *la'allakum **tatafakkarūn*** — "so that you may **reflect**." Closing the passage on intoxicants, gambling and spending.
- **2:221** — *la'allahum **yatadhakkarūn*** — "so that they may **remember**." Closing the verse on whom to marry.
- **2:242** — *la'allakum **ta'qilūn*** — "so that you may **reason**." Closing the whole passage.

**Three blocks, three faculties: reflection, remembrance, reasoning.** The Qur'an is not asking for the same response to every subject. It asks you to *think through* the calculus of benefit and harm; to *remember* what you already know when desire is pulling; and to *reason* your way through the law as a whole.

**And *'aql* is the highest of the three here, and it is placed at the end of the longest block.** *'Aql* is, at root, binding and restraining — the faculty that holds a thing and connects it to its consequence. **The surah closes its most detailed legislation by asking the reader to bind the rulings together and see the system.** Not merely to obey them one at a time.

**That is, in effect, the warrant for the whole discipline of *maqāṣid al-sharī'ah*** — the reading of the law for its purposes. A law that explains itself this thoroughly and then says "so that you may reason" has licensed its readers to ask *why*. And it has also warned them: **the one who takes a single ruling out of the system and uses it against the system's purpose has not done what this verse asks.**

## Verse 243

**"Have you ˹O Prophet˺ not seen those who fled their homes in the thousands for fear of death? Allah said to them, “Die!” then He gave them life. Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity, but most people are ungrateful."**

In simple terms: have you not considered those who went out from their homes, and they were thousands, in fear of death? And God said to them, "Die" — then He gave them life. Truly God is full of bounty to mankind, but most people do not give thanks.

*A-lam tara ilā alladhīna kharajū min diyārihim wa hum ulūf ḥadhara al-mawt, fa-qāla lahumu Allāhu mūtū thumma aḥyāhum, inna Allāha la-dhū faḍlin 'alā al-nāsi wa lākinna akthara al-nāsi lā yashkurūn.*

***A-lam tara ilā*** — "have you not **seen**/**considered**?"

The same formula that will open 2:246. **It does not mean physical sight.** It is an invitation to consider a case and draw the conclusion — the Qur'an's standard way of turning history into evidence. The Prophet ﷺ was not present at this event; he is being asked to *look at it* in the sense of holding it in the mind.

*Wa hum **ulūf*** — "and they were **thousands**."

***Ulūf*** is the plural of *alf*, a thousand. **And note what the verse does not do: it does not say how many.** The transmitted reports supply numbers — four thousand in one narration from Ibn 'Abbās, and other figures in other reports. **None of those numbers is in the text, and they vary between themselves.** A reader should hold to "thousands" and treat the specific figures as reports.

*Ḥadhara al-mawt* — "**in fear of** death." *Ḥadhar* is wariness, precaution. The phrase is an accusative of cause: **the thing that moved them was the fear.**

*Fa-qāla lahumu Allāhu **mūtū** thumma aḥyāhum* — "and God said to them: **Die!** Then He gave them life."

**Note the command.** *Mūtū* — "die" — and they died. This is the *kun fa-yakūn* pattern of 2:117 ("when He decrees a matter He only says to it 'Be' and it is") applied to death rather than to existence. **The same creative command that brings things into being takes them out of it.** No angel of death is mentioned, no illness, no mechanism. He said it, and it was.

**Now the transmitted accounts, which I must flag clearly as transmitted and varying.**

- Reported from Ibn 'Abbās (by Wakī' ibn Jarāḥ in his tafsīr): they were four thousand who fled a plague that had broken out in their land, saying **"let us go to a land in which there is no death."** When they reached a certain place, God said to them "Die," and they all died. A prophet passed by them afterwards and supplicated, and God restored them to life.
- Reported by Ibn Abī Ḥātim, also from Ibn 'Abbās: they were the people of a village called **Dāwardān**, some miles from Wāsiṭ in Iraq.
- Reported from several of the Salaf: they were the people of a city among the Children of Israel during an epidemic. They fled into the wilderness and came to a broad valley, filling it from side to side. **God sent two angels, one at the lower end of the valley and one at the upper, and they cried out once, and every one of them died instantly — like the death of a single man.** Then, after a time, a prophet passed by their remains and supplicated, and God restored them, and they rose praising God. Some reports name the prophet as **Ḥizqīl** (Ezekiel); others give other names. Some reports say the interval was eight days; others, long enough for the bones to be scattered.

**None of this should be asserted.** The place, the number, the name of the prophet, the interval, and the mechanism are all transmitted reports, they differ between themselves, and much of this material reaches us through *Isrā'īliyyāt* channels that the commentators themselves treat with caution. **What the verse states is what a Muslim is bound to: they went out in their thousands fearing death; God said to them "Die"; then He gave them life.**

**And now the two things the verse is actually for.**

**The first is the moral, and it is devastating.** These people organised their entire lives around avoiding death — they abandoned their homes, their city, their property, and went into the wilderness for it — **and death took them instantly, at the moment of their flight, by a single command.** As Ibn Kathīr puts it: **no caution can avert the decree, and there is no refuge from God except to God Himself.** They sought long life and got the opposite of what they sought.

**The second is the argument.** Bringing these people back to life is stated in the verse as a fact, and the commentators have always read its function as **a proof of the bodily Resurrection.** If God can restore a people who had died and — on some reports — whose bones had scattered, then the raising of the dead on the Last Day is not a harder thing. **This is why the story is here.** It is not an anecdote; it is a demonstration placed immediately before a command to go out and fight.

**Which brings us to the transmitted ruling that answers this story directly, and it is in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s.**

Recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from 'Abd al-Raḥmān ibn 'Awf, and in other transmissions from Usāmah ibn Zayd and 'Ā'ishah: the Prophet ﷺ said, concerning plague: **"If you hear of it in a land, do not enter it. And if it breaks out in a land while you are in it, do not leave it, fleeing from it."**

**And this is the antithesis of what these people did, and it is the Islamic basis for quarantine.** They fled the afflicted land. The Sunnah forbids exactly that — **and forbids the mirror-image error too**: do not walk into an afflicted land either. The ruling is not "flee" and it is not "be reckless." It is: **stay where you are, and do not go toward it.**

And there is a second narration, also in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, from 'Ā'ishah, in which the Prophet ﷺ said that the plague is a punishment God sends upon whom He wills, and **God has made it a mercy for the believers: there is no one who stays in his land through it, patient and expecting reward, knowing that nothing will befall him except what God has decreed for him, but he has the reward of a martyr.**

**That is the positive form of 2:243's lesson.** The people in the verse fled and died anyway. The believer stays — not because staying is safer, but because **staying is obedience, and safety was never on offer either way.**

**A note for the present.** The 2020s made this verse and this hadith impossible to read as distant history. Every community on earth has recently had to decide whether to flee an epidemic, whether to enter an affected area, and whether the vulnerable should be protected by separating them. **The tradition's answer was already written: quarantine is real, flight from the decree is futile, and the one who stays patiently for God's sake is not abandoned.** The Islamic ruling here is not a metaphor for something else. It *is* the ruling.

*Inna Allāha la-**dhū faḍlin 'alā al-nāsi** wa lākinna akthara al-nāsi **lā yashkurūn*** — "truly God is full of **bounty** to mankind, but most people do not **give thanks**."

***Faḍl*** — bounty, the extra, the unearned. And the *faḍl* named here is the giving of the sign and the giving back of life. **And the closing on ingratitude brings the surah back round to 2:211** — "whoever trades God's favour after it has come to him" — and to 2:152 ("remember Me... and give thanks to Me, and do not be ungrateful to Me"). **The surah's great theme returns: the default human response to bounty is not to notice it.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 244

**"Fight in the cause of Allah, and know that Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: and fight in the path of God, and know that God is Hearing, Knowing.

*Wa qātilū fī sabīli Allāhi wa-'lamū anna Allāha samī'un 'alīm.*

**This verse is eight words long, and it is the direct antithesis of the one before it. Read the two in sequence and the argument is complete.**

**2:243: a people who organised everything around not dying — and died.**
**2:244: go out and fight.**

**The sequence is not accidental and it is not a non-sequitur. The logic runs: since flight does not save you, stop organising your life around survival.** The people in 243 abandoned their homes to preserve their lives and lost both. The believer is now told to go out — **not because dying is safe, but because survival is not the thing to be optimising.**

That is one of the hardest sequences in the Qur'an, and it should not be softened. **The surah does not say "fight, and you will be protected." It says: the people who tried to protect themselves died, so fight.**

*Wa qātilū fī **sabīli Allāhi*** — "and fight in the **path of God**."

Note the phrase: *sabīl Allāh*, the same as 2:218 (those who "emigrated and strove in the path of God") and 2:246 (below). **The qualifier is doing the work.** Fighting is commanded *in the path of God* — which excludes fighting for tribe, for spoil, for pride, or for a flag. The same restriction I have held throughout: **this verse operates inside the framework of 2:190–193** — fight those who fight you, do not transgress, cease when they cease, and incline to peace if they incline to it (8:61). A reading of 2:244 detached from that framework is a misuse.

*Wa-'lamū anna Allāha **samī'un 'alīm*** — "and know that God is **Hearing, Knowing**."

**Two attributes, and both are aimed at the fighter.**

**Hearing** — because going out to fight involves speech: the declaration, the oath, the slogan, the promise, the shouted thing said in the moment. **All of it is heard.** A person can tell himself and everyone else that he is fighting for God while saying things that betray a different motive. *Samī'* means the words are on the record.

**Knowing** — because the motive is interior, and a person can be mistaken about his own. *'Alīm* means **God knows what you were actually fighting for, including the parts of it you have not admitted to yourself.** That is the verse's check: the command is given and the audit is announced in the same breath.

**Note the structure: command, then the epistemic ground — the same pairing as 2:216** ("Perhaps you dislike a thing... and God knows, and you do not know"). The surah consistently pairs an obligation with the fact of being fully known. **Obedience here is not blind; it is obedience in front of a witness.**

**For practice.** This verse is usually read only as a war-verse, which is a pity, because its reasoning is the same reasoning that governs every costly obedience. **The person who declines the hard thing in order to preserve himself is making the calculation of 2:243.** The calculation does not work — not because the hard thing is safe, but because avoidance is not a strategy. **Nothing in this verse promises safety. It promises that survival was never the currency.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 245

**"Who will lend to Allah a good loan which Allah will multiply many times over? It is Allah ˹alone˺ who decreases and increases ˹wealth˺. And to Him you will ˹all˺ be returned."**

In simple terms: who is it that will lend God a goodly loan, so that He may multiply it for him many times over? And God withholds and extends, and to Him you will be returned.

*Man dhā alladhī yuqriḍu Allāha qarḍan ḥasanan fa-yuḍā'ifahu lahu aḍ'āfan kathīrah, wa Allāhu yaqbiḍu wa yabsuṭu, wa ilayhi turja'ūn.*

**The whole verse turns on one word, and the word is a problem.**

***Qard*** — a **loan**.

**And God does not need a loan.** Everything in the heavens and the earth is His (2:107, 2:284). He is the Free of need, and you are the needy (47:38). **So why does the verse call charity a loan to Him?**

The commentators answer with a figure of speech — *isti'āra*, a borrowing of language — and then they say what the figure is **for**, and that is the important part.

**A loan is repaid. That is what a loan is.** Charity could in principle be given and never returned; a loan cannot. **So by calling it a loan, God has committed Himself to repayment in a way the word "charity" would not have conveyed.** The metaphor is not decoration. **It is a guarantee.** He has bound Himself, in His own words, to the debtor's obligation.

And note that He has done it by taking the **lower position**: God is the borrower, the human being the lender. The Lord of the worlds describes Himself as asking. **That condescension is the mercy in the verse.**

***Qarḍan ḥasanan*** — "a **goodly** loan."

**What makes the loan goodly?** The commentators are consistent, and their list is practical:
- It is from **lawful earnings** — God accepts only what is good (2:267, and the hadith).
- It is given **willingly**, not under pressure or for show.
- It is **not followed by reminders of the favour or by hurt** — which is exactly 2:262's ruling (*do not undo your charity with reminders and injury*).
- It is given **for God's sake alone**.

**Note that the adjective qualifies the manner, not the amount.** A small amount given well is a goodly loan; a large amount given with a reminder of it is not. **The verse asks for a quality, and the poorest person can supply it.**

*Fa-yuḍā'ifahu lahu **aḍ'āfan kathīrah*** — "so that He may **multiply** it for him **many multiplications**."

Note the double intensification: the verb *yuḍā'ifahu* (He multiplies it) and the object *aḍ'āfan kathīrah* (multiplications, many). **The verse deliberately does not fix the number.** The hadith supply the range: in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* from Abū Hurayrah — **"Every deed of the son of Adam is multiplied: a good deed by ten up to seven hundred times"** — and in the *ḥadīth qudsī* also in Muslim: **"Spend, O son of Adam, and I shall spend on you."** And 2:261, which we will reach, gives the agricultural image of seven ears with a hundred grains each.

**The unfixed number is itself the point: the return is described as beyond calculation because it is.**

*Wa Allāhu **yaqbiḍu wa yabsuṭu*** — "and God **withholds and extends**."

**This clause is the verse's answer to the anxiety that stops people giving, and it carries two readings.**

**The first, and the plain one in context, is about provision.** *Qabḍ* is constriction; *basṭ* is expansion. **God is the one who tightens and widens your livelihood.** Which means the fear that giving will impoverish you has mistaken the agent: the God who could withhold is the same God who extends, and your giving is not a variable in that equation in the way you think it is. **You cannot out-give God.**

**The second reading is creedal.** *Qabḍ* and *basṭ* are among the attributes of act, and the tradition affirms them **as they came, without likening them to the acts of creatures and without denying them** — the same rule I have applied throughout this surah to *istiwā'* (2:29), the Face (2:115), *ma'iyyah* (2:153), nearness (2:186) and the coming (2:210). **He withholds and He extends truly, in a manner befitting Him, not as a miser grips and a spender opens his hand.** Some commentators also connect *qabḍ* here to the taking of souls, on the basis of related texts; the primary sense in this verse is provision, and both senses are held by the tradition without either being denied.

*Wa ilayhi **turja'ūn*** — "and to Him you will be **returned**."

**The closing is eschatological, and it is the answer to the objection everyone makes.** "I gave and nothing came back." **The return is not scheduled for this life.** It is lodged with the One to whom you are returning. **A loan whose repayment date is the Resurrection is not a bad investment — it is the only one whose guarantor cannot default.**

**Now the two narrations that belong to this verse, and they are both essential.**

**The first is a *ḥadīth qudsī* in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (2569), from Abū Hurayrah**, and it resolves the theological problem in the metaphor completely.

God will say on the Day of Resurrection: **"O son of Adam, I asked you for food and you did not feed Me."** He will say: "My Lord, how should I feed You, when You are the Lord of the worlds?" He will say: **"Did you not know that My servant so-and-so asked you for food and you did not feed him? Did you not know that had you fed him, you would have found that with Me?"** — and the same for drink, and the same for visiting the sick.

**That hadith is the gloss on *yuqriḍu Allāha*.** The servant raises the exact objection the metaphor creates — *how can God be fed?* — and the answer is: **He is not fed. The loan is taken through His servant.** So the "loan to God" *is* a gift to a person in need — and God identifies Himself with the claim so completely that withholding it is described as withholding from Him.

**Which means the metaphor is doing something precise: it makes the claim of the poor the claim of God.** Not metaphorically. In the hadith's own terms.

**The second is the narration of Abū al-Daḥdāḥ, and it must be flagged as transmitted rather than *ṣaḥīḥ*.** Reported from Ibn Mas'ūd and recorded by Ibn Abī Ḥātim and others:

When this verse was revealed, **Abū al-Daḥdāḥ al-Anṣārī** said: "O Messenger of God, does God ask us for a loan?" The Prophet ﷺ said: **"Yes, O Abū al-Daḥdāḥ."** He said: "Give me your hand" — and the Prophet ﷺ placed his hand in his — and he said: **"I have lent my garden to my Lord."** It held six hundred date-palms, and his wife and children were living in it. He went and called from outside it: "Umm al-Daḥdāḥ!" She said: "At your service." He said: **"Come out, for I have lent it to my Lord."**

**And she said: "You have made a profitable trade, Abū al-Daḥdāḥ."** And she moved their goods and their children out.

Some versions add that the Prophet ﷺ had advised him to keep one of his two gardens for his family, and that he gave the better of the two anyway; and that the Prophet ﷺ said, **"How many a laden date-cluster awaits Abū al-Daḥdāḥ in Paradise."** The narration is transmitted with varying wordings and its chain is discussed by the hadith critics. **It should be cited as a report, not as a *ṣaḥīḥ* hadith.**

**But the detail to hold is the wife's answer.** Her husband had just given away the only property they owned, with her and their children standing in it, and she said: ***rabiḥa bay'uka*** — **your trade has profited.**

That is 2:245 believed. **Not "let's discuss this." Not "what about the children." She heard the verse, heard what he had done with it, and called it a profitable sale.** A man and a woman both understood that the Borrower was good for it.

**And there is a third narration that belongs here, in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s**, from Abū Hurayrah: **"Whoever gives in charity the equivalent of a date from lawful earnings — and God accepts only what is lawful — God takes it in His right hand and tends it for its owner as one of you tends his foal, until it becomes like the mountain of Uḥud."**

**The "right hand" is an attribute of God, and it is affirmed as it came** — without likening it to the hand of a creature and without denying it, which is the rule this commentary has applied to every attribute in the surah. **And the image is the verse's economics in miniature: the smallest lawful gift, taken by God and grown until it is a mountain.**

**Two further hadith worth having.** In *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*: **"Charity does not decrease wealth."** And in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*: **"Protect yourselves from the Fire even with half a date."**

**For practice.** This is the only investment instrument in which the principal is guaranteed, the return is unspecified because it is beyond calculation, and the Borrower cannot default. **The one condition is *ḥasanan* — that it be goodly.** Which means: from what you earned lawfully, given willingly, and never mentioned again. **A person who gives and then reminds has voided the adjective while keeping the noun.**

## Verse 246

**"Have you not seen those chiefs of the Children of Israel after Moses? They said to one of their prophets, “Appoint for us a king, ˹and˺ we will fight in the cause of Allah.” He said, “Are you not going to cower if ordered to fight?” They replied, “How could we refuse to fight in the cause of Allah, while we were driven out of our homes and ˹separated from˺ our children?” But when they were ordered to fight, they fled, except for a few of them. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers."**

In simple terms: have you not considered the notables of the Children of Israel after Moses, when they said to a prophet of theirs, "Send us a king and we will fight in the path of God"? He said: "Might you not, if fighting were prescribed for you, fail to fight?" They said: "And why should we not fight in the path of God, when we have been driven out of our homes and our children?" But when fighting was prescribed for them, they turned away — except a few of them. And God is Knowing of the wrongdoers.

*A-lam tara ilā al-mala'i min banī isrā'īla min ba'di Mūsā idh qālū li-nabiyyin lahumu ib'ath lanā malikan nuqātil fī sabīli Allāh, qāla hal 'asaytum in kutiba 'alaykumu al-qitālu allā tuqātilū, qālū wa-mā lanā allā nuqātila fī sabīli Allāhi wa qad ukhrijnā min diyārinā wa abnā'inā, fa-lammā kutiba 'alayhimu al-qitālu tawallaw illā qalīlan minhum, wa Allāhu 'alīmun bi-al-ẓālimīn.*

**A caution before anything else.** The long accounts of this episode in the tafsir literature — the detailed narrative of what happened after Moses, the condition of the Israelites, the worship of idols, the interview with the prophet — come substantially through **Wahb ibn Munabbih**, who is one of the principal conduits of *Isrā'īliyyāt* and whose reports the hadith critics and the commentators have always treated with caution. **Ibn Kathīr transmits these accounts and then, characteristically, flags them.** I will cite from them where they illuminate, and mark them as reports throughout. **What the verse states is what is binding; the elaboration is not.**

*A-lam tara ilā **al-mala'i** min banī isrā'īla* — "have you not considered the **notables** of the Children of Israel?"

***Al-mala'*** — the assembly of chiefs, the council of notables, the people who matter. **Note that the request comes from the elite, not from the crowd.** And note what that sets up: this is a request that will turn out to be hollow, made by the people with the most standing to lose.

*"Min ba'di Mūsā"* — after Moses. So in the period of the judges, before the monarchy.

*Idh qālū li-**nabiyyin lahumu*** — "when they said to **a prophet of theirs**."

**Note: the Qur'an does not name him.** Mujāhid said he is **Shamwīl (Samuel)**, and that is the standard identification in the tafsir. Some reports name others. **The identification is transmitted, not textual.** And the anonymity is itself worth noticing in a book that names so many prophets: **this account is not about the prophet. It is about the people and what they did with their own request.**

*Ib'ath lanā **malikan** nuqātil fī sabīli Allāh* — "send us a **king**, and we will fight in the path of God."

***Malik*** — a king. Note that they are asking for a *political* office, not a religious one, and they are asking for it in religious language.

**Now the prophet's answer, and it is one of the most important sentences in the batch.**

*Qāla hal 'asaytum in kutiba 'alaykumu al-qitālu **allā tuqātilū*** — "He said: **might you not**, if fighting were prescribed for you, **fail to fight**?"

**He does not say "excellent."** They have come with a pious request, in the correct vocabulary, on real grounds — and the prophet's first response is to question whether they will do it.

**That is the pattern of the Qur'an throughout, and it has already appeared in this surah:** at 2:214, "or did you suppose you would enter Paradise without the like of what came to those before you?" — a confident assumption met with a question. **When someone makes a confident religious declaration, the Qur'an asks whether the cost has been counted.** The prophet of Israel does the same thing here. He is not cynical; he is a shepherd who has watched this flock before.

**And note the verb he uses: *in kutiba 'alaykum al-qitāl*.** ***Kutiba*** — "if it were **prescribed** for you." **That is the exact verb of 2:216** — "**prescribed** for you is fighting, though you dislike it." The Qur'an is holding a mirror up to its own audience: **these people asked for the thing that has been prescribed for you, and then did not do it. And you?**

*Qālū wa-mā lanā allā nuqātila fī sabīli Allāhi wa qad ukhrijnā min diyārinā wa abnā'inā* — "They said: **and why should we not fight** in the path of God, when we have been driven out of our homes and our children?"

**Two things here.**

**The first: they answer indignation with a rhetorical question of their own.** *Wa-mā lanā allā nuqātila* — "what is wrong with us, that we should not fight?" **The suggestion that they might hang back is received as an insult.** That is how confident people sound when they are about to fail.

**The second: look at what they actually name.** *Ukhrijnā min **diyārinā wa abnā'inā*** — "we have been driven out of our **homes** and our **children**." **Their stated grievance is territorial and familial — their houses and their offspring — and they have dressed it in the language of *fī sabīli Allāh*.**

**I want to be careful and fair here rather than clever.** The two are not necessarily in tension: a people driven from their homes and separated from their children by an enemy *can* have a genuinely religious cause. The Qur'an itself uses this language for the Muslims at 2:217 ("expelling the worshippers from the Sacred Mosque"). **But the verse records precisely the words they chose, and the reader is being invited to notice the order of their reasons — and then to watch what happens next.**

*Fa-lammā kutiba 'alayhimu al-qitālu **tawallaw illā qalīlan minhum*** — "but when fighting was prescribed for them, **they turned away, except a few of them**."

**And it collapses.** *Tawallaw* — they turned their backs. The same verb used throughout the surah for those who turn away from what they have been given (2:64, 2:205, and here).

***Illā qalīlan minhum*** — "except a few of them." **The first of the batch's two "few."** And note that the exception is stated in the very clause that records the failure: **the defection is total, and the remnant is small.**

*Wa Allāhu 'alīmun bi-**al-ẓālimīn*** — "and God is **Knowing of the wrongdoers**."

**Note the closing attribute is knowledge, not punishment.** *'Alīm bi-al-ẓālimīn* — He knows the wrongdoers. **The gap between what they said and what they did is fully known, and fully recorded.** That is the warning the verse leaves, and it is aimed at anyone who has ever made a promise to God in confident language.

**The lesson, and it is one of the most useful in the whole surah for a religious person.** The request was pious. The vocabulary was correct. The grievance was real. **And they still did not do it.** Saying "we will" is not doing. **The prophet asked the right question, the people gave the wrong answer, and the verse records both.**

**The contemporary forms are everywhere.** The person who says "I'll change" and doesn't. The community that resolves loudly in the mosque on Friday and does nothing on Saturday. The convert's first year of promises. The "in shā' Allāh" that means no. **2:246 is the verse for the gap between declaration and performance, and it does not treat the declaration as worthless — it treats it as a debt.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 247

**"Their prophet told them, “Allah has appointed Saul to be your king.” They protested, “How can he be our king when some of us are more deserving of kingship than he, and he has not been blessed with vast riches?” He replied, “Allah has chosen him over you and blessed him with knowledge and stature. Allah grants kingship to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.”"**

In simple terms: and their prophet said to them: God has sent you Ṭālūt as king. They said: how can he have kingship over us, when we are more entitled to kingship than he, and he has not been given amplitude of wealth? He said: God has chosen him over you, and increased him in breadth of knowledge and stature. God gives His kingship to whom He wills; and God is All-Encompassing, Knowing.

*Wa qāla lahum nabiyyuhum inna Allāha qad ba'atha lakum Ṭālūta malikan, qālū annā yakūnu lahu al-mulku 'alaynā wa naḥnu aḥaqqu bi-al-mulki minhu wa lam yu'ta sa'atan mina al-māl, qāla inna Allāha iṣṭafāhu 'alaykum wa zādahu basṭatan fī al-'ilmi wa al-jismi, wa Allāhu yu'tī mulkahu man yashā'u, wa Allāhu wāsi'un 'alīm.*

*Inna Allāha qad **ba'atha** lakum Ṭālūta malikan* — "God has **sent** you Ṭālūt as king."

**Note the verb: *ba'atha* — sent, raised up.** The same verb used for the sending of prophets. **The appointment is God's, not the prophet's.** Which pre-empts the suspicion recorded in some commentaries — that the prophet had chosen him himself. **He did not. He was sent.**

*Ṭālūt* is Saul. **The Qur'an gives a name not found in this form in the Bible; the identification is transmitted.** And one caution worth stating: **the Qur'an does not say Ṭālūt was a prophet.** He is called a *malik* — a king. Appointment to rule does not entail prophethood, and the two should not be conflated.

*Qālū **annā yakūnu lahu al-mulku 'alaynā*** — "They said: **how can he have kingship over us**?"

**And now their two objections, and both are pure status:**

**1. Lineage: *wa naḥnu aḥaqqu bi-al-mulki minhu*** — "we are more entitled to kingship than he." The commentators explain the claim: **kingship among them was in the house of Judah, and prophethood in the house of Levi — and Ṭālūt was from neither.** The reports describe him as a soldier, and in some accounts a tanner or a water-carrier. **His objection, in their mouths, is that he is not from the right family.**

**2. Wealth: *wa lam yu'ta sa'atan mina al-māl*** — "and he has not been given amplitude of wealth." He is not rich.

**Now stop and look at what they did *not* object to.** They did not say he is irreligious. They did not say he is unjust. They did not say he is incompetent, or cowardly, or a bad man. **Their two objections are birth and money.**

**That is the single most diagnostic thing in this verse, and it is why the verse matters so much:** it shows a community selecting a leader on exactly the two criteria that have nothing to do with the job.

*Qāla inna Allāha **iṣṭafāhu 'alaykum*** — "He said: God has **chosen** him over you."

***Iṣṭafā*** — chose, elected, selected and purified. It is the vocabulary of divine election throughout the Qur'an: Adam and the family of Ibrāhīm (3:33), Maryam (3:42), Mūsā (7:144). **To say God has *iṣṭafā* him is to say the choice is not negotiable and not to be second-guessed on human criteria.**

*Wa zādahu **basṭatan fī al-'ilmi wa al-jismi*** — "and increased him in **breadth of knowledge and of stature**."

**And here are the counter-criteria — two, matching their two — and they are exactly the two things the objectors did not mention:**

- ***'Ilm*** — knowledge.
- ***Jism*** — physique, stature. The reports describe him as tall and striking, and some commentators extend *jism* to presence and capacity generally. Both readings held.

**Note the order: knowledge first, then stature.** And note the structure of the rebuttal: **God does not defend Ṭālūt on their terms.** He does not say "actually he is well-born" or "actually he has money." **He replaces the criteria.** You said lineage and wealth; I say knowledge and capacity. **That is the whole answer, and it is a standing rule for how communities should choose leaders.**

*Wa Allāhu **yu'tī mulkahu man yashā'u*** — "and God **gives His kingship to whom He wills**."

**This is the theological centre of the verse, and it destroys the objection at its root.** Kingship is not inherited, not bought, not owed to a family. ***Mulk* is His, and He gives it to whom He wills.** The parallel is 3:26: "Say: O God, Possessor of kingship, You give kingship to whom You will and You take kingship from whom You will."

**Which means: the question was never "is he from the right family?" The question is "whom has God given it to?"** Once authority is understood as a gift rather than a possession, lineage and wealth become irrelevant to it.

*Wa Allāhu **wāsi'un 'alīm*** — "and God is **All-Encompassing, Knowing**."

**And the closing answers both objections one more time.** *Wāsi'* — vast, boundless in bounty and capacity — **so He is not short of wealth to give, and the fact that Ṭālūt is poor now says nothing.** *'Alīm* — Knowing — **so the choice is not a mistake, and the objection that He did not know better is closed.**

**For practice, this verse is a standing rule for how a community chooses — and rejects — its leaders.** A community that selects on lineage and wealth and dismisses knowledge and competence has inverted the criteria this verse lays down. And the converse is also true and more uncomfortable: **a community that rejects a qualified person because he is not from the right family, or not rich enough, has reproduced the objection of 2:247 exactly.** The verse records the objection in order to have it answered in God's own words, permanently.

**And there is a second application that is more personal.** Most of us have been Ṭālūt — passed over by people who thought they were more entitled. **The verse's answer to that is not "they were wrong." It is *Allāhu yu'tī mulkahu man yashā'*.** What has been given to you was given; what has not, has not. **The One who gives is *Wāsi'* and *'Alīm*, which means neither His resources nor His judgement is the problem.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 248

**"Their prophet further told them, “The sign of Saul’s kingship is that the Ark will come to you—containing reassurance from your Lord and relics of the family of Moses and the family of Aaron, which will be carried by the angels. Surely in this is a sign for you, if you ˹truly˺ believe.”"**

In simple terms: and their prophet said to them: the sign of his kingship is that the Ark will come to you, in it a *sakīnah* from your Lord, and a remnant of what the family of Moses and the family of Aaron left, carried by the angels. Indeed in that is a sign for you, if you are believers.

*Wa qāla lahum nabiyyuhum inna āyata mulkihi an ya'tiyakumu al-tābūtu fīhi sakīnatun min rabbikum wa baqiyyatun mimmā taraka ālu Mūsā wa ālu Hārūna taḥmiluhu al-malā'ikatu, inna fī dhālika la-āyatan lakum in kuntum mu'minīn.*

*Inna āyata mulkihi an ya'tiyakumu **al-tābūtu*** — "the sign of his kingship is that **the Ark** will come to you."

***Al-tābūt*** — the chest, the Ark. **And note what kind of sign this is.** They asked for a king on the criteria of lineage and wealth; God had answered with knowledge and capacity; **and now He gives them a sign that is not on their criteria at all.** It is not "he will be richer." It is not "his pedigree will be confirmed." **It is: something is going to happen that only God can do.**

**That is the answer to a demand for credentials.** The people wanted a credential they could check. God gives them a miracle they cannot.

*Fīhi **sakīnatun** min rabbikum* — "in it is a ***sakīnah*** from your Lord."

**This is the word to linger on, and it is one of the great words of the Qur'an.**

*Sakīnah* is from *sakana* — to be still, to settle, to dwell. **It is the calm that comes down and stays.** And it appears five times in the Qur'an: here, at 9:26 and 9:40 (**the cave** — "then God sent down His *sakīnah* upon him and supported him with soldiers you did not see"), and at 48:4, 48:18 and 48:26 (**al-Ḥudaybiyyah** — "He it is who sent down the *sakīnah* into the hearts of the believers").

**And there is a pattern in every single instance: the *sakīnah* comes down in a moment of fear, and it is always God's, always "sent down," and always the thing that holds people still when they would otherwise break.**

**Which means the sign that comes with the appointment of a king who is about to lead them into battle is: calm.** Not weapons. Not numbers. **Reassurance.** And that is exactly what an army about to face Goliath needs before anything else.

**There is a lesson here about what God sends first.** When the Israelites asked for a commander, they were thinking about the enemy. **God sent them the thing that would stop them shaking.** That is the right order, and it is the same order as 2:250's supplication — patience first, then firm feet, then victory.

*Wa **baqiyyatun** mimmā taraka ālu Mūsā wa ālu Hārūn* — "and a **remnant** of what the family of Moses and the family of Aaron left."

**Now the reports, and they are almost entirely *Isrā'īliyyāt* and they vary enormously.** Transmitted accounts of the Ark's contents include: fragments of the tablets of the Torah, the staff of Moses, his sandals or garments, a measure of the manna, Aaron's turban, and Aaron's rod that had budded. **None of this is established and none of it should be asserted.**

**What the text says is: "a remnant."** And that word is itself the point. ***Baqiyyah*** is what survives of something that has been broken. **The Israelites had lost the Ark more than once; their history had been interrupted; they had just been driven out of their homes and separated from their children (2:246).** And the sign that comes with their new king is **the return of what was left of Moses and Aaron.**

**That is a restoration of continuity.** A people that has lost its homes is being given back its past. **The sign is not only that God can do a miracle. It is that He is putting them back in their own story.**

*Taḥmiluhu **al-malā'ikatu*** — "carried by **the angels**."

Ibn Jurayj reported from Ibn 'Abbās: **the angels came down carrying the Ark between the sky and the earth until they set it down before Ṭālūt, with the people watching.** This is a transmitted report; flag it as such.

**But note what it accomplishes in the argument.** The objection was *how can he have kingship over us?* **The answer is a public, visible, angelic delivery.** The appointment is ratified in front of the objectors, by beings who do not make mistakes about whom God has sent. **There is no room left for the question.**

*Inna fī dhālika la-āyatan lakum **in kuntum mu'minīn*** — "indeed in that is a sign for you, **if you are believers**."

**Note the condition, and do not misread it.** The sign is a sign *for a believer*. This is not circular reasoning and it is not a bait-and-switch. It is the Qur'an's consistent account of how evidence works, stated identically at 2:118: **"We have made the signs clear for a people who have certainty."**

**The signs are there, objectively, for everyone. What differs is whether they function *as* signs for the person looking at them.** A person demanding a sign that will compel him will not get one — not because God cannot give it, but because a sign that removes the possibility of refusal is not a sign, it is coercion, and it would end the period of testing (2:210, 6:158). **The Ark was carried by angels in front of everyone, and some still went home.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 249

**"When Saul marched forth with his army, he cautioned: “Allah will test you with a river. So whoever drinks ˹his fill˺ from it is not with me, and whoever does not taste it—except a sip from the hollow of his hands—is definitely with me.” They all drank ˹their fill˺ except for a few! When he and the ˹remaining˺ faithful with him crossed the river, they said, “Now we are no match for Goliath and his warriors.” But those ˹believers˺ who were certain they would meet Allah reasoned, “How many times has a small force vanquished a mighty army by the Will of Allah! And Allah is ˹always˺ with the steadfast.”"**

In simple terms: and when Ṭālūt set out with the soldiers, he said: God is going to test you with a river. So whoever drinks from it is not of me, and whoever does not taste it — except one who scoops a single handful with his hand — is of me. But they drank from it, except a few of them. Then when he crossed it, he and those who believed with him, they said: "We have no capacity today against Jālūt and his soldiers." Those who were certain that they would meet God said: "How often has a small company overcome a large company by God's permission! And God is with the steadfast."

*Fa-lammā faṣala Ṭālūtu bi-al-junūdi qāla inna Allāha mubtalīkum bi-naharin, fa-man shariba minhu fa-laysa minnī wa-man lam yaṭ'amhu fa-innahu minnī illā mani ightarafa ghurfatan bi-yadihi, fa-sharibū minhu illā qalīlan minhum, fa-lammā jāwazahu huwa wa alladhīna āmanū ma'ahu qālū lā ṭāqata lanā al-yawma bi-Jālūta wa junūdihi, qāla alladhīna yaẓunnūna annahum mulāqū Allāhi kam min fi'atin qalīlatin ghalabat fi'atan kathīratan bi-idhni Allāh, wa Allāhu ma'a al-ṣābirīn.*

*Qāla inna Allāha **mubtalīkum bi-naharin*** — "he said: God is **testing you with a river**."

**The word is *mubtalī* — testing.** The root is *balā'*, the same as 2:155 ("We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger") and 2:214. **So the first thing to understand is that this is not logistics. It is an examination.** The river is not an obstacle on the way to the battle; **the river *is* the battle.**

*Fa-man shariba minhu **fa-laysa minnī** wa-man lam yaṭ'amhu **fa-innahu minnī*** — "whoever drinks from it is **not of me**, and whoever does not taste it is **of me**."

**Note the starkness.** Not "will be punished" — ***is not of me***. The test divides the army into two groups by a single act, and Ṭālūt identifies himself with the outcome. **Drinking is not a minor infraction. It is disclosure.**

***Illā mani ightarafa ghurfatan bi-yadihi*** — "**except the one who scoops a single handful with his hand**."

**And this exception is the whole verse.**

**A test with no exception would be a test of endurance — can you go without? A test with a one-handful exception is a test of *obedience* — can you stop at the line?** And that is a far harder and far more realistic examination. **Any army can be ordered to abstain entirely. The real question is whether a thirsty man standing in a river will take exactly one handful and no more.**

**And that is where most moral failure actually happens.** Almost no one falls by openly rejecting a prohibition. **People fall by taking slightly more than was allowed, when no one is watching and the river is right there.** The verse legislates the exact boundary precisely so that the boundary can be crossed — and then watches who crosses it.

***Ghurfatan*** — one scoop, from *gharafa*. **One handful.** Not a cup, not a waterskin, not "a little." **One.** The reports add that most of those who drank filled themselves.

*Fa-sharibū minhu **illā qalīlan minhum*** — "and they drank from it, **except a few of them**."

**The second "few" of the batch, in the same words as 2:246 (*illā qalīlan minhum*).** The surah is counting, and the arithmetic is consistent: **a few kept the covenant at 2:246, and a few kept the discipline at 2:249.**

The transmitted reports give numbers for those who crossed — variously, with some reports giving a figure in the low hundreds. **The number is transmitted and disputed; the text says only *qalīl*, and that is what should be held.** The vagueness is deliberate: **the point is the proportion, not the census.**

*Fa-lammā jāwazahu huwa wa alladhīna āmanū ma'ahu qālū **lā ṭāqata lanā** al-yawma bi-Jālūta wa junūdih* — "and when he crossed, he and those who believed with him, they said: **we have no capacity today** against Jālūt and his soldiers."

**Two things about this, and both are important.**

**First: the despair comes *after* the test, not before.** They passed. They obeyed. They crossed. **And then they looked at the enemy and their nerve went.** Passing the test did not make them confident. **Obedience is not a feeling; it is an act, and the fear can arrive afterwards.**

**Second: note that the verse records their words without rebuking them.** *Lā ṭāqata lanā* — "we have no capacity." ***Ṭāqah*** — capacity, the ability to bear. **This is the same root as 2:286's closing prayer ("Lord, do not burden us with what we have no capacity to bear") and as *lā tukallafu nafsun illā wus'ahā* (2:233, 2:286).** They are describing their real state accurately. **They are not being told they were wrong to feel it.** They were outnumbered, and they said so.

*Qāla alladhīna **yaẓunnūna annahum mulāqū Allāh*** — "said those who **were certain that they would meet God**..."

**And here is the verse's defining distinction.** The remnant is not described as the bravest, the strongest, or the best-equipped. **It is described as: those who are certain they will meet God.**

A note on the verb: *yaẓunnūna* can mean "suppose" or "think," but *ẓann* in the Qur'an carries both doubt and firm conviction, and context decides. Here it is certainty — as at 2:46 (*those who are certain that they will meet their Lord*) and 69:20. **The same people described at 2:46 as those who are certain of the meeting are the ones who hold the line here.**

**And that is the verse's central insight, and it is the answer to the despair: the people who do not collapse are the people oriented toward a meeting.** Not the more numerous, not the better armed, not the less frightened. **The ones who remember where this is going.**

*Kam min **fi'atin qalīlatin** ghalabat fi'atan kathīratan **bi-idhni Allāh*** — "**how often has a small company overcome a large company by God's permission!**"

***Kam*** — not a question but *kam al-khabariyyah*, the *kam* of profusion: **how many times!** The same device as 2:211 ("how many a clear sign We gave them"). **This is not presented as a rare marvel. It is presented as a recurring pattern.**

***Bi-idhni Allāh*** — "**by God's permission.**" **The victory is attributed to permission, not to numbers, strategy, or courage.** That is the verse's entire epistemology of victory, and it cuts both ways:

- It is the ground of hope for anyone outnumbered.
- **And it is the check on presumption: if the victory is *bi-idhnillāh*, it is not a promise that small numbers always win, and it is never a licence to be unprepared, untrained, or reckless.** The permission is His to give or withhold, and the people who invoke this line while neglecting the means are quoting it against itself.

*Wa Allāhu **ma'a al-ṣābirīn*** — "and God is **with the steadfast**."

**This clause appears verbatim at 2:153:** "O you who believe, seek help through patience and prayer; **God is with the steadfast**." The surah stated it as a promise when legislating the test of 2:155, **and now it restates it, in the same words, at the moment of the test itself.**

**That is a bookend worth having.** 2:153 told the believers *God is with the patient*. 2:249 shows a small, frightened, outnumbered remnant standing on it. **The promise and the demonstration are separated by ninety-six verses, and the surah means you to connect them.**

**And on the *ma'iyyah* — the "with-ness" of God — the rule is the same one I have applied throughout this commentary:** it is affirmed as it came, **without likening it to the presence of a creature** (God is not spatially located beside the patient) **and without denying it** (it is a real "with": His aid, His support, His protection, His knowledge encompassing them). The Salaf's formula holds: *bi-lā kayf* — without asking how — and without stripping the meaning.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 250

**"When they advanced to face Goliath and his warriors, they prayed, “Our Lord! Shower us with perseverance, make our steps firm, and give us victory over the disbelieving people.”"**

In simple terms: and when they went forth against Jālūt and his soldiers, they said: "Our Lord, pour out patience upon us, and make firm our feet, and give us victory over the disbelieving people."

*Wa lammā barazū li-Jālūta wa junūdihi qālū rabbanā afrigh 'alaynā ṣabran wa thabbit aqdāmana wa-nṣurnā 'alā al-qawmi al-kāfirīn.*

**This is one of the great supplications of the Qur'an, and the order of its three petitions is the whole lesson.**

***Rabbanā afrigh 'alaynā ṣabran*** — "Our Lord, **pour out** patience upon us."

***Afrigh*** is from *afrāgha* — to pour out, to empty a vessel over something. **Patience is asked for as a liquid, poured from above.**

And that image carries three things at once:

- **It comes from outside them.** They do not say "we will be patient." They say *pour it on us*. **Patience is not produced by the will. It is given.**
- **It is asked for in abundance.** You pour; you do not drip. *Ṣabran* is indefinite and unmeasured.
- **And it is asked for *upon* them** — *'alaynā* — covering, not merely placed within.

**So the first petition is an admission:** we do not have this, and we know it cannot be manufactured. **The people who have just been described as "certain they will meet God" (2:249) are the ones praying to be given patience.** Certainty about the meeting and self-distrust about the endurance are not in tension. **They go together.**

***Wa thabbit aqdāmanā*** — "and **make firm our feet**."

**The second petition is not for victory. It is for standing.**

*Thabbit* — make firm, fix, establish. ***Aqdām*** — feet, and by extension footing, position, the line. **The image is of a rank that does not break.** They are asking not to be moved.

**And note the order: patience first (the interior), then firmness of foot (the exterior).** The heart is settled first, and then the stance follows. **You cannot hold a line with an unsettled heart.**

**And note something more pointed.** Before asking to win, they ask **not to run.** Their stated fear is not defeat. **It is breaking.** That is the right ordering, and it is the opposite of how most people pray before a hard thing — we ask for the outcome and skip the character required to survive it. **A person given a victory he cannot bear is not blessed.**

***Wa-nṣurnā 'alā al-qawmi al-kāfirīn*** — "and **give us victory** over the disbelieving people."

**The victory is asked last.** After the capacity to endure, after the firmness to stand. **The sequence is: make us able, then let us win.**

**And note that this is the verse's answer to 2:249's despair.** In 2:249 they said *lā ṭāqata lanā* — "we have no capacity." **In 2:250 they take that exact incapacity to God and ask for it to be supplied.** The complaint and the prayer are about the same thing. **The difference is who they are addressed to.**

**Now note how the verse is constructed, and this is worth naming explicitly: the verse ends on the prayer. Not on the outcome.**

**The surah leaves the believing remnant in the posture of asking, and the reader has to wait for the answer.** That is a deliberate compositional choice. **It puts the reader in the same position as the army: standing in front of Goliath, having asked, not yet knowing.** The victory — and the killing of Jālūt at the hands of Dāwūd — comes in 2:251, the next verse, and I will handle it there rather than anticipating it.

**But the parallel to Badr should be drawn now, because the commentators have always drawn it.** At Badr the Muslims were the small company facing the large, and the Prophet ﷺ stood in supplication. Recorded in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* from 'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb: the Prophet ﷺ looked at the polytheists — a thousand strong — and his Companions were three hundred and some, and he faced the *qiblah* and prayed: **"O God, grant me what You have promised me. O God, bring about what You have promised me. O God, if this band of the people of Islam is destroyed, You will not be worshipped on earth."** And he kept at it until his cloak fell from his shoulders, and Abū Bakr picked it up and said: **"O Prophet of God, that is enough of your supplicating your Lord; He will fulfil for you what He has promised."**

**The pattern of 2:249 is the pattern of Badr: the small company, the larger army, the permission of God, and the prayer that precedes the outcome.** I cite the Badr supplication as a *thematic* parallel — **I do not claim that the Prophet ﷺ recited this specific verse at Badr, and I have found no sound narration for that.**

**What can be said without qualification is that this *du'ā* is one of the most valuable things a believer can memorise, and that its order is the lesson.** When you are standing in front of something you cannot handle: **first ask to be made able to bear it, then ask not to run, and only then ask to win.**

**And note the last detail: the supplication is plural.** *Afrigh 'alaynā... thabbit aqdāmanā... wa-nṣurnā* — **upon *us*, our feet, give *us* victory.** They are few, and they pray as one. **The remnant does not pray alone.**

## Synthesis: Verses 241–250

**The arc.** Two verses close the marriage law (241–242). Three short verses then turn to death, war, and money (243–245). Then five verses open the narrative of Ṭālūt, the Ark, the river, and the prayer before Goliath (246–250).

**The hinge is 243–244, and the logic is easy to miss.** 2:243 is a people who fled death and died anyway. 2:244 is the command to go out and fight. **The two are not merely adjacent; the second is the conclusion of the first.** *Since flight does not save you, stop organising your life around survival.* And then 2:245 completes the thought: **since God withholds and extends, stop organising your life around provision either.** Three verses, three anxieties — death, danger, money — each answered the same way: **the thing you are protecting is not yours to protect, and the One who holds it is the One you are being asked to trust.**

**The governing motif of the narrative half is *qalīl* — "a few."** It appears twice, in identical words, four verses apart: at 2:246 they promised and **a few** did not turn away; at 2:249 they were tested and **a few** did not drink. **The surah is doing arithmetic, and the sum is not flattering.** The many declare; the few perform. And the few are never described as braver or stronger — at 2:249 they are described as **those who are certain they will meet God.**

**And the second governing motif is the replacement of criteria.** At 2:247 the notables object on lineage and wealth, and God answers with **knowledge and capacity**, and then removes the ground of the objection altogether: ***Allāhu yu'tī mulkahu man yashā'*** — God gives authority to whom He wills. At 2:248 the sign they are given is not a credential they can check but a miracle they cannot produce. **In both cases the answer is not a defence on their terms. It is a change of terms.**

**Two threads connect back across the surah and are worth holding.** First: **"God is with the steadfast" appears verbatim at 2:153 and 2:249** — stated as a promise when the test was legislated, and restated at the moment of the test itself. Second: the closing faculties of the three legislative blocks form a sequence — **reflect** (2:219), **remember** (2:221), **reason** (2:242) — and the last of them is a warrant for reading the law as a system rather than a list.

**A caution that must be restated because it governs 246–250.** This is the most *Isrā'īliyyāt*-exposed passage in the surah so far. The identity of the prophet, the number who fled in 2:243, the name of the place, the contents of the Ark, the number who crossed the river, and the location of the river are **all transmitted reports that vary between themselves**, many reaching us through Wahb ibn Munabbih and comparable channels. **The Qur'anic text is deliberately spare** — it names a prophet without naming him, says "a remnant" without listing it, and says "a few" where the reports say a number. **Hold the text firmly and the reports loosely.** Every reported detail in this batch has been flagged as reported.

**Where the supplied translation resolves an ambiguity, it has been marked.** "Saul" for Ṭālūt and "Goliath" for Jālūt are the transmitted identifications. The supplied rendering of 2:245 as "decreases and increases" takes the *qabḍ*/*basṭ* clause as provision, which is the primary sense; the creedal reading has been noted alongside it. And at 2:249 the supplied rendering "those who were certain" correctly takes *yaẓunnūna* as conviction rather than doubt, which the context and 2:46 require.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: whether the *mut'ah* of 2:241 is obligatory or recommended and whom it covers; the identity of the prophet in 2:246; whether *jism* in 2:247 means physical stature or capacity; whether *yaqbiḍu wa yabsuṭu* refers to provision or is read more broadly as an attribute; and the circumstances, number, place and prophet of the people of 2:243.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout, and with especial care in 2:243 and 2:246–249, where the material is largely *Isrā'īliyyāt*-derived: the four thousand of Dāwardān near Wāsiṭ; the two angels crying out from either end of the valley; the name of the prophet who supplicated for their revival; the contents of the Ark; the number of those who crossed the river; and the long narrative attributed to Wahb ibn Munabbih. **None of these is in the text, and none should be asserted.** The narration of Abū al-Daḥdāḥ has been flagged as transmitted with a discussed chain rather than as a *ṣaḥīḥ* hadith. And the parallel between 2:249–250 and Badr has been drawn thematically, with the explicit note that I have no sound narration for the claim that the Prophet ﷺ recited this verse or this supplication at Badr.

Three boundaries have been observed. On 2:245: the "right hand" of God in the charity hadith has been affirmed as it came, without *tashbīh* or *ta'tīl*, consistently with the treatment of every attribute earlier in this surah. On 2:244: the command to fight has again been fenced in by 2:190–193 and 8:61, because this verse more than most is quoted in the service of what it does not permit. And on 2:249: the promise that a small company may overcome a large one has been held together with the phrase that governs it — ***bi-idhni Allāh*** — so that it reads as ground for hope and never as a licence for recklessness or a guarantee that numbers are irrelevant.

The most important practical sentence in the batch is in 2:250, and it is the order of its three requests: **pour out patience upon us, make firm our feet, give us victory.** The believing remnant asked to be made able before they asked to win — and the surah closes the verse on the prayer rather than on the answer, so that every reader since has had to stand where they stood and ask in the same order.
