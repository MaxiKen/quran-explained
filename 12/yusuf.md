# Sūrah 12 — Yūsuf (Joseph)

*111 verses; Makkan. **[Maʿārif]** With the exception of four verses it is wholly a Makkan sūrah, and in it
the story of the Prophet Yūsuf is described with continuity and order — and it appears in this sūrah alone,
nowhere repeated in the whole Qurʾān as such, the only exceptions being 6:84 and 40:34, where merely his
name is mentioned as a Messenger of God in an appropriate context. **[Study Quran]** It is the third in a
series of six sūrahs whose opening verses include the letters alif, lām, rāʾ and speak of the Book, and it
belongs to the Makkan period; it recounts the entire story of the prophet Joseph and so constitutes the
longest and most continuous single narrative in the Qurʾān (vv. 3–101), whose general outline corresponds to
Genesis 37:1–46:7, though with several significant differences from the biblical account. **[Qurṭubī]** The
occasion reported for its revelation is that the Jews of Madīnah sent men to Makkah to test the Prophet ﷺ:
they asked him, "Tell us about a man among the prophets who was in Syria, whose son was taken out to Egypt,
and who wept for him until he went blind" — and there was then no one of the People of the Book in Makkah
and no one who knew the reports of the prophets — so God sent down Sūrat Yūsuf entire, containing everything
that is in the Torah about it and more, and that was a sign for the Prophet ﷺ in the rank of Jesusʾ raising
of the dead. **[Ṭabarī]** **[Ibn Kathīr]** Other reports say the Companions asked the Prophet ﷺ to narrate
stories to them, and "We relate to you the best of stories" (12:3) came down in answer — one report adds
that they first grew bored and asked for speech, and God sent down "God has sent down the best of speech"
(39:23); then they asked for something beyond speech yet less than the Qurʾān, meaning narratives, and
12:1–3 was revealed: they wanted speech, so He guided them to the best of speech; they wanted stories, so
He guided them to the best of stories. **[Qurṭubī]** The scholars differed as to why this story was named
the best of stories: because no story in the Qurʾān contains such lessons and wisdoms; because of Yūsufʾs
magnanimous pardon of his brothers, his patience under their harm and his nobility until he said "no
reproach upon you this day" (12:92); because it mentions prophets and the righteous, angels and devils, jinn
and men, cattle and birds, the conduct of kings and kingdoms, merchants and scholars and the ignorant, men
and women and their wiles — together with tawḥīd, fiqh, sīrah, the interpretation of dreams, politics,
social conduct and the management of livelihood, benefits enough for religion and for the world; because it
tells of the beloved and the loved one; because aḥsan here means "most wondrous"; or, as some of the people
of meanings held, because everyone mentioned in it came in the end to happiness — Yūsuf, his father, his
brothers, the wife of the ʿAzīz, the king who is said to have accepted Islam through Yūsuf, the cupbearer and
the witness, so that the affair of them all issued in good.*

**The sūrah.** It opens with the letters and the claim that these are the verses of the Book that makes
things clear, sent down in Arabic so that its hearers may understand, and with the announcement that God
will relate to His Messenger the best of stories (12:1–3). The narrative then runs unbroken for some
ninety-eight verses: the dream and the fatherʾs warning (12:4–6), the brothersʾ envy and their plot
(12:7–14), the well and the caravan and the sale in Egypt (12:15–22), the attempt of the ʿAzīzʾs wife and
Yūsufʾs refusal, the witnesses and the women of the city and the prison (12:23–35), the two dreams in the
prison and their interpretation (12:36–49), the kingʾs dream, Yūsufʾs exoneration and his rise to the
treasury of Egypt (12:50–57), the brothersʾ two journeys for grain and the detention of Binyāmīn
(12:58–93), Yaʿqūbʾs grief and his blindness and his unbroken hope (12:84–87), the shirt, the recovery of
sight, the journey to Egypt and the prostration that fulfils the dream (12:93–100); and the sūrah closes
with Yūsufʾs prayer and with the lesson drawn for the Prophet ﷺ and for all who have minds (12:101–111).
**[Maʿārif]** The Qurʾān treats history not as an end in itself but as instruction: whatever part of a
story serves to drive home a lesson or tender good counsel on a given occasion is the part mentioned there,
and it is repeated where it is needed again, so that the earlier part of a story sometimes comes later and
the later part earlier — a standing rule that reading or remembering world history is not the goal, but
that every person should draw a lesson from every story and cull advice from every piece of information;
speech divides into the descriptive (*khabar*) and the imperative (*inshāʾ*), and the imperative is the
real objective, so the wise man learns an imperative from every description and uses it to reform himself.
Here alone, however, the story is told in sequence — because historiography is itself a discipline with
rules: the narration must be neither so brief that understanding becomes impossible nor so long that
reading and remembering it becomes difficult. **[Saʿdī]** And a warning stands over the whole sūrah: God
said He would relate the best of stories, then related this one and spread it out, so it is a complete and
perfect story; whoever tries to complete it or beautify it with the *Isrāʾīliyyāt* — reports of unknown
chain and unknown transmitter, most of them lies — is correcting God and completing something he claims is
deficient, and how ugly a thing that is; the folds of this sūrah have been filled in many commentaries with
lies and with shocking matters that contradict what God Himself related. The servantʾs duty is to understand
from God what God has related, and to leave whatever is not transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ. **[Study Quran]**
Perhaps more than any other Qurʾānic narrative this story has supplied material for an astonishing array of
artistic, literary and spiritual works: Yūsuf, said to have possessed exceptional physical beauty, is
associated in Islamic literature with the reflection of Divine Beauty, with patience in adversity and with
the art of dream interpretation; the incident between him and Zulaykhā — never named in the Qurʾān — became
the basis of mystical and literary romances, most famously Jāmīʾs long Persian poem *Yūsuf wa Zulaykhā*
(d. 898/1492), while al-Suhrawardī (d. 587/1191) used its motifs in *On the Reality of Love* to explain the
metaphysics of love, beauty, joy, sadness and longing; many read the story symbolically as the separation of
the human soul from its Divine Origin and its return, and at the highest level as an exalted love tale of the
human lover and the Divine Beloved. Al-Maybudī writes: "How beautiful the story of Joseph is! It is a story
of lover and beloved, a report of separation and union. [From it,] the pain-stricken one ought to read of
those who are pain-stricken, and the lover ought to receive news of the pain of love and the burning desire
of lovers."

## 12:1

*"Alif. Lām. Rāʾ. These are the verses of the Book that makes things clear."*

**Meaning.** The sūrah opens with three of the disjointed letters and then a pointing gesture — *tilka*,
"those" — to verses that are not physically present, which the commentators take as a way of magnifying
them: these are the verses of that Book whose character is *mubīn*, making plain. **[Ṭabarī]** refers the
letters back to his discussion at the head of al-Baqarah and the sūrahs of Yūnus and Hūd, saying he has
already given the disagreement of the interpreters and the position he chose, so there is no need to repeat
it here; on the rest of the verse he records two readings of the early community. One is that the Book
"makes clear" its ḥalāl and its ḥarām, its right guidance and its direction — Mujāhid is cited for the
first *(weak)*, the editor noting that the chain runs through al-Walīd b. Salamah al-Filasṭīnī, whom the
critics called a liar who fabricated reports against trustworthy men, and through ʿAbd al-Wahhāb b. Mujāhid,
whom Sufyān called a liar and of whom Aḥmad said he never heard anything from his father; Qatādah is cited
for the second without that defect: "Yes, by God, it is clear — God made clear its guidance and its right
direction." A third report, from Muʿādh b. Jabal through Khālid b. Maʿdān, says the "clear Book" is clear
because it set out the six letters that dropped out of the tongues of the non-Arabs *(weak)* — the editor
again identifies al-Walīd b. Salamah as the flaw in the chain. **[Ṭabarī]**ʾs own verdict: the meaning is
"these are the verses of the Book that makes clear to whoever recites it and ponders what is in it of its
ḥalāl and ḥarām, its prohibitions and all the kinds of meaning it contains," because God described it as
*mubīn* without restricting that clarity to one part of it, so it applies to the whole of it, the whole of
it being clear about what is in it. **[Jalālayn]** The annexation "verses of the Book" carries the sense of
a partitive *min* — "some of the verses of the Book" — and *al-mubīn* means the Book that manifests truth,
distinguishing it from falsehood. **[Ibn Kathīr]** likewise, recalling his treatment of the separate letters
at the start of al-Baqarah: "these are the verses of the Book" refers to the clear Qurʾān, plain and
apparent, which explains and makes known the matters that are unclear. **[Maʿārif]** On *alif lām rāʾ* the
universal verdict of the majority of the Companions and the Successors is that these letters are a secret
between God the speaker and the Prophet ﷺ the addressee, which a third person cannot grasp and which it is
neither possible nor appropriate for anyone to strain after. Then: "these are the verses of the Book which
delineates the limits and restrictions of what is lawful and unlawful, and of other things in all the
departments of human life, and so gives people a simple, straight and moderate system of living" — as
promised in the Torah and as already known to the Jews.

**Language.** *Al-mubīn* is active in form: not "clear" merely in itself but "making clear" to another.
That is why **[Ṭabarī]** insists the clarity covers everything the Book contains, and why **[Jalālayn]**
glosses it as the Book that shows truth apart from falsehood rather than simply as a lucid text.

**Cross-references.** The same letters open 10:1, 11:1, 13:1, 14:1 and 15:1; *tilka āyāt al-kitāb* recurs
at 26:2 and 28:2, and the pair of "clear Book" oaths at 43:2 and 44:2 **[Study Quran]**. The letters are
found at the head of twenty-nine sūrahs, and their ultimate meaning, most commentators maintain, is known
only to God (2:1) **[Study Quran]**. *Al-kitāb al-mubīn* may also point to the Preserved Tablet (85:22), and
at 10:1 the adjective "wise" applied to the Book is understood by some as another way of saying "clear"
**[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** A sūrah about a familyʾs secret — a dream told only to a father — opens with letters whose
meaning God keeps to Himself. The first lesson is about the limits of curiosity: some things are given to be
acted on, not decoded, and **[Maʿārif]**ʾs rule that the letters are a confidence between speaker and
addressee is a discipline as much as a doctrine. What is not hidden is the Bookʾs function: it makes plain
what is lawful and unlawful and sets out a middle way of living. Before the story begins, the reader is told
what to look for in it — not marvels, but clarity.

## 12:2

*"Truly We have sent it down as an Arabic Qurʾān, that haply you may understand."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** We sent this clear Book down as an Arabic *qurʾān* upon the Arabs, because their
tongue and their speech are Arabic; so We sent the Book down in their tongue in order that they might grasp
it and understand from it — that is the sense of "that you may understand." **[Qurṭubī]** *Qurʾānan* stands
in the accusative as a circumstantial qualifier, meaning "gathered together," with *ʿarabiyyan* as its
adjective; or it may be a qualifier introduced by way of preface — as one says "I passed by Zayd, a
righteous man" — with *ʿarabiyyan* as the real circumstantial phrase, that is, "recited in your language, O
company of Arabs." He adds a lexical point: *aʿraba* means *bayyana*, to make plain, which is why one says of
a previously married woman that she *tuʿribu ʿan nafsihā*, she speaks for herself. "That you may understand"
means so that you may know its meanings and grasp what is in it. And al-Naḥḥās held that *anzalnāhu* here
means "We sent down the story of Yūsuf," which fits the context best: it is transmitted that the Jews said,
"Ask him why the family of Yaʿqūb moved from Syria to Egypt, and about the affair of Yūsuf," so God sent
this down at Makkah in agreement with what is in the Torah, together with an addition they did not have —
and for a Prophet who never read a book and lived nowhere near books, this stood in the same rank as
Jesusʾ raising of the dead. **[Ibn Kathīr]** The Arabic language is the most eloquent, plain, deep and
expressive of the meanings that can arise in the mind; therefore the most honourable Book was revealed in
the most honourable language, to the most honourable Prophet and Messenger ﷺ, brought by the most
honourable angel, in the most honourable land on earth, and its revelation began in the most honourable
month of the year, Ramadan — so the Qurʾān is perfect in every respect. **[Jalālayn]** We revealed it as an
Arabic Qurʾān, in the language of the Arabs, so that you, people of Makkah, might comprehend its meaning.
**[Saʿdī]** Part of the Bookʾs clarity is that God sent it down in the Arabic tongue, the noblest and most
lucid of tongues, which makes plain everything people need of beneficial truths; all this elucidation is
"that you may understand" — that you may grasp its limits, its roots and branches, its commands and
prohibitions. Once you have grasped that with certainty and your hearts are described by knowing it, the
fruit is the action of the limbs and submission to it. **[Maʿārif]** Because those who asked to be told what
had happened to Yūsuf were the Jews of Arabia, God revealed the story in their own language, that they might
ponder it, attest the truthfulness of the Prophet ﷺ and set their lives by the instructions that emerge from
it; and *laʿalla* is brought here in the sense of "so that" because the disposition of these addressees was
already known — that even with the clearest verses before them they would still doubt and delay accepting
the truth.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** Some of the Arabs bring *an* with *laʿalla* by analogy with *ʿasā*, and the
*lām* in *laʿalla* is an extra letter of emphasis, as in the rajaz of al-ʿAjjāj quoted by the poetʾs
daughterʾs line, "O father, perhaps you, or maybe you." Another reading of the phrase: "that you may be upon
a hope of pondering it," so that the shade of uncertainty in *laʿalla* falls on the hearers and not on the
Book or on God. **[Saʿdī]** adds a second sense to the same words: "that you may understand" also means that
your intellects may increase as these noble, high meanings recur upon your minds, so that you pass from one
state to states higher and more perfect than it.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** A tradition relevant to the sufficiency of this Book: Aḥmad recorded
from Jābir b. ʿAbdullāh that ʿUmar b. al-Khaṭṭāb came to the Prophet ﷺ with a book he had taken from some of
the People of the Book and began reading it to him, and the Prophet ﷺ grew angry and said, "Are you
bewildered about it, Ibn al-Khaṭṭāb? By the One in whose hand is my soul, I have come to you with it white
and pure. Do not ask them about anything, for they might tell you something true and you would reject it, or
something false and you would believe it. By the One in whose hand is my soul, if Mūsā were alive he would
have no choice but to follow me." Aḥmad also recorded from ʿAbdullāh b. Thābit that ʿUmar said, "O Messenger
of God, I passed by a brother of mine from Qurayẓah and he wrote out for me some comprehensive statements
from the Torah — shall I read them to you?" The face of the Messenger of God ﷺ changed, and ʿUmar said, "We
are pleased with God as our Lord, Islam as our religion and Muḥammad as our Messenger," whereupon the
Prophetʾs anger subsided and he said, "By the One in whose hand is the soul of Muḥammad, if Mūsā were to
appear among you and you followed him and abandoned me, you would have strayed. You are my share of the
nations, and I am your share of the prophets."

**Cross-references.** "An Arabic Qurʾān" also at 20:113, 39:28, 41:3 and 42:7; cf. 43:3 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The verse gives the reason for the choice of language before it gives any of the story:
revelation comes in the tongue of the people who first have to live by it, so that nothing stands between
the hearer and understanding. And the two traditions about ʿUmarʾs book mark the boundary of that
self-sufficiency — a community that has been given the best of stories in the clearest of languages does not
need to go shopping among other peopleʾs scriptures, where it may deny a truth or credit a lie.

## 12:3

*"We relate to you the best of stories in what We have revealed to you of this Qurʾān, though before it you
were among the heedless."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** God says to His Prophet ﷺ: We narrate to you, Muḥammad, the best of narratives by
Our revelation of this Qurʾān to you, informing you in it of past news, the reports of nations gone by and
the books We sent down in earlier ages; "though you were, before it, among the heedless" — before We
revealed it to you, you knew none of that, nor anything of it. Qatādah: "the best of stories" from the books
that have passed and Godʾs dealings with nations gone by, "though before it you were among the heedless."
**[Qurṭubī]** *Aḥsan al-qaṣaṣ* is a verbal noun, the estimate being "We have narrated the best of
narrating"; the root of *qaṣaṣ* is following the track of a thing — hence "she said to his sister, follow
his track" (28:11) — so the narrator follows traces and reports them. The beauty, then, attaches to the
telling rather than to the tale: one says "so-and-so is good at narrating a report," meaning good at
presenting it. Others hold that *qaṣaṣ* is not a verbal noun but a noun in the sense of the passive, as
when one says "God is our *rajāʾ*," meaning the One we hope in; on that reading the sense is "We inform you
of the best of reports." *Bi-mā awḥaynā* means "by Our revelation," the *mā* with the verb standing in place
of a verbal noun; *hādha al-Qurʾān* is accusative as an adjective, a substitute or an explicative
apposition — al-Farrāʾ allowed the genitive on repetition, which the Basrans explain as substitution for
*mā*, and Abū Isḥāq allowed the nominative on an implied subject, as though a questioner asked about the
revelation and was answered, "It is this Qurʾān." "Though before it you were among the heedless" means
heedless of what We have now made known to you. **[Jalālayn]** We relate to you the best of narratives in
what We have revealed of this Qurʾān, and *wa-in* is softened from *wa-innahu*; before it you were of the
heedless. **[Study Quran]** In 39:23 the Qurʾān is called the most beautiful of speech, and part of that
speech is the story of Joseph referred to here as the most beautiful of stories; it is so described because
it brings together all the truths pertaining to oneʾs religious life as well as oneʾs worldly affairs. On the
one hand it speaks of the oneness of God; of the nature of prophets, angels, saints, the righteous, evil
people, people of knowledge and people of ignorance; of dream interpretation, forgiveness and Divine Love.
On the other it speaks of kingship, earthly trials and punishments, human love, travel, business
transactions, social etiquette, politics and human deception. "Beautiful" may refer to the beauty of the
storyʾs language and eloquence as well as of its content; and "thou wert among the heedless" means that the
Prophet had no knowledge of this story before this sūrah was revealed. **[Saʿdī]** It is the best of stories
because of its truthfulness, the smoothness of its expression and the brilliance of its meanings — through
what this Qurʾān contains, which We have revealed to you and by which We have preferred you over all other
prophets; and that is pure favour and beneficence from God. "You knew neither the Book nor faith before God
revealed to you; rather We made it a light by which We guide whom We will of Our servants."

**Context.** **[Ṭabarī]** It is mentioned that this verse came down upon the Messenger of God ﷺ because his
Companions asked him to narrate stories to them. Ibn ʿAbbās is reported to have said, "They said: O
Messenger of God, if only you would narrate to us! So *We relate to you the best of stories* was revealed"
*(weak)* — the editor notes the transmitter Ayyūb b. Sayyār, of whom Bukhārī said "rejected in ḥadīth" and
Ibn Ḥibbān said he inverted chains and raised mursal reports, and suspects he inverted this one, since he
elsewhere reports it as a stopped saying of ʿAmr b. Qays. **[Ṭabarī]** A fuller account comes from ʿAwn b.
ʿAbdullāh *(weak — mursal)*: the Companions of the Messenger of God ﷺ grew weary of a weariness and said, "O
Messenger of God, speak to us!" so God sent down "God has sent down the best of speech, a Book consistent in
its parts" (39:23); then they grew weary again and said, "O Messenger of God, speak to us — something beyond
speech but less than the Qurʾān," meaning narratives; so God sent down *alif lām rāʾ … We relate to you the
best of stories*: they wanted speech, and He directed them to the best of speech; they wanted stories, and
He directed them to the best of stories. **[Ṭabarī]** A third chain, from Saʿd b. Abī Waqqāṣ through his son
Muṣʿab, has the order reversed: the Qurʾān was recited to them for a while, then they said "if only you
would narrate to us," and 12:1–2 came down; then it was recited to them for a while, and they said "if only
you would speak to us," and 39:23 came down; and one narrator adds a third stage, 57:16, "Has the time not
come for those who believe that their hearts should be humbled at the remembrance of God?" — al-Ḥākim graded
this chain sound by the criteria of the two Ṣaḥīḥs and al-Dhahabī agreed, though correcting one name in it.
**[Ibn Kathīr]** cites the same Ibn ʿAbbās report through Ibn Jarīr: "They said, ʿO Messenger of God, why do
you not narrate stories to us?ʾ Later this verse was revealed." **[Maʿārif]** Another account of the
occasion: the Jews of Madīnah sent a group of their men to Makkah to investigate and test the claim to
prophethood, and put the question vaguely — "if you are a true prophet, tell us about the prophet one of
whose sons was taken from Syria to Egypt, an event which caused his father to go blind from constant weeping
during his absence." They chose this because it was not widely known and no one in Makkah was aware of it,
and there was then no member of the People of the Book living in Makkah from whom any part of the story
could be ascertained; so the whole sūrah was revealed, relating the affair of Yaʿqūb and Yūsuf in detail
that does not appear even in the Torah and the Gospel, and when the Prophet ﷺ described it, it was an open
miracle at his hands. **[Study Quran]** The story is also said to have been revealed when some Jewish
scholars in Makkah persuaded the Makkan idolaters to ask the Prophet about details of Josephʾs life and
legacy; according to another report God revealed it after the Companions asked him for a story.

**Rulings.** None — the verse is a preface to narrative. **[Maʿārif]** notes only its polemical effect: the
Jewsʾ test turned out to prove the Messengerʾs excellence and authenticity more evidently than before, for it
became clear that he was unlettered and had no knowledge of world history, and that his now knowing it
admits of no explanation but that he was divinely taught and blessed with revelation.

**Stories & occasions.** **[Saʿdī]** closes with a standing rule for this sūrah *(digression on
Isrāʾīliyyāt)*: God said He relates the best of stories, then told this story and spread it out, so it is a
complete and perfect story; whoever wishes to complete it or beautify it with *Isrāʾīliyyāt* of unknown
chain and unknown transmitter — most of which are lies — is correcting God and completing something he
claims to be deficient, and it is enough to show how ugly a thing is that it ends there. The folds of this
sūrah have been filled, in many commentaries, with lies and with shocking matters that contradict what God
Himself related. The servant must understand from God what God has narrated and leave whatever is not
transmitted from the Prophet ﷺ *(Isrāʾīliyyāt)*.

**Reflection.** Three groups are said to have asked for this: bored Companions wanting something easier
than the Qurʾān, and Jews wanting a trap. Both got more than they asked for. The Companions were answered
with the best of speech and then the best of stories, which is to say that the appetite for narrative is not
rebuked but redirected — it is a real appetite, and God feeds it properly. The trap became the proof. And
"though before it you were among the heedless" is the hinge: the one telling the story did not know it
yesterday, so the story is not his. That single clause is the argument of the whole sūrah.

## 12:4

*"When Joseph said to his father, "O my father, I saw eleven stars and the sun and the moon; I saw them
prostrating to me."*"*

**Meaning.** *Idh* is circumstantial: remember, and mention to them, the time when Yūsuf said this.
**[Ṭabarī]** God says to His Prophet: though you were heedless of the news of Yūsuf b. Yaʿqūb b. Isḥāq b.
Ibrāhīm — when he said to his father Yaʿqūb b. Isḥāq, "O my father, I saw eleven stars," that is, I saw in
my sleep eleven stars. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Mention to your people, Muḥammad, among the stories you narrate to
them, the story of Yūsuf: he told his dream to his father Yaʿqūb, son of Isḥāq, son of Ibrāhīm. The scholars
of tafsīr explained that the eleven stars represent his brothers, who were eleven, and that the sun and the
moon represent his father and his mother; this explanation is collected from Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḍaḥḥāk, Qatādah,
Sufyān al-Thawrī and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Zayd b. Aslam. The vision became reality forty years later — or, as
some say, eighty — when Yūsuf raised his parents onto the throne while his brothers stood before him and
"they fell down prostrate before him," and he said, "O my father, this is the interpretation of my dream of
old; my Lord has made it come true" (12:100). **[Jalālayn]** "O my father" — read *yā abati*, the kasrah
marking the omitted *yāʾ* of annexation, or *yā abata*, marking an alif that was originally a *yāʾ* and has
been dropped; "I saw them" is repeated for emphasis, and *sājidīn*, the sound masculine plural, is used of
the prostration because prostration is an attribute of rational beings. **[Qurṭubī]** Ibn ʿAbbās and Qatādah
said the stars are his brothers, the sun his mother and the moon his father; Qatādah also said the sun is
his maternal aunt, because his mother had already died and his aunt was married to his father. **[Maʿārif]**
expands this: though Yūsufʾs mother had passed from this world before the event, in her place his father was
married to her sister, and a maternal aunt already loves her sisterʾs children as their natural mother
would; when after her sisterʾs death she becomes the fatherʾs wife, she is customarily called mother.
**[Saʿdī]** This vision was a preface to the elevation Yūsuf attained in this world and the next: when God
wills some great affair He sends ahead of it a preface, preparing the way for it, easing it, readying the
servant for the hardships that will come upon him, and showing kindness and beneficence to him. Yaʿqūb
interpreted it — the sun his mother, the moon his father, the stars his brothers — to mean that Yūsufʾs
circumstances would turn until he came to a state in which they would submit to him and prostrate before
him in honour and esteem; and that this would not come about except through causes preceding it: Godʾs
choosing him and electing him, His completion of His favour upon him with knowledge and action and
establishment in the land, and the extension of that favour to the household of Yaʿqūb, who prostrated to
him and became followers of him in it.

**Belief.** **[Ṭabarī]** It is said that the dreams of the prophets were revelation: Ibn ʿAbbās, through
Saʿīd b. Jubayr, on this verse — "the dream of the prophets was revelation." **[Study Quran]** It is widely
accepted that Josephʾs vision was a true dream, and in Islamic tradition true dreams are bestowed not only
on prophets but on ordinary human beings; they are distinguished from common dreams in that they convey a
truth from another level of reality or represent a manifestation of the spirit in the imaginal world. Some
commentators observe that the dreams of prophets are a form of divine revelation; others place the beginning
of Josephʾs prophetic mission slightly later (see 12:15), in which case this vision is a portent of his
impending prophetic function, as was the case with the Prophet Muḥammad. **[Maʿārif]** All the dreams of the
prophets are free of admixture, and for that reason their dreams have the standing of revelation (*waḥy*).

**Stories & occasions.** **[Ṭabarī]** Jābir is reported to have said that a Jew called Bustānah came to the
Prophet ﷺ and asked, "Tell me about the stars Yūsuf saw prostrating to him — what are their names?" The
Messenger of God ﷺ fell silent and answered nothing, then Jibrīl came down and told him their names; he sent
for the man and said, "Will you believe if I tell you their names?" He said yes. The Prophet ﷺ said: Jaryān,
al-Ṭāriq, al-Dhayyāl, Dhū al-Kanafāt, Qābis, Waththāb, ʿAmūdān, al-Falīq, al-Muṣbiḥ, al-Ḍarūḥ, Dhū al-Fargh,
al-Ḍiyāʾ and al-Nūr. The Jew said, "By God, those are their names!" *(weak; Isrāʾīliyyāt)* — the editor
notes that al-Ḥakam b. Ẓahīr al-Fazārī, through whom it comes, is abandoned; that al-Jūzjānī said of him
"fallen, because of his inclinations and the oddities of his reports, and he is the one who narrates the
report of Yūsufʾs stars"; that al-ʿUqaylī rejected this very report; and that ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Sābiṭ never
heard from Jābir, making it mursal. **[Qurṭubī]** records the same list through al-Ḥārith b. Abī Usāmah, with
al-Suhaylī glossing several of the names and observing that these are stars not catalogued by the
astronomers *(Isrāʾīliyyāt)*.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** The common reading is *Yūsuf* with a ḍammah on the sīn; Ṭalḥah b. Muṣarrif read
*Yūʾsif* with hamzah and kasrah, and Abū Zayd reported *Yūʾsaf* with hamzah and fatḥah. The name does not
take nunation because it is a foreign name — though some said it is Arabic. Abū al-Ḥasan al-Aqṭaʿ, who was
a wise man, was asked about *Yūsuf* and said: *al-asaf* in the language is grief, and *al-asīf* is the
servant, and both are joined in Yūsuf, which is why he was named Yūsuf. On *yā abati*: the kasrah is the
reading of Abū ʿAmr, ʿĀṣim, Nāfiʿ, Ḥamzah and al-Kisāʾī, and for the Basrans the *tāʾ* is a feminine marker
substituted for the *yāʾ* of annexation, admitted only in vocatives — feminine markers do enter masculine
words, as in *rajul nukahah huzʾah*. Sībawayh held that since the *tāʾ* replaces the *yāʾ*, one may not
stop on it except with *hāʾ*; his proofs are that *yā abah* conveys the meaning of *yā abī*, that the form
is used only with a definite noun, that the Arabs use it only in the vocative, and that one does not say
*yā abatī*, since the *tāʾ* stands in the place of the *yāʾ* and the two are not joined. Al-Farrāʾ claimed
that the kasrah indicates the *yāʾ* alone, the *yāʾ* being present in intention; Abū Isḥāq said this is
wrong, and he is right — how can the *yāʾ* be in intention when *yā abatī* is never said? Abū Jaʿfar,
al-Aʿraj and Ibn ʿĀmir read *yā abata* with fatḥah: they intended *yā abatī*, the *yāʾ* became an alif, the
alif was dropped and the fatḥah remained on the *tāʾ*; or the kasrah was exchanged for a fatḥah as the
*yāʾ* is exchanged for an alif in *yā ghulāman aqbil*. Al-Farrāʾ also allowed *yā abatu* with a ḍammah. On
the number: there is no disagreement among the grammarians that one says *aḥada ʿashara* in the nominative,
accusative and genitive alike, and the same for the compounds from thirteen to nineteen; the two names are
made into one name and given the lightest of the vowels. *Raʾaytuhum* is a repetition for emphasis.

**Cross-references.** The fulfilment: "he raised his parents upon the throne and they fell down before him
prostrate, and he said: O my father, this is the interpretation of my vision of before" (12:100); "when he
reached his full strength We gave him judgment and knowledge" (12:22) **[Ṭabarī]**. The Abrahamic line of
prophets: 6:84 **[Study Quran]**. The prostration of created beings before a created being, and the trial
that follows it, recalls the angelsʾ bowing to Adam and his expulsion soon after (2:35) **[Study Quran]**.
Cf. Genesis 37:10 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** records how Jacob read the dream: on the belief that one created being
does not prostrate to another except that the one prostrated to will undergo a severe trial, he understood
the vision to portend a great calamity for his son. That is the double edge of honour in this story — the
same image that means exaltation means affliction first, and al-Rāzī reads the prostration of the heavenly
bodies as a sign that Josephʾs brothers, father and mother would eventually come under his care. **[Saʿdī]**
adds the pastoral point: great elevations are announced early, and the announcement is not a spoiler but a
preparation, so that the servant is steadied for what must be endured before what is promised arrives.

## 12:5

*"He said, "O my son, do not relate your vision to your brothers, lest they contrive against you some
contriving. Truly Satan is to man a manifest enemy."*"*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** Yaʿqūb said to his son: do not tell this vision of yours to your brothers, so
that they will not envy you and contrive a contrivance against you — they would seek your ruin, set
themselves against you in enmity and obey Satan in your affair. "Satan is to man a clear enemy": to Ādam and
his sons an enemy who has made his enmity evident and shown it plainly. So beware that Satan incite your
brothers against you through their envy of you, if you relate your vision to them. He said this because envy
from them had already become apparent to him before this. Al-Suddī said: Yaʿqūb settled in Syria and his
whole concern was Yūsuf and his brother, so his brothers envied him when they saw their fatherʾs love for
him; and Yūsuf saw in sleep eleven stars and the sun and the moon prostrating to him, and told his father,
who said, "O my son, do not relate your vision to your brothers." **[Jalālayn]** Lest they scheme to kill
you out of envy, knowing that the interpretation of the dream is that the eleven planets are your brothers,
the sun your mother and the moon your father. **[Ibn Kathīr]** God narrates the reply Yaʿqūb gave his son
when Yūsuf told him the vision indicating that his brothers would be under his authority — subjugated to
him to the point of prostrating before him in respect, honour and appreciation. Yaʿqūb feared that if Yūsuf
told any of his brothers, they would envy him and plot evil against him; "lest they contrive a contriving
against you" means they may contrive something that causes your demise. **[Saʿdī]** Once its interpretation
was complete for Yūsuf, his father said to him: do not relate your vision to your brothers, "lest they
contrive against you a contriving" — out of envy arising in their own selves, that you should be the noble
chief over them. "Satan is to man a clear enemy": he does not slacken from him by night or by day, secretly
or openly, so keeping away from the means by which he gains power over the servant is the more fitting
course. Yūsuf obeyed his fatherʾs command and did not inform his brothers; he kept it hidden from them.
**[Study Quran]** Jacob told Joseph not to relate the dream because he feared his brothersʾ jealousy, knowing
that the dream meant Joseph would not only undergo tribulations but attain an exalted position; some say
Jacob understood it to mean that God had chosen him for prophethood and therefore feared he would suffer
from his brothersʾ jealousy and oppression. According to one account, however, because of the innocence of
his young age Joseph did relate his vision to his brothers (see v. 8). **[Maʿārif]** The gloss given here is
that Satan is an open enemy for mankind "for he seduces people to take such action for the sake of worldly
wealth and power."

**Language.** **[Ṭabarī]** The grammarians differed over the *lām* in *fa-yakīdū laka kaydā*. Some of the
Basran grammarians said it means "they will prepare a plot for you" (*fa-yattakhidhū laka kaydā*), and that
it is not like "if you are interpreters of dreams" (12:43), where the verb is joined by *lām* as it is
joined by *bāʾ* — as one says "I served him food" with *lām* meaning "to him"; compare "they eat what you
provided for them" (12:48) and "say: God guides to the truth" (10:35). Or, if you wish, *fa-yakīdū laka*
means "they will plot against you," the *lām* being like that in "they fear for their Lord" (7:154), which
is in the place of "they fear their Lord." Others said the *lām* enters here as it enters in "I praised for
you" and "I thanked for you" alongside "I praised you" and "I thanked you": it is a *lām* drawn in by the
verb, so the sense is "they will plot against you," or "they will aim at you and aim for you" — and
*kaydan* is an accusative of confirmation. **[Qurṭubī]** *Fa-yakīdū laka kaydā* means they will contrive
your destruction, because the interpretation of the dream is obvious, so Satan may then drive them to intend
evil against you; the *lām* in *laka* is for emphasis, as in "if you are interpreters of dreams."

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** A confirmed report in the Sunnah: "If any of you sees what he loves,
let him speak of it; and if he sees what he dislikes, let him turn over onto his other side, dry-spit three
times to his left, seek refuge with God from its evil and tell it to no one — then it will not harm him."
Aḥmad and the collectors of the Sunan recorded from Muʿāwiyah b. Ḥaydah al-Qushayrī that the Messenger of
God ﷺ said, "The dream hangs upon the leg of a bird as long as it is not interpreted; when it is
interpreted, it falls" — that is, it comes true. Hence one should keep an expected blessing concealed until
it has occurred and become known. And the Prophet ﷺ said, "Seek help for the fulfilment of your needs by
concealing them, for every possessor of a blessing is envied."

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** treats this verse under eleven *masāʾil*, beginning with the standing of the
dream. The true dream is a noble condition and a high station: "Nothing remains after me of the givers of
glad tidings except the true, sound dream that a righteous man sees, or that is seen for him"; and "the
truest of you in dream is the truest of you in speech." The Prophet ﷺ ruled that it is one part of
forty-six parts of prophethood; it is also transmitted as one part of seventy, and from Ibn ʿAbbās one part
of forty, from Ibn ʿUmar one part of forty-nine, from al-ʿAbbās one part of fifty, from Anas one part of
twenty-six, and from ʿUbādah b. al-Ṣāmit one part of forty-four. Ibn Baṭṭāl said the sound figure is
forty-six, next in soundness seventy, and that Muslim recorded only these two in his Ṣaḥīḥ while the rest
belong to the reports of shaykhs; Abū ʿAbdullāh al-Māzirī said the majority and the sounder view of the
ḥadīth people is forty-six. **[Ṭabarī]**ʾs harmonisation, which **[Qurṭubī]** adopts: most or all of these
reports are sound and each has an intelligible resolution — "one part of seventy" is a general statement
about every true, sound dream seen by any Muslim in whatever state, while "forty" or "forty-six" concerns a
dreamer in the condition described of al-Ṣiddīq (Abū Bakr), so that whoever perfects his ablution in bitter
cold, is patient under what he dislikes for Godʾs sake, and waits for one prayer after another, his dream is
— God willing — one part of forty parts of prophethood, while one whose state is between these has a true
dream between the two figures, not falling below seventy and rising above forty. Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr made the
same point: the differing figures are not contradictory, since the sound dream varies with truthfulness of
speech, discharge of trust, firmness of religion and soundness of certainty; as people differ in these
things their dreams differ in parts, and whoeverʾs intention in worshipping his Lord is pure, his certainty
sound and his speech true, his dream is truer and nearer to prophethood — just as the prophets themselves
excel one another, "and We preferred some of the prophets over others" (17:55). **[Qurṭubī]** concludes that
this interpretation gathers the scattered reports and is better than explaining some and discarding others.

**Belief.** **[Maʿārif]** gives the underlying psychology at length, citing Qāḍī Thanāʾullāhʾs *Tafsīr
Maẓharī*: the reality of a dream is that when the human self, through sleep or unconsciousness, is freed
from managing the active body, it sees shapes through the faculty of imagination. Dreams are of three kinds,
two of them wholly false and without substance — self-suggestion (*ḥadīth al-nafs*), when what one saw
awake returns to one transformed, and Satanʾs seductive input (*taswīl al-shayṭān*), when he introduces
pleasing or terrifying forms into a personʾs mind — and neither can be interpreted in any real sense. The
third kind is true and correct: a mode of inspiration (*ilhām*) activated to warn a servant of God or give
him glad tidings, God placing things in his mind and heart out of His unseen treasures. The Prophet ﷺ said,
"The dream of a believer is a conversation in which he has the honour of speaking with his Lord" — reported
by al-Ṭabarānī with a sound chain *(as graded by Maẓharī)*. The Sufis explain this by the world of images
(*ʿālam al-mithal*), a universe in which not only substantial objects and physical realities but also
attributes and non-corporeal meanings have particular forms; freed from the bodyʾs concerns, the dreaming
self sometimes attaches to that world and sees representative forms shown to it from the unseen. Temporary
disturbances can mix false imaginings with the real, which is why interpreters sometimes go wrong; and even
among true dreams some cannot be interpreted because the reality of the event is not clear. Only a dream
originating by Godʾs command, free of discordant elements and correctly interpreted, becomes a true
inspiration and a proven reality. The dreams of the prophets are all of that kind, which is why they have
the standing of revelation; the dreams of ordinary believing Muslims are not free of probability, so they
are not a basis for law or belief.

**Cross-references.** For Satan as a manifest enemy see 2:168, 2:208, 6:142 and 7:22 **[Study Quran]**. Cf.
Genesis 37:5, 9–10 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The fatherʾs advice is practical before it is mystical: a blessing announced too early
becomes a target. **[Ibn Kathīr]**ʾs two traditions — narrate the good dream, conceal the bad one; and keep
a dream unspoken so that it stays "on the leg of a bird" — are matched by the worldly maxim that every
possessor of a blessing is envied. Yaʿqūb knew his sonsʾ hearts better than they knew them, and what he
feared was not the dream but the envy that hearing it would feed; the verse ends by naming that fearʾs real
author. Satan does not create the brotherʾs resentment, he finds it and gives it a plan — which is why
**[Saʿdī]** makes the lesson the avoidance of occasions rather than the winning of contests.

## 12:6

*"And thus will your Lord choose you, and teach you the interpretation of reports, and complete His favour
upon you and upon the house of Jacob, as He completed it upon your two forefathers before, Abraham and
Isaac. Truly your Lord is Knowing, Wise."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** God relates Yaʿqūbʾs words to his son after the dream: "and thus will your Lord
choose you" — just as your Lord showed you the stars, the sun and the moon prostrating to you, so will He
select you; ʿIkrimah glossed *yajtabīka* as *yaṣṭafīka*, He will choose you; Qatādah said He chose him and
selected him and taught him the interpretation of reports, "which is *taʾwīl al-aḥādīth*." "And He will
teach you the interpretation of reports" means He will teach you knowledge of what peopleʾs reports about
what they see in their sleep come to — that is, the expression of dreams; Mujāhid said *ʿibārat al-ruʾyā*,
while Ibn Zayd read *taʾwīl al-kalām*: knowledge and speech. Yūsuf was the most skilled interpreter of
people, and God said, "when he reached his full strength We gave him judgment and knowledge" (12:22). "And
He will complete His favour upon you" — by His choosing you, electing you and teaching you the
interpretation of reports — "and upon the household of Yaʿqūb," meaning the people of Yaʿqūbʾs religion and
his way, among his descendants and others, "as He completed it upon your two forefathers before, Ibrāhīm and
Isḥāq," by taking the one as an intimate friend and rescuing him from the fire, and ransoming the other with
a mighty sacrifice; ʿIkrimah said the favour upon Ibrāhīm was His saving him from the fire and the favour
upon Isḥāq His saving him from slaughter. "Truly your Lord is Knowing" — knowing the places of bounty and
who is worthy of being chosen and favoured — "Wise" in His management of His creation. **[Qurṭubī]** The
*kāf* of *kadhhālika* stands in the accusative as an adjective of an implied verbal noun, and so does the
*kāf* in "as He completed it," with *mā* a restraining particle; or the sense is "as He honoured you with
the vision, so will He choose you and do you good by making the vision come true" — Muqātil said, by their
prostration to you, and al-Ḥasan said, by prophethood. *Ijtibāʾ* is the choosing of the highest things for
the one chosen; its root is *jabaytu al-shayʾ*, I gathered or obtained it, whence *jabaytu al-māʾ fī
al-ḥawḍ*, I collected water in the basin, as al-Naḥḥās noted. This is Godʾs praise of Yūsuf and an
enumeration of the favours He gave him — establishment in the land and the teaching of the interpretation
of reports — and all agree that the latter means the interpretation of dreams. ʿAbdullāh b. Shaddād b.
al-Hād said the interpretation of Yūsufʾs dream was fulfilled after forty years, and that is the outer limit
for a dreamʾs fulfilment. "The reports" (*al-aḥādīth*) are what people see in sleep, and this was a miracle
for Yūsuf, since no error befell him in it; he was the most knowledgeable of people in its interpretation,
our Prophet ﷺ was much the same, Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq was among the most skilled of interpreters, and the
science passed to Ibn Sīrīn. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Yaʿqūb said to his son: just as God chose you to see the
eleven stars, the sun and the moon prostrating to you in a vision, "thus will your Lord choose you" —
designate and assign you as a prophet from Him — "and teach you the interpretation of *aḥādīth*"; Mujāhid
and several others said this refers to interpreting dreams. "And He will perfect His favour upon you" — by
His message and His revelation to you — which is why Yaʿqūb added, "as He perfected it aforetime upon your
fathers, Ibrāhīm," Godʾs intimate friend, "and Isḥāq," Ibrāhīmʾs son; "truly your Lord is All-Knowing,
All-Wise" — He knows best whom to choose for His messages. **[Jalālayn]** Thus, in the way that you have
seen, will your Lord prefer you — choose you — and teach you the interpretation of events, that is, the
interpretation of visions, and perfect His grace upon you with prophethood, and upon the house of Yaʿqūb,
upon his sons, as He perfected it formerly upon your forefathers Ibrāhīm and Isḥāq with prophethood. Truly
your Lord is Knower of His creatures, Wise in what He does with them. **[Saʿdī]** "Thus will your Lord
choose you" — He will select and choose you by the noble attributes and fine qualities with which He has
favoured you; "and teach you the interpretation of reports" — the expression of dreams, and the explanation
of what true reports come to, such as the heavenly books and the like; "and complete His favour upon you" in
this world and the next, by giving you good in this world and good in the next, "as He completed it upon
your two forefathers before, Ibrāhīm and Isḥāq," when God bestowed on them great and wide favours, religious
and worldly. "Truly your Lord is Knowing" — His knowledge encompasses all things and what the breasts of the
servants contain of righteousness and otherwise, so He gives each what His wisdom and His praiseworthiness
require; "Wise" — He puts things in their places and settles them in their stations. **[Maʿārif]** enumerates
three promises here: first, "your Lord will choose you" — He will choose him for His blessings and favours,
which manifested itself when wealth, recognition and power came into his hands in Egypt; second, "and teach
you the interpretation of events," where *al-aḥādīth* means the dreams of people, so God will teach him to
interpret dreams, which also tells us that dream interpretation is a standing area of expertise bestowed by
God upon a chosen few — not everyone is fit to have it; third, "and will perfect His bounty upon you," which
refers to the bestowal of prophethood, alluded to in the words that follow, "as He perfected it earlier upon
your fathers Ibrāhīm and Isḥāq" — a statement which also indicates that the skill of interpreting dreams
given to Yūsuf was taught to Ibrāhīm and Isḥāq as well. The verse ends, "Surely your Lord is All-Knowing,
All-Wise," and so He is: teaching an art to someone is not difficult for Him, nor does He, as His wisdom
requires, teach this art to just anyone; rather He elects someone in His wisdom and grants him the
expertise.

**Rulings.** **[Maʿārif]** draws one from ʿAbdullāh b. Shaddādʾs report, which appears in al-Qurṭubī: the
interpretation of Yūsufʾs dream was actualised after forty years, which shows that an immediate
actualisation of an interpretation is not necessary either.

**Belief.** **[Study Quran]** "Thus shall thy Lord choose thee" means that just as God chose Joseph to have
so exalted a vision, so too would He choose him to accomplish great tasks — which some understand as a
reference to prophethood. "The interpretation of events" refers to the ability to interpret dreams (see also
vv. 21 and 44); in traditional Islamic psychology this art entails knowing the source of dreams, true dreams
being said to come from an angelic source and false dreams from the lower self or a demonic force. It can
also refer to the ability to discern the hidden meanings within scripture (see 3:7), or more generally to
the gift of discerning the true nature of things. Godʾs completing His blessing upon Joseph and upon the
house of Jacob (cf. 19:6) may mean the blessing of prophethood, in which case "as He completed it upon thy
forefathers, Abraham and Isaac" refers to their prophethood; or it may mean the combination of blessings
they will receive in this world and the next, in which case the blessing on the forefathers refers to
Abrahamʾs being taken by God as a friend (4:125) and saved from the fire into which his people cast him
(21:69; 37:97), and to Isaacʾs obeying Abrahamʾs request to be his sacrifice and then being ransomed by the
substitution of a ram at Godʾs command (37:102–7).

**Cross-references.** 12:22 (judgment and knowledge at full strength); 12:37, 12:41 and 12:47–49 (Josephʾs
interpretations); 12:21 and 12:44; 19:6; 4:125; 21:69; 37:97; 37:102–7 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The father does not merely interpret the dream; he preaches from it. Between the vision and
its fulfilment — forty years by one reckoning, eighty by another — lie the well, the slave market, the
seduction and the prison. **[Saʿdī]**ʾs point is that Godʾs favours arrive with their causes attached:
election, then knowledge and action, then establishment in the land. And the last clause settles the
question of fairness: He is Knowing of what breasts contain and Wise in where He places things, so the
dream-interpretation that raised Yūsuf above his brothers was not a favour arbitrarily distributed but a
trust given to the one who would carry it through prison without bitterness.

## 12:7

*"There were certainly signs in Joseph and his brothers for those who asked."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** There were in Yūsuf and his eleven brothers signs — meaning lessons and reminders
— for those who ask about their news and their story; and God intended by that His Prophet Muḥammad ﷺ, for
He sent this sūrah down to him teaching him in it what Yūsuf met with from his nearest kin and his brothers
of envy, together with Godʾs honouring of him, so that he might be consoled by it for what he was meeting
from his own kin and relatives among the idolaters of Quraysh. That was what Ibn Isḥāq used to say: God
related to Muḥammad the news of Yūsuf, and the transgression of his brothers against him and their envy of
him when he mentioned his dream, because of what the Messenger of God ﷺ saw of his peopleʾs transgression
and envy when God honoured him with prophethood, so that he might take it as his model and be comforted by
it. **[Qurṭubī]** The people of Makkah read *āyah* in the singular, and Abū ʿUbayd preferred the plural
*āyāt* because it is "much good"; al-Naḥḥās held the singular to be a fine reading — that there was, for
those who asked about Yūsufʾs news, a sign in what they were told, since they asked the Prophet ﷺ in Makkah,
"Tell us about a man among the prophets who was in Syria, whose son was taken out to Egypt and who wept for
him until he went blind," when there was no one in Makkah of the People of the Book and no one who knew the
reports of the prophets — the Jews having sent from Madīnah to ask him this — so God sent down Sūrat Yūsuf
entire, containing everything in the Torah of that story and more, and that was a sign for the Prophet ﷺ in
the rank of Jesusʾ raising of the dead. *Āyāt* means admonitions; it is also said to mean a lesson (*ʿibrah*)
— and it is reported that in some codices the word reads *ʿibrah* — or insight (*baṣīrah*), or a wonder
(*ʿajab*), as when one says "so-and-so is an *āyah* in knowledge and beauty," meaning a marvel. Al-Thaʿlabī
said in his tafsīr that when the dream reached Yūsufʾs brothers they envied him; and Ibn Zayd said they were
prophets, and that they said, "He is not content that his brothers prostrate to him until his parents do
too!" and so sought him in enmity — a view already refuted. **[Jalālayn]** Verily in the tale of Joseph and
his brethren — who were eleven — are signs, lessons, for those who inquire about their tale. **[Ibn Kathīr]**
There are *āyāt*, lessons and wisdom to be learned from the story of Yūsuf and his brothers, for those who
ask about their story and seek its knowledge; their story is unique and worthy of being narrated.
**[Saʿdī]** There were in Yūsuf and his brothers signs — lessons and proofs of many good matters — for the
askers, that is, for everyone who asks about it with the tongue of circumstance or with the tongue of
speech; for the askers are those who benefit from signs and lessons, while those who turn away benefit
neither from signs nor from stories nor from clear proofs. **[Maʿārif]** In this verse a warning is served
that the story of Yūsuf must not be taken as a common story, for in it are great signs of Godʾs perfect
power, and His instructions, both for those who asked and for those who seek guidance through it. The signs
referred to are for the Jews who put the Prophet ﷺ to the test by asking him to relate this story: when he
was in Makkah the news of him reached Madīnah, and the Jews living there sent a group of their men to Makkah
to investigate and test his claim, putting the question in a deliberately vague form — "if you are a true
prophet of God, tell us about the prophet one of whose sons was taken from Syria to Egypt, an event which
caused his father to become blind from constant weeping during his absence." They chose it because it was
not widely known and no one in Makkah was aware of it, and because at that time there was no member of the
People of the Book living in Makkah from whom any part of this story, as it appears in the Torah and the
Gospel, could be ascertained; so the whole sūrah was revealed, in details that do not appear even in those
books, and when the Prophet ﷺ described it, it was an open miracle at his blessed hands. Alternatively, the
verse may mean that the event itself — apart from the Jewsʾ question — was full of great signs of Godʾs
perfect power, and that in it are major elements of divine guidance, instruction and injunction. One may
imagine the destiny of a child thrown into a pit, whom Godʾs power took over and carried from stage to
stage, guarding him all along from childhood to youth; then God blessed him with a divine colouring, as He
does for His special servants, for he stood steadfast as His servant against trials that make obedience
difficult — all the more difficult when one is young and challenged by opportunity. But here he is, armed
with the fear of God: he holds his ground, controls himself from desiring the undesirable, and walks out
clean from the stranglehold of temptation. Then the story tells how God rewards a person who takes
righteousness and the fear of God as his conscious and determined way of life, raising him higher than his
adversaries in power and recognition until they stand subdued before him. These are lessons and truths
pointing to the great signs of divine power, which anyone may realise who cares to look and find out.

**Stories & occasions.** **[Qurṭubī]** preserves the genealogy: their names were Rūbīl, the eldest,
Shamʿūn, Lāwī, Yahūdhā, Zayālūn and Yashjar, and their mother was Layyā daughter of Layyān, who was the
daughter of Yaʿqūbʾs maternal uncle; four sons were born to him by two concubines — Dān, Naftālī, Jād and
Āshir; then Layyā died and Yaʿqūb married her sister Rāḥīl, who bore him Yūsuf and Binyāmīn, so the sons of
Yaʿqūb were twelve men. Al-Suhaylī added that Yaʿqūbʾs mother was named Rifqā, that Rāḥīl died in the
childbirth of Binyāmīn, and that Layyān b. Nāhir b. Āzar was Yaʿqūbʾs maternal uncle. It is said the two
handmaids were named Layyā and Taltā, that one belonged to Rāḥīl and the other to her sister Layyā, and that
both had been given to Yaʿqūb; Yaʿqūb had thus combined two sisters, which was not lawful for anyone after
him, given Godʾs words "and that you should combine two sisters, except what has already passed" (4:23)
*(Isrāʾīliyyāt)*. **[Maʿārif]** gives the same outline: the ten eldest were from Yaʿqūbʾs first wife Layyā,
daughter of Layyan; after her death he married her sister Rāḥīl (Rachel), who became the mother of Yūsuf and
Binyamīn (Benjamin), so Binyamīn was Yūsufʾs only full brother and the other ten were his half-brothers from
the fatherʾs side. Every one of the twelve sons had children and their families prospered, and since
Yaʿqūbʾs title was Isrāʾīl, all twelve families came to be known as Banū Isrāʾīl, the children of Israel.

**Readings.** **[Ṭabarī]** The readers differed over *āyāt li-l-sāʾilīn*: the general reading of the readers
of the cities is the plural *āyāt*, while Mujāhid and Ibn Kathīr are reported to have read it in the
singular. The plural is the more correct of the two readings, because of the consensus of the authoritative
readers upon it. **[Qurṭubī]** records the same division — the people of Makkah reading the singular — and
Abū ʿUbaydʾs preference for the plural.

**Cross-references.** "In their stories there is a lesson for those who possess minds" (12:111), which
**[Qurṭubī]** cites as the demonstration of the first reason this story is called the best of stories. Cf.
11:120 and 20:99 **[Study Quran]**ʾs note that the signs are lessons for those who wish to gain true
knowledge and learn the universal import of the narrative.

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** One moral lesson of this narrative is the ultimate futility of envy, which
motivated Josephʾs brothers and which led the Prophet Muḥammadʾs own kin to reject and harm him; yet both
Muḥammad and Joseph were assisted by God, and their enemies ended up under their authority. The Prophet is
reported to have said, "Beware of envy, for it consumes good deeds as fire consumes wood." The story also
carries signs for those seeking an answer to their existential situation, counselling patience in
tribulation on one hand and gratitude for Godʾs blessings on the other, and it shows the many ways God
gently takes care of His friends through His protection of them; it contains signs, too, that Godʾs love is
realised through trial and tribulation. **[Ṭabarī]**ʾs framing is the more particular and the more
consoling: the sūrah was sent down as company for a man being envied by his own relatives — the first
hearer of the story needed it more than the Jews who set the test.

## 12:8

*"When they said, "Truly Joseph and his brother are more beloved to our father than we are, though we are a
hardy band. Surely our father is in manifest error!""*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** This is joined to the previous verse: there were signs for whoever asked about
their affair, at the time when Yūsufʾs brothers said, "Yūsuf and his brother by his mother are more beloved
to our father than we are, though we are an *ʿuṣbah*" — a company of number, eleven men. An *ʿuṣbah* among
people is ten and upwards, said to extend to fifteen, and it has no singular formed from its own root, like
*al-nafr* and *al-rahṭ*. "Our father is in manifest error": they meant that their father Yaʿqūb was
mistaken in what he did, in preferring Yūsuf and his full brother over them in love; *mubīn* means an error
that makes it plain to whoever considers and looks at it that it is an error. Al-Suddī said "his brother"
means Binyāmīn, and that the speakers were ten; Ibn Zayd said *ʿuṣbah* means a group. **[Qurṭubī]** *La-Yūsufu*
is raised as the subject of an oath-receiving *lām* of emphasis — as though they said "by God, Yūsuf …" —
with "his brother" conjoined to it and "more beloved to our father than we" its predicate; the predicate is
not made dual or plural because it carries the sense of a verb. They said this because news of the dream had
reached them, so they conspired in plotting against him. "Though we are an *ʿuṣbah*" means a group, and they
were ten; an *ʿuṣbah* is between one and ten, it is said, or up to fifteen, or between ten and forty, and it
has no singular from its own root, like *al-nafr* and *al-rahṭ*. As for "surely our father is in manifest
error," they did not mean error in religion — if they had, they would have been unbelievers — but that he
had departed from the right course of management, in preferring two over ten when all were equally
attributed to him; or that he was in a plain mistake in preferring Yūsuf and his brother over them.
**[Jalālayn]** Mention when they — that is, when some of Josephʾs brothers said to some of the others:
"Surely Joseph" — *la-Yūsufu* is the subject — "and his brother," his full brother Benjamin, "are dearer" —
*aḥabbu* is the predicate — "to our father than we are, though we be a hardy band," a group. "Lo, our father
is in plain aberration," in manifest error, for preferring those two to us. **[Ibn Kathīr]** They swore,
according to their false thoughts, that Yūsuf and his brother Binyāmīn, Yūsufʾs full brother, were dearer to
their father than they, while they were an *ʿuṣbah*, a group. **[Saʿdī]** When they said among themselves:
"Yūsuf and his brother" — Binyāmīn, his full brother, though all of them are brothers — "are dearer to our
father than we, though we are a group": so how should he prefer them over us in love and tenderness? "Surely
our father is in manifest error" — in a clear mistake, in that he preferred them over us without any reason
we can see and without any matter we can observe. **[Maʿārif]** From this verse the story proper begins: the
brothers saw that their father loved Yūsuf unusually more than them, though they were older than he, and
this made them envy him; and it is possible that they had somehow learned of Yūsufʾs dream, which made them
uncomfortable at the prospect of his coming rise in status and so envious of him. They talked it over among
themselves: our father loves Yūsuf and Binyamīn much more than us although we are ten and older than they;
we have the ability and the group strength to manage the affairs of the family, while both of them are small
children who cannot do much; our father should have noticed this, and we are the ones he should have loved
more.

**Language.** **[Study Quran]** *ʿUṣbah* conveys the sense of a large group of people, usually understood to
range from ten to forty in number; "though we are a group" can thus be read as an expression of shock or
astonishment, for the brothers thought that as a larger and more powerful group they had more right to
their fatherʾs love than his two young children. In tribal cultures having many sons to help with work and
to defend the family and tribe was itself a reason for valuing them; by that logic many grown sons would be
worth more than two young ones, since the latter could not suffice as defenders of their father or as
powerful bearers of his legacy. **[Study Quran]** also records that Joseph in particular was so loved that
Jacob would keep him with him night and day, never parting company.

**Belief.** The charge of "manifest error" is a serious one, and **[Qurṭubī]** explicitly limits it: they
did not mean error in religion, since if they had meant that they would themselves have been unbelievers,
but a lapse in the ordering of household affections. This is one of the grounds on which the claim that the
brothers were prophets is contested — **[Qurṭubī]** notes at 12:7 that Ibn Zaydʾs view has already been
refuted, and **[Maʿārif]** argues the point at length in the next verse.

**Cross-references.** The same complaint returns at 12:84 and 12:95, where Yaʿqūbʾs family call his grief
an "old error"; the envy that drives it is named at 4:32 and 113:5, and the Prophetic warning about it at
12:7 **[Study Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The brothersʾ arithmetic is worldly and not absurd: ten grown men, useful, defensive,
numerous, against two children. What is false is the conclusion that love is distributed according to
usefulness, and that therefore their father has made an administrative error. Envy rarely presents itself
as malice; it presents itself as a claim about justice — *we are a group* — and then as a verdict on the
one who does not satisfy it. **[Qurṭubī]**ʾs note that the news of the dream had reached them is worth
sitting with: Yaʿqūbʾs precaution failed, or was never fully observed, and the story that follows is what
happens when a promise of elevation reaches the ears of those who will have to live beneath it.

## 12:9

*"Kill Joseph, or cast him out to some land, so that your fatherʾs face may be free for you; and be, after
that, a righteous people."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The brothers of Yūsuf said to one another: kill Yūsuf, or cast him into some land
among the lands — meaning a place on earth — "so that your fatherʾs face may be free for you": free of his
preoccupation with Yūsuf, for Yūsuf has occupied him and turned his face away from us towards him; "and
that you may be, after that, a righteous people" — they meant that they would repent of killing Yūsuf and of
the sin they would commit in it, and so become, by their repentance after his destruction, a righteous
people. Al-Suddī said: "you will repent of what you have done, or of your doing." **[Qurṭubī]** There is an
elision in the speech — a speaker among them said "kill Yūsuf" — so as to cut off the matter at its root.
"Or cast him out to some land" means into a land, the preposition being dropped and the noun accusative, for
which Sībawayh cites the verse of Saʿīdah b. Juʾayyah in which *fī* is omitted; al-Naḥḥās observed that the
omission is particularly apt here because the verb governs two objects, one of them with a preposition, so
that when the preposition is dropped the verb governs it directly. The implied sense is a land far from
their father, and this suppression is necessary because Yūsuf was with his father in a land. The speaker is
said to have been Shamʿūn, according to Wahb b. Munabbih; Kaʿb al-Aḥbār said Dān; and Muqātil said Rūbīl —
God knows best. *Yakhlu* is apocopated because it is the answer to the command, and means "becomes free and
pure"; "your fatherʾs face" then turns to you wholly. "And you may be after that" means after the sin, or
after Yūsuf, "a righteous people" — that is, repentant, so that you may bring about a repentance afterwards
and God may accept it from you; in this there is evidence that the repentance of a killer is accepted,
because God did not censure them for this saying. Others read *ṣāliḥīn* as meaning that your standing with
your father will be set right, without preference or favouritism. **[Jalālayn]** Kill Joseph, or cast him
away into some land — into some distant land — so that your father might be solely concerned with you, and
that thereafter, after killing Joseph or casting him away, you might be a righteous folk by repenting.
**[Ibn Kathīr]** "And after that you will be righteous folk" — thus intending repentance before committing
the sin. **[Saʿdī]** "Kill Yūsuf, or cast him into a land" — hide him from his father in a distant land
where he cannot see him; for if you do one of these two things, "your fatherʾs face will be free for you":
he will be at leisure for you and turn to you with tenderness and love, since his heart has been occupied
with Yūsuf to the point that it has no room for you. "And you will be, after that, a righteous people": you
will repent to God and seek His forgiveness after your sin. They put the resolve to repent ahead of the
committing of the sin, to make it easier to do and to strip away its heinousness. **[Maʿārif]** The brothers
exchanged opinions: some suggested that Yūsuf be killed, others that he be thrown into a desolate pit so
that the thorn in their side could be removed and they could become the sole recipients of their fatherʾs
attention; as for the sin they would earn by killing him or throwing him into a pit, that was something to
be taken care of later, when they could repent and thus become righteous — this is the meaning of "and after
that you may become a righteous people" according to some reports. Alternatively the verse may be taken to
mean that things will come out right for them after Yūsufʾs killing, because that focus of their fatherʾs
attention will no longer be there; or that they would become normal as before once they had apologised to
their father. **[Study Quran]** The brothers believed that slaying Joseph or banishing him to some land would
remove him from Jacobʾs attention and cause Jacob eventually to set his heart on them instead; "and be,
thereafter, a righteous people" means that they intended to repent later to God for their transgression
against Joseph and be upright thereafter, or that after disposing of Joseph they would seek to mend their
relationship with their father.

**Belief.** **[Maʿārif]** This verse proves that these brothers of Yūsuf were not prophets, for here they
committed — or fully intended — many major sins: the intention to kill an innocent person, disobedience to
their father and the causing of pain to him, contravention of a pledge, conspiracy, and the like. Such sins,
by the consensus of Muslims, cannot be committed by the noble prophets, peace be upon them all, even before
they are ordained as prophets. **[Ibn Kathīr]** offers the counterweight on the divine side: their plot to
kill Yūsuf could not have succeeded, because God willed that Yūsuf fulfil a mission that had to be
fulfilled and completed — that he receive Godʾs revelation and become His prophet, and be a powerful man in
Egypt governing it; so God did not allow them to persist in their intent against him.

**Language.** The elision of *fī* before *arḍan* and the apocopation of *yakhlu* as the answer to a command
are both **[Qurṭubī]**ʾs notes; *arḍan* here is indefinite not because the land is unknown but because any
land far enough will do.

**Reflection.** The most revealing words in the verse are the last. Envy does not ask permission to sin; it
arranges the repentance in advance, as a line item. **[Saʿdī]** names the mechanism exactly: they placed the
resolve to repent before the sin, to make the sin easier and to remove its ugliness. **[Qurṭubī]** draws
the legal comfort from the same words — God did not rebuke the statement, so the repentance of a killer is
accepted — while **[Maʿārif]** draws the doctrinal limit: men who could plan this were not prophets. Both
readings can stand together, and together they say something severe and merciful at once: the plan was
monstrous, the door of return was already open, and the brothers would in the end walk through it — but only
after twenty years and only when the brother they plotted against held the power to punish them.

## 12:10

*"A speaker among them said, "Do not kill Joseph, but cast him into the depths of the well, that some
caravan may pick him up — if you are going to act."*"*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** A speaker among the brothers of Yūsuf said: do not kill Yūsuf. It is said that the
speaker was Rūbīl, who was Yūsufʾs maternal cousin. Qatādah said: it was related to us that it was Rūbīl,
the eldest of the company, and he was Yūsufʾs maternal cousin, and he forbade them from killing him. Ibn
Isḥāq said: it was related to me — and God knows best — that the one who said this was Rūbīl, the eldest of
the sons of Yaʿqūb, and that he was the most upright of them in judgment concerning it *(Isrāʾīliyyāt)*.
**[Qurṭubī]** treats the verse under thirteen *masāʾil*, beginning with the identity of the speaker: Ibn
ʿAbbās said it was Yahūdhā, the eldest of Yaʿqūbʾs sons; others said Rūbīl, his maternal cousin, who is the
same man who later said "I will never leave this land" (12:80); others said Shamʿūn. Then the language: the
people of Makkah, Baṣrah and Kūfah read *fī ghayābati al-jubb* in the singular, while the people of Madīnah
read *ghayābāt* in the plural; Abū ʿUbayd chose the singular because they cast him into a single place, and
al-Jumaḥ denied the plural outright. Al-Naḥḥās held that this is a restriction upon the language, and that
the plural is permissible on two grounds: Sībawayh reported the expression "he was travelled upon for
evenings and late-afternoons" meaning one evening and one late-afternoon, making each part of the time an
evening and a late-afternoon — so likewise each part of what conceals may be called a *ghayābah*; and the
other ground is that there may in fact be several recesses within the well. One says *ghāba yaghību
ghayban wa-ghayābatan wa-ghiyāban*, as in the poetʾs line "stay two months, or half of a third — here I am,
my hiding has hidden me." Al-Harawī said *al-ghayābah* is like a hollow eaten out at the side of a basin or
well by the water, becoming like a small cave, or a niche in the well just above the waterline, where a
thing is lost to the eye; Ibn ʿAzīz said everything that hides a thing from you is a *ghayābah*; and
**[Qurṭubī]** adds that hence the grave is called a *ghayābah*, quoting the poet "if one day my *ghayābah*
hides me, then follow my course among the tribe and the family." *Al-jubb* is a well that has not been lined
with stone; once lined it is called a *biʾr*, as in al-Aʿshāʾs line "if you were in a *jubb* eighty fathoms
deep, and were raised to the causes of the sky by a ladder …"; it is called *jubb* because it was cut into
the earth. **[Jalālayn]** One of them, namely Judah, said: "Do not kill Joseph, but cast him — throw him —
into the bottom of a well"; *ghayābat al-jubb* is the darkest recess of a well, and a variant reading has
the plural *ghayābāt*; "so that some caravan might pick him up, if you are to do anything" of what you
desire in the way of separating Joseph from us — so content yourselves with this solution. **[Ibn Kathīr]**
Qatādah and Muḥammad b. Isḥāq said the speaker was the oldest among them, named Rubil (Reuben); al-Suddī
said his name was Yahūdhā (Judah); and Mujāhid said it was Shamʿūn (Simeon), who said, "Kill not Yūsuf" — do
not let your enmity and hatred towards him reach this level, of murder. Their plot to kill Yūsuf would not
have succeeded, because God willed that Yūsuf fulfil a mission that must be fulfilled: he would receive
Godʾs revelation and become His prophet, and God willed him to be a powerful man in Egypt and to govern it;
consequently God did not allow them to persist in their intent, and brought about Reubenʾs words and his
advice that if they must do something they should throw him down to the bottom of a well, "that he be picked
up by some caravan" of travellers passing by — this way, he said, you will rid yourselves of this bother
without having to kill him; "if you are going to act" means, if you still insist on getting rid of him. Ibn
Isḥāq reflected that they had agreed upon a particularly vicious crime, one involving the cutting of the
ties of the womb, undutiful treatment of parents, and harshness towards the young, the helpless and the
sinless; it was harsh too towards the old and weak, who have the right to be respected, honoured and
appreciated, and to be honoured for Godʾs sake and for the parental rights they hold over their offspring.
They sought to separate a beloved father — who had reached old age, whose bones had grown weak, and who held
a high station with God — from his beloved young son, in spite of the sonʾs weakness, tender age and need of
his fatherʾs compassion and kindness. May God forgive them; He is the most merciful of those who show mercy,
for they intended to carry out a grave error. **[Study Quran]** "One among them" refers to their oldest
brother, who is not identified in the Qurʾān but whose action resembles that of either Judah or Reuben (cf.
Genesis 37:22–28); according to the majority of commentators the suggestion to cast him into the depths of
the well so that some caravan might pick him up did not meet with the brothersʾ approval until later — see
12:15.

**Rulings.** **[Maʿārif]** explains two words on which al-Qurṭubī builds law. *Ghayābah* literally means
everything that hides a thing or makes it disappear, which is why a grave is also called a *ghayābah*;
*jubb* is a well without raised sidings. And *yaltaqiṭhu* derives from *luqṭah*: something left or dropped
which is found by someone without any desire of his own for it; if the thing found is inert it is called
*luqṭah*, and if it is a living being it is identified in the terminology of the Muslim jurists as *laqīṭ*.
A human being is called *laqīṭ* when he or she is a child, not rational and not yet pubertal. From this
word al-Qurṭubī proves that when Yūsuf was thrown into the pit of the well he was a pre-pubertal child; and
Yaʿqūbʾs later words, "I fear lest a wolf should devour him" (12:13), point the same way, since being eaten
by a wolf can only be imagined in the case of a child. As reported by Ibn Jarīr, Ibn al-Mundhir and Ibn Abī
Shaybah, Yūsufʾs age at that time was seven years *(Isrāʾīliyyāt)*. **[Maʿārif]** then sets out a principle
from the law of lost property *(digression)*: al-Qurṭubī gives here the full details of the rulings on
*luqṭah* and *laqīṭ*, for which this is not the appropriate occasion, but one underlying matter must be
understood — Islam has a system of its own in which the protection of the life and property of common
people, the upkeep and cleanliness of walkways and streets, and similar civic duties are not left in the
sole charge of government departments; instead every individual is made obligated to keep them safe and
clean. The ḥadīth sternly warns those who make things difficult for passers-by by crowding, lingering or
depositing or throwing their belongings on public walkways: "The jihād of a person who blocks or clogs the
passageway of Muslims is not acceptable." The same principle covers thorns, nails, broken glass or rocks
lying on a walkway that pose a danger to those who pass.

**Cross-references.** The advice is carried out at 12:15, "when they took him away and agreed to put him in
the depths of the well"; the elder brotherʾs later refusal to leave Egypt is at 12:80; the fear of the wolf
at 12:13; and the picking up by the caravan at 12:19 **[Study Quran]**. Cf. Genesis 37:22–28 **[Study
Quran]**.

**Reflection.** The verse holds the storyʾs first mercy, and the commentators cannot agree on who showed
it: Reuben, Judah or Simeon. What matters is its shape — a man who could not stop the crime limited it. He
did not argue that Yūsuf was innocent, nor appeal to their fatherʾs grief; he offered a cheaper way of
getting what they wanted, and in doing so placed his brother in the hands of God rather than of his
brothers. **[Ibn Kathīr]**ʾs gloss is that God put those words in his mouth because He had already willed
the mission Yūsuf was to fulfil: the well was not the end of the plan but the first stage of another one.
And the pit itself — an unlined shaft with a hollow just above the water, dark enough to hide a child —
becomes the image the whole sūrah will keep returning to: the place where Godʾs care begins to look like
abandonment.
