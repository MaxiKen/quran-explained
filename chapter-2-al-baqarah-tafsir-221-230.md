# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 221–230

This batch is the surah's passage on marriage: whom to marry, what happens inside the marriage, and how it ends. It is also the batch in which the Qur'an legislates most concretely about the most private areas of a person's life, and the way it does so is worth watching — it does not treat these as embarrassed asides. It places a verse about menstruation next to a verse about intimacy, next to verses about oaths used as weapons, and then legislates the dissolving of a marriage with the word *ḥudūd* — limits — repeated three times in a single verse.

The sequence runs: **whom to marry** (221), **the body and its boundaries** (222–223), **the abuse of oaths** (224–225), **withdrawal and its limit** (226–227), and **divorce, its structure, and its exit** (228–230). And 222 adds one more to the surah's "they ask you" series — this time a question the men were evidently embarrassed to ask.

One structural note that will help throughout: every one of these verses after 221 closes with a pair of divine names, and the pair changes with the subject. Returning to a wife is met with *Ghafūr, Raḥīm*. Resolving on divorce is met with *Samī', 'Alīm*. The rights of spouses are closed with *'Azīz, Ḥakīm*. The names are not decoration; they are the verse's own comment on what has just been legislated.

## Verse 221

**"Do not marry polytheistic women until they believe; for a believing slave-woman is better than a free polytheist, even though she may look pleasant to you. And do not marry your women to polytheistic men until they believe, for a believing slave-man is better than a free polytheist, even though he may look pleasant to you. They invite ˹you˺ to the Fire while Allah invites ˹you˺ to Paradise and forgiveness by His grace. He makes His revelations clear to the people so perhaps they will be mindful."**

In simple terms: do not marry polytheist women until they believe — a believing slave-woman is better than a polytheist woman, even though she pleases you. And do not marry [your women] to polytheist men until they believe — a believing slave-man is better than a polytheist man, even though he pleases you. Those invite to the Fire, while Allah invites to the Garden and to forgiveness, by His permission. And He makes clear His signs to the people, so that they may remember.

*Wa lā tankiḥū al-mushrikāti ḥattā yu'minna, wa la-amatun mu'minatun khayrun min mushrikatin wa law a'jabatkum, wa lā tunkiḥū al-mushrikīna ḥattā yu'minū, wa la-'abdun mu'minun khayrun min mushrikin wa law a'jabakum, ulā'ika yad'ūna ilā al-nāri wa Allāhu yad'ū ilā al-jannati wa al-maghfirati bi-idhnih, wa yubayyinu āyātihi li-al-nāsi la'allahum yatadhakkarūn.*

**The first thing to notice is that the verse addresses men twice, in two different capacities, and the Arabic marks the difference with two different verbs.**

*Wa lā **tankiḥū** al-mushrikāt* — this is form I: "do not **marry** polytheist women." The subject is the man marrying for himself.

*Wa lā **tunkiḥū** al-mushrikīn* — this is form IV (or II): "do not **give** [your women] **in marriage** to polytheist men." The subject here is the guardian — the father, brother, or trustee — who gives a woman in marriage.

**That grammatical distinction carries the whole social argument.** The verse is not only telling men whom they may choose; it is telling the people who control women whom they may be given to. In a seventh-century Arabian kinship system, women's marriages were largely arranged. So the prohibition had to be addressed twice, or it would have been a prohibition with a hole in it: a man could obey it personally and still marry his daughter to a polytheist. The Qur'an closes the hole.

**Now the comparison, which is the most socially explosive sentence in the verse.**

*Wa la-amatun mu'minatun khayrun min mushrikatin wa law a'jabatkum* — "and a believing **slave-woman** is better than a polytheist woman, even though she pleases you."

**Note what is being compared: the lowest-status believer against the highest-status non-believer.** Not believer against believer, and not free woman against free woman. A *believing slave woman* against a *polytheist free woman*. In a society organised by lineage, tribal standing, and freedom, this is a demolition of the entire scale. The verse is saying: on this question — the question of whom to marry — the only metric that counts is *īmān*, and by that metric the person at the very bottom of your society outranks the person near the top of it.

**A note on the translation.** The supplied rendering says "better than a **free** polytheist" — the word *free* is supplied, not present in the Arabic, which simply says *mushrikah*, "a polytheist woman." The supply is correct and it is what the commentators say: the point of the contrast only lands if the polytheist woman is free and the believing woman is enslaved. But it is worth knowing that the Arabic achieves the contrast by naming the believer's low status explicitly (*amah* — a female slave) and leaving the other party's high status to be inferred. The verse spends its one explicit word on the believer's humility, not on her rival's advantage.

**And the verse anticipates the objection.**

*Wa law a'jabatkum* — "even though she **pleases** you." *A'jaba* is to excite admiration, to be pleasing, striking, attractive. The verse is answering the argument that everyone makes: but she is beautiful. But he is wonderful. But we love each other. But you should see her.

The answer is not "no she isn't." The answer is: **yes, and still.** The attractiveness is conceded in the same breath as the prohibition. The verse does not ask anyone to pretend the polytheist spouse is unappealing; it says that appeal is not the relevant currency.

**Now the reason, and it is a reason rather than a bare command.**

*Ulā'ika yad'ūna ilā al-nār* — "**those invite to the Fire**."

Why would a spouse invite to the Fire? The commentators are consistent on the mechanism: marriage is the most intimate and most formative human association. A spouse shapes the religion of the household, the religion of the children, and — most of all — the religion of the other spouse, slowly, through affection. You become like the person you love. The polytheist does not have to proselytise; he simply has to be who he is, and the pull is constant.

**Which means the real subject of this verse is not religion-as-a-label. It is direction. The question the verse asks about a marriage is: which way does it pull?**

*Wa Allāhu yad'ū ilā al-jannati wa al-maghfirati bi-idhnih* — "and Allah invites to the Garden and to forgiveness, by His permission."

Note that the two invitations are grammatically parallel: they call, and He calls. **A marriage is a contest of calls.** And note that God's call is to *al-jannah* **and** *al-maghfirah* — the Garden **and** forgiveness. Not to Paradise alone. Which tells you that the audience being addressed has things that need forgiving.

*Wa yubayyinu āyātihi li-al-nāsi la'allahum yatadhakkarūn* — "and He makes clear His signs to the people, so that they may **remember**." *Yatadhakkarūn* — the closing word of the verse is *remembrance*. The verse ends by saying: this is not a new burden I am laying on you; it is something you already knew and are being reminded of.

**Now the scope question, which is genuinely disputed and must be reported as such.**

Does *mushrikāt* — polytheist women — include women of the People of the Book (Jews and Christians)?

**The position of the majority of the jurists, and the one the tradition has acted on:** no. The term *mushrik* in this verse is taken to refer to the Arab idolaters who had no revealed scripture, and 5:5 explicitly permits marriage to "chaste women from among those who were given the Scripture before you." So the two verses are read as complementary: 2:221 prohibits idolaters; 5:5 permits the People of the Book.

**Al-Tabari's position** appears to be that the verse does not apply to women of the People of the Book, who are not *mushrik* in the relevant sense.

**Al-Qurṭubī reports a contrary view** — that 2:221 includes the People of the Book, and that 5:5 is therefore abrogated by 2:221 — and then rejects it on chronological grounds: Sūrat al-Baqarah was revealed before Sūrat al-Mā'idah, and an earlier verse does not abrogate a later one. His rejection is decisive on the mechanics.

**The second and harder question is the asymmetry.** The tradition's settled position is that a Muslim man may marry a chaste woman of the People of the Book, while a Muslim woman may not marry a non-Muslim man, whether polytheist or of the People of the Book. The textual basis for the prohibition on women is 60:10 ("then if you find them to be believing women, do not return them to the disbelievers; they are not lawful for them, nor are they lawful for the women"), combined with the absence of any verse parallel to 5:5 permitting it.

**This asymmetry is a live, contested question in contemporary scholarship, and it should be reported as contested rather than as settled consensus.** The classical rationale, set out at length by the commentators, runs through the husband's role as maintainer and head of the household, and the resulting likelihood that the wife would be drawn toward his religion — the same *'illah* (effective cause) as 2:221's "they invite to the Fire." Contemporary revisionist readings argue from the same *'illah*: if the cause is the pull, then the ruling should follow the cause rather than the gender, and in social conditions where the pull runs the other way the reasoning does not hold. Others reply that the *'illah* is not the only ground and that the Qur'anic texts are being read selectively.

A commentary that presents either side as *the* position is not telling the truth. What can be said without controversy is this: **no verse of the Qur'an states a general permission for a Muslim woman to marry a non-Muslim man, and the juristic tradition has been unanimous in prohibiting it.** Both sides agree on that; they differ on whether it is revisable.

**One further application of the *'illah* that is not disputed and that matters enormously in practice.** The verse's reason is the pull toward the Fire. The commentators drew the obvious conclusion: the ruling attaches to the *actual* religion of the person, not to the name of the community they were born into. A woman who is called a Christian and does not believe in God, or in the prophethood of Jesus, or in the Gospel as revealed scripture, is not a woman "of the Book" in the sense 5:5 means, and marriage to her is not covered by the permission. The point is made explicitly in the classical commentary and is worth restating now, when "Christian" and "Jewish" frequently describe a cultural background rather than a faith: **the verse asks what the person actually believes and where they actually pull you, not what their family was.**

**For practice, and for anyone counselling someone considering marriage.** The verse gives a single test, and it is not "is she a good person" or "does he treat me well" or "will my family accept it." It is: *which way does this pull me?* Toward gratitude and obedience, or away from them? Everything else — beauty, compatibility, kindness, wealth, family feeling — the verse concedes in the phrase *wa law a'jabatkum*, and then rules against. That is hard, and the verse knows it is hard, which is why it says "even though she pleases you" instead of pretending she doesn't.

And for the parent or guardian reading this: the second half of the verse is addressed to you. The prohibition on giving a woman in marriage is yours to answer for.

## Verse 222

**"They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about menstruation. Say, “Beware of its harm! So keep away, and do not have intercourse with your wives during their monthly cycles until they are purified. When they purify themselves, then you may approach them in the manner specified by Allah. Surely Allah loves those who always turn to Him in repentance and those who purify themselves.”"**

In simple terms: and they ask you about menstruation. Say: it is a hurt. So keep away from women during menstruation, and do not approach them until they are pure. Then when they have purified themselves, come to them from where Allah has commanded you. Indeed, Allah loves those who are constantly repentant, and He loves those who purify themselves.

*Wa yas'alūnaka 'an al-maḥīḍ, qul huwa adhan, fa-i'tazilū al-nisā'a fī al-maḥīḍi wa lā taqrabūhunna ḥattā yaṭhurna, fa-idhā taṭahharna fa'tūhunna min ḥaythu amarakumu Allāh, inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-tawwābīna wa yuḥibbu al-mutaṭahhirīn.*

**This is another of the "they ask you" verses, and it is the one the Companions were most reluctant to ask. The narration explains why, and the answer begins by refusing the premise behind the question.**

**The first word of the answer does enormous work:** *qul huwa **adhan*** — "say: it is a **hurt**."

*Adhan* is from *adhā*: harm, hurt, discomfort, injury. **It is not the word for filth, and it is not the word for impurity.** The Qur'an had words available for that — *najas*, *rijs* — and did not use them. It chose the word that means *this is harmful*.

**That single lexical choice is the whole theology of this verse.** Menstruation is not a moral state. It is not a pollution. The woman is not unclean, and she is not in a condition of sin or defilement. It is a physical condition that is, for reasons of health and wisdom, harmful to engage with in a particular way. And the Prophet ﷺ said it directly: **"The believer is not made impure"** — *inna al-mu'mina lā yanjusu* — recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim.

*Fa-i'tazilū al-nisā'a fī al-maḥīḍ* — "so keep away from women during menstruation."

**Note the object: keep away from *the women* — not from an act.** The commentators are agreed that what is meant is sexual intercourse, and the next clause makes that explicit. But the wording is worth pausing on: *i'tazilū al-nisā'* is "withdraw from the women," and *lā taqrabūhunna* is "**do not approach them**."

**And here is a point the supplied translation resolves that the Arabic leaves open.** The Arabic says *lā taqrabūhunna* — **do not approach them** — and the supplied translation renders it "do not have intercourse with." That rendering is the correct *legal* meaning and it is what the commentators say. But the word the Qur'an chose is *approach*, not *have intercourse with*, and that is the same verb used elsewhere in the surah to fence off an act rather than merely prohibit it — *lā taqrabā hādhihi al-shajarah* ("do not approach this tree," 2:35), and *wa lā taqrabūhunna* in 2:187 for the fast and the retreat. The pattern is consistent: **the Qur'an puts a boundary around the thing rather than at its edge.** A prohibition on approaching is a prohibition with a margin.

**Now the two stages, and the Arabic distinguishes them carefully.**

*Ḥattā **yaṭhurna*** — "until they **become pure**." Form I, intransitive: the state comes about. This is the **cessation of the bleeding**.

*Fa-idhā **taṭahharna*** — "then when they **purify themselves**." Form V, with the reflexive sense of doing something to oneself. This is the **major ablution (ghusl)**.

Two different verbs from the same root, in sequence. **This is not stylistic variation; it is the ruling.** Intercourse does not become lawful the moment the bleeding stops. There is a first stage — the flow ends — and then a second stage — the woman washes — and the verse permits approach only after the second.

The supplied translation preserves both clauses ("until they are purified... When they purify themselves") but uses the same English verb for both, which softens the distinction. **The Arabic is doing legal work here, and the distinction is why the schools rule that intercourse before the ghusl is not lawful.** There is a subsidiary difference among the schools about the case of someone without access to water, and about the Ḥanafī ruling allowing the passage of a prayer time as a substitute in some circumstances — the details are school-specific and should not be flattened into a single rule.

*Fa'tūhunna min ḥaythu amarakumu Allāh* — "then come to them **from where Allah has commanded you**."

The commentators read *from where* as the place: the vagina, the site of the *tilth*. So the clause both permits — after purification, approach is commanded rather than merely allowed — and delimits. This is the same point 2:223 will make explicit, and the same point the hadith makes plainer still.

**And now the closing, which is one of the most beautiful and most misread endings in the surah.**

*Inna Allāha yuḥibbu al-tawwābīna wa yuḥibbu al-mutaṭahhirīn* — "indeed, Allah **loves** those who are constantly repentant, and He **loves** those who purify themselves."

**A verse about menstruation closes with two declarations of divine love.** Sit with what that does.

The passage could have ended on the restriction, and a person encountering it that way could reasonably conclude that this is a verse about a woman's deficiency — a condition that removes her from something, that must be waited out, that inconveniences everyone. Instead it ends on God's love for two categories of people: *al-tawwābīn*, those who turn back constantly — the intensive form, meaning the turning is repeated — and *al-mutaṭahhirīn*, those who purify themselves — also the intensive form, meaning they keep purifying.

**Three things happen in that ending worth naming.**

The first: the restriction has just been reframed as a *site of obedience*, and obedience done purely because God said so is precisely what *tawbah* looks like in practice. The one who abstains not because he is repelled but because he was told to is a *tā'ib*.

The second: *al-mutaṭahhirīn* covers both meanings at once — physical purification, the ghusl the verse just legislated, and the purification of the heart. The two are held together in one word, which is the Qur'an's habitual way of refusing to let the body and the soul be separated.

And the third: **the verse that could most easily have been heard as a statement about a woman's impurity is closed with a statement about God's love.** The last word on the subject is not "she is set apart." It is "He loves." Anyone who has read this verse as a verse about a woman's uncleanness has stopped one line short of the point.

**The Sunnah places the verse between two extremes, and the narration is in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*.**

Anas ibn Mālik reported: when a woman among the Jews menstruated, they would not eat with her, nor mix with her in the houses. The Companions of the Prophet ﷺ asked about this, and God revealed this verse, and the Messenger of God ﷺ said: **"Do everything except intercourse."** When this reached the Jews, they said: "What does this man want? He does not leave any of our affairs without opposing us in it!"

Note the two extremes the ruling avoids. The Jews of Madinah, on this report, practised **total separation** — not sharing a table, not sharing a room. The pre-Islamic Arabs, on the same report, practised **no separation at all**. And the Prophet ﷺ legislated the middle: **everything except the one act.**

And note the detail in the narration: Usayd ibn Ḥudayr and 'Abbād ibn Bishr heard the Jews' remark and said, in effect, "then let us have intercourse with them too, to oppose them," and the Prophet ﷺ was angered and they brought milk to make amends. **That detail — transmitted in the Sunan collections and reported as such — carries its own principle: the religion is not defined by opposition to other people's practices.** The middle way is not "the opposite of what they do." It is what God legislated, and it is arrived at independently of who else is doing what.

**And the Sunnah shows what "everything except intercourse" looked like in the Prophet's own house.** Recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from 'Ā'ishah: the Prophet ﷺ would recline in her lap and recite Qur'an while she was menstruating. Recorded by Muslim: when one of his wives was menstruating and he wanted intimacy, he would tell her to wrap something around her waist, and then he would embrace her — and 'Ā'ishah added: "And who among you can control his desire as the Messenger of God ﷺ controlled his desire?" Recorded likewise: he would put his head out to her from the mosque while in retreat and she would wash his hair while she was menstruating.

**Every one of those is a deliberate, public dismantling of the idea that a menstruating woman is untouchable.** He ate with her, lay against her, recited the Qur'an with his head in her lap, had her wash his hair, and embraced her. The tradition could not have been clearer if it had tried, and it was clearer than most communities have been willing to notice.

**On the ruling and its expiation.** Intercourse during menstruation is, by agreement, a grave sin requiring repentance. **On whether a specific expiation is prescribed, the honest answer is that it is disputed because the hadith is weak.** There is a narration — in the Sunan collections, from Ibn 'Abbās — prescribing a dīnār or half a dīnār in charity. The hadith critics have graded it variously, with several holding it to be *mawqūf* (a statement of a Companion rather than a Prophetic ruling) and others noting weakness in the chain. The majority of the jurists therefore hold that **no specific expiation is prescribed**, while some recommended voluntary charity. That distinction matters, because people are often told a fixed penalty that the evidence does not support.

**Two further points of genuine disagreement that a reader will meet.** Whether a menstruating woman may recite Qur'an, and whether she may touch a copy of the Qur'an: the schools differ, with the majority prohibiting recitation during menses (some permitting it from memory for fear of forgetting) and prohibiting touching the *muṣḥaf*. Whether she may sit in the mosque: the majority prohibit it, and others permit it with precautions, and the narrations on the point are themselves graded variously. **These are juristic differences, and a person should follow the scholarship they trust rather than be told there is one answer.**

**The contemporary relevance of the word *adhan* deserves one careful sentence.** The verse gives a *reason* — harm — rather than a bare taboo, and the reason is the kind that can be investigated. Modern medicine does describe increased vulnerability to infection during menstruation. **That is corroboration, not proof, and it is not why the ruling is binding.** The ruling is binding because God legislated it, and the stated wisdom is *adhan*. But it is worth noting that a scripture which had said "she is unclean" would have closed the door to enquiry, whereas a scripture that says "it is a hurt" leaves it open — and that is precisely what we find.

**The pastoral point to end on.** For many women, this verse has been used as an instrument of shame. The verse itself is the opposite of that: it names a physical condition, restricts one act for a stated reason, and then closes by declaring God's love for the repentant and the purified. A woman who reads it and concludes she is defiled has been taught by someone other than this text.

## Verse 223

**"Your wives are like farmland for you, so approach them ˹consensually˺ as you please. And send forth something good for yourselves. Be mindful of Allah, and know that you will meet Him. And give good news to the believers."**

In simple terms: your women are a tilth for you, so come to your tilth as you wish, and send forward for yourselves. And fear Allah and know that you will meet Him. And give good news to the believers.

*Nisā'ukum ḥarthun lakum fa'tū ḥarthakum annā shi'tum, wa qaddimū li-anfusikum, wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-'lamū annakum mulāqūhu, wa bashshir al-mu'minīn.*

**This verse is at once the most misused and the most misunderstood in the batch, so it needs to be taken apart slowly.**

*Nisā'ukum **ḥarthun** lakum* — "your women are a **tilth** for you."

*Ḥarth* is tilled land, a cultivated field, the prepared ground in which seed is sown. The metaphor is agricultural, and it is **about destination and fruitfulness**, not about ownership or use. A field is where you plant in order to grow something. The word carries within it the purpose: progeny, continuation, a harvest. The commentators are direct about this — al-Ṭabarī glosses it: "your women are the sowing-place of your children."

**Which means the metaphor is the most relational and most purposeful one available in that vocabulary.** The verse does not call the wife a possession, an object, or a vessel. It calls her the *ground in which a human being is grown*. The gravity of that image is the opposite of what its critics usually assume.

*Fa'tū ḥarthakum **annā shi'tum*** — "so come to your tilth **as/when/however you wish**."

**Here is the structure, and it is precise: one thing is fixed, one thing is free.**

The fixed thing is the *place*: ***ḥarthakum*** — your tilth. The emphatic repetition of the word (it appears twice in one sentence: *nisā'ukum ḥarthun* ... *fa'tū ḥarthakum*) is deliberate. You come to the field. That is not negotiable.

The free thing is the *manner*: ***annā shi'tum***. *Annā* is one of the widest adverbs in Arabic — it means *how*, *when*, *whence*, *from where*. The verse grants latitude across all of them: position, timing, manner.

**So the verse is not a licence. It is a licence bounded by a fixed point, and the fixed point is named twice.**

**And the Sunnah fixes the boundary explicitly.** Recorded by al-Bukhārī, Muslim and Abū Dāwūd from Jābir ibn 'Abdillāh: the Jews used to say that if a man penetrates his wife from behind, the child born of it will be cross-eyed. Then God revealed: *your women are a tilth for you, so come to your tilth as you wish*. In the wording collected by Muslim: "if he wishes, from behind, and if he wishes, from the front — as long as it is in the one place [the vagina]." And in a related narration: "penetrate your wives from the front and from the back, but avoid the anus and menstruation."

And the explicit hadith, reported in the Sunan collections — by al-Nasā'ī and Ibn Mājah, and by al-Tirmidhī and al-Nasā'ī from Ibn 'Abbās: the Prophet ﷺ said, **"God is not shy of the truth"** — said three times — **"do not have intercourse with women in their anuses."** Al-Tirmidhī graded the narration from Ibn 'Abbās *ḥasan*.

**On the ruling itself:** the overwhelming majority of the jurists hold anal intercourse to be prohibited. Reports exist of a contrary view attributed to some early figures; these are not acted upon and the settled position of the schools is prohibition. That is the honest summary, and the hadith is plain.

**A second transmitted occasion should be reported alongside the first.** Sa'īd ibn al-Musayyib is reported to have said that the verse was revealed about *'azl* — coitus interruptus. And other transmissions connect it to the objection of the Anṣār women to the positions their Qurayshī husbands were accustomed to. These are transmitted variants in the literature; the Jābir narration, via Bukhārī and Muslim, is the strongest and the best known.

**Now a note on the supplied translation's gloss.** The rendering inserts *˹consensually˺*: "approach them **˹consensually˺** as you please." **The word is not in the Arabic**, which says only *annā shi'tum*. The gloss is supplying a condition that is certainly established elsewhere in the religion — a wife cannot be forced, and the Prophet ﷺ annulled a marriage in which a woman had been married off without her consent, and he said, in the narration recorded by Muslim, that a previously-married woman has more right over herself than her guardian, and that a virgin's permission must be sought. So the *ruling* the gloss asserts is sound. But a reader should know the word is supplied, because it means the "as you please" of the Arabic has been narrowed before the reader sees it — and because the narrowing is exactly what the rest of the tradition does.

*Wa **qaddimū li-anfusikum*** — "and **send forward for yourselves**."

**This clause is the verse's pivot, and it is extraordinary where it is placed.** In the middle of a verse about sexual intimacy, an instruction about good deeds. The commentators give two readings: the general one (do righteous deeds) and the specific one — reported from Ibn 'Abbās and others — that it means the *tasmiyah*, saying "in the name of God," before intercourse.

Both are right, and they are the same point: **nothing in a believer's life is outside the frame of worship.** Not even this. The most private act is also a place where something can be sent ahead.

**And there is a hadith that makes the point unforgettable, in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*** from Abū Dharr: the Companions said, "O Messenger of God, when one of us satisfies his desire, is there a reward in it?" He said: **"Do you not see that if he did it in an unlawful way he would be sinning? So if he does it in a lawful way, he has a reward."** That is *wa qaddimū li-anfusikum* in a sentence. The lawful satisfaction of desire is a site of reward, because the alternative was a site of sin, and choosing the lawful over the unlawful is the deed that is sent forward.

*Wa-ittaqū Allāha wa-'lamū annakum **mulāqūhu*** — "and fear Allah, and know that you will **meet Him**."

**Note where this is placed.** Not in a verse about prayer or charity or war, but in the verse about sexual intimacy. Theologically, this is doing something specific: it is removing the last private space. There is no room in a human life where the meeting with God is not relevant. The act that no one else witnesses is still-lived before the One you will meet.

*Wa bashshir al-mu'minīn* — "and give good news to the believers."

The verse closes with glad tidings, in a passage that has just restricted and commanded. **The restriction is not the last word.** The one who observes the limits is promised good news — and given the structure, the good news is about exactly the thing under discussion: that desire, rightly ordered, is rewarded rather than merely tolerated.

**Now the misunderstandings, and there are three.**

**The first:** that *annā shi'tum* is unlimited. It is not. The verse itself fixes the place; the Sunnah fixes it further; and the rest of the law sets times when intimacy is forbidden — during menstruation (2:222), during the day in Ramaḍān while fasting, during retreat in the mosque (2:187), and during the pilgrimage state of *iḥrām*. "As you wish" is latitude *within* the law, which is the only kind of latitude there is.

**The second:** that the *tilth* metaphor licenses treating a wife as an instrument. The metaphor says the opposite. A farmer who ruins his field has ruined his own harvest; the field is where his children are grown. The image places the husband under an obligation of care toward the very thing the verse calls his.

**And the third, which runs the other way:** that this verse is crude or degrading. It is worth saying plainly that a scripture which never mentions the subject cannot govern it, and one that mentions it only in euphemism cannot legislate it. The Qur'an here speaks about the most intimate area of life with a metaphor that is dignified, purposeful, and bounded, in a passage that closes with the fear of God and good news. That is not coarseness. It is a religion that declines to treat any part of human life as beneath its attention.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 224

**"Do not use Allah’s Name in your oaths as an excuse for not doing good, not guarding against evil, or not making peace between people. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: and do not make Allah, in your oaths, a hindrance to your being righteous and fearing [Him] and setting right between people. And Allah is Hearing, Knowing.

*Wa lā taj'alū Allāha 'urḍatan li-aymānikum an tabarrū wa tattaqū wa tuṣliḥū bayna al-nāsi, wa Allāhu samī'un 'alīm.*

**The difficult word is *'urḍah*, and the whole verse turns on it.**

*'Urḍah* is, at root, a thing placed **across** — a barrier, a buttress, something set in the way, a target exposed. The phrase *lā taj'alū Allāha 'urḍatan li-aymānikum* has been read in several ways, and the readings are worth having because they add up to a complete picture rather than competing.

**The first and dominant reading** — the one al-Ṭabarī and Ibn Kathīr favour — is: **do not make God's name a pretext that prevents you from doing good.** Do not say "I swore by God" in order to get out of an obligation you should be fulfilling. The oath becomes a shield you hide behind.

**The second reading:** do not make swearing by God into such a habit that your oaths become a snare — swearing constantly until every future good act is a potential perjury.

**The third reading:** do not expose God's name to your oaths, i.e., do not treat it lightly by invoking it constantly.

**All three are true, and they converge on the same practical point: God's name is too great to be used as a tool for getting out of things.**

**Now the three goods that an oath must never block:**

*An **tabarrū*** — that you should be **righteous**. *Birr* is the widest word for goodness in the Qur'an — it covers obedience, dutifulness, kindness to parents, maintaining ties of kinship, and good character. This is the word that appeared in 2:177, where the whole definition of righteousness was set out.

*Wa **tattaqū*** — that you should **fear** [God]. *Taqwā*, the surah's governing word.

*Wa **tuṣliḥū bayna al-nāsi*** — that you should **set right between people**. Reconciliation.

**That third item is the one to hold.** The verse names *iṣlāḥ bayna al-nās* — mending relations between people — as something so valuable that an oath cannot be allowed to obstruct it. **Piety may not be used as a reason to refuse reconciliation.** That sentence should be written over every family feud conducted in the name of religion, and it is the plain meaning of this verse.

**And the connection to the verses that follow is immediate and deliberate.** 2:224 is placed directly before the passage on *īlā'* (226) and divorce (228–230), and the classic case is the man who swears "by God I will never go back to her" or "by God I will never speak to him again" — and then wants to. The verse says: that oath is not a wall. Break it and expiate.

**The Sunnah is unambiguous.** Recorded by Muslim from Abū Hurayrah: the Prophet ﷺ said, **"Whoever swears an oath and then sees something better than it, let him do what is better and expiate his oath."** And recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from 'Abd al-Raḥmān ibn Samurah: **"If you swear an oath and then see something better than it, then do what is better, and expiate your oath."**

Note the structure of the ruling: **do the better thing, and then expiate.** Not "expiate instead." Not "keep the oath because an oath is an oath." The better act is done, and the oath is discharged by its prescribed expiation.

**And the expiation is set out at 5:89:** feeding ten poor people from the average of what you feed your family, or clothing them, or freeing a slave; and whoever cannot find the means, fasting three days. The religion therefore prices the release — it is not free, but it is available, and it is deliberately not crippling. **An oath is serious enough to require expiation and not so serious that it cannot be undone.**

**A related verse is worth pairing with this one:** "Allah has already ordained for you [the way of] the dissolution of your oaths" (66:2), revealed, as the narration in al-Bukhārī and Muslim from 'Ā'ishah relates, concerning the Prophet's own prohibition of something lawful to himself. If the Prophet ﷺ was shown the way out of an oath, no one else's oath is a life sentence.

*Wa Allāhu **samī'un 'alīm*** — "and Allah is **Hearing, Knowing**."

**And the closing names fit the subject exactly.** An oath is an *utterance* — it is heard. And an oath is an *intention* — it is known. **Hearing** covers the words that left your mouth; **Knowing** covers what your heart was actually binding itself to. And that pairing is precisely the subject of the very next verse.

**For practice, this verse is a scalpel for a very common human situation.** The person who says: "I swore I'd never lend him money again" — and then his brother needs money. "I promised God I'd never set foot in that house" — and then a death in the family. "I took an oath I'd never speak to her" — and then years pass. The verse says the oath does not hold. Do the better thing and pay the expiation. **God's name was invoked to make the vow impressive, and God is now declining to be used as a wall.**

And the obverse: the person who swears constantly — "wallāhi, billāhi" on every second sentence — is doing exactly what the second and third readings prohibit. He is making God's name into a habit, and he is setting snares for his own future obedience.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 225

**"Allah will not hold you accountable for unintentional oaths, but for what you intended in your hearts. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing."**

In simple terms: Allah does not take you to task for the *laghw* [the vain, unintended element] in your oaths, but He takes you to task for what your hearts have earned. And Allah is Forgiving, Forbearing.

*Lā yu'ākhidhukumu Allāhu bi-al-laghwi fī aymānikum wa lākin yu'ākhidhukumu bimā kasabat qulūbukum, wa Allāhu ghafūrun ḥalīm.*

**This verse completes 2:224. Read them together: 224 says an oath may never be used as a barrier to good; 225 says an oath is not binding unless the heart bound it.** Together they establish both that oaths have real weight and that the weight is exactly as heavy as the intention.

**The key word is *laghw*, and it is genuinely disputed.**

*Laghw* is, at root, speech that is vain, idle, falling away — what is said without counting. Applied to oaths, the commentators divide:

**The first reading** — the one reported from 'Ā'ishah and followed by al-Shāfi'ī and many others — is that *laghw* is the oath that slips out without intent: a person's habitual "no, by God" and "yes, by God" spoken as emphasis or filler, with no oath in the heart. The analogy the commentators draw is to everyday speech in which the formula is used as punctuation.

**The second reading** — the position attributed to Abū Ḥanīfah and the Ḥanafīs — is that *laghw* is swearing about a **past** matter believing it to be so when it is not: a person swears "by God, I paid him" honestly believing he paid, and he had not. There is no sin, because there was no lie, and — on this reading — no expiation.

**The third strand** in the literature includes oaths spoken in anger or in jest, without resolve.

**The disagreement is real and it is not cosmetic, because it changes the expiation.** On the first reading, the filler-oath carries nothing at all. On the second, there is no expiation in this world for the oath of honest error, though some held a ruling of expiation by analogy. The schools' positions on which cases require *kaffārah* should be consulted rather than collapsed into one answer.

**What is not disputed is the principle stated in the second clause, and it is the verse's real content.**

*Wa lākin yu'ākhidhukumu bimā **kasabat qulūbukum*** — "but He takes you to task for what your **hearts have earned**."

**Liability tracks the heart.** Not the tongue. Not the wording. Not the formula. The heart.

**And that is the single greatest principle in the whole of Islamic law, stated here in a verse about oaths.** The hadith that every collection opens with — recorded by al-Bukhārī and Muslim from 'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb — is: **"Actions are only by intentions, and every person has only what he intended."** This verse is that hadith applied to a specific case. And its companion, also in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s: **"God has forgiven my community for what their souls whisper to them, as long as they do not speak it or act on it."**

*Wa Allāhu **ghafūrun ḥalīm*** — "and Allah is **Forgiving, Forbearing**."

**The closing pair is chosen for this precise subject.** *Ghafūr* — forgiving. And *Ḥalīm* — the One who is not hasty, who does not seize, who gives room. **An oath invokes the name of God, and invoking the name of God lightly is a serious matter. The verse could have closed with warning. It closes with forbearance.**

That ending is not incidental. It is the answer to a specific and very common human condition, and it deserves to be named directly.

**Scrupulosity about oaths is one of the most widespread and least discussed forms of religious distress.** A person says something in passing, then cannot remember whether it was an oath; or swore something years ago and cannot recall the terms; or made a vow in a state of panic and has been living under it ever since; or has the thought "what if that counted?" several hundred times a day. This is a real and recognised phenomenon, and it is enormously common.

**2:225 is the direct answer, and it is worth stating in the plainest possible terms.** God does not take you to task for what your heart did not bind. The stray formula, the half-remembered vow, the word spoken under duress or in confusion — the verse says these do not create liability, and it closes by naming God as Forgiving and Forbearing rather than as waiting to catch you out. **A person in that state does not need a new ruling; he needs to read this verse and stop.**

**And note the sequence of the two names again.** It is the same pair, in the same order, that closed 2:226 in the next verse — *Ghafūr, Raḥīm* there, *Ghafūr, Ḥalīm* here. The two verses about the interior life of oaths and of marriage both end in forgiveness.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 226

**"Those who swear not to have intercourse with their wives must wait for four months. If they change their mind, then Allah is certainly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: for those who swear abstention from their wives there is a wait of four months. Then if they return — indeed, Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.

*Li-alladhīna yu'lūna min nisā'ihim tarabbuṣu arba'ati ashhurin, fa-in fā'ū fa-inna Allāha ghafūrun raḥīm.*

**This verse legislates *īlā'*, and to see what it is doing you have to know what it replaced.**

*Īlā'* is the oath of abstention: a man swears not to have intercourse with his wife. In pre-Islamic Arabia the practice existed, and it had a specific function — **it allowed a man to suspend a marriage indefinitely while keeping his wife bound to it.** She was neither a wife in substance nor free to marry anyone else. She hung.

**The verse's single legislative act is to put a number on it: four months.** After four months the suspension ends one way or the other. That is the whole of the ruling, and it is a ruling of enormous mercy, because it converts an indefinite suspension into a decision with a deadline.

**A note on the supplied translation.** It renders the verse as "Those who swear... **must wait** for four months," which makes the waiting the wife's act and the husband's duty. The Arabic says: *li-alladhīna yu'lūna min nisā'ihim **tarabbuṣu arba'ati ashhur*** — "for those who swear abstention from their wives [there is] a **waiting of four months**." The construction is nominal and the waiting belongs to the situation rather than being assigned to a party. The supplied reading is defensible and captures the effect, but the Arabic is terser, and the elastic point is the *limit on the oath* rather than a duty of patience imposed on anyone.

**What happens at the end of the four months is a genuine disagreement, and it should be reported as one.**

**The first position** — attributed to 'Uthmān, 'Alī, Ibn Mas'ūd and others among the Companions, and held by the Ḥanafīs and the Ḥanbalīs in one narration — is that when the four months pass without resumption, a **revocable divorce takes effect automatically.** The oath itself, having run its term, becomes the divorce.

**The second position** — held by Mālik, al-Shāfi'ī and the majority — is that the husband is **brought before the judge** at the end of the four months and ordered either to resume or to divorce; if he refuses, the judge pronounces the divorce himself.

Both positions agree on the destination — **the marriage cannot remain suspended** — and differ on the mechanism. That agreement is the verse's point, and it is worth stating plainly: **the one thing the law will not permit is the indefinite suspension.**

**Further details the schools differ on:** whether the four months run from the oath or from the actual separation; whether abstention without an oath counts as *īlā'*; and what counts as resumption in the case of absence or illness. These are genuine juristic differences.

**Now the word for returning.**

*Fa-in **fā'ū*** — "then if they **return**."

*Fā'a* is from *fay'* — the returning of something to its place, used classically of a shadow coming back as the sun moves. **It is a word of restoration, not of resumption.** The man who returns is not merely doing again what he stopped doing; he is coming back to where he was supposed to be. The oath had displaced him; he has returned.

*Fa-inna Allāha **ghafūrun raḥīm*** — "then indeed, Allah is **Forgiving, Merciful**."

**Note what this implies about the oath itself.** The return is met with *forgiveness* — *ghafūrun raḥīm*, the pair that answers sin. Which means the oath of abstention, used as it was used, was **not a neutral act.** It was a wrong that needs forgiving, and returning repairs it.

**This is a significant and often-missed point.** The verse does not treat *īlā'* as a man exercising a right over his own body or his own marriage. It treats it as something that requires *maghfirah* when abandoned — and by implication, something for which he is answerable while it persists. The weaponisation of intimacy is a sin, not a prerogative.

**The contemporary application is direct, and it is one of the most practically useful things in this batch.**

The silent treatment in a marriage. The withdrawal of intimacy used as punishment or leverage. The man who stays legally married, provides financially, and makes his wife invisible for months or years. The "I'm still here, aren't I?" defence. The undefined separation that is neither a marriage nor an ending, maintained so that one party keeps the benefits of both.

**2:226 is the verse for that situation, and its ruling is: there is a limit.** Four months is what the law set in the seventh century in a society far less protective of women than any modern one — and the principle it establishes is not really about the number. **The principle is that one party may not hold a marriage in indefinite suspension by an act of will.** There must be an end to it, and the end must be either a return or a release.

For anyone advising in that situation — and it is one of the most common things a counsellor, imam or family elder is asked about — the verse supplies both the diagnosis and the timetable. Name the suspension. Set the term. And at the end of the term, require the decision.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 227

**"But if they settle on divorce, then Allah is indeed All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: and if they resolve upon divorce, then indeed Allah is Hearing, Knowing.

*Wa in 'azamū al-ṭalāqa fa-inna Allāha samī'un 'alīm.*

**This is the shortest verse in the batch — seven words — and it is placed where it is for a reason.**

**It is the second half of a pair, and the pairing is exact.** 2:226 ends: *if they return* — Allah is **Forgiving, Merciful**. 2:227 says: *if they resolve on divorce* — Allah is **Hearing, Knowing**.

**Two outcomes, two different pairs of divine names.** Return is met with forgiveness and mercy. Resolving on divorce is met with hearing and knowing.

**Why Hearing and Knowing for divorce? Three layers, and all three are intended.**

**The first:** divorce is a **speech act.** It happens by being said. In the juristic understanding, the pronouncement is the mechanism. So the relevant attribute is *Samī'* — the One who **hears** the words you uttered. Nothing about this is inaudible to God.

**The second:** *'Azm* — **resolve** — is an interior act. The verse does not say "if they *pronounce* divorce"; it says *'azamū*, "if they **resolve** upon it," which is firmer and more deliberate than merely deciding. Resolve is a state of the heart, and the relevant attribute is *'Alīm* — the One who **knows** what was in it.

**And the third, which is the point: it is a warning.** The words you use to end a marriage are heard, and the condition of your heart in uttering them — whether you meant it or were performing anger, whether you were punishing someone or freeing them, whether you said it in a rage and meant to take it back the moment you calmed down — is known. **Divorce is not a private technicality, and it is not a magic word that works regardless of you.**

**On the seriousness of the pronouncement, there is a hadith that every person who has ever said it in anger needs to hear.** Reported by Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah from Abū Hurayrah, and graded *ḥasan* by al-Tirmidhī: **"There are three matters in which earnestness is earnest and jest is jest: marriage, divorce, and taking back [a wife]."** In other words: saying "I divorce you" as a joke, or in a rage, is not automatically voided by your saying afterwards that you didn't mean it. **The saying of it matters.**

**Which brings us to the most consequential disagreement in this whole passage, and it must be reported honestly.**

**Does three pronouncements of divorce uttered at once count as three, or as one?**

**The majority position** of the jurists — held by the four schools — is that three pronouncements in a single utterance count as three, with the consequence that the divorce is final and she does not become lawful to him again unless she marries another man who consummates the marriage and then divorces her (2:230).

**The minority position** — argued at length by Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim, and adopted by a number of contemporary scholarly bodies and by the legislation of several Muslim-majority countries — is that three uttered at once counts as **one**, on the ground that 2:229 says *al-ṭalāqu marratān* — "divorce is **two times**" — which describes a structure of successive, revocable pronouncements rather than a single triple utterance.

**And there is a narration that sits at the heart of this, in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*** from Ibn 'Abbās: **"In the time of the Messenger of God ﷺ, and of Abū Bakr, and for two years of 'Umar's caliphate, a threefold divorce was counted as one. Then 'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb said: 'People have become hasty in a matter in which they used to be deliberate. I am going to enforce it upon them.' And he enforced it."**

**That narration is the crux, and it has to be read honestly in both directions.** It establishes that the original practice was to count three-at-once as one. It also establishes that the second Caliph changed the administrative ruling, for a stated reason: people had begun to abuse it. So the majority are not enforcing something they invented, and the minority are not reviving something novel — **both sides are arguing from the same hadith about whether 'Umar's ruling was a permanent legal change or a disciplinary measure taken for a specific time and reason.** This is a genuine, unresolved, consequential disagreement, and it should be presented as such rather than resolved by assertion.

**A second narration is relevant and must be graded honestly.** It is reported — by Aḥmad and Abū Dāwūd — that Rukānah ibn 'Abd Yazīd divorced his wife three times in one sitting and then regretted it, and the Prophet ﷺ asked him how he had done it and treated it as one. **The chain of this narration is discussed, and a number of the hadith critics have graded it weak.** It is frequently cited in this debate. It should be cited as a report whose chain is disputed, not as a *ṣaḥīḥ* hadith.

**The practical upshot, stated plainly because the consequences are severe.** A man who says "I divorce you, I divorce you, I divorce you" in a rage may have ended his marriage permanently — on the majority view, irrevocably and with no route back except 2:230. **That is why the tradition's constant counsel is: do not say it.** Say anything else. Say it another day. Say it through someone else. The phrase is not a phrase to be used experimentally, and 2:227's *Samī' 'Alīm* is the reason: it is heard, and the heart behind it is known.

**And the pastoral note for the person who has already said it.** The disagreement between the positions is real, and a person in that situation should consult qualified scholarship rather than accept either the despair of "it is finished forever" or the carelessness of "it didn't count." Both answers are given by people who know the texts, and neither should be delivered to someone in crisis as though the other did not exist.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 228

**"Divorced women must wait three monthly cycles ˹before they can re-marry˺. It is not lawful for them to conceal what Allah has created in their wombs, if they ˹truly˺ believe in Allah and the Last Day. And their husbands reserve the right to take them back within that period if they desire reconciliation. Women have rights similar to those of men equitably, although men have a degree ˹of responsibility˺ above them. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise."**

In simple terms: and divorced women wait by themselves for three *qurū'*. And it is not lawful for them to conceal what Allah has created in their wombs, if they believe in Allah and the Last Day. And their husbands have a better right to take them back during that [period], if they desire to set things right. And due to them is the like of what is expected of them, according to what is reasonable — and for the men over them is a degree. And Allah is Exalted in Might, Wise.

*Wa al-muṭallaqātu yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna thalāthata qurū'in, wa lā yaḥillu lahunna an yaktumna mā khalaqa Allāhu fī arḥāmihinna in kunna yu'minna bi-Allāhi wa al-yawmi al-ākhiri, wa bu'ūlatuhunna aḥaqqu bi-raddihinna fī dhālika in arādū iṣlāḥan, wa lahunna mithlu alladhī 'alayhinna bi-al-ma'rūfi, wa li-al-rijāli 'alayhinna darajah, wa Allāhu 'azīzun ḥakīm.*

*Wa al-muṭallaqātu **yatarabbaṣna bi-anfusihinna*** — "and divorced women **wait, holding themselves**."

Form V with the reflexive: they keep themselves back. The verb *tarabbuṣ* carries watching and waiting with a purpose, not merely passing time. **The waiting is active and self-directed, and the verse puts the woman as its subject** — she is not described as being kept; she is described as waiting.

**Now the disputed word: *qurū'.***

This is one of the famous homonyms of the Qur'an — a word that means two opposite things, and the disagreement is real and consequential.

**Meaning one: menstruation (*ḥayḍ*).** On this reading the 'iddah is three menstrual periods.

**Meaning two: the period of purity between menstruations (*ṭuhr*).** On this reading the 'iddah is three periods of purity.

**A long list of the early authorities held the first**, as Ibn Kathīr catalogues: Sa'īd ibn al-Musayyib, 'Alqamah, al-Aswad, Ibrāhīm, Mujāhid, 'Aṭā', Ṭāwūs, Sa'īd ibn Jubayr, 'Ikrimah, Muḥammad ibn Sīrīn, al-Ḥasan, Qatādah, al-Sha'bī, al-Rabī', Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān, al-Suddī, Makḥūl, al-Ḍaḥḥāk and 'Aṭā' al-Khurasānī.

**The evidence cited** is a hadith reported by Abū Dāwūd and al-Nasā'ī from Fāṭimah bint Abī Ḥubaysh, in which the Prophet ﷺ said to her: **"Leave the prayer during the days of your *aqrā'*"** (the plural of *qar'*). **And Ibn Kathīr himself notes the weakness: one of its narrators, al-Mundhir, is unknown, as Abū Ḥātim stated, although Ibn Ḥibbān included him among the trustworthy.** That caveat is important and should be carried with the citation.

**On the schools:** the Ḥanafīs take *qurū'* as menstruation; the majority of the other schools take it as the periods of purity. **The disagreement changes the length of the waiting period and therefore the point at which the woman is free to remarry, and the schools' calculations differ accordingly.** It is a genuine juristic difference, and it should be presented as one rather than resolved.

**The purpose of the waiting period is twofold, and both purposes appear in this verse.** The first is to establish whether there is a pregnancy, so that lineages are not confused — that is the concealment clause. The second is to create a space in which the marriage can be reconsidered — that is the return clause. **One 'iddah, two purposes, both stated.**

*Wa lā yaḥillu lahunna an yaktumna mā khalaqa Allāhu fī arḥāmihinna* — "and it is not lawful for them to conceal what Allah has created in their wombs."

**Note what this clause does: it makes the woman the authority on her own body.** The commentators — Ibn 'Abbās, Ibn 'Umar, Mujāhid, al-Sha'bī, al-Ḥakam ibn 'Utaybah, al-Rabī' ibn Anas, al-Ḍaḥḥāk and others — read the concealed thing as pregnancy or menstruation, and they observe the consequence: **because no one else can know, God entrusted the determination to her, and warned her against falsifying it.** She is able to shorten or lengthen her own waiting period by what she says, and the verse forbids that on the ground of her belief in God and the Last Day.

That is a remarkable structure. The law could have appointed witnesses, examiners, or an oath. Instead it appointed **her word**, and bound her conscience. It is simultaneously the greatest practical trust placed in a woman's testimony and the clearest statement of the accountability that comes with it.

*Wa bu'ūlatuhunna aḥaqqu bi-raddihinna fī dhālika **in arādū iṣlāḥan*** — "and their husbands have a better right to take them back during that [period], **if they desire to set things right**."

**The condition is everything, and it is the most neglected clause in the verse.** *In arādū iṣlāḥan* — **if they want *iṣlāḥ*, setting right, repair.**

The right of return is **conditioned on the intention to repair the marriage.** It is not a right to resume a marriage in order to punish, or to keep a woman in indefinite suspension, or to avoid the financial consequences of the divorce, or to stop her marrying someone else. The word is *iṣlāḥ* — the same word used for reconciling between people in 2:224, and for the claim the hypocrites made about themselves in 2:11.

**Which means a husband who takes his wife back during the 'iddah for any reason other than making the marriage work is acting outside the permission this verse grants.** That is a ruling, and it is stated in the text, and it is the direct answer to the最常见的 abuse of *raj'ah* — the take-back-and-drop-again manoeuvre. It is also the reason 2:231 will say, in so many words: "do not hold them back in order to harm them."

**Now the two clauses that everyone quotes, and they must be read in order.**

*Wa lahunna **mithlu** alladhī 'alayhinna **bi-al-ma'rūf*** — "and due to them is **the like of** what is expected of them, **according to what is recognised as good**."

**This is one of the most important sentences about gender in the Qur'an, and the structure is precise.**

*Mithlu* — **the like of**. Not more than, not less than. The rights mirror the obligations, in kind and in measure. And *bi-al-ma'rūf* — according to what is recognised as good, which is the standard that runs through this whole passage and which keeps the whole thing anchored to lived goodness rather than to abstract claim.

The commentators take it as fully general: **everything expected of her, the like of it is due to her from him.** Not some things. Not the spiritual things. Everything.

**And the Sunnah says it too.** In the Farewell Sermon, recorded by al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah from 'Amr ibn al-Aḥwas: **"Behold, indeed you have rights over your women, and they have rights over you."** And recorded by al-Tirmidhī and Ibn Mājah: **"The best of you are the best to their wives, and I am the best of you to my wives."** And in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, the famous description: **"The most complete of the believers in faith is the best of them in character, and the best of you are those who are best to their women."**

*Wa li-al-rijāli 'alayhinna **darajah*** — "and the men have **a degree** over them."

**This clause must be handled with complete honesty in both directions, because it is the site of a major contemporary argument and because the text itself is more careful than either side usually is.**

**A note on the supplied translation first.** It renders the clause as "men have a degree **˹of responsibility˺** above them." **The bracketed words are supplied, not in the Arabic,** which says only *darajah* — "a degree," "one step." The supplied gloss adopts one of the classical readings. A reader is entitled to know that, because the gloss substantially shapes the meaning.

**What the text actually says:** *darajah* — a **step**, a single rung. Not *faḍl* (superiority), not *sharaf* (nobility), not *sulṭah* (dominion). One degree. The minimising form is part of the meaning.

**The classical readings of what the degree consists in:**

1. **The juristic asymmetry in this very verse** — the husband's right to initiate the return and to pronounce the divorce, paired against the financial obligations he carries: the bridal gift and maintenance. On this reading the degree is a bundle in which a right is matched by a liability.
2. **The obligation of maintenance alone** — that the additional *burden* is itself the degree, and that he is commanded to treat her well and to overlook some of what is due to him.
3. Read together with **4:34** (*al-rijālu qawwāmūna 'alā al-nisā'* — men are maintainers of women), which states the *qiwāmah* explicitly and is the verse the tradition has consistently paired with this one.

**And the contestation, stated honestly.** Traditional readings hold that this verse and 4:34 together establish a settled structural authority of the husband within the household, bounded by the obligations that accompany it. Revisionist readings argue that the *darajah* is functional and limited — that it names the specific juristic asymmetries in this verse (initiating divorce and return) in exchange for specific financial liabilities, that it is a "degree" rather than a category difference, and that it is not a statement about ontological worth or general authority. **Both readings exist within contemporary Islamic scholarship; neither should be presented as the only one, and a reader is entitled to know that the disagreement is live.**

**What can be said without controversy is the shape of the sentence.** Reciprocal rights stated first and stated as *equal in kind*; then a single degree; then *'Azīz, Ḥakīm*. The inequality is stated, it is **bounded** by the word *darajah*, and it is **attributed to God's wisdom rather than to any inherent worth** — which is precisely what the closing says.

*Wa Allāhu **'azīzun ḥakīm*** — "and Allah is **Exalted in Might, Wise**."

The same pair that closed 2:220, for the same reason, and it answers the same two misreadings. *'Azīz* — nothing compels Him, so the distribution of rights in this verse is not a concession extracted by a culture. *Ḥakīm* — Wise, so it is not arbitrary. **The verse anticipates that the *darajah* will be objected to, and it answers the objection not with an argument but with the two names: this is from the Mighty and the Wise.**

**One last thing, and it is the most practically important sentence in this verse.** The *mithlu* clause comes **first** and the *darajah* clause comes **second**. The reciprocity is the rule; the degree is the exception. **Any reading that begins with the degree and treats the reciprocity as a qualification of it has the verse backwards.**

## Verse 229

**"Divorce may be retracted twice, then the husband must retain ˹his wife˺ with honour or separate ˹from her˺ with grace. It is not lawful for husbands to take back anything of the dowry given to their wives, unless the couple fears not being able to keep within the limits of Allah. So if you fear they will not be able to keep within the limits of Allah, there is no blame if the wife compensates the husband to obtain divorce. These are the limits set by Allah, so do not transgress them. And whoever transgresses the limits of Allah, they are the ˹true˺ wrongdoers."**

In simple terms: divorce is twice. Then either retaining according to what is recognised as good, or releasing with excellence. And it is not lawful for you to take anything of what you gave them, unless the two of them fear that they will not keep the limits of Allah. Then if you fear that they will not keep the limits of Allah, there is no blame upon either of them concerning that by which she ransoms herself. Those are the limits of Allah, so do not transgress them. And whoever transgresses the limits of Allah — those are the wrongdoers.

*Al-ṭalāqu marratān, fa-imsākun bi-ma'rūfin aw tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin, wa lā yaḥillu lakum an ta'khudhū mimmā ātaytumūhunna shay'an illā an yakhāfā allā yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāh, fa-in khiftum allā yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāhi fa-lā junāḥa 'alayhimā fī-mā ftadat bihi, tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi fa-lā ta'tadūhā, wa man yata'adda ḥudūda Allāhi fa-ulā'ika humu al-ẓālimūn.*

*Al-ṭalāqu **marratān*** — "**divorce is two times**."

**A note on the supplied translation, which renders this "Divorce may be retracted twice."** The Arabic is terser: *al-ṭalāqu marratān* — "divorce [is] two times." The supplied rendering supplies the juristic conclusion, which is the standard one — that the first two pronouncements are revocable and the third is final. But the Arabic states a **structure** rather than a rule about retraction, and the structure is what the whole passage is built on: two, then a decision, then a finality.

**The two-term structure is the verse's heart.**

*Fa-imsākun bi-ma'rūfin **aw** tasrīḥun bi-iḥsānin* — "then either **retaining in the recognised good way**, or **releasing with excellence**."

**Two options. Not three.** After the first and second pronouncements there is no third category. There is no "keep her while making her life difficult," no "keep her while looking for a way out," no "keep her so that she cannot marry anyone else." The verse names two doors and closes everything else.

**And note the vocabulary, which is not symmetrical.** Keeping is *bi-al-**ma'rūf*** — according to what is recognised as good. Releasing is *bi-**iḥsān*** — with *iḥsān*, excellence, the highest word in the ethical vocabulary, the word the hadith of Jibrīl used to define the apex of the religion: "that you worship God as though you see Him."

**Some commentators observed that the word for releasing is the stronger word, and drew the conclusion from it:** if you are going to let go, let go beautifully. The lesser word is attached to keeping her and the greater to releasing her — which is a striking thing for a verse whose whole thrust is to discourage divorce. It means the law is as concerned with *how* a marriage ends as with whether it does.

**Now the financial ruling, stated in the plainest possible terms.**

*Wa lā yaḥillu lakum an ta'khudhū mimmā ātaytumūhunna shay'an* — "and it is not lawful for you to take **anything** of what you **gave** them."

**This is a direct prohibition of taking back the bridal gift on divorce.** Note the emphases: *anything* — *shay'an*, not "a part of it"; and *what you gave them* — the completed gift, now hers.

**The pre-Islamic practice this abolished was straightforward: a man could dissolve a marriage and reclaim what he had paid.** The verse ends that in one clause. **The mahr is hers. It does not come back.** This is one of the clearest economic protections for women anywhere in the scripture, and it is stated without qualification or exception-except-one.

*Illā an yakhāfā allā yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāh* — "**unless the two of them fear** that they will not keep the limits of Allah."

**Note the dual: *yakhāfā* — the two of them fear.** This is not the husband's decision and it is not the wife's alone. The exception begins with both.

**And this is *khul'*.** *Fa-lā junāḥa 'alayhimā fī-mā **ftadat** bihi* — "then there is no blame upon either of them concerning that by which **she ransoms herself**." *Iftadat* is from *fīdā'* — ransoming, buying out. She gives back what he gave, or part of it, or something else by agreement, and is released.

**The verse permits it not as a favour to either party but as a remedy for a marriage that has failed in substance.** The condition is not fault. The condition is: **the two of them cannot keep within the limits of God.**

**And the hadith that defines it is in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī*, and it is one of the most important narrations in the whole law of marriage.**

The wife of Thābit ibn Qays ibn Shammās came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: **"O Messenger of God, I do not blame Thābit for any defect in his character or in his religion — but I am afraid that I, being a Muslim, may become unthankful for God's blessings [i.e., may fail in my duties toward him]."** The Prophet ﷺ said to her: **"Will you return his garden to him?"** She said: "Yes." So she returned it, and he told Thābit: **"Accept the garden, and divorce her once."**

**Sit with what she said, because the whole meaning of khul' is in it.**

**She accused him of nothing.** No abuse. No failure to maintain her. No cruelty. No neglect. She said, in as many words: there is nothing wrong with him — he is a good man and a good Muslim — **and I cannot stay.**

**And the Prophet ﷺ did not ask her to justify it.** He did not ask her to name a fault. He did not send her back. He did not tell her to be patient, or to consider the community, or to think of the children. He asked one question — will you give back the garden? — and when she said yes, he ended it.

**That is the mercy of khul', and it is worth stating as plainly as the verse states it:** *it exists for the marriage that has failed without anyone being guilty.* A marriage can be over because it is over. A woman can be in a marriage that no one would call abusive and be unable to continue in it, and the law of God has a door for her. **The door is real, it is in the Qur'an, and the Prophet ﷺ opened it for a woman who could not name a single thing against her husband.**

**One further detail from the narration, and it is a limit, not a loophole.** In the version recorded by Ibn Mājah, the Prophet ﷺ told Thābit to take back the garden **and no more than that.** From this the jurists derived that the husband may not take **more** than what he gave. The majority hold that if she voluntarily offers more, and he accepts, it is permissible; but he may not demand it.

**On the fiqh of khul',** the genuine disagreements should be noted: whether it requires the husband's consent (the majority: yes — it is a contract requiring his agreement; where he refuses and there is harm, the route is a judicial dissolution, *faskh*, which is a different mechanism with its own rules); and whether it consumes one of the three pronouncements or operates as an irrevocable separation outside that count (the schools differ).

*Tilka **ḥudūdu Allāhi** fa-lā ta'tadūhā* — "**those are the limits of Allah**, so do not transgress them."

*Wa man yata'adda **ḥudūda Allāhi** fa-ulā'ika humu al-ẓālimūn* — "and whoever transgresses the **limits of Allah** — those are the wrongdoers."

**And the phrase has now appeared three times in one verse.** *Ḥudūdu Allāh* — the limits of God — in the exception clause, in the prohibition, and in the warning. Three times in as many lines.

**Why?** Because divorce is the area of human life in which people are most reliably able to justify themselves. Everyone in a failing marriage has a story in which they are the wronged party. Everyone can produce reasons. And so the boundary is named three times, in a single verse, so that no one can say they did not see it.

**The contemporary application is immediate.** The woman who cannot get out — because he will not pronounce, because her family will not hear of it, because a council tells her to try harder for another year, because she has no grounds anyone recognises. **2:229 is her verse, and the Prophet's handling of Thābit's wife is her precedent.** And the abuse of khul' — the husband who demands far more than he gave, the family that negotiates her out of everything she owns — is answered by "and no more than that."

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 230

**"So if a husband divorces his wife ˹three times˺, then it is not lawful for him to remarry her until after she has married another man and then is divorced. Then it is permissible for them to reunite, as long as they feel they are able to maintain the limits of Allah. These are the limits set by Allah, which He makes clear for people of knowledge."**

In simple terms: then if he divorces her, she is not lawful to him after that until she marries a husband other than him. Then if he [the second husband] divorces her, there is no blame upon the two of them if they return to each other, if they suppose that they can keep the limits of Allah. And those are the limits of Allah, which He makes clear to a people who know.

*Fa-in ṭallaqahā fa-lā taḥillu lahu min ba'du ḥattā tankiḥa zawjan ghayrahu, fa-in ṭallaqahā fa-lā junāḥa 'alayhimā an yatarāja'ā in ẓannā an yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāh, wa tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi yubayyinuhā li-qawmin ya'lamūn.*

**A note on the supplied translation first.** The Arabic opens *fa-in ṭallaqahā* — "then if he divorces her." The supplied rendering inserts *˹three times˺*, which is supplied rather than stated. **The supply is necessary and correct** — this is the third pronouncement, following 2:229's "divorce is two times" — but a reader should know the terseness of the Arabic, because the terseness is what generates the disagreement about whether three uttered at once counts as three (discussed at 2:227).

**Now the ruling, and it is one of the most consequential in the whole law of marriage.**

*Fa-lā taḥillu lahu min ba'du ḥattā tankiḥa zawjan ghayrahu* — "she is not lawful to him after that **until she marries a husband other than him**."

**Why would the law do this? The stated reason, and the reason the commentators give, is deterrence.**

If the third divorce is final and irreversible, then the first two are expensive. **The whole structure of 2:229–230 is designed to make a man think before the first pronouncement,** because he knows that the third one ends it permanently. The severity of the third is what gives the first two their gravity. A divorce regime in which everything is infinitely reversible produces casual pronouncement; a regime with a hard stop produces deliberation.

**And the report that confirms this reading is in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim***, discussed at 2:227: 'Umar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb said, **"People have become hasty in a matter in which they used to be deliberate. I am going to enforce it upon them."** The concern being addressed is haste. The structure is a brake.

**Now the condition, and the hadith defines it precisely.**

*Ḥattā tankiḥa **zawjan ghayrahu*** — "until she marries a **husband other than him**."

Note: the verse says *marries.* It does not say "marries and divorces." And the Sunnah establishes that the marriage must be **consummated** — the contract alone is not enough.

**The narration is in *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* and *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, from 'Ā'ishah.**

The wife of Rifā'ah al-Qurazī came to the Prophet ﷺ and said: "I was Rifā'ah's wife, and he divorced me and made it irrevocable. Then I married 'Abd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Zubayr — and what he has is like the fringe of a garment." She took the hem of her garment to indicate it. **The Prophet ﷺ smiled** and said: **"Do you want to return to Rifā'ah? No — not until you taste his sweetness and he tastes your sweetness."**

And in another transmission: "Not until the second husband tastes of her sweetness what the first one tasted."

**Three things in this narration deserve to be named, and they are all instructive.**

**The first is the word.** *'Usaylah* is the **diminutive** of *'asal*, honey — the smallest, most delicate form of the word. The Prophet ﷺ was discussing this woman's sexual complaint in front of her, in front of 'Ā'ishah, in front of her husband's denials — and he chose the most delicate word the language offered. **That is the Sunnah's manner of speaking about intimate matters, and it is the opposite of coarseness.** It is also, less obviously, the opposite of embarrassment: he did not change the subject or tell her to be quiet. He answered her question, in the most decorous language available, without flinching.

**The second is that he heard both parties.** 'Abd al-Raḥmān said: "By God, she is lying — I am able, but she is disobedient and wants to go back to Rifā'ah." The Prophet ﷺ did not simply accept the woman's account or the man's denial. He noticed the two boys standing with 'Abd al-Raḥmān and asked: "Are these your sons?" — "Yes." — **"This that you claim — by God, they resemble him more than a crow resembles a crow."** Evidence, produced in front of both parties, with a smile, and the matter settled without humiliation on either side.

**The third is that he ruled for her.** She was not sent back to an unconsummated marriage. She was not told to be patient. Her complaint — that the marriage was a marriage in name — was taken seriously and the ruling followed from it.

**Now the boundary, and it is the most important practical point in this verse.**

**The Prophet ﷺ cursed *al-muḥallil* and *al-muḥallal lahu*** — the man who marries a thrice-divorced woman in order to make her lawful to her first husband, and the first husband for whom it is done. The narration is reported by Aḥmad, Abū Dāwūd, al-Tirmidhī, al-Nasā'ī and Ibn Mājah, from 'Alī, Ibn Mas'ūd and 'Uqbah ibn 'Āmir, with varying chains, and it is generally strengthened by their convergence. And in the version from 'Uqbah ibn 'Āmir: **"Shall I not tell you of the hired billy-goat?"** — and they said yes — **"He is *al-muḥallil*. May God curse *al-muḥallil* and *al-muḥallal lahu*."**

**And this is where a careful distinction has to be drawn, because the two narrations look contradictory and are not.**

The wife of Rifā'ah's case is **not** *tahlīl*. The reason is precise: **the intention that makes a marriage *tahlīl* must be the second husband's intention.** 'Abd al-Raḥmān ibn al-Zubayr married her and, by every indication in the narration, wanted to keep her — he denied her claim and defended himself. *She* wanted to return to Rifā'ah. **But the power of divorce was not in her hand**, so her intention could not make the marriage a sham, and the Prophet ﷺ — knowing full well that she wanted to go back — did not invalidate anything. He simply told her the condition had not yet been met.

**The scholars therefore reconcile the two narrations as follows:** a marriage contracted by the second husband with the intention of releasing her to the first is *tahlīl* and is invalid and cursed. A genuine marriage, which the wife or her family may privately hope will end so she can return, is valid — because what counts is the intention of the one who holds the divorce.

**And the contemporary application has to be stated without hedging, because it is one of the clearest cases of a widespread practice among Muslims being directly named and cursed in the hadith.**

"Halala" as practised in some communities — a marriage arranged, sometimes paid for, consummated, and then dissolved, purely so a woman can return to her first husband — **is *tahlīl*, and it is exactly what the Prophet ﷺ cursed.** It is not a loophole. It is not a technical workaround. It is the thing the narration names, and the harm falls almost entirely on women, who are subjected to a transaction conducted over their bodies. **A person who has been told this is permissible has been given bad counsel, and the hadith is unambiguous.**

*Fa-in ṭallaqahā fa-lā junāḥa 'alayhimā an yatarāja'ā in **ẓannā** an yuqīmā ḥudūda Allāh* — "then if he divorces her, there is no blame upon the two of them if they return to one another, **if they suppose** that they can keep the limits of Allah."

**The verb is *ẓannā* — they *suppose*, they *think*, they *form a considered judgement*. Not *ya'lamū* — they know. Not *yaqtinū* — they are certain.**

**And that is a mercy, and it should be named as one.** A couple that has been through three divorces and a second marriage is not asked for a guarantee. They are not required to prove that it will work this time. They are asked only whether, on their best judgement, they can now keep within the limits of God. **The door back is open on the strength of a considered hope, not a certainty** — which is the right standard for people who have already failed once and are trying again.

*Wa tilka ḥudūdu Allāhi yubayyinuhā **li-qawmin ya'lamūn*** — "and those are the limits of Allah, which He makes clear **to a people who know**."

**The closing varies from 2:229.** There, the limits were named and the warning was "do not transgress them." Here, they are "made clear to a people who **know**." The difference is the point: **the whole of 2:229–230 is a structure of counted pronouncements, conditional exceptions, and defined conditions that cannot be navigated by someone who has not learned it.** The law of divorce is not intuitive. It requires knowledge — which is why the verse closes by requiring it, and why this is the area in which a person should least rely on what they have picked up from their family.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis: Verses 221–230

**The arc.** How a marriage is entered (221), what happens inside it — the body and its limits (222–223), the misuse of solemn words (224–225), the weaponisation of withdrawal (226–227) — and how it ends (228–230). It is the most complete treatment of a single human institution anywhere in the surah, and it moves from the most public decision to the most private one to the most painful one.

**The governing word of the batch is *ḥudūd*.** "The limits of God" appears five times across 2:229–230, three of them in a single verse. The whole passage on divorce is framed as a set of boundaries rather than a set of rights. **That framing is the passage's most important and most counter-intuitive feature.** In the area of life where people are most certain they are the wronged party, the Qur'an does not begin by asking who is right. It begins by naming a line, and then naming it again.

**And the second governing feature is the divine name-pair at the end of each verse, which tracks the subject precisely.** Returning to a wife after an oath: *Ghafūr, Raḥīm*. Resolving on divorce: *Samī', 'Alīm* — because it is a word uttered and a resolve held. Unintended oaths: *Ghafūr, Ḥalīm* — because the subject is scrupulous anxiety. Spousal rights: *'Azīz, Ḥakīm* — because the distribution is contested and is therefore anchored to might and wisdom rather than to argument. **Read the closing names and you have the verse's own verdict on what it just legislated.**

**Three things in this batch are answered by the Sunnah in a way that changes the reading, and they are worth collecting.**

The first: **2:222 could be read as a verse about impurity**, and the Prophet's own conduct — reclining in 'Ā'ishah's lap and reciting Qur'an while she menstruated, embracing her, having her wash his hair — makes it the opposite. The closing of the verse says the same thing: *He loves those who purify themselves.*

The second: **2:229's khul' could be read as requiring fault**, and the wife of Thābit ibn Qays — who named none — establishes that it does not. She said there was nothing wrong with him and she could not stay, and the Prophet ﷺ asked one question and ended the marriage.

The third: **2:230's condition could be read as a technicality to be satisfied**, and the Prophet's curse on *al-muḥallil* and *al-muḥallal lahu* closes that reading permanently. The distinction that reconciles it with the wife of Rifā'ah's case is precise: the intention that corrupts is the second husband's, because he holds the divorce.

**A pattern worth noticing across the whole surah.** 2:222 adds to the "they ask you" series — 189, 215, 217, 219, 220, 222 — and it behaves like the others. The Companions asked about menstruation because the Jews of Madinah had a practice they found severe, and the answer refused both that severity and its opposite. It is the sixth of these questions in the surah, and in every case the answer has relocated the questioner: from amount to recipient, from amount to surplus, from property to brotherhood, and here from impurity to harm.

**And a structural point about where the emphasis falls.** The most-quoted clause of 2:228 — "men have a degree over them" — is the last clause of its sentence, and it follows "due to them is the like of what is expected of them." **Reciprocity is stated first and as the rule; the degree is stated second and as a single step.** A reader who starts with the degree has the verse backwards, and a great deal of damage has been done by people who did exactly that.

**Where the supplied translation resolves an ambiguity, it has been marked.** The bracketed *˹consensually˺* in 2:223, *˹of responsibility˺* in 2:228, *˹three times˺* in 2:230, and "free polytheist" in 2:221 are all supplied rather than stated, and each adopts one of several possible readings. The most consequential of these is 2:228's *˹of responsibility˺*, because it resolves the single most contested clause in the batch — and the contestation has been reported rather than closed.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: the scope of *mushrikāt* and whether it includes the People of the Book; whether a Muslim woman may marry a non-Muslim man; the meaning of *qurū'* as menstruation or purity; the meaning of *laghw* in oaths and which cases require expiation; what takes effect at the end of the four months of *īlā'*; whether three pronouncements at once count as three or as one; whether *khul'* requires the husband's consent and whether it consumes one of the three; and the reading of the *darajah* in verse 228.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the variant occasions offered for 2:223; the report that Sa'īd ibn al-Musayyib connected it to *'azl*; the narration of Rukānah, whose chain is disputed; and the report of Usayd ibn Ḥudayr and 'Abbād ibn Bishr. Two narrations have been flagged as **weak or disputed where the evidence is weak**: the expiation of a dīnār or half a dīnār for intercourse during menses, which several critics hold to be *mawqūf* or weak, with the majority of the jurists therefore holding that no specific expiation is prescribed; and the hadith of Fāṭimah bint Abī Ḥubaysh on the *aqrā'*, whose narrator al-Mundhir was declared unknown by Abū Ḥātim.

Three boundaries have been observed. On verse 222: the word *adhan* has been held firmly against every reading that makes this a verse about a woman's impurity, and the verse's own closing with divine love has been presented as the text's answer to that misuse. On verse 228: the *mithlu* clause has been placed before the *darajah* clause and the order treated as decisive, and the contemporary contestation over the *darajah* has been reported as live rather than resolved, including the note that the supplied translation has adopted one of the classical readings by supplying *˹of responsibility˺*. And on verse 230: the practice of *tahlīl* has been named as what the Prophet ﷺ cursed, without hedging, because it is a live harm to women and because the hadith is unambiguous — with the distinction drawn that reconciles it with the case of Rifā'ah's wife.

The most important practical sentence in the batch is in verse 229, and it is the one the Prophet ﷺ enacted: **a woman does not have to prove that anyone did anything wrong in order to be released.**
