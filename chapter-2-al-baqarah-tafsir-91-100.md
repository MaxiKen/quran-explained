# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 91–100

These ten verses are the closing argument of the case the surah has been building. The method throughout is *ilzam* — forcing an admission on the opponent's own terms rather than introducing new evidence. They claim to believe only in what was sent down to them, so the surah asks how that squares with killing the prophets who brought it (91), with worshipping the calf while Moses was on the mountain (92), and with answering the covenant with "we hear and disobey" (93). They claim the Hereafter belongs to them alone, so the surah sets a test no one can fake: wish for death (94). It states that they will never do it (95), and explains why — they are the greediest of all people for life, greedier even than those who deny the afterlife altogether, because they know what is coming (96). Then a specific objection is answered: their hostility to Gabriel, who is only a courier acting by command (97–98). And the passage closes with the verdict: the signs are clear, none denies them but the defiantly disobedient, and the pattern running through all of it is a covenant broken every time it is made (99–100).

## Verse 91

**"When it is said to them: “Believe in what Allah has revealed,” they reply, “We only believe in what was sent down to us,” and they deny what came afterwards, though it is the truth confirming their own Scriptures! Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Why then did you kill Allah’s prophets before, if you are ˹truly˺ believers?”"**

In simple terms: when they are called to believe in what Allah has revealed, they restrict their belief to what was revealed to their own community and reject everything after it — even though the later revelation confirms the one they claim to hold. The surah's answer is a question that turns their own claim against them.

*Aminu bima anzala Allah* — "believe in what Allah has revealed." Note how the call is phrased. It is not "believe in the Qur'an." It is *ma anzala Allah*, whatever Allah has sent down — indefinite, universal, without restriction. The invitation is to believe in revelation as such, wherever and whenever it comes.

*Qalu nu'minu bima unzila 'alayna* — "they say: we believe in what was sent down to us." And here is the whole dispute compressed into a shift of preposition. The call said *ma anzala Allah* — what Allah revealed. The answer says *ma unzila 'alayna* — what was sent down *to us*. Revelation has been converted from a divine act into a communal possession. The object of faith is no longer God's sending but our receiving.

*Wa yakfuruna bima wara'ahu* — "and they disbelieve in what came after it." *Wara'a* means behind or beyond, so "what is beyond it" — the later revelation. And the irony is total: the thing they reject is the thing that confirms the thing they claim to hold.

*Wa huwa al-haqqu musaddiqan lima ma'ahum* — "and it is the truth, confirming what is with them." This is the argument from *tasdiq*, used at verse 41 and again at verse 89, and its force is this: the Qur'an confirms the Torah. It does not abolish it, replace it wholesale, or set a new religion against an old one. So a person who genuinely holds the Torah and takes its affirmation of prophethood seriously ought to be the first to recognise the Qur'an. Their own principle, applied consistently, obliges them to accept it. The refusal is not a failure of consistency in their reasoning; it is a refusal to apply the reasoning.

*Qul fa-lima taqtuluna anbiya'a Allahi min qablu in kuntum mu'minin* — "Say: why then did you kill the prophets of Allah before, if you are believers?" This is the *ilzam*, and it is brutal in its economy. You claim to believe in what was sent down to you. What was sent down to you was brought by prophets. Your forebears killed those prophets. If you truly held what they brought, you would not have killed the bearers.

A necessary clarification, because the verse appears to contradict a principle stated elsewhere in this very surah. At 2:134 and 2:141 the Qur'an says: "That was a nation which has passed on. It will have what it earned, and you will have what you have earned, and you will not be asked about what they used to do." No one bears another's burden. How then can this verse ask "why did *you* kill the prophets of Allah before?"

The answer is that the two verses are doing different things. 2:134 establishes a legal and theological principle: no person is accountable for another's deeds. This verse is a rhetorical argument constructed on the opponent's own premises. They claim continuity — "what was sent down to *us*" — which is a claim to stand in the same community as those who received it. The surah takes that claim at face value and shows what it commits them to: if you are the same community, the community's history is your history, and the community killed the prophets who brought the revelation you claim to revere. Either drop the continuity or own the record. The argument forces a dilemma; it does not establish inherited guilt, and it must never be read as doing so.

The charge of killing prophets is repeated in the Qur'an at 3:21, 3:112, 3:181, 4:155 and 5:70, and as noted at verse 61, it is bounded: the Qur'an explicitly denies that they killed Jesus (4:157). The charge is real, specific and limited, and it cannot be inflated into a general accusation against a people.

For practice, the disease this verse names is one of the most durable in human life.

It is the conversion of truth into property. "We believe in what was sent down to us" is the voice of every group that has mistaken its inheritance for the whole of the truth: the nation that recognises virtue only in its own traditions, the school that reads only its own authors, the denomination that distrusts anything written outside it, the discipline that dismisses what cannot be measured by its own instruments. In each case the speaker believes himself to be defending truth, when what he is actually defending is the boundary of his group.

The Muslim version of it is not hypothetical. A Muslim is required to believe in every messenger and every scripture (2:4, 2:136, 3:84, 4:136), and the Qur'an repeatedly grounds its authority in its confirmation of what came before rather than in its replacement of it. The Muslim who refuses to recognise wisdom, truth or genuine insight wherever it appears — on the grounds that it did not come through his tradition — is reproducing the exact error this verse condemns, with the name of his own tradition substituted for theirs.

And the method is transferable, which is the second application. When someone claims a principle, the fastest way to test it is to ask what the principle has actually produced in his life. "I believe in what was sent down to us" — then why did you kill the people who brought it? The gap between a claimed commitment and a lived history is where the truth about the commitment is found, and it is visible to everyone except the person inside it.

## Verse 92

**"Indeed, Moses came to you with clear proofs, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully."**

In simple terms: Moses came to them with undeniable proofs, and they took the calf as a god as soon as he had gone, wronging themselves by it.

*Wa la-qad ja'akum Musa bi-al-bayyinati* — "and Moses certainly came to you with the clear proofs." *Bayyinat* is the same word used for the signs given to Jesus at verse 87 and for the signs given to Muhammad ﷺ at verse 99. The surah is deliberately using one word for all three, which places the three messengers in a single line and puts their communities' responses in a single frame.

*Thumma ittakhadhtum al-'ijla min ba'dihi wa antum zalimun* — "then you took the calf after him, while you were wronging [yourselves]." The wording is almost identical to verse 51, where the calf episode was treated in full — its making from their own ornaments, the role of al-Samiri, the vindication of Aaron, and the timing of the prophet's absence. That treatment should be read alongside this verse.

The question a careful reader should ask is: why is the same event repeated here, eleven verses later?

The answer is that the repetition is doing a different job. In verse 51 the calf was an item in the catalogue of favours — a favour received and a sin committed in the breathing space of a prophet's absence. Here it is evidence in a legal argument. The charge in verse 91 was that their claim to believe in their own revelation is false. Verse 92 supplies the proof: Moses came to you with clear signs, and you made an idol the moment his back was turned. Whatever you were doing with Moses, you were not believing him.

This is one of the Qur'an's habitual techniques, and it is worth naming because readers often mistake it for redundancy. The Qur'an repeats its narratives, but it rarely repeats them identically. Each retelling selects, adds or omits in order to serve the argument of the passage it appears in. The account of the calf at 2:51, at 2:92–93, at 7:148–150 and at 20:85–97 is not the same account four times; it is four angles on one event, each taken because that angle is what the surrounding verses need. A reader who notices this will find that the repetitions he once skimmed are among the most deliberate writing in the Book.

For practice, the verse gives a test that is short enough to carry and hard enough to matter.

The test is the gap between the claim and the interval. These people did not disbelieve Moses in a moment of crisis, after years of absence, with the evidence faded. They had the clear signs in front of them, and the clear signs' bearer had been gone for weeks. The claim and the contradiction were simultaneous. That simultaneity is what makes the evidence conclusive: there is no story in which they believed him and then lost their way gradually.

The modern versions are easy to find. The person who says he follows a teacher and does the opposite of what the teacher taught, in the teacher's own lifetime, while citing him. The community that names a founder and has inverted every principle he stood for. The reader who claims to be guided by a text and has not opened it in a year. In each case the defence is some version of "but I do believe in it" — and in each case the answer of this verse is that the belief and the behaviour are one subject, not two, and that the behaviour is the one with the better claim to be believed.

There is a gentler use of the same test, and it is the better one to begin with: apply it to yourself before applying it to anyone. Between what you say you hold and what you did last week, is there a gap? Everyone has one. The question is whether you have looked at it.

## Verse 93

**"And when We took your covenant and raised the mountain above you ˹saying˺, “Hold firmly to that ˹Scripture˺ which We have given you and obey,” they answered, “We hear and disobey.” The love of the calf was rooted in their hearts because of their disbelief. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “How evil is what your ˹so-called˺ belief prompts you to do, if you ˹actually˺ believe ˹in the Torah˺!”"**

In simple terms: the covenant was taken beneath the mountain, and when they were told to take the scripture with resolve and to listen, they answered with an insolent refusal. The love of the calf had been soaked into their hearts. The surah's verdict is withering: what a wretched thing your faith commands you to do.

*Wa idh akhadhna mithaqakum wa rafa'na fawqakum al-tura* — "and when We took your covenant and raised the mount above you." This is the same event described at verse 63, and the two accounts should be read together because the difference between them is instructive.

Verse 63 said: "Take what We have given you with determination, and **remember what is in it**, that you may be mindful." Verse 93 says: "Take what We have given you with determination, and **listen**." The first account gives the internal requirement — hold the contents in mind until they govern you. This account gives the external one — hear, and therefore obey. Between them they state the whole obligation: to receive the revelation inwardly and to submit to it outwardly. Neither verse contradicts the other; each supplies what the other's argument needed.

*Qalu sami'na wa 'asayna* — "they said: we hear and we disobey." There is a question here about whether this was literally spoken. The majority of commentators, following the transmitted reports, held that they said it aloud — and the insolence is more pointed if they did, because *sami'na wa ata'na*, "we hear and we obey," is the formula of the believer throughout the Qur'an (2:285, 4:46, 5:7, 24:51, 64:16). Their reply takes the believer's confession and inverts its second half. They heard the sound and refused the command. A minority read the clause as describing their condition rather than their utterance — that their conduct amounted to saying it. Both readings are held, and the substance is unaffected: the response to a clear directive was a refusal that was fully conscious of what it was refusing.

That inversion is the most quotable thing in the verse, and it is worth holding up to the light. Every religious community has a formula of assent, and every religious community contains people who recite the formula while reversing its meaning in the same breath. The Friday congregation says "we hear and we obey" and then spends the afternoon constructing the exception that will let it not obey. The inversion is always in the second half, never the first, because nobody has any difficulty hearing.

*Wa ushribu fi qulubihim al-'ijla bi-kufrihim* — "and the calf was made to be drunk into their hearts because of their disbelief." This is among the most striking metaphors in the Qur'an and it deserves to be dwelt on.

*Ushribu* is the passive of the verb meaning to give to drink. Not "they loved the calf," and not even "love of the calf entered their hearts," but: the calf itself was given to them to drink, into their hearts. Three things are carried by that construction.

The first is saturation. A drink pervades the body it enters; it does not sit in one place. The love of the calf was not one appetite among others in these people. It had soaked through them.

The second is passivity. The verb is passive — *they were made to drink it*. And the cause is stated immediately: *bi-kufrihim*, because of their own disbelief. This is the same causal structure as verse 88, where the curse follows their denial rather than preceding it. The condition overtook them, and it overtook them as a consequence of what they themselves had chosen. The surah is describing a process in which repeated choices harden into a condition, and the condition then feels like a nature.

And the third is the one that should stop a reader. **The calf in this verse is the calf they were already forgiven for.** Verse 52 announced the pardon: "Then We pardoned you after that, that perhaps you would be grateful." And here, forty verses later, the calf is still in them — not the guilt of it, which was lifted, but the love of it, which was not. Forgiveness was granted and the attachment survived the forgiveness.

That is one of the most searching observations in the whole surah, and it applies to almost everyone. How many people have been forgiven for something they still love? The sin was remitted; the taste for it was not surrendered. The person prays for forgiveness in the evening for what he intends to return to in the morning, and the prayer is not insincere — he genuinely wants not to be guilty — but he has not wanted to stop. He has asked to be pardoned for the thing without giving up the thing.

Repentance in the Qur'an has a shape, and it is not that shape. It requires regret, abandonment, resolve, and the restoration of what is owed to others. The abandonment is not optional decoration on the regret; it is what distinguishes regret from repentance. A person who has been forgiven and goes on loving what he was forgiven for has not yet completed the second step, and the surah's diagnosis of that condition is that the thing has been drunk into the heart.

*Qul bi'sama ya'murukum bihi imanukum in kuntum mu'minin* — "Say: wretched is what your faith commands you to do, if you are believers." *Bi'sama* is the verb of censure: how evil is that which. The sentence is one of the sharpest in the Qur'an, and it is sarcasm deployed for a serious purpose. If killing prophets, worshipping a calf and answering a covenant with "we hear and disobey" is what your belief produces, then your belief is a wretched thing — and the phrase *in kuntum mu'minin*, "if you are believers," makes the condition rhetorical. No one holding genuine faith would produce this.

The underlying principle is one the Qur'an states explicitly elsewhere and that every reader should take as a working instrument: a claim is verified by its fruits. "Say: You will not be questioned about what we criminals did" — hmm, that's 34:25. Better: 34:25 is "You will not be asked about what we committed." For the fruits principle: 47:30? Hmm. The clearest statement is the general Qur'anic method of judging claims by outcomes, and the hadith: "The strong man is not the one who overcomes people by his strength, but the strong man is the one who controls himself while angry" (al-Bukhari and Muslim) — no, that's not it either. The relevant one is the hadith of the man who prayed badly: the Prophet ﷺ told him to repeat his prayer three times, and when the man asked to be taught, he said: "When you stand for prayer, say takbir, then recite what is easy for you of the Qur'an..." That's about practice.

The cleanest instrument here is the verse's own logic: what does your faith command you to do? Not what does it permit, or what does it excuse — what does it command? The answer to that question, observed over a month rather than declared in a moment, is the most honest available measure of what a person actually believes.

## Verse 94

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter with Allah is exclusively for you ˹Israelites˺ out of all humanity, then wish for death if what you say is true!”"**

In simple terms: if you are certain that the Hereafter belongs to you alone, then long for death — because death is the door to it. If you are telling the truth about your claim, you will not be afraid of the door.

*Qul in kanat lakum al-daru al-akhiratu 'inda Allahi khalisatan min duni al-nasi* — "Say: if the final Home with Allah is exclusively yours, to the exclusion of all other people." *Al-dar al-akhirah* is the last abode — the Hereafter, and specifically the reward of it. *Khalisah* means pure, unmixed, exclusive: belonging to you and not shared with anyone. That is the claim being tested: not merely that they would enter, but that they alone would.

*Fa-tamannaw al-mawta in kuntum sadiqin* — "then wish for death, if you are truthful."

The logic is airtight and it rests on a psychological certainty rather than a scriptural one. If a person genuinely believes that death is the doorway to a state of exclusive and unending reward, then death is the most desirable event available to him, and he will long for it. The fact that he does not long for it proves that he does not believe what he says. There is no way to fake the result, because the test is not what a person says about death but what he feels about it.

The same challenge is issued in identical terms at 62:6–7: "Say: O you who are Jews, if you claim that you are allies of Allah apart from all other people, then wish for death, if you should be truthful. But they will not wish for it, ever, because of what their hands have put forth. And Allah is Knowing of the wrongdoers." The near-verbatim repetition confirms that the surah means this as a standing challenge rather than a local retort.

Now, a necessary boundary, because a reader could easily draw the wrong conclusion from this verse and the several hadiths that touch it.

**Islam does not teach Muslims to wish for death.** The Prophet ﷺ forbade it explicitly: "None of you should wish for death because of a calamity that has befallen him. If he must do something, let him say: 'O Allah, keep me alive as long as life is better for me, and cause me to die when death is better for me'" (al-Bukhari and Muslim, from Anas). The verse before us is a challenge issued to prove a claim in a specific argumentative context. It is not a general instruction, and it is not a spiritual aspiration.

The distinction that resolves the apparent tension is the one the Prophet ﷺ himself drew. 'A'ishah, may Allah be pleased with her, reported that he said: "Whoever loves to meet Allah, Allah loves to meet him; and whoever hates to meet Allah, Allah hates to meet him." She said: "O Prophet of Allah, do you mean hating death? For all of us hate death." He said: "It is not that. But when the believer is given the glad tidings of the mercy of Allah and His pleasure, he loves to meet Allah, and Allah loves to meet him. And when the disbeliever is given the tidings of the punishment of Allah and His wrath, he hates to meet Allah, and Allah hates to meet him" (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

That distinction is enormously liberating and it is the whole point. Dreading the process of dying, the pain, the loss, the leaving of people you love — none of that is a spiritual defect. It is human, and the Prophet ﷺ wept at a deathbed and said at the grave of his own son, "The eye weeps and the heart grieves, and we say only what pleases our Lord." What this verse tests is something narrower and much more searching: confidence about what comes **after**.

For practice, that gives a self-examination that is uncomfortable but clarifying.

Ask: if I were as certain of my standing with Allah as I say I am, would I be eager to meet Him? Most believers will find that the honest answer is not the one they would like. And the gap between the two answers is not proof of hypocrisy — it is information. It tells a person where his actual assurance sits as opposed to his professed one, and that is the first thing that has to be known before anything can be done about it.

The second application is about exclusive claims, which is what the verse is literally about. "The Hereafter belongs to us alone" is the most poisonous sentence any religious community can say about itself, and it has been said by every community, including the Muslim one. The verse's answer is not a counter-claim. It is a test. Put your confidence where your mouth is. Any group that cannot pass that test should reconsider the sentence.

And the third is the direction of the remedy. The reason the test works is that assurance about the afterlife is not produced by asserting it; it is produced by the thing the next verse names — what your hands have sent ahead. Confidence at the point of death is a by-product of a life, not a declaration made at the end of one.

## Verse 95

**"But they will never wish for that because of what their hands have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the wrongdoers."**

In simple terms: they will never wish for death, ever, because of what they have sent ahead to the Hereafter — and Allah knows the wrongdoers completely.

*Wa lan yatamannawhu abadan bima qaddamat aydihim* — "and they will never wish for it, ever, because of what their hands have sent ahead." *Lan* with *abadan* is the strongest negation Arabic can construct: never, to the end of time. The Qur'an is making a prediction, and it makes it emphatically.

Three observations about it.

The first is that this is a falsifiable claim made in public. The challenge was issued in Madinah, to a community that could have met it with a single sentence at any moment across more than a decade — and by the universal testimony of the historical record, no one did. Muslim writers on the inimitability of the Qur'an have cited this for over a thousand years as an instance of the text'sknowledge of the unseen. That is a genuine argument and it deserves to be stated as an argument: a challenge issued repeatedly, in a setting where compliance was trivially easy and would have destroyed the challenger's claim, was never taken up. It is cumulative rather than coercive evidence — a person who does not already accept the Qur'an's origin will not find it decisive on its own — but it is not nothing, and it should not be presented as either a proof that compels or a coincidence to be waved away.

The second observation is the phrase *bima qaddamat aydihim*, "because of what their hands have sent ahead." *Qaddamat* means to send forward, to dispatch in advance. The image is of provisions sent ahead of a traveller to the place he is going. Everything a person does is a package forwarded to his own destination, arriving before him and waiting for him.

And the third, which is the one that carries the verse: **the reason they will not wish for death is that they know what they have sent.** They are not people who doubt the Hereafter. Their terror of death is not the terror of annihilation; it is the terror of arrival. The fear is the evidence of the belief. A person who genuinely expected nothing after death would have no reason to fear it beyond the pain of the process, and a person who expected reward would welcome it. These people are in a third state: they know, and what they know is why they cannot bear to go.

That is the same diagnosis the surah made at verse 76, where they plotted in private to keep the believers from acquiring the proof that would be used against them *before their Lord*. They believed in the Lord, and in the Day, and in the argument that would be made there — and they tried to suppress the evidence anyway. The surah has been building one consistent portrait across these verses: a people who know, and who cannot make themselves act on what they know. That is the most tragic condition the Qur'an describes, and it is far more common than outright denial.

*Wa Allahu 'alimun bi-al-zalimin* — "and Allah is Knowing of the wrongdoers." Note that it says knowing *of the wrongdoers* rather than of all things, and the commentators observed that the specification is a threat: He knows them, and nothing of their wrongdoing is hidden from Him.

For practice, the verse turns a fear into a diagnostic instrument.

Most people carry some degree of unease about death, and most believers feel guilty about carrying it, because they have been told that a Muslim should welcome the meeting with his Lord. That guilt is misplaced, for the reason the hadith in the previous verse established: the natural dread of dying is not the same as lacking confidence in what follows, and the Prophet ﷺ wept at gravesides.

But there is a second kind of unease, and it is different in kind. It is the specific inability to think about the accounting. The person who changes the subject when the afterlife comes up, who cannot read the descriptions of the Day without a physical wish to be elsewhere, who has never once in his life considered in detail what it would mean to stand before Allah — that is not natural dread. That is the condition this verse names, and it is information about what he believes his hands have sent ahead.

The remedy is the only one there has ever been. The way to become unafraid of arriving is to change what is waiting there. Repentance, the return of what is owed, the corrected relationship, the undone wrong — these are not abstractions and they are not available at the last moment by declaration. They are the things that change the contents of the package, and they are the reason the Prophet ﷺ said what the next verse records about longevity.

## Verse 96

**"You will surely find them clinging to life more eagerly than any other people, even more than polytheists. Each one of them wishes to live a thousand years. But even if they were to live that long, it would not save them from the punishment. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do."**

In simple terms: they are the most covetous of all people for life — more so even than idolaters — and each of them would love to be granted a thousand years. But even that would not move him an inch away from the punishment.

*Wa la-tajidannahum ahrasa al-nasi 'ala hayatin* — "and you will surely find them the most covetous of people for life." *Ahras* is the intensive form of greed, craving, covetousness — the word is stronger than mere desire. And note the address: *la-tajidannahum*, "you will certainly find them," said to the Prophet ﷺ. This is presented as an observable fact about actual people, not a theological claim.

*Wa min alladhina ashraku* — "and [more covetous] than those who associate partners with Allah." The construction is elliptical; the sense is *ahrasa min alladhina ashraku* — greedier for life than the idolaters.

And here is the puzzle that has occupied the commentators, and it is a genuine one: why would they cling to life more fiercely than people who do not believe in an afterlife at all? If you deny the Hereafter, this life is all there is, and losing it is losing everything. If you believe in the Hereafter and believe it is yours, death is a doorway. Either way you should be less attached to life than someone who believes in the Hereafter and knows he is not ready for it.

That is precisely the answer the tradition gives, and it is devastating. They cling harder than the idolaters *because they know*. The idolater fears only extinction. These people fear judgement. Knowledge without repentance does not decrease the terror of death; it multiplies it, because it supplies the terror with content.

The same pattern holds at the level of the individual, and it explains something that otherwise looks like a paradox: some of the most anxious people about death are the ones with the most religious knowledge and the least religious practice. Knowledge of the standard, combined with the absence of the repair, produces exactly the condition this verse describes.

*Yawaddu ahaduhum law yu'ammaru alfa sanatin* — "each of them would love to be granted life of a thousand years." *Yu'ammar* is the passive of a form meaning to give long life. A thousand years functions in Arabic as the idiomatic extreme of a long span rather than as a precise figure, and the Qur'an uses round thousands elsewhere for the same purpose (22:47, 32:5, 97:3). Some commentators also connected the figure to transmitted claims about expectations of longevity among them; those are reports and should be cited as such.

*Wa ma huwa bi-muzahzihihi min al-'adhari an yu'ammara* — "but he would not be one to remove him from the punishment, that he should be granted long life." *Muzahzih* is a rare and emphatic word meaning one who pushes away, shifts, distances. Even the faintest movement away from the punishment is denied.

This is the verse's central thrust, and it is aimed directly at the most successful evasion in human life. What is being refused here is the assumption that more time solves the problem. It does not. The punishment is not averted by postponement, because the punishment is not a deadline that might be missed. Granting the thousand years would not move him closer to escape; it would only move him closer to arrival.

*Wa Allahu basirun bima ya'malun* — "and Allah is All-Seeing of what they do." Note the shift from *'alim*, knowing, in verse 95 to *basir*, seeing. The two are not interchangeable: seeing is direct observation of the act as it is done.

For practice, this verse is the most practically useful in the batch, and it has three edges.

The first is the deferral. "I will deal with that later" is the single most effective strategy the self has ever devised, and this verse dismantles it in a clause. Later is not a solution; later is the mechanism by which the problem grows. The person who will repent at forty, at retirement, when the children are grown, when the career is settled, has not scheduled a repair. He has scheduled a delay, and the delay is itself the thing the verse diagnoses. The question is never whether there will be time. The question is whether time is what is missing — and it almost never is.

The second is the thousand years, which sharpens the same point. Ask honestly: if you were granted a thousand years, would you do it? Not "could you" — would you? For almost everyone, the honest answer is no, and that answer proves that the obstacle is not the clock. Whatever is stopping you now would still be stopping you in year nine hundred.

And the third is the paradox of knowledge. The person who knows what is required and does not do it is in a worse state than the person who does not know, not a better one. That is not an argument for ignorance — it is an argument for closing the gap, and it explains why religious knowledge that is not acted upon so reliably produces anxiety, hardness and eventually a kind of weary cynicism. The knowledge was supposed to produce motion. Without the motion, it produces only dread.

## Verse 97

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whoever is an enemy of Gabriel should know that he revealed this ˹Quran˺ to your heart by Allah’s Will, confirming what came before it—a guide and good news for the believers.”"**

In simple terms: if anyone is hostile to Gabriel, let him know that Gabriel is the one who brought this Qur'an down into your heart by Allah's command, confirming the earlier scriptures, and bringing guidance and glad tidings to those who believe.

The setting, which is transmitted in the commentaries. The reports state that the Jews of Madinah asked the Prophet ﷺ about the angel who brought him revelation, and when he told them it was Jibril (Gabriel), they said that Jibril was their enemy — the angel of severity, punishment and war — and that had it been Mika'il (Michael), who brings rain and mercy, they would have believed him. This passage was revealed in response. The report is transmitted on the authority of Ibn 'Abbas and others, and it is one of the better-attested occasions of revelation for this part of the surah; it also fits the text exactly, since the following verse names both angels. It remains transmitted material rather than Qur'anic text, and should be cited as such.

*Qul man kana 'aduwan li-Jibrila* — "Say: whoever is an enemy of Gabriel." Jibril is named three times in the Qur'an — here, at verse 98, and at 66:4 — and he is described elsewhere as *al-Ruh al-Amin*, the Trustworthy Spirit (26:193), and as *Ruh al-Qudus*, the Holy Spirit (2:87, 16:102). He is the angel of revelation, and belief in the angels is an article of Islamic faith (2:177, 2:285, 4:136).

*Fa-innahu nazzalahu 'ala qalbika bi-idhni Allahi* — "then indeed he brought it down upon your heart by the permission of Allah." Two things here are load-bearing.

The first is *'ala qalbika*, "upon your heart." Not "to you" and not "upon your hearing." The same phrase appears at 26:193–194: "The Trustworthy Spirit has brought it down upon your heart, that you may be among the warners, in a clear Arabic language." The commentators drew several points from it: that the revelation was fixed and preserved in his heart rather than merely heard; that it was received by the whole person and not only by the ear; and that he carried it within him rather than reading it from a text — which is the point of 29:48, "And you did not recite before it any scripture, nor did you inscribe one with your right hand." The same theme appears at 75:16–18: "Do not move your tongue with it to hasten with recitation of the Qur'an. Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation."

The second is *bi-idhni Allah*, "by the permission of Allah." And this is the argument of the verse. Gabriel did not bring the Qur'an on his own initiative. He acted by command. Enmity to the one who was sent is therefore enmity to the One who sent him — which is exactly what the next verse makes explicit. The objection is exposed as incoherent: you cannot honour the sender while declaring yourself the enemy of his emissary, any more than you can accept a letter while assaulting the postman who carried it.

*Musaddiqan lima bayna yadayhi* — "confirming what was before it." The same argument from *tasdiq* that runs through this whole passage at verses 41, 89 and 91.

*Wa hudan wa bushra lil-mu'minin* — "and a guidance and glad tidings for the believers." Note the precision. Guidance is offered to all (2:2, 2:185), but the glad tidings are specified for those who believe — because good news is only good news to someone who receives it as such. The parallel at 27:2 reads identically.

For practice, three things carry.

The first is the rejection of a message on account of its messenger, which is the oldest form of refusal there is and has never gone out of style. The message is dismissed because of the institution that carries it, the country the speaker comes from, the school he belongs to, the platform he speaks on, or the fact that someone disreputable also said something similar. The verse's answer is that a courier acting under orders is not an independent party to the message. If you have a problem with the message, address the message.

The second is more pointed and it is the specific content of their objection. They wanted the angel of mercy and rejected the angel of severity. They wanted the revelation that comforts and refused the revelation that warns. That demand has never changed. Every generation wants a religion of consolation without a religion of accountability, and every generation produces teachers who supply it — and this verse is the standing refusal. The same angel brings both, because the message contains both, and a religion that has only the first half has been edited by someone.

And the third is the *qalb*. The Qur'an was brought down into a heart, and it is meant to be carried in one. The Muslim practice of memorisation is not cultural decoration; it is the community's response to the fact that the revelation was deposited in the Prophet's heart rather than handed to him as a document. A person who has some of the Qur'an inside him — carried, available, recovered in the night, recalled in the moment he needs it — is participating in the very form in which it was sent down.

## Verse 98

**"Whoever is an enemy of Allah, His angels, His messengers, Gabriel, and Michael, then ˹let them know that˺ Allah is certainly the enemy of the disbelievers."**

In simple terms: whoever sets himself against Allah, His angels and His messengers — Gabriel and Michael among them — should know that Allah is, in turn, opposed to those who reject.

*Man kana 'aduwan lillahi wa mala'ikatihi wa rusulihi wa Jibrila wa Mika'ila* — "whoever is an enemy of Allah and His angels and His messengers and Gabriel and Michael." Note the structure: the general first — Allah, His angels, His messengers — and then the particular, Gabriel and Michael, singled out after already being included in the general category. The rhetoricians call this *'atf al-khass 'ala al-'amm*, coordinating the specific upon the general, and its function is to single something out for emphasis. The effect here is to say: and yes, including these two specifically, since you named them.

*Mika'il*, Michael, is named once in the Qur'an, here. He is traditionally associated in the reports with rain, provision and mercy — which is why they named him as the acceptable alternative to Gabriel. The verse's answer is that the distinction is false: both are angels of Allah, both act by His command, and setting one against the other is setting oneself against the One who sends both.

*Fa-inna Allaha 'aduwun lil-kafirin* — "then indeed Allah is an enemy of the disbelievers."

This clause requires care, because it attributes something to Allah that, taken in its human sense, would be imperfection. The works of creed are precise here and the precision should be reproduced.

Ahl al-Sunnah affirm the attributes Allah has affirmed for Himself — including love, pleasure, wrath, and enmity — without *tashbih* (likening Him to creation) and without *ta'til* (denying the attribute). Allah's enmity is not like a human being's enmity. It does not involve agitation, distress, need, or the possibility of being harmed. It means that He withdraws His protection, His friendship and His care from such a person, and treats him as the enemies of His friends are treated. The Maturidi and Ash'ari discussions of the divine attributes handle these terms at length, and the same method was noted at verse 29 in connection with *istawa'*.

Two further points should be made about the clause.

The first is its conditionality. The verse is constructed as "whoever is an enemy... then Allah is an enemy." The enmity is a response to theirs, not a prior and unexplained state. That ordering is the same ordering the surah has maintained throughout: the curse follows the denial (2:88), the sealing follows the refusal (2:7), the hardening follows the exposure (2:74). Nothing in this surah describes Allah closing a door before a person closes it on himself.

The second is the shift in subject: from "whoever is an enemy" to *lil-kafirin*, "the disbelievers." The commentators noted that this is an identification rather than a generalisation — setting oneself against Allah's messengers *is* disbelief, so the two descriptions denote the same people.

For practice, the verse establishes two things a reader should hold.

The first is the gravity of what is ordinarily treated as a minor matter. Setting oneself against the religion — mocking it, working against it, opposing those who carry it — is not a neutral position from which one might later be persuaded. This verse names it as enmity, and enmity is reciprocal. The person who has spent years opposing the truth is not in the same condition as the person who has simply not got round to it, and the difference is of his own making.

The second is the demand for a religion without the warning, which is what the underlying objection was. They wanted Michael and refused Gabriel. They wanted the rain and not the reckoning. That is the same demand that produced the comfortable eschatology of verse 80 — the Fire will only touch us for a few days — and the same demand that produces, in every generation, a version of faith stripped of everything difficult. The verse's answer is that the two angels come from the same sender and cannot be separated, and that a person who takes only the half he likes has not taken the religion at all.

## Verse 99

**"Indeed, We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ clear revelations. ˹But˺ none will deny them except the rebellious."**

In simple terms: We have sent down to you signs that are clear in themselves, and no one rejects them except those who have gone out of obedience altogether.

*Wa la-qad anzalna ilayka ayatin bayyinatin* — "and We have certainly sent down to you clear signs." The word *ayat* carries both senses at once: the verses of the Qur'an and the proofs they constitute. They are called *bayyinat*, clear and self-evidencing, because — on the Qur'an's own claim — their clarity is the argument. That claim is the surah's challenge at verse 23: produce a surah like it. The evidence for the revelation is the revelation.

*Wa ma yakfuru biha illa al-fasiqun* — "and none denies them except the defiantly disobedient." *Al-fasiqun* is the word the surah used at verse 27 for those who break the covenant of Allah after its confirmation, sever what He has ordered to be joined, and spread corruption on the earth. It is a bookend: the surah opened by defining *fisq* and closes this passage by naming the deniers with the same word. The connection is deliberate and it locates the cause of denial squarely in the will rather than in the evidence.

The parallel at 29:49 states the same thing in near-identical words: "Rather, it [the Qur'an] is clear verses in the breasts of those who have been given knowledge. And none reject Our verses except the wrongdoers."

Now, the necessary boundary, and it must be stated every time this verse is explained.

**This verse does not permit any human being to look at a person who does not believe and conclude that he is a *fasiq*.** The verse describes the condition of someone to whom the clear signs have come in their clarity and who has rejected them in full knowledge. Whether that description applies to any given individual is not something any human being can establish, because it depends on things we cannot see: whether the message reached him in a recognisable form, whether its clarity was obscured by the conduct of those who claimed to represent it, whether he is in the category the scholars call *ahl al-fatra* — those who lived in an interval between messengers — and what he did with whatever light he had.

The tradition has always held these two things together without tension: on the one hand, that the signs are clear and that the denial of them is attributed to something other than the evidence; on the other hand, that judgment about individuals belongs to Allah alone. The Qur'an says so in the plainest terms: "Indeed, those who have believed and those who were Jews and the Sabians and the Christians and the Magians and those who associated others with Allah — Allah will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection" (22:17).

For practice, this verse is meant to be turned inward, and it is almost always turned outward instead.

Used outwardly, it becomes a way of dismissing people: he rejects it because he is corrupt, and nothing further needs to be said. That use is a misuse, and it has done enormous damage to the credibility of the religion in every age. Used inwardly, the verse becomes one of the sharpest instruments available. It asks a question that is difficult to answer honestly: when I resist something in this religion, is my resistance about the evidence, or is it about what accepting it would cost me?

That question is worth running properly. Take the specific thing you find hardest — a ruling, an obligation, a prohibition, a claim. Now ask: if this were the other way round — if the religion commanded what I already do and forbade what I already avoid — would I find it equally difficult? If the answer is no, the difficulty is not intellectual. And the verse's word for that condition is *fisq*: going out, stepping outside the boundary, setting oneself apart from what one has been given.

There is a second application that is gentler and more useful. The verse says the signs are *bayyinat* — clear. That is a claim the reader is entitled to test, and the tradition has always insisted that he test it. The Qur'an does not ask to be believed before it is examined; it asks to be examined and promises that the examination will not fail. A person who has never actually read it from beginning to end has not tested the claim, whatever he thinks about it.

## Verse 100

**"Why is it that every time they make a covenant, a group of them casts it aside? In fact, most of them do not believe."**

In simple terms: whenever they enter into a covenant, a party among them throws it aside — and the deeper truth is that most of them do not believe at all.

*A-wa-kullama 'ahadu 'ahdan nabadhahu fariqun minhum* — "why is it that whenever they make a covenant, a party of them casts it aside?" *'Ahada 'ahdan* is the cognate accusative, used for emphasis: they made a covenant-making. *Nabadhahu* is to throw something away, to cast it off — a more violent and more casual word than "break." The image is of something picked up and tossed over the shoulder.

Compare 2:27, which used a different verb for the same family of acts: *yanquduna 'ahda Allahi min ba'di mithaqihi* — they break the covenant of Allah after its confirmation. *Naqada* is to untwist a rope strand by strand; *nabadha* is to fling it away. One describes erosion and the other describes dismissal, and the surah has now used both.

Note *fariqun minhum*, "a party of them." This is the sixth time in this stretch of the surah that a condemnation has been qualified rather than applied wholesale — "those who wronged" (59), "those among you who transgressed" (65), "a party of them" (75), "except a few of you" (83), "a party of you" (85), and now here. The consistency is the argument. In a passage whose entire function is to indict, the text refuses, six times running, to indict everyone. No reader of this surah may generalise where the Qur'an particularises, and the refusal is modelled rather than merely instructed.

*Bal aktharuhum la yu'minun* — "rather, most of them do not believe." *Bal* is a reversal: not merely that, but something deeper. The verse moves from the act to the root. The first clause is precise — a party violates. The second goes further — most of them do not believe at all. The violation is a symptom; the absence of faith is the disease.

And note, even in the broader statement, the qualifier holds: *aktharuhum*, most of them — not all of them. Twice in one verse the surah declines to say everyone.

Covenant-breaking is one of the structural themes of this surah. The covenant appears at 2:27 (broken), 2:40 (fulfil Mine and I will fulfil yours), 2:63 and 2:93 (taken beneath the mountain), 2:83 and 2:84 (its contents), 2:100 (violated), 2:125 (given to Abraham), and 2:177 (fulfilling one's contracts as an element of righteousness). The thread running through all of it is that the relationship between Allah and a human being has the structure of a covenant: something given, something promised in return, and a real obligation incurred.

The Qur'an states the obligation generally and not only for them: "And fulfil the covenant. Indeed, the covenant is ever [that about which one will be] questioned" (17:34). And: "And fulfil the covenant of Allah when you have taken it, and do not break oaths after their confirmation while you have made Allah, over you, a surety" (16:91). And: "O you who believe, fulfil [all] contracts" (5:1).

And the Prophet ﷺ placed covenant-breaking at the centre of the diagnosis of hypocrisy: "The signs of the hypocrite are three: when he speaks, he lies; when he promises, he breaks it; and when he is entrusted, he betrays the trust" (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

For practice, this verse gives the pattern rather than the instance, and the pattern is what convicts.

Anyone can break a promise once. What the verse names is the *kullama* — "every time." The recurrence is the diagnosis. A single failure is an event and can be repented of; a pattern is a condition, and it tells you something about the person's actual relationship to the thing he keeps promising.

The applications are everywhere, and they are not only religious. The covenant with Allah — the commitment made in a moment of clarity and quietly dropped by Tuesday. The marriage. The contract. The promise to a child. The undertaking to oneself. In each case the failure is usually not a dramatic repudiation but a *nabdh*: the thing is simply set aside, without ceremony, and then set aside again.

And the verse's second clause supplies the remedy, which is the same one the whole passage has been pointing to. The reason the covenants do not hold is that the faith underneath them does not hold. Promises are kept by people who believe that promises matter, and what makes them matter is the One to whom they are ultimately made. A person who wants to stop breaking his covenants will not get there by resolving harder. He will get there by strengthening the thing underneath, which is the conviction that there is Someone who will ask — as 17:34 says — about the covenant itself.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Verses 91 to 100 are a closing argument, and they close it by taking the opponents' own claims and showing what those claims commit them to.

They say they believe in what was sent down to them, and the surah answers with the record: the prophets who brought it were killed, the calf was worshipped while Moses was on the mountain, and the covenant was met with "we hear and disobey" (91–93). And it adds the detail that settles the matter — the love of the calf had been drunk into their hearts, so that they were forgiven for it and still carried it.

They say the Hereafter belongs to them alone, and the surah sets a test no one can counterfeit: wish for death (94). It states that they will never do it, ever, because of what their hands have sent ahead (95), and it explains that they are greedier for life than anyone, greedier even than idolaters, because they know what is waiting — and that a thousand years would not move them an inch away from it (96).

They object to the angel who brought the revelation, preferring the angel of mercy to the angel of warning, and the surah answers that a courier acting by command cannot be separated from the One who commanded him (97–98).

And the passage closes with the verdict. The signs are clear; none denies them but those who have stepped outside obedience (99). The pattern running through all of it is a covenant taken and cast aside, every time it is taken — and beneath the pattern, the root: most of them do not believe (100).

Three conclusions hold these verses together. First, the method is *ilzam* throughout — no new evidence is introduced, because none is needed; the claim is tested against its own history and found insufficient. Second, the diagnosis is never intellectual: the failure is located in desire (92), in a drunk-in love (93), in the deeds sent ahead (95), in covetousness (96), in enmity (97), and in *fisq* (99). And third, the surah's discipline never breaks: six times in this stretch it qualifies where it could have generalised, and it closes by saying "most of them" rather than "all of them." Whatever this passage convicts, it does not convict a people. It convicts a posture — and the posture is available to everyone.

**Other works**
- Works of creed — on the affirmation of the divine attributes without likening or denial, on belief in the angels as an article of faith, and on the conditional rather than arbitrary nature of the divine enmity described in verse 98
- Works on *i'jaz al-Qur'an* — on the predictive claim of verse 95 as an argument, its place in the literature, and the proper limits of that argument
- Works on *balaghah* — on repetition with difference, and on *sami'na wa ata'na* as the believers' formula

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators genuinely differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: whether "we hear and disobey" was spoken or descriptive, whether the report connecting the thousand years to expectations of longevity should be pressed, and how the divine enmity of verse 98 is to be understood.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the occasion of revelation concerning Gabriel and Michael in verses 97–98. It is one of the better-attested occasions in this part of the surah and it fits the text's naming of both angels, but it is transmitted and not Qur'anic.

Four boundaries have been observed. On verse 91: the argument there has been explicitly identified as a rhetorical *ilzam* constructed on the opponents' own claim to continuity, and it has been distinguished from the legal principle of 2:134 and 2:141 which abolishes inherited guilt, so that the verse cannot be used to attribute the killing of prophets to a later people. On verse 94: the challenge to wish for death has been sharply distinguished from the prohibition on wishing for death, with the hadith and the supplication given, and with the Prophet's own distinction between dreading the process of death and lacking confidence in what follows. On verse 95: the predictive claim has been presented as a genuine but cumulative argument rather than as a coercive proof. And on verse 99: the attribution of denial to *fisq* has been stated alongside the strict boundary that no human being may apply it to any individual, since it depends on what reached him and in what form, and judgment belongs to Allah alone (22:17).
