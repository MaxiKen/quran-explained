## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:1

> O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate,[170] and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.

**Commentary:**

### A Universal Call

Sūrah al-Nisāʾ begins by addressing all humanity, not one ethnicity, class, or religious group. The command to have *taqwā*—mindful reverence and protective obedience toward Allah—is the foundation for the social laws that follow. Justice toward orphans, women, relatives, and the vulnerable cannot be separated from worship.

The verse teaches that social ethics are religious obligations. A person who claims devotion to Allah but abuses dependants or breaks family ties has misunderstood the purpose of *taqwā*.

### One Human Origin

Allah created humanity from a single soul, *nafs wāḥidah*. Classical exegetes commonly identify this with Ādam عليه السلام and his mate with Ḥawwāʾ, while the wording itself emphasizes the common origin of all human beings. Men and women come from one human source and share the same created dignity.

The verse does not present one sex or ethnicity as a separate species. Differences of role, ability, and circumstance do not erase the fundamental kinship of humanity before Allah.

### Marriage and the Spread of Humanity

From the first pair Allah spread countless men and women. Human diversity is therefore part of divine creation, not a reason for pride or contempt. Every family, nation, and community ultimately returns to the same human origin.

The verse also places marriage and family within a sacred framework. Sexual relationships, children, inheritance, and kinship are not merely private arrangements; they carry duties and accountability.

### Calling upon Allah’s Name

People appeal to one another in Allah’s Name when requesting rights, protection, assistance, or fulfilment of an obligation. Because Allah’s Name is invoked in social dealings, it must not be used manipulatively. A person should not swear falsely, pressure the weak, or demand what is unjust while claiming religious authority.

### Honouring Family Ties

The command to honour *al-arḥām*, family ties, includes maintaining contact, showing kindness, supporting relatives in need, resolving disputes, and avoiding unjust estrangement. Family ties do not require obeying relatives in sin or tolerating abuse. They require justice, mercy, and appropriate boundaries.

### Allah Is Watchful

Allah is *raqīb*, ever Watchful. He sees private family conduct, hidden financial abuse, neglected responsibilities, and acts of kindness that no one else notices. This awareness protects the vulnerable and keeps the powerful accountable.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:1–2 on human origin, family ties, and the protection of orphans.
- Qur’an 49:13 on humanity’s common origin and honour through *taqwā*.
- Qur’an 30:21 on marriage as a sign of tranquillity, affection, and mercy.
- Qur’an 16:72 and 25:54 on family, marriage, and human relationships as blessings from Allah.
- Qur’an 13:21 and 47:22–23 on maintaining family ties and the harm of severing them.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 6:152 on justice even when family interests are involved.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on maintaining family ties, honouring women, and the common origin of humanity.
- For *taqwā*, *nafs wāḥidah*, *arḥām*, and *raqīb*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:1.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:2

> Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin.

**Commentary:**

### Orphans’ Wealth Is a Trust

The command concerns children who have lost their fathers and whose property is placed under guardianship. Their wealth does not become the guardian’s property. It is an *amānah*, a trust that must be preserved and returned when the orphan reaches maturity and demonstrates sound judgement.

The guardian serves the child; the child does not exist to finance the guardian’s lifestyle.

### Do Not Exchange the Inferior

Guardians are forbidden from substituting their inferior property for the orphan’s valuable property. This could involve taking good livestock and giving weak livestock in return, exchanging sound investments for failing ones, or using quality assets while recording worthless replacements.

The prohibition exposes a subtle form of theft that can be disguised as an exchange. A guardian must deal as though the orphan’s wealth were his own—indeed, more carefully, because he has no ownership claim over it.

### Do Not Mix Wealth to Conceal Abuse

Mixing one’s property with an orphan’s can make misuse difficult to detect. Shared accounts, household expenses, business records, or informal arrangements must not become a means of consuming the child’s share. If genuine management requires some pooling, accurate records and transparent accounting are essential.

The principle extends today to trustees, executors, foster carers, charitable organisations, and anyone managing property for a vulnerable person.

### Maturity and Delivery

The verse connects giving the wealth back with reaching maturity. Verse 6 adds the requirement of testing competence and sound judgement. Age alone does not justify handing over complex assets without ensuring that the person can manage them safely.

Guardians should prepare the child through education, gradual responsibility, and honest financial guidance.

### A Great Sin

Allah calls this a great sin because it combines betrayal, exploitation, and abuse of weakness. The orphan often lacks the power to challenge an adult guardian. Divine law therefore places the matter under severe moral warning and requires community oversight.

A guardian who has taken what is not his must repent, return the property, and compensate for loss where possible.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:2, 4:6, and 4:10 on returning orphans’ property and the punishment for consuming it.
- Qur’an 6:152 and 17:34 on approaching an orphan’s wealth only in the best manner.
- Qur’an 2:220 on improving the condition of orphans and the limits of mixing with them.
- Qur’an 8:27 and 23:8 on fulfilling trusts.
- Qur’an 59:7 on preventing wealth from circulating only among the powerful.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports warning against the major sin of consuming an orphan’s property and commanding the fulfilment of trusts.
- For *yatāmā*, *amānah*, *tabdīl*, consuming wealth wrongfully, and guardianship, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:2.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:3

> If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one[171] or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession.[172] This way you are less likely to commit injustice.

**Commentary:**

### Protecting Female Orphans

The immediate context concerns male guardians who managed the wealth and lives of orphan girls. Some wished to marry a wealthy or beautiful orphan while offering her less than a fair dowry or failing to treat her properly. Allah directs them away from exploiting their guardianship: if they fear injustice, they should marry other women.

This is a protection of the orphan’s consent, wealth, dignity, and marital rights. Guardianship must never become a route to pressure a vulnerable girl into marriage.

### The Limit and Permission of Plural Marriage

The verse permits marriage to two, three, or four wives, but it does not command every man to practise polygyny. The numbers establish a maximum of four, not a requirement or an unrestricted licence. A valid marriage still requires consent, a dowry, maintenance, and fulfilment of rights.

The permission is placed within a passage focused on justice and protection, not male privilege detached from accountability.

### Justice Is the Condition

If a man fears that he cannot maintain justice, he is commanded to marry only one. Justice includes equitable financial maintenance, housing, time, protection, and outward treatment. Emotional equality is difficult, which is why verse 4:129 later warns against inclining entirely toward one wife and leaving another suspended.

A person who knows he will be abusive, financially incapable, or persistently partial should not enter a plural marriage while claiming the verse as permission.

### The Historical Reference to Bondwomen

The phrase “those in your possession” refers to a historical system of captives and bondwomen that existed in the societies addressed by the Qur’an. The Qur’an regulated an existing institution, protected the rights of those in bondage, encouraged emancipation, and prohibited sexual exploitation outside lawful frameworks. It does not provide a licence for kidnapping, trafficking, or modern slavery.

In the modern legal context, where slavery is prohibited, no person may claim ownership of another or use this verse to justify sexual abuse. Lawful marriage remains the relevant route for intimacy, and all human beings possess dignity before Allah.

### The Aim Is to Avoid Injustice

The verse ends by saying this is more likely to prevent injustice. The central principle is not the multiplication of relationships; it is protection from *ẓulm*. Any interpretation that produces coercion, exploitation, or abandonment contradicts the moral purpose of the verse.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:2–6 on orphans’ rights, guardianship, competence, and property.
- Qur’an 4:19 and 4:129 on treating women fairly and avoiding emotional abandonment.
- Qur’an 24:33 on encouraging emancipation and prohibiting the coercion of bondwomen into prostitution.
- Qur’an 30:21 on marriage, affection, and mercy.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 16:90 on justice as a governing principle.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the report of ʿĀʾishah explaining the concern about guardians marrying orphan girls without giving them their full dowries; reports on justice among wives and emancipation.
- For *yatāmā al-nisāʾ*, *taʿdil*, *taʿdilū*, *nikāḥ*, *mā malakat aymānukum*, and the limits of plural marriage, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:3.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:4

> Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience.

**Commentary:**

### The Bridal Gift Belongs to the Wife

The dowry, *mahr* or *ṣadāq*, is a mandatory gift from the husband to the wife. It belongs to her personally, not to her father, guardian, family, or community. A marriage cannot turn the bride into property; the dowry recognizes her dignity and establishes a financial right.

The command to give it graciously means that it should be offered willingly, respectfully, and without resentment or humiliating reminders.

### Consent and Ownership

If a wife voluntarily gives up part of her dowry, the husband may accept it with a clear conscience. The condition of willingness is essential. A waiver obtained through intimidation, emotional pressure, threats, deception, or financial desperation is not the gracious gift described here.

A wife remains free to keep, invest, spend, or donate her dowry as she chooses.

### No Family Appropriation

In some cultures, families demand the bride’s dowry or use marriage to transfer wealth away from her. This verse rejects that practice. A guardian may facilitate the marriage, but he cannot claim the woman’s property without her free consent.

The same principle applies to wedding payments, property, and gifts connected with marriage.

### Marriage Is a Covenant of Responsibility

The dowry is not a purchase price for the woman. It is part of the financial responsibilities that accompany a lawful marriage. The husband also owes maintenance, kindness, and protection, while the wife retains her independent property rights.

Reducing marriage to a transaction undermines the Qur’anic vision of affection, mercy, and mutual responsibility.

### A Clear Conscience

The verse acknowledges that spouses may mutually adjust financial arrangements, but only after the wife’s right has been established and she chooses freely. “With a clear conscience” means the husband must be certain that he has not manipulated her and that the matter does not conceal injustice.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:4, 4:19–21, and 4:24–25 on dowries, marriage, women’s rights, and the seriousness of the marital covenant.
- Qur’an 2:236–237 on dowry when divorce occurs before consummation.
- Qur’an 30:21 on marriage as affection, mercy, and tranquillity.
- Qur’an 2:188 and 4:29 on consent in financial dealings and the prohibition of consuming wealth wrongfully.
- Qur’an 4:32 and 4:7 on women’s independent financial rights.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the dowry being given to the bride and the permissibility of a modest dowry when offered sincerely.
- For *ṣadāq*, *niḥlah*, willingness, and the wife’s ownership of her bridal gift, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:4.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:5

> Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly.

**Commentary:**

### Wealth as a Means of Support

Allah calls wealth a means of support, *qiyām*, because it sustains life and allows people to meet their responsibilities. It is not an object of worship or a reason to humiliate the poor. Since wealth supports families and communities, it must be managed with care.

The verse concerns those who lack the competence to manage property safely, such as young children, people with severe cognitive impairment, or adults whose judgement has become seriously impaired.

### Protecting, Not Oppressing

The instruction not to hand over wealth to an incapable person is a protective measure, not permission to seize it permanently. A guardian must manage the property for the owner’s benefit, preserve it, and return control when competence is established.

The verse should never be used to deny competent women, orphans, or other adults their independent financial rights.

### Provision from the Wealth

Those under guardianship must be fed and clothed from the wealth entrusted to their care. Their property is meant to support them, not to be locked away while they suffer deprivation. Reasonable expenses should be recorded and directed toward their welfare.

A guardian who lives comfortably from another person’s wealth while neglecting that person’s needs has betrayed the trust.

### Kind Speech

Financial dependence can expose people to shame and manipulation. Allah therefore commands kind speech. Caregivers must explain decisions respectfully, avoid insults, and not make vulnerable people feel like burdens.

Even when a restriction is necessary, dignity remains necessary.

### Ethical Guardianship

Modern applications include trustees, parents managing children’s assets, professional carers, estate administrators, and institutions handling donations. Good guardianship requires transparent accounts, safeguards against conflicts of interest, independent oversight, and regular review of competence.

The goal is supported independence, not permanent control.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:2, 4:5–6, and 4:10 on managing orphans’ wealth and protecting vulnerable dependants.
- Qur’an 2:220 on improving the affairs of orphans and sharing life with them responsibly.
- Qur’an 17:26–29 on balanced spending and the rights of relatives and the needy.
- Qur’an 2:263 and 93:9–10 on kind speech and not oppressing the vulnerable.
- Qur’an 4:32 and 4:7 on the financial rights of men and women.
- Qur’an 59:7 on preventing wealth from remaining only among the rich.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on guardianship, the care of dependants, and the prohibition of consuming another person’s wealth.
- For *sufahāʾ*, *qiyām*, guardianship, financial competence, provision, and dignified care, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:5.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:6

> Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age.

> Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation;

> but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner.

**Commentary:**

### Testing Competence

Guardians are commanded to test the competence of orphans before returning their wealth. The Arabic concept of *rushd* includes sound judgement, practical understanding, and the ability to manage property responsibly. Reaching physical maturity alone does not prove financial competence.

Testing should be gradual and appropriate: allowing the young person to manage small amounts, understand contracts, budget, save, and make decisions while receiving guidance.

### Marriageable Age Is Not a Command to Marry

The reference to a marriageable age establishes a stage of maturity for reviewing guardianship. It does not command the orphan to marry or make marriage a condition for owning property. A person’s property rights must not be delayed to pressure them into marriage.

Any marriage must also satisfy consent, maturity, protection from harm, and the lawfully recognized rights of the person involved.

### Return the Property Promptly

Once sound judgement is established, the guardian must return the orphan’s wealth. Delaying delivery in order to continue benefiting from it is a form of theft. Guardians must not consume it wastefully or spend quickly before the young owner becomes able to claim it.

The property should be preserved, invested lawfully, and managed transparently for the orphan’s benefit.

### Compensation for Guardians

A wealthy guardian should not take compensation from the orphan’s property. A poor guardian may take a reasonable provision for necessary work and care, without extravagance or hidden enrichment. The allowance must reflect genuine need and service, not an opportunity to drain the estate.

Clear records and independent review protect both the orphan and an honest guardian from suspicion.

### Witnesses and Documentation

Witnesses should be present when the property is handed over. Documentation, inventories, account statements, and independent witnesses are contemporary means of fulfilling this command. They prevent disputes and make accountability visible.

A guardian who refuses transparency has reason to fear that the trust has been mishandled.

### Allah as the Reckoner

Allah is a vigilant Reckoner, *ḥasīb*. He sees the years of management, every expense, every delay, and every act of care. Even if human law fails to recover an orphan’s property, divine accounting does not fail.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:7

> For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares.

**Commentary:**

### A Right for Men and Women

This verse establishes that both men and women have legally protected shares in inheritance. In pre-Islamic Arabian practice, women and children could be excluded from property because they were not expected to fight or earn tribal power. The Qur’an rejected that injustice by recognizing women as heirs in their own right.

A woman does not inherit because a man chooses to give her something; her share is assigned by Allah.

### Little or Much

The right applies whether the estate is small or large. The value of the inheritance does not determine whether the principle matters. A poor family’s modest property is still protected, and a wealthy estate must be distributed with equal legal seriousness.

No heir may be deprived because his or her share is inconvenient, embarrassing, or considered too small to matter.

### Obligatory Shares

The shares are *farāʾiḍ*, obligatory assignments. They are not optional cultural customs that families may cancel through pressure. Later verses set out specific shares for children, parents, spouses, and certain siblings, while Islamic jurisprudence explains debts, valid bequests, and the order of distribution.

A person may make lawful gifts during life, but a post-death estate must respect the rights Allah has established.

### Women’s Independent Property

The inheritance share belongs to the woman herself. It cannot automatically be taken by her husband, brothers, father, or other relatives. She may retain it, invest it, spend it, or give it away voluntarily after receiving it.

This principle works with the Qur’anic recognition of women’s dowries and earnings as their own property.

### Family Responsibility and Justice

Inheritance is not only about wealth transfer; it preserves family rights and prevents powerful relatives from monopolising the estate. Proper distribution can support widows, children, elderly parents, and other dependants after a death.

The process must be transparent, and no heir should be coerced into waiving a share before it is known and delivered.

- Qur’an 4:7, 4:11–12, and 4:176 on the obligatory inheritance rights of men and women.
- Qur’an 4:32 on each person’s share from what they earn and inherit.
- Qur’an 2:180–182 on bequests, fairness, and preventing harm to heirs.
- Qur’an 4:4 on women’s ownership of dowries.
- Qur’an 4:127 and 89:17–19 on protecting vulnerable orphans and not devouring inheritance.
- Qur’an 2:188 and 4:29 on the prohibition of consuming wealth wrongfully.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on distributing the fixed shares and giving each heir his or her prescribed right.
- For *naṣīb*, *farīḍah*, inheritance, women’s ownership, and the rejection of pre-Islamic exclusion, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:7.
## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:8

> If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans,[174] or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly.

**Commentary:**

### Compassion at the Distribution

Inheritance distribution can place grief, wealth, and family tension in the same room. Allah instructs the heirs and administrators to remember relatives who do not receive a fixed share, as well as orphans and needy people who are present. They should be offered a provision and treated with kindness.

The command prevents a gathering from becoming a display of wealth in front of people who are vulnerable or grieving.

### Fixed Rights and Voluntary Giving

The fixed shares established by Allah must not be reduced. The provision for non-inheriting relatives and the needy is an act of generosity and social care, not a reason to confiscate the shares of lawful heirs. Heirs should give from the estate where the law and circumstances permit, or from their own wealth.

Classical jurists discuss whether this command is obligatory or strongly recommended depending on circumstances. Its ethical purpose is clear: no vulnerable person should be sent away humiliated and empty-handed when generosity is possible.

### Kind Speech Is Part of the Gift

Allah joins material provision with kind speech. A small gift delivered with contempt can wound, while respectful words can preserve dignity even when the estate cannot provide much. The family should explain honestly what can be offered and avoid promises it cannot fulfil.

### Grief and Social Responsibility

Relatives who do not inherit may still have emotional, practical, or financial needs. Orphans and the poor may be especially affected by the death. Inheritance law therefore operates within a wider ethic of *ṣadaqah*, compassion, and maintaining family ties.

A family that distributes property accurately but abandons the vulnerable has fulfilled only the letter and missed the spirit of mercy.

### Protecting the Estate

Generosity must also be responsible. Administrators should first identify debts, valid bequests, and fixed shares. A well-intentioned gift must not destroy the rights of minor heirs or dependants. Transparent advice and fair documentation help balance kindness with trust.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:9

> Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably.

**Commentary:**

### Empathy through Imagination

Allah asks guardians to imagine their own children left helpless after their death. This moral exercise turns an abstract duty into personal understanding. A guardian who would want protection, honest management, education, and kindness for his children must extend the same care to orphans entrusted to him.

The verse creates empathy without making the orphan dependent upon the guardian’s mood. The child’s rights are established by Allah whether or not the adult feels emotionally connected.

### Fear of Leaving Vulnerable Children

The reference to one’s own helpless children reminds adults of how fragile families can become through death, illness, poverty, and conflict. It encourages responsible planning, wills, guardianship arrangements, and community systems that protect children beyond the life of one caregiver.

### Taqwā in Guardianship

Guardians must be mindful of Allah because they may manage property and make decisions away from public oversight. *Taqwā* supplies an inner accountability that law alone cannot guarantee. The guardian should ask what he would defend if he were the orphan’s parent and knew Allah was watching.

### Equitable Speech

“Speak equitably” translates *qawl sadīd*: truthful, upright, appropriate, and fair speech. Guardians should communicate honestly with orphans, relatives, witnesses, and courts. They must not make false promises, conceal the estate’s value, frighten the child, or manipulate family members.

Truthful speech is especially important when the orphan is too young to challenge adult explanations.

### The Wider Principle

The verse applies to foster care, adoption-related guardianship, wills, trusteeship, and all responsibility for children without adequate protection. It also asks communities to build systems that do not rely solely on private virtue: oversight, education, safeguarding, and accessible legal remedies are part of justice.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:10

> Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell!

**Commentary:**

### The Severity of the Warning

Allah describes the unlawful consumption of an orphan’s wealth as consuming fire. The money may appear to be food, clothing, land, or profit in this life, but its spiritual reality is destructive. The image exposes what greed hides: property taken from a vulnerable child becomes a cause of punishment.

The wording “unjustly” does not make lawful management blameworthy. Guardians may spend for the orphan’s needs and may take only the reasonable provision allowed in verse 4:6 when genuinely poor.

### A Hidden Crime

Financial abuse of orphans often occurs privately. A guardian can alter documents, sell property below value, borrow without repayment, or describe personal expenses as care. Because the child may not understand the accounts, the crime can continue for years.

Allah’s warning assures the powerless that hidden injustice is still seen and warns guardians that secrecy does not remove accountability.

### Fire in the Belly

The expression connects bodily consumption with spiritual consequence. What enters the body unlawfully affects the heart and the Hereafter. It is a reminder that food, clothing, and comfort do not become pure merely because they were obtained through a legal-looking transaction.

A Muslim must examine the source of wealth as well as the amount of worship performed with it.

### Repentance Requires Restoration

A guardian who has consumed an orphan’s wealth must repent sincerely and restore the property or its value. If the child has suffered loss, compensation may be necessary. A public trust cannot be repaired through private words alone.

If direct restitution creates further harm, the guardian should seek qualified legal and religious guidance while ensuring the orphan’s rights are secured.

### The Community’s Duty

The protection of orphans cannot be left entirely to family reputation. Trustees, courts, relatives, charities, and community leaders should establish oversight and intervene when exploitation is suspected. Defending an orphan’s property is an act of worship and justice.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:11

> Allah commands you regarding your children: the share of the male will be twice that of the female.[175] If you leave only two ˹or more˺ females, their share is two-thirds of the estate. But if there is only one female, her share will be one-half. Each parent is entitled to one-sixth if you leave offspring.[176] But if you are childless and your parents are the only heirs, then your mother will receive one-third.[177] But if you leave siblings, then your mother will receive one-sixth[178]—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts.[179] ˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children, as you do not ˹fully˺ know who is more beneficial to you.[180] ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah.

Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Commentary:**

### Allah Assigns the Shares

The verse opens with Allah commanding the distribution of children’s shares. Inheritance is not left entirely to the preferences of the deceased or the strongest relatives. The fixed shares, *farāʾiḍ*, protect people who might otherwise be excluded by custom, anger, or family power.

The detailed shares must be calculated only after identifying all heirs and settling the estate according to the rules of Islamic law. This verse is a foundation, not a complete standalone calculation for every family situation.

### The Share of Sons and Daughters

Where sons and daughters inherit together, the male receives the share of two females. This distribution is connected to the financial duties placed upon men in the Qur’anic system: maintenance of wives, children, and dependants. It is not a statement that a man is more valuable or spiritually superior than a woman.

A woman’s inheritance remains hers alone. She is not required to spend it on household maintenance, while men carry corresponding financial responsibilities.

### Daughters without Sons

If the deceased leaves only two or more daughters, they collectively receive two-thirds; if there is one daughter, she receives one-half. Other heirs may receive the remainder according to the complete inheritance framework. No daughter may be excluded simply because she is female or unmarried.

Families should obtain qualified advice rather than distributing an estate from memory or custom.

### The Parents’ Shares

When the deceased leaves children, each parent receives one-sixth. If there are no children and the parents are the only heirs, the mother receives one-third, with the remaining share determined by the other applicable rules. If siblings exist in the circumstances described, the mother’s share becomes one-sixth.

The complexity of inheritance is a reason for careful documentation, not an excuse for powerful relatives to improvise.

### Debts and Bequests

The shares are applied after valid debts and bequests have been addressed. A bequest cannot be used as a weapon to deprive lawful heirs, and a person may not create artificial debts to transfer property unfairly. Classical law discusses the limits of bequests and the order in which estate obligations are settled.

The guiding principle is fulfilment without harm.

### Human Preference and Divine Wisdom

Allah says that people do not fully know which parents or children will be more beneficial to them. A person may favour one relative emotionally or assume that one heir deserves more, but human knowledge is partial. Allah knows family responsibilities, hidden needs, and long-term consequences.

### An Obligation, Not a Suggestion

Inheritance shares are an obligation from Allah. Cultural pressure, forged consent, threats, and forced waivers violate the command. Once an heir receives the right, he or she may voluntarily give some of it away, but not before coercion has been removed and the entitlement is understood.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:12

> You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless. But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless. But if you have children, then your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother’s side˺, they will each inherit one-sixth, but if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate[181]—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺.[182] ˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.

**Commentary:**

### The Spouses’ Fixed Shares

This verse establishes the inheritance shares of husbands and wives. A husband receives one-half of his wife’s estate when she leaves no child; if she leaves a child, his share is one-fourth. A wife receives one-fourth of her husband’s estate when he leaves no child; if he leaves a child, her share is one-eighth.

If a deceased man leaves more than one wife, the wives collectively share the stated quarter or eighth. The share belongs to the surviving spouse and cannot be removed through custom or family pressure.

### Marriage Creates Financial Rights

The spouse’s share recognizes the legal and emotional bond of marriage. It also protects widows and widowers at a vulnerable moment. A widow is not merely a temporary member of her husband’s family who can be sent away without property; she is an heir with a defined right.

The right applies regardless of whether the surviving spouse is wealthy. Allah’s law does not make a person’s existing resources a reason to deny the share.

### Maternal Siblings

The verse then addresses *kalālah*: a deceased person who leaves neither parents nor children, in the situation specified. A brother or sister from the mother’s side receives one-sixth; if there are more than one, they share one-third equally.

These are uterine siblings, connected through the mother. The full-sibling rules are addressed at the end of the sūrah in verse 4:176. Careful identification of relationships is therefore essential before calculating an estate.

### Debts and Bequests without Harm

The shares apply after valid debts and bequests. The instruction “without harm” forbids using a will, false debt, sham transaction, or deliberate delay to injure heirs. A person must not manipulate the estate so that a lawful heir receives less than Allah assigned.

Debts should be verified honestly, and bequests must remain within the limits recognized by Islamic law.

### Commandment from Allah

The shares are described as a commandment, not a family custom. People may think a different arrangement is more convenient, but Allah knows the needs and relationships they cannot fully see. The believer submits to the divine framework while seeking qualified advice for complex cases.

### Allah Is Forbearing

Allah is *Ḥalīm*, Most Forbearing. He gives people time to learn, repent, and correct unjust inheritance practices. His forbearance should not be mistaken for permission to delay justice indefinitely.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:13

> These ˹entitlements˺ are the limits set by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph!

**Commentary:**

### Divine Limits

The inheritance shares are called *ḥudūd Allāh*, limits set by Allah. They mark rights that human beings may not cross through greed, custom, patriarchy, family rivalry, or forged consent. A limit protects both the person inside it and the person who might otherwise be harmed by crossing it.

The command sits within a wider social system protecting orphans, women, dependants, and family members.

### Obedience in Financial Matters

Obedience to Allah and His Messenger is not limited to prayer and fasting. It includes distributing wealth honestly, paying debts, documenting estates, and giving each heir his prescribed share. A family cannot claim piety while treating inheritance law as optional.

When the details are complex, obedience requires consulting qualified scholars and legal professionals rather than relying on guesswork.

### Paradise as the Outcome

Allah promises Gardens beneath which rivers flow to those who obey. The promise gives the believer a reason to surrender personal preference when the divine ruling is inconvenient. Upholding the rights of a sister, widow, orphan, or less powerful relative may be difficult, but Allah sees that sacrifice.

### Ultimate Triumph

The ultimate triumph is *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm*: lasting salvation and honour with Allah. Inheritance wealth is temporary, while the reward for justice is eternal. A person who takes an unjust share may appear to win an estate but lose the far greater reward of obedience.

### No Cultural Override

The phrase “limits set by Allah” challenges traditions that give property only to sons, require women to surrender shares, or allow elders to decide which relatives deserve to inherit. Culture may help people administer the law, but it cannot cancel a right established by revelation.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:14

> But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and exceeds their limits will be cast into Hell, to stay there forever. And they will suffer a humiliating punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Crossing Allah’s Boundaries

This verse follows the promise of Paradise with a warning against disobeying Allah and His Messenger and crossing the limits established in the preceding laws. In context, this includes deliberately altering inheritance rights, consuming an orphan’s property, stealing a woman’s dowry, or using family power to deprive an heir.

The warning shows that financial injustice is not a minor administrative error. It can be an act of disobedience to Allah.

### Deliberate Violation and Mistake

The verse addresses wilful disobedience and persistent violation. A person who misunderstands a complex calculation, then seeks correction and restores rights, is not the same as someone who knows the ruling and deliberately cheats. Allah knows intention, knowledge, opportunity, and repentance perfectly.

This is why Muslims should avoid casually declaring particular people destined for Hell.

### Humiliation of False Ownership

The punishment is humiliating because the wrongdoer used wealth, status, or authority to appear superior. In the Hereafter, the person discovers that the property was never an absolute possession and that every hidden injustice is exposed. What was used to humiliate the weak becomes evidence against the powerful.

### A Serious Warning to Families

Families may treat inheritance disputes as private matters, but Allah treats the rights of heirs as sacred. Emotional pressure, threats of estrangement, forged signatures, and forced waivers are not made lawful by the language of family unity.

True family unity is built on justice, not on silencing the person who has been deprived.

### Repentance and Restoration

The warning should lead to immediate correction. A person who has crossed Allah’s limits must repent, return property, compensate losses, apologise where appropriate, and seek lawful resolution. Allah’s mercy remains open to the sincere, but repentance cannot be used to avoid restoring another person’s rights.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:15

> ˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves. If they testify, confine the offenders to their homes until they die or Allah ordains a ˹different˺ way for them.

**Commentary:**

### A Strict Evidentiary Standard

The verse addresses the grave offence of *zinā*, unlawful sexual intercourse, and requires four witnesses from the believing community. This is an exceptionally high standard designed to prevent suspicion, gossip, revenge, and invasions of privacy from becoming accusations.

A private suspicion, rumour, pregnancy alone, or a person’s reputation is not the four-witness proof required for a criminal conviction. Accusations carry their own severe consequences when made without evidence.

### Protection from Public Shaming

The instruction to confine the accused to their homes, in the historical legal setting of this early ruling, prevented public mob punishment and uncontrolled retaliation. It placed the matter within communal legal authority rather than giving individuals permission to attack or shame people.

No person, family, or vigilante group may enforce criminal penalties independently.

### A Transitional Ruling

Classical Qur’anic commentators explain that this was an early legal ruling and that Allah later ordained a different framework through the punishment legislation in Qur’an 24:2 and the Prophetic Sunnah. The phrase “until Allah ordains a different way” itself points to later clarification.

This progression shows that Islamic law must be studied as a connected body of revelation, not through an isolated verse detached from its later legal explanation.

### Protecting Honour

Sexual accusation can destroy a person’s marriage, family, safety, and reputation. The high evidentiary standard protects innocent people from irreversible harm. At the same time, proven sexual exploitation and abuse require serious justice and protection of victims.

A victim should not be blamed for an assault, and the evidentiary rules must not be twisted to silence reports of abuse or prevent lawful investigation.

### Repentance and Reform

The following verse addresses both parties involved in the offence and calls for repentance and reform. The Qur’anic approach combines legal restraint, moral seriousness, privacy, and the open door of repentance.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:16

> And the two among you who commit this sin—discipline them. If they repent and mend their ways, relieve them. Surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### The Companion Ruling

This verse refers to two people who commit the sexual sin mentioned in the surrounding passage. Classical exegetes discussed the precise scope of its early ruling, while the later legal framework in Qur’an 24:2 and the Sunnah clarifies the treatment of unlawful intercourse.

The passage must therefore be read with the principle of gradual legislation and the complete body of evidence.

### Discipline under Lawful Authority

“Discipline them” does not authorize relatives, neighbours, or vigilantes to inflict violence. Any legal response belongs to a legitimate judicial authority operating with evidence, due process, and protection from abuse. Private revenge and public shaming are themselves forms of wrongdoing.

### Repentance Changes the Response

If the offenders repent and mend their ways, the community is commanded to relieve them. Repentance, *tawbah*, is not merely fear of being caught; it involves stopping the sin, regretting it, seeking Allah’s forgiveness, and repairing harm. “Mending their ways” indicates sustained change.

Once sincere repentance and reform are evident, people should not continue humiliating them or using their past as a weapon.

### Mercy without Moral Confusion

Allah’s mercy does not make the sin harmless, and legal accountability does not cancel mercy. The verse holds both truths together. Sexual wrongdoing can damage individuals, families, and society, but a sinner is not beyond return.

Victims of coercion or assault are not morally equivalent to perpetrators. Justice must distinguish between consent, coercion, exploitation, and abuse.

### Privacy and Reform

The aim of discipline is protection and reform, not entertainment or revenge. Muslims are forbidden from spreading indecency and exposing people’s private faults without legitimate need. Where repentance is sincere, concealment of past sin and support for a lawful new life are forms of mercy.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:17

> Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after[184]—Allah will pardon them. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.

**Commentary:**

### The Door of Repentance

Allah announces acceptance of repentance for those who commit evil in ignorance or recklessness and then return soon afterward. The verse gives hope to sinners who recognize their wrong and do not allow shame to keep them from Allah.

The Arabic concept of *jahl* can include acting in ignorance, under passion, carelessness, or a moment of moral blindness. It does not excuse wrongdoing, but it describes the weakness through which a person disobeys.

### Repent Soon

“Soon after” does not set a human timetable measured only in hours or days. It warns against deliberate postponement. The longer a person delays, the more deeply the sin can become a habit and the heart can become less responsive.

Sincere repentance should begin as soon as the wrong is recognized. It includes stopping the sin, regretting it, seeking forgiveness, and resolving not to return. If another person was harmed, restitution and repair are required.

### Acceptance Comes from Allah

Repentance is not a transaction that forces Allah’s hand. Allah accepts it by His grace and wisdom. The servant approaches with humility, while Allah’s names *al-Tawwāb* and *al-Raḥīm* give confidence that a sincere return will not be rejected.

The verse also protects people from despair after a serious mistake.

### Knowledge and Wisdom

Allah is All-Knowing of the deed, the intention, the circumstances, the sincerity of the apology, and the reality of reform. He is All-Wise in judging repentance and its consequences. Human beings should not pretend to know the heart merely from a public display of regret, but neither should they deny every possibility of change.

### Repentance and Accountability

Forgiveness does not always remove worldly consequences or another person’s right. A thief must return stolen property, and an abuser must be prevented from further harm. Allah’s pardon and social accountability can coexist.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:18

> However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Repentance Is Not a Last-Second Strategy

The verse warns those who knowingly persist in sin and postpone repentance until death is visibly approaching. A declaration made at the point when the unseen has become certain is not the same as a life-changing return to Allah during the period of choice.

Repentance is an act of faith and transformation, not an insurance policy kept unused until the final moment.

### The Door Is Open before Death

This warning makes the present moment valuable. As long as a person has genuine capacity to choose, he can stop the sin, seek forgiveness, and reform. No one should delay because he imagines he will repent later.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah accepts repentance before the soul reaches its final stage. The exact boundary belongs to Allah’s knowledge, so a believer should never intentionally approach it.

### Persistence Hardens the Heart

Knowingly persisting in sin can normalise wrongdoing, silence conscience, and turn an occasional act into a chosen identity. The danger is not only the deed but the refusal to acknowledge that it is wrong. Repeated repentance, however, remains meaningful when the servant genuinely returns each time and does not plan to continue the sin.

### Dying upon Disbelief

The verse separately mentions those who die as disbelievers. Faith cannot be postponed beyond death. Since the Hereafter is the realm of recompense, not worldly testing, a person cannot begin a new life of belief after the final opportunity has ended.

Muslims should fear this outcome for themselves while continuing to invite others with wisdom and hope.

### Allah’s Justice and Mercy

The painful punishment is not arbitrary. Allah knows who knowingly rejected, who was confused, who was coerced, who repented sincerely, and who merely spoke words at the end. His judgement is perfectly informed and wise.

The verse balances the preceding promise of forgiveness: mercy is vast, but it must not be treated as permission to persist.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:19

> O believers! It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will[185] or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺—unless they are found guilty of adultery.[186] Treat them fairly. If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing.

**Commentary:**

### Women Are Not Inherited Property

The verse abolishes a pre-Islamic practice in which a man could treat a deceased relative’s widow as part of the estate, preventing her from leaving or compelling her to marry him or another person. Women are not property to be inherited, controlled, or transferred.

Marriage requires consent, and a widow has an independent right to decide her future.

### No Dowry Ransom

Husbands are forbidden from mistreating wives in order to force them to return some of the dowry. Emotional cruelty, deliberate neglect, confinement, threats, and financial pressure cannot be used to manufacture a “voluntary” repayment. The dowry belongs to the wife and may not be reclaimed through harassment.

The exception regarding proven adultery belongs to a serious legal and moral context. It does not permit private accusations, violence, or unilateral confiscation. Any allegation requires proper evidence and lawful authority, while victims of coercion or assault must not be treated as adulterers.

### Living Together Fairly

“Treat them fairly” translates *ʿāshirūhunna bil-maʿrūf*: live with wives according to recognized goodness, kindness, dignity, and justice. It includes respectful speech, financial support, physical safety, emotional consideration, and honouring the marital covenant.

A husband’s authority never becomes permission for abuse.

### Dislike and Patience

A spouse may dislike a quality or experience difficulty in a marriage, yet Allah may place great blessing in what the person does not immediately appreciate. Patience can allow character to mature, children to be protected, and virtues to become visible.

This is not a command to remain in a dangerous or destructive marriage. Where serious harm persists, reconciliation, mediation, separation, or divorce may be necessary. The verse challenges impulsive contempt, not the protection of victims.

### Marriage Beyond Attraction

Marriage is more than changing emotion. Attraction may fluctuate, while mercy, commitment, responsibility, and shared worship can sustain a household. A spouse should not be discarded merely because perfection is absent or because another person appears more appealing.

## Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:20

> If you desire to replace a wife with another and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺, do not take any of it back. Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully?

**Commentary:**

### The Dowry Cannot Be Reclaimed

If a husband chooses to divorce one wife and marry another, he may not take back the dowry he gave the first wife, even if it was an enormous amount. The imagery of a *qintār*, a great mass or stack of gold, emphasizes that the value does not matter. A large dowry remains the wife’s property.

Divorce does not transform a lawful gift into a debt.

### A Rhetorical Moral Challenge

The question, “Would you still take it unjustly and very sinfully?” exposes the ugliness of using marital power to reclaim wealth. The husband may feel disappointed or may prefer another relationship, but personal preference does not cancel the former wife’s rights.

A person must not convert the end of a marriage into financial revenge.

### Separation with Justice

Islam permits divorce when a marriage cannot continue, but it commands that separation be handled with *maʿrūf*: fairness, dignity, and fulfilment of obligations. The husband must settle unpaid dowry, maintenance during the waiting period where due, debts, and other rights.

The desire to remarry does not excuse delaying documents, hiding property, or threatening the former wife into surrendering her wealth.

### Gifts and Voluntary Return

If a wife voluntarily gives a gift after receiving her full right, that is a separate act. It must be genuinely voluntary and free from pressure. The husband cannot manufacture consent by making her life unbearable or portraying her lawful entitlement as disloyalty.

### Financial Justice Protects Dignity

The prohibition is not only about money. A woman who is forced to return her dowry may be left without security after the end of a marriage. Protecting her property acknowledges that she remains a moral and legal person, not a failed investment.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:4, 4:19–21 on dowry, marital conduct, and the firm covenant of marriage.
- Qur’an 2:229–231 on divorce, release with kindness, and not taking back what was given.
- Qur’an 65:1–7 on the waiting period, housing, maintenance, and avoiding harm during separation.
- Qur’an 4:29 and 2:188 on consent and the prohibition of consuming wealth wrongfully.
- Qur’an 30:21 on marriage as affection and mercy, even when a marriage later ends.
- Qur’an 24:4 on avoiding false accusations used to damage a woman’s honour.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the rules of divorce, the rights of wives, and the prohibition of taking property unjustly.
- For *qintār*, *ṭalāq*, *mahr*, *ẓulm*, *ithm*, and financial justice at divorce, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 4:20.
