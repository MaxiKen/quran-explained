## Verse 1

Verse 1 says: *O humanity! Be mindful of your Lord Who created you from a single soul, and from it He created its mate, and through both He spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah—in Whose Name you appeal to one another—and ˹honour˺ family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.*

In simple terms, the verse opens Surah An-Nisa with a direct address to all of humanity, not only to the believers: fear and remain mindful of your Lord, the One who created you from one soul, created its mate from it, and spread from the two of them countless men and women. You invoke Allah and make requests in His Name, so honour the family ties that bind you to one another. Allah is always watching over you.

The address itself is significant. The verse begins *yā ayyuhā al-nās* — "O humanity" — and this is the same universal address that opens the earliest portions of Surah Al-Baqarah (2:21). An-Nisa is a Madinan surah, and the classical sources confirm this. Ibn Kathir reports that Ibn 'Abbas, through Al-'Awfi, and also Ibn Az-Zubayr and Zayd bin Thabit, held that An-Nisa is Madinan. The reason matters: the surah was revealed while the Muslim community in Madinah was being built into a society with laws of inheritance, marriage, finance, and social justice, and its opening line frames all of those rulings inside a single theological claim — that the One who legislates for you is the One who created you. Ibn Kathir also reports Ibn Mas'ud's famous saying that there are five verses in Surat An-Nisa that he would prefer to the life of this world and everything in it: the verses on Allah not wronging anyone by the weight of an atom (4:40), on avoiding major sins (4:31), on Allah not forgiving shirk but forgiving whatever is lesser for whom He wills (4:48), on obeying the Messenger (4:64), and on seeking Allah's forgiveness (4:110). The opening verse belongs to the same register: everything that follows in the surah is anchored in the majesty and watchfulness of the Creator.

The central command is *ittaqū rabbakum* — "be mindful of your Lord." The word *taqwa*, from which the command comes, is built on the root *wa-qā-ya*, meaning to protect, to shield, or to guard oneself. A person with taqwa is not merely afraid; he is conscious of Allah in a way that shapes his conduct. Here the object of that consciousness is specified by what follows: the Lord who created you. Ibn Kathir notes that remembering the creation is itself the reason for taqwa — because the One who made you from nothing is the One whose watchfulness you cannot escape, and whose commands you cannot ignore without cost.

The description of creation is precise. Allah created humanity from *nafs wāhidah* — a single soul — and from it created its mate, then spread from the pair *rijālan kathīran wa nisā'* — many men and women. The classical commentators identify the single soul with Adam and the mate with Hawwa' (Eve). Ibn Kathir reports that Eve was created from Adam's left rib while he slept, that when Adam woke and saw her he was drawn to her and she to him, and he cites the authentic hadith recorded by Al-Bukhari and Muslim: "Woman was created from a rib. Verily, the most curved portion of the rib is its upper part, so if you try to straighten it, you will break it, but if you leave it as it is, it will remain crooked." There is a deep instruction in that image: the relationship between man and woman is not to be forced into a straightness that will break it. The creation of the mate "from" the first soul, rather than apart from it, also establishes a shared origin and a shared dignity.

The verse then anchors the second command in ordinary human practice. The phrase *tastaqsimūna bihi* appears in the translation as "in Whose Name you appeal to one another" — the usage reflected in the reading *tasā'alūna bihi*, where people said to one another, "I ask you by Allah," or, "For Allah's sake." Al-Jalalayn notes the variant readings *tatasā'alūna* and *tasā'alūna*, and explains that the verse refers to the custom of invoking Allah when making requests and pledging. The point is profound: the very Name you use to bind one another to promises is the Name of the One who created you — so be mindful of Him, and let that mindfulness show in how you treat the people for whose sake you invoke Him.

The command to honour family ties follows in the same breath. The Arabic reads *wa-al-arhām* — literally "and the wombs" or "the kinship ties" — and classical readers understood this either as an additional object of the command (fear Allah and fear kinship ties) or, on a variant reading, as an oath-like emphasis connected to the divine Name. Al-Muntakhab paraphrases it: "revere the wombs that bore you and regard them as having a divine and sacred character." The word *rahim* (womb) is the same root as *rahmah* (mercy) and *al-Rahmān* (the Most Merciful), and the classical scholars noted the connection between maintaining kinship and receiving mercy. The verse's closing word is *raqīb* — translated "ever Watchful" — meaning one who observes constantly and never overlooks. Allah is watchful over your creation, your speech, and your family bonds.

For the modern reader, the verse gathers four realities into a single opening statement. First, humanity is one family: every ethnic group, nation, and lineage descends from one soul, so racism and tribal superiority have no theological foundation. Second, the Creator is the basis of ethics: fear and mindfulness of Him precede the commands about family, money, and marriage that fill the rest of the surah. Third, kinship is sacred: the name of Allah is invoked in the very relationships of family, and severing those ties is a violation of the Name. Fourth, watchfulness is real: nothing in the home, the clan, or the community is outside the sight of Allah, and this awareness is what makes the law that follows meaningful rather than burdensome.

## Verse 2

Verse 2 says: *Give orphans their wealth ˹when they reach maturity˺, and do not exchange your worthless possessions for their valuables, nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own. For this would indeed be a great sin.*

In simple terms, the verse commands guardians to hand over the property of orphans in their care once the orphans reach maturity, forbids substituting worthless goods for the orphans' valuable ones, and forbids blending orphan wealth with one's own in a way that consumes it. Doing so is declared a great sin.

The verse follows immediately after the opening command on family ties, and the connection is deliberate. The first verse spoke of the wombs and kinship; the second speaks of the orphan, the most vulnerable member of that kinship circle. Ibn Kathir titles his commentary on the passage "Protecting the Property of the Orphans" and explains that the guardian must surrender the orphans' property in full at adolescence. The phrase in the translation, "when they reach maturity," renders the Arabic condition understood from the command and from the fuller explanation in 4:6, which specifies that the wealth is returned when the orphan reaches marriageable age and demonstrates sound judgment.

The verse names two specific corrupt practices. The first is *wa-lā tabaddalū al-khabītha bi-al-tayyib* — "do not exchange the bad for the good." Ibn Kathir reports interpretations of this from the early authorities. Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib and Az-Zuhri said it means: do not substitute a weak animal of yours for a fat animal from the orphans' herd. Ibrahim An-Nakha'i and Ad-Dahhak said: do not give something of bad quality in exchange for something of good quality. As-Suddi gives the practice a vivid picture: the caretaker would take a fat sheep from the orphan's property, put a weak sheep of his own in its place saying "a sheep for a sheep," take a good dirham and replace it with a forged one saying "a dirham for a dirham." The Qur'an forbids the whole transaction, because the guardian is not buying from the orphan: he is holding property that belongs to someone else, and any substitution is theft dressed as exchange.

The second practice is *wa-lā ta'kulū amwālahum ilā amwālikum* — "do not consume their wealth into your wealth," which the translation renders "nor cheat them by mixing their wealth with your own." Mujahid, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Muqatil bin Hayyan, As-Suddi, and Sufyan bin Husayn all explain that this refers to the guardian who mixes the orphans' money with his own and then consumes the whole — so that, as Ibn Kathir puts it, he eats up both. The prohibition is not against keeping the orphan's wealth in the same purse while fully accounting for it; it is against blurring the boundary so that the orphan's share is devoured unnoticed. This is why the classical exegetes stressed scrupulous separation and careful record-keeping for anyone entrusted with another's property.

The verse closes with *kāna ḥūban kabīran* — "this would indeed be a great sin." Ibn Kathir lists the authorities who described the sin as major and substantial: Ibn 'Abbas, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, Ibn Sirin, Qatadah, Muqatil, Ad-Dahhak, Abu Malik, Zayd bin Aslam, and Abu Sinan. The word *ḥūb* is stronger than ordinary sin; it carries the sense of a heavy, accumulated offence, and its position here is a warning to the guardian who might regard the orphan's funds as a convenient, interest-free resource.

The ruling has broad application. Classical jurists extended the principle beyond literal orphans to anyone holding another person's property in trust — the executor of a will, the administrator of a waqf, the agent of a business partner, the treasurer of a community fund. The underlying test is always the same: is the trustee treating the property as an amanah (trust) to be preserved and delivered, or as a resource to be enjoyed? The verse also teaches the ethics of substitution: replacing something of good quality for the beneficiary while taking the benefit oneself is morally equivalent to theft, whatever paperwork or verbal agreement accompanies it.

There is a spiritual dimension as well. The orphan is mentioned repeatedly in the Qur'an with commands to kindness, and the one who protects the orphan's property in this life is promised what the next verses will develop — the fire for the devourer and a reckoning for everyone. The verse does not merely prohibit theft; it builds a mindset. The believer holding the property of another should remember that Allah sees both the mixing and the motive, and that the day of the hand-over is a day of accountability.

## Verse 3

Verse 3 says: *If you fear you might fail to give orphan women their ˹due˺ rights ˹if you were to marry them˺, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you are afraid you will fail to maintain justice, then ˹content yourselves with˺ one or those ˹bondwomen˺ in your possession. This way you are less likely to commit injustice.*

In simple terms, the verse addresses a man who is the guardian of an orphan girl: if he fears that marrying her would make him fail to give her a fair dowry and her due rights, then he should marry other women instead — up to four. But if he fears he cannot maintain justice among multiple wives, then he should confine himself to one wife, or to bondwomen he possesses. The rule is designed so that injustice becomes less likely.

The verse has a precise occasion of revelation, and understanding it prevents a serious misreading. 'A'ishah, as recorded by Al-Bukhari and cited by Ibn Kathir, explained that the verse was revealed about a man who took care of a female orphan and then married her — not because he desired her, but because he wanted her money — while failing to give her an appropriate dowry. In another narration, 'A'ishah through her nephew 'Urwah bin Az-Zubayr explains: "This is about the orphan girl who lives with her guardian and shares his property. Her wealth and beauty may tempt him to marry her without giving her an adequate dowry... So such guardians were forbidden to marry such orphan girls unless they treated them justly and gave them the most suitable dowry; otherwise they were ordered to marry women besides them." When the people again asked about marrying orphan girls, Allah revealed the fuller supplement in 4:127. Ibn Kathir notes that the phrase "yet whom you desire to marry" refers to the guardian who does not desire the orphan girl because she is neither wealthy nor beautiful — and who therefore might marry her off unfairly or neglect her rights.

The permission to marry "two, three, or four" is the Quranic license for polygyny, and the classical exegetes were careful to state that these are upper limits, not recommendations. Ibn Kathir records the decisive proof from Imam Ahmad: Salim reported from his father that Ghilan bin Salamah Ath-Thaqafi had ten wives when he embraced Islam, and the Prophet told him, "Choose any four of them," divorcing the rest. Ibn Kathir reasons that had more than four been permitted, the Prophet would have allowed Ghilan to keep more than four, since they all embraced Islam with him. The collectors of the Sunan and Al-Bayhaqi recorded the hadith, with variants in how much of the story (including 'Umar's later conversation with Ghilan) was included.

The condition attached to the permission is what most commentators emphasize: *in khiftum allā tuqsitū fī al-yatāmā* — if you fear you cannot do justice with respect to the orphans — then marry others. The condition is then sharpened at the verse's end: if you fear you cannot maintain justice between wives, then one wife only, or what your right hands possess. Ibn Kathir and the majority read the permission as specifying a maximum of four, and note that perfect equality between wives is something Allah has explicitly declared impossible in 4:129 — "You will never be able to do perfect justice between wives even if it is your ardent desire" — so the justice demanded here is the justice that can be delivered: fair marriage contracts, fair dowries, fair maintenance, and fair division of time.

The crucial phrase is *adnā allā ta'ūlū* — translated "less likely to commit injustice." The root *'awla* carries the sense of leaning, deviation, or oppression. Ibn 'Abbas, 'A'ishah, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, Al-Hasan, Abu Malik, Abu Razin, An-Nakha'i, Ash-Sha'bi, Ad-Dahhak, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani, Qatadah, As-Suddi, and Muqatil bin Hayyan all explain *ta'ūlū* here as deviating from justice, as Ibn Kathir records. 'A'ishah's report is also recorded by Ibn Abi Hatim, Ibn Marduwyah, and Ibn Hibban's Sahih, with Ibn Abi Hatim noting that his father regarded the attribution to the Prophet as a mistake — it belongs to 'A'ishah herself. Al-Jalalayn paraphrases the verse in plain terms: "Marry such women as seem good to you, two, three, or four; each man may marry two, three or four but do not exceed this. And if you fear you will not be equitable in expenses and individual share, marry only one or restrict yourself to slavegirls, since these do not have the same rights as wives."

The verse is therefore not an endorsement of multiple marriage as an ideal; it is a regulated answer to a specific social problem. A man who can provide justice to one wife should not take a second; a man who cannot do justice to an orphan girl in his guardianship should not use guardianship as a route to her wealth. In contemporary application, the verse's logic is a floor of responsibility, not a ceiling of permission: polygyny is allowable only where genuine justice — financial, emotional, and legal — can be delivered, and the burden of proof rests on the man who wishes to marry more than once. The verse's closing line, meanwhile, is its lasting ethical standard: the law exists so that injustice becomes less likely, not more convenient.

## Verse 4

Verse 4 says: *Give women ˹you wed˺ their due dowries graciously. But if they waive some of it willingly, then you may enjoy it freely with a clear conscience.*

In simple terms, the verse commands that every woman a man marries must be given her full dowry as a gracious, unconditional gift — her right, not a fee or a bride-price paid to her family. If she herself, with a willing heart, waives part or all of it, then the husband may take what she waives without any guilt.

The key Arabic word is *nihlah*. Ibn Kathir records the explanations of the early authorities: 'Ali bin Abi Talhah reports from Ibn 'Abbas that *nihlah* means the dowry; Muhammad bin Ishaq reports from Az-Zuhri, from 'Urwah, from 'A'ishah, that it means "obligatory"; Muqatil, Qatadah, and Ibn Jurayj say "obligatory," and Ibn Jurayj also says "specified." Ibn Zayd explains that *nihlah* in Arabic refers to what is necessary and due, and Ibn Kathir draws the conclusion: "Allah is commanding: do not marry unless you give your wife something that is her right. No person after the Prophet is allowed to marry a woman except with the required dowry, nor by giving false promises about the dowry." The word's root carries the sense of a gift given sincerely and fully, and this is why the verse says "graciously" — the dowry is not a price paid to purchase a person, but a gift that honours the woman and gives her financial security within the marriage.

The second half of the verse guards the wife's autonomy: *fa-in ṭibna lakum ʿan shayʾin minhu nafsan* — "if they themselves, of their own pleasure, remit any part of it to you." Three conditions are implicit and were drawn out by the commentators: the waiver must be voluntary (not coerced or pressured), it must be from the wife herself (not her family or guardian), and it must be made with a contented heart (*ṭibna nafs* — "with a willing soul") rather than under duress, anger, or fear. Ibn Kathir notes that the man "must pay the dowry with a good heart just as he gives a gift," and then, if the wife remits part or all of it voluntarily, "take it, and enjoy it without fear of any harm." The verse's phrase "with a clear conscience" renders that *hanīʾan marīʾan* — enjoyment that is lawful, wholesome, and free of the bitterness that comes from taking what is not yours.

The verse establishes a set of lasting principles. First, the dowry is the wife's own property from the moment of marriage; it does not belong to her father, her brothers, or her husband, and the husbands in the pre-Islamic custom who treated the dowry as a payment to the family were legislated against. Second, the dowry must be real and specified at the time of the contract — a marriage conducted with vague promises of "later" or with no dowry at all is defective under the majority understanding, which is why the classical jurists spoke of a *mahr al-mithl* (the comparable dowry of women of similar standing) where no amount was specified. Third, the gift must be given graciously — the verse's *ʿātiyah* framing (a giving that is complete, not grudging) is a comment on attitude as much as on amount. A husband who gives a large dowry with resentment has missed the verse's spirit; one who gives a modest dowry with sincerity and honour has embodied it.

The verse also connects forward to 4:19–20, which forbid taking anything back from the dowry. The logical sequence within the surah is deliberate: first prescribe the dowry (4:4), then forbid its confiscation (4:19), then forbid reclaiming it on divorce or replacement (4:20). Money given to a wife is hers, and the only way it returns to the husband is through her own willing gift or, in specific legal circumstances, through the recognized procedure of *khul'* — the wife's own redemption of the marriage with a mutual agreement.

In modern life the verse has practical application in every direction: it protects wives in societies where the dowry is collected by the groom's family and spent on the wedding itself, leaving the bride with nothing; it protects women where marriage is treated as a commercial arrangement in which the bride price is a means to secure the match; and it sets a tone of generosity and voluntary giving within marriage — with the wife's choice at the centre. A fair financial foundation is not materialism; in the Quranic ethic it is the precondition for honour, and honour is the first thing a marriage must protect.

## Verse 5

Verse 5 says: *Do not entrust the incapable ˹among your dependants˺ with your wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you—but feed and clothe them from it, and speak to them kindly.*

In simple terms, the verse warns against handing one's wealth over to people who are not capable of managing it — children, the mentally incapacitated, women under guardianship, or anyone who is financially irresponsible. The wealth is not theirs to destroy; it is a means of support placed in the hands of the capable guardian, whose duty is to feed and clothe the dependants from it and to speak to them kindly.

The word translated "the incapable" is *al-sufahāʾ*, the plural of *safīh*, which carries a range of meanings in the classical sources. Ibn Kathir notes that Ad-Dahhak and Ibn 'Abbas explain it as referring to children and women; Ibn Mas'ud, Al-Hakam bin 'Uyaynah, Al-Hasan, and Ad-Dahhak say "women and boys"; Sa'id bin Jubayr says the orphans; Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, and Qatadah say women. The second-century jurists extended the term to anyone whose reckless judgment would dissipate capital: a spendthrift adult, a debtor whose property needs to be held in escrow until creditors are settled, or an heir prone to waste. Al-Jalalayn notes the verse applies "because of youth (for young people cannot make wise decisions), insanity, erratic behaviour, weak intellect or religious practice, and bankruptcy when debtors ask that a bankrupt's property be put in escrow."

The phrase "which Allah has made a means of support for you" is the heart of the verse's theology. The Arabic *qiyāman* (or *qiwāman*) means a support, a stand, an established means of life. Ibn Kathir reports 'Ali bin Abi Talhah from Ibn 'Abbas: do not give your wealth, which Allah made your responsibility and means of sustenance, to your wife or children; hold on to it and be the one who spends on them for clothes, food, and provision. The wealth is thus described as a *qiwām* — the pillar that holds a household and a society upright. This reframes property ownership entirely: the guardian is not a possessor for his own pleasure but a steward for the community's survival, and the command is that the pillar should remain in the hands that can support the structure.

The verse then gives the duties of the steward. First, feed and clothe the dependants from the wealth: the guardian who holds a minor's money must actually spend it on the minor's maintenance — food, clothing, medical care, education — and this spending is itself part of the preservation the command demands. Second, "speak to them kindly": the Arabic *qawlan maʿrūfan* — a statement recognized as good, fitting, and honourable. Mujahid renders it "speak to them words of kindness and justice." This is not a small instruction. The Qur'an repeatedly joins the care of vulnerable dependants with gracious speech, and the pairing here is exact: the one who controls the purse must also control the tongue. Financial provision without dignity is not the full command; a child or a widow who is fed but despised, or lectured and humiliated, is not being treated as the verse requires. The dependants' own dignity is part of the wealth's purpose.

There is also a protective logic in the verse that modern guardians should grasp. The incapacity being addressed is not primarily age but judgment: the test is whether the dependant can manage capital without destroying it. This is why the next verse commands testing the orphans "until they reach a marriageable age" and returning the wealth upon evidence of sound judgment — the incapacity is a stage, not a status. The guardian's task is therefore developmental, not possessive: he is to feed, clothe, speak kindly, and prepare the dependant for the day of hand-over, and the verse's prohibition protects the dependant from a guardian who would either exploit the wealth or surrender it carelessly.

Applied to the modern world, the verse speaks to financial trusteeship in every form: the executor of a will for a minor beneficiary, the guardian of a person with intellectual disability, the administrator of a trust fund, the treasurers of community and family resources. In each case the rule is the same — the person who cannot manage capital must not control it, and the person who controls it must use it for the dependant's benefit with kindness. The verse also corrects a common domestic error: the withdrawal of control from dependants who are competent, or the granting of control to dependants who are not. The Quranic middle path is competence-based stewardship: wealth stays with those who can keep it a support, and it is spent on those it supports — with respect, provision, and honour.

## Verse 6

Verse 6 says: *Test ˹the competence of˺ the orphans until they reach a marriageable age. Then if you feel they are capable of sound judgment, return their wealth to them. And do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up ˹to demand it˺. If the guardian is well-off, they should not take compensation; but if the guardian is poor, let them take a reasonable provision. When you give orphans back their property, call in witnesses. And sufficient is Allah as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the instruction of the previous verse: guardians must test orphans' competence until they reach the age of marriage, return their property when the orphans show sound judgment, avoid consuming it carelessly or in haste before then, and regulate the guardian's own compensation — the wealthy guardian takes nothing, the poor guardian may take a reasonable amount. The hand-over must be witnessed, and Allah stands as the final Reckoner.

The testing described is not a single examination but a period of observation. Al-Jalalayn explains that the guardians test the orphans in the duties of religion and before they can legally manage their own affairs, until the age of marrying — which al-Shafi'i fixed at the completion of fifteen years, while other jurists used the signs of puberty and maturity. The test is practical: the orphan is given small responsibilities and observed to see whether he keeps accounts, keeps promises, controls spending, and acts with sense in religious and worldly matters. Ibn Kathir frames it as the point where the guardian perceives "right judgement in matters of religion and their property."

The return of the property is conditional on *rushd* — rendered here "capable of sound judgment." *Rushd* is more than physical adulthood; it is the capacity to manage one's affairs without ruining oneself. This is why the Qur'an does not fix a single age in absolute terms: the criterion is competence, and competence is assessed. The jurists built an elaborate law on this verse — the *ḥajr* (legal restriction on disposition of property) of minors, the mentally ill, and spendthrifts, and the test of sound judgment before the restriction is lifted. The underlying principle is that guardianship exists for the ward's benefit, and the moment the ward is able to manage his own property, the guardianship must end.

The verse then forbids two destructive attitudes. First, *wa-lā taʾkulūhā isrāfan wa-bidāran* — "do not consume it wastefully and hastily." Ibn Kathir explains this as spending beyond what is needed and eating it in haste, "fearing they grow up and you will be obliged to hand it over." The verses came down precisely because some guardians were consuming orphans' property while waiting for the orphans to mature, treating the delay as a window for spending. The Qur'an names both the wastefulness (*isrāf*) and the haste (*bidār*), because the guardian's motive is often not greed for the property itself but greed for the time in which it is held. The warning is that the property is not a salary for a job the guardian never finished.

The treatment of the guardian's compensation is one of Islam's most careful social rulings: the rich guardian abstains entirely; the poor guardian may consume *bi-al-maʿrūf* — honourably, in line with the wage for his work. Al-Jalalayn explains: "Rich guardian abstains; poor guardian consumes honourably — in line with the wage for his work." The rule means the guardian must not use custody as a source of income when he does not need it; and where he is poor, his service is real work and the law permits him to take what is reasonable — not a commission or a cut, but provision in proportion to the work actually done for the ward. This is the Quranic model of trusteeship: service without exploitation, and reward without extortion.

The command to call witnesses at the hand-over — *wa-ashhidū ʿalayhim* — is presented by Al-Jalalayn as "so you are absolved of the obligation and if any dispute occurs you are able to refer to a clear proof." Ibn Kathir notes the command is intended for guidance and protection. The witnesses do not make the transaction legal; they make it demonstrable. A hand-over that leaves no trace invites false accusations and denied claims. In the same way, guardians who keep written records of property in their care are following the spirit of the verse, and the closing clause — "sufficient is Allah as a vigilant Reckoner" — reminds both sides that even where witnesses are absent or records lost, the accounting is not lost.

For modern practice the verse has clear applications. Guardians, executors, and trustees should set testing criteria and a defined age at which the ward's competency is assessed rather than clinging to control indefinitely; they should keep records precisely so that the hand-over can be witnessed and verified; they should pay themselves from their own means where they can, and only where genuinely poor take a modest, reasonable fee commensurate with work done; and they should never spend the ward's capital merely because the ward cannot yet ask for it. The whole verse is a system of checks — the ward is protected from guardians, and guardians are protected from suspicion — and at the head of the system is the Reckoner who misses nothing.

## Verse 7

Verse 7 says: *For men there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share in what their parents and close relatives leave—whether it is little or much. ˹These are˺ obligatory shares.*

In simple terms, the verse establishes that both men and women are entitled to inherit from parents and close relatives — no matter whether the estate is small or large — and that these shares are fixed and obligatory. It overturns the pre-Islamic practice in which men took everything and women and children received nothing.

The verse responds to a real historical practice. Ibn Kathir reports that the idolators used to give adult men a share of the inheritance and deprive women and children of it; Sa'id bin Jubayr and Qatadah are cited for the same point. A man's inheritance went to his male heirs — often his adult sons or brothers — while daughters, wives, mothers, and young children got nothing, on the reasoning that only men who could fight, earn, and carry the family name had a claim to property. The verse's structure answers this directly: *li-al-rijāl naṣībun mimmā taraka al-wālidāni wa-al-aqrabūn, wa-li-al-nisāʾ naṣībun...* — for men there is a share, and for women there is a share. The repetition is not accidental: it places women absolutely on the same plane of entitlement as men. Ibn Kathir explains that "everyone is equal in Allah's decision to inherit, though shares vary by degree of relationship."

The phrase "whether it is little or much" is also deliberate. *Mimmā qalla minhu aw kathur* — from what is little of it or much. The pre-Islamic Arabs and some later customs tried to distinguish: a daughter might receive a nominal share when the estate was small, or nothing when a man's male heirs were numerous. The Qur'an closes that loophole: the *naṣīb* (share) applies to every estate, regardless of size, and no estate is too small to fall under the rule. This has practical legal consequence — even a small inheritance must be divided according to the prescribed limits, and heirs cannot agree to bypass the shares on the ground that the estate is trivial.

The occasion of revelation clarifies the verse's social force. Ibn Marduwyah records from Jabir that Umm Kujjah came to the Prophet and said, "I have two daughters whose father died, and they do not own anything," and Allah revealed this verse. Maududi, following Al-Sabuni's Safwat al-Tafasir, adds the well-known tradition of Sa'd bin Ar-Rabi' after the Battle of Uhud: Sa'd was killed leaving two daughters, and his brother took the entire property, telling Sa'd's widow, "I will look after them" — meaning he kept the inheritance for himself. The two girls were left with nothing, and the situation was brought to the Prophet, who sent word that Sa'd's daughters be given two-thirds and the widow one-eighth — the ruling that later appears in 4:11 and 4:12.

The verse's final words, "obligatory shares," render *naṣīban mafrūḍan* — a share apportioned and made obligatory. The term *farḍ* is the same root as *farāʾiḍ*, the science of Islamic inheritance law, and it signals that inheritance is not a matter of the deceased's preference or the family's custom: it is a divine allocation. This is why the classical scholars, following 4:11–12, treated the fixed shares as belonging to the heirs by right and not subject to the deceased's will except within the permitted third.

The modern significance of the verse is immediate and serious. In countries where inheritance law discriminates against women, the Quranic principle stands as the criterion; in Muslim communities where daughters are pressured to sign away their shares "to keep the property in the family," the verse declares such pressure a violation of an obligatory right; and in families where the sisters' shares are treated as optional charity, the verse returns the matter to law. The verse does not say women may be given shares; it says they have shares. And the fact that the share is *mafrūḍ* — fixed — means the entitlement can be claimed, not merely hoped for.

For the reader, the verse also teaches a deeper point about the Islamic law of inheritance: the system is not a single arithmetic formula but a set of shares that vary by relationship, built on the twin principles of entitlement and responsibility. The equality announced here is equality in the right to inherit, and the different amounts set in the coming verses are a matter of that same divine allocation, balancing nearness, dependency, and responsibility in a way the reader will see as the surah unfolds.

## Verse 8

Verse 8 says: *If ˹non-inheriting˺ relatives, orphans, or the needy are present at the time of distribution, offer them a ˹small˺ provision from it and speak to them kindly.*

In simple terms, the verse commands that when an estate is being divided, relatives who are not entitled to inherit, as well as orphans and the needy who are present, should be given something from the estate and addressed with kindness. The inheritance feast is not to be an occasion that deepens the pain of those who have no share.

The occasion is vivid. Ibn Kathir reports Al-'Awfi from Ibn 'Abbas: when the poor relatives who are ineligible to inherit, and orphans and the poor, attend the division of an estate — sometimes it is substantial — their hearts "will feel eager to have a share, seeing each eligible person assuming his share; while they are desperate, yet are not given anything." The pre-Islamic system had deprived women and children entirely, and even after the Quranic shares were assigned, some categories of kin — such as a poor uncle or a married daughter's children, or distant relations — remained outside the fixed shares. When these kin watched the heirs divide the estate, the injustice of their own position was laid bare. The verse commands: give them something, and give them kind words.

The classical scholars disagreed about whether the provision is obligatory or recommended, and Ibn Kathir sets the dispute out honestly. Al-Bukhari records Ibn 'Abbas as saying the verse "was not abrogated" — meaning its instruction remains in force — and Ibn Jarir records the same Ibn 'Abbas saying it still applies and should be implemented. Mujahid, through Ibn Abi Najih via Ath-Thawri, says the share is required from those who have anything to inherit, paid from whatever portions their hearts are satisfied giving away; similar statements are reported from Ibn Mas'ud, Abu Musa, 'Abdur-Rahman bin Abi Bakr, Abu Al-'Aliyah, Ash-Sha'bi, and Al-Hasan. Ibn Sirin, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Makhul, Ibrahim An-Nakha'i, 'Ata' bin Abi Rabah, Az-Zuhri, and Yahya bin Ya'mar also said it is obligatory. Other authorities held that the verse refers to bequeathal at death, and still others considered it abrogated by 4:7's fixed shares.

The strength of the majority position lies in the verse's own language: the command *irzuqūhum minhā* — "provide for them from it" — is an imperative, and the phrase "and speak to them kind words" is added in the same imperatival form. Ibn Kathir summarizes: the poor relatives who are ineligible, the orphans, and the poor who attend the division should be given "as an act of kindness, charity, compassion and mercy." The amount is not fixed — the translation's "small provision" reflects that the giver gives according to his means and the size of the estate — but the giving itself is a requirement of the occasion. The jurists who held the verse obligatory typically fixed the amount as whatever the heirs find suitable, with no upper limit beyond the estate's thirds and no lower limit beyond a meaningful gift.

There is a social philosophy here that deserves attention. The division of an inheritance is not a private family transaction; it is a public social moment. People gather, property is displayed, and the gap between those who receive and those who do not becomes visible. In such moments the community's character is tested. The verse requires the heirs to convert the moment of division into a moment of provision and honour rather than exclusion — giving the poor and the grieving a portion, however small, and addressing them not as intruders but as people whose presence is recognized with *qawlan maʿrūfan*, words that are known to be good.

Applied today, the verse covers the distribution of estates, the division of property among children, the settlement of family businesses, and even the reading of wills in front of relatives who were left out. The heirs who divide an inheritance while a widowed mother, a poor uncle, or a disabled relative watches are being addressed by this verse. The command's spirit also reaches beyond inheritance: any occasion on which resources are distributed — zakat collection, endowments, community funds, corporate bonuses in family firms — carries the same obligation to remember those present without a share, and to speak to them with kindness rather than embarrassment.

The verse is also a guard against a wrong reading of the fixed inheritance system. Islam's shares are precise, but they were never intended to produce an atmosphere of calculation and coldness in which the poor relation, the orphan, and the needy are treated as obstacles. The Qur'an wraps the precision of law inside the warmth of mercy: give the shares, give the fixed portions, and then — because people are watching and hearts are present — give as well. Justice and generosity are not rivals in the Quranic ethic; they are two faces of the same command.

## Verse 9

Verse 9 says: *Let the guardians be as concerned ˹for the orphans˺ as they would if they were to ˹die and˺ leave ˹their own˺ helpless children behind. So let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably.*

In simple terms, the verse addresses all who supervise the affairs of orphans and vulnerable dependants: let your care for them be the care you would want for your own young children if you were the one who died and left them behind. Therefore fear Allah and speak justly — especially in advising about wills and inheritances.

The verse's force is the psychology of a dying man. Ibn Kathir records 'Ali bin Abi Talhah from Ibn 'Abbas: the verse refers to a man near death who dictates a will that harms the rightful inheritors — for example, he leaves everything or most of it to strangers or to one child, or he makes bequests within the estate in a way that strips his children. Whoever hears such a will must fear Allah and direct the dying man to what is right and fair, "being as eager to protect the inheritors of the dying man as he would be with his own." The command is addressed to the guardians and witnesses: the one who stands beside the dying man has power in that moment — the power to encourage, to redirect, to object, to witness. The verse tells him to use that power as if his own children were the ones about to be harmed.

The instruction is tied to a famous prophetic scene. The Two Sahihs record that when Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas was ill in Mecca, the Prophet visited him. Sa'd asked whether he might give two-thirds of his wealth in charity; the Prophet said no. Half? No. One-third? The Prophet said, "One-third, and even one-third is too much," and added: "You'd better leave your inheritors wealthy rather than leaving them poor, begging from others." Sa'd said he was wealthy and had only one daughter, and the Prophet's answer was clear: the primary duty of a man's property is to his own inheritors, and a bequest that leaves them destitute is not charity — it is a failure of duty. The verse before us generalizes the same principle to every guardian and every witness of a will.

The verse's language is built on reciprocity: *li-yakhsha alladhīna law tarakū min khalfihim dhurriyyatan ḍiʿāfan khāfū ʿalayhim* — "let those fear who, if they left behind weak offspring, would fear for them." The word *ḍiʿāf* means weak, helpless, vulnerable. A dying man's imagination of his own children's future is the measure against which the guardian is asked to measure every orphan he is responsible for. This is the Quranic version of the golden rule: what you would want for your own flesh and blood, do for those whom Allah has placed in your care.

Then follows the double command: *fa-li-yattaqū Allāha wa-li-yaqūlū qawlan sadīdan* — "so let them be mindful of Allah and speak equitably." The word *sadīd* means straight, correct, well-aimed. The demand is not only for truthful words but for *useful* truth — words that serve the situation, that protect the vulnerable, and that are spoken at the right moment. Mutatis mutandis, the modern application reaches the lawyer drafting a will, the witness present at a deathbed declaration, the executor, the judge deciding custody, the doctor, and the family member with the ear of an elderly parent. Each of them is asked: speak what is straight, and let the imaginary future of your own children govern your advice.

The verse also warns against a subtle selfishness — the guardian who ensures the orphan is comfortable while the will is read, but then quietly lets his own advice strip the orphan's rights; or the executor who accepts fees, watches the family divide, and says nothing. Quranic ethics here is active: the guardian is not merely forbidden from harm, he is commanded to act. "Let them be as concerned as they would if they were to die and leave their own helpless children behind" turns passivity into an offence.

Finally, the verse prepares the reader for 4:10, which follows immediately: the threat of fire for those who consume orphans' wealth. The sequence is a complete psychology of guardianship — first, in 4:2–6, the rules of property; then, in this verse, the inner state required of every witness and guardian; then the verdict for those who violate the trust. The believer is being trained at every level: the rule, the intention, and the consequence. The guardian who feels his own children's fear in the orphan's face is being formed into the kind of trustee the Qur'an can safely entrust with another's life.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 10

Verse 10 says: *Indeed, those who unjustly consume orphans’ wealth ˹in fact˺ consume nothing but fire into their bellies. And they will be burned in a blazing Hell!*

In simple terms, the verse delivers the verdict on the conduct forbidden in the previous verses: those who take the wealth of orphans unjustly are, in reality, swallowing fire into their stomachs, and they will enter a blazing fire that consumes them.

The verse's striking image is *innamā yaʾkulūna fī buṭūnihim nāran* — "they are only eating fire into their bellies." The Qur'an chooses the language of eating deliberately, because the sin is an eating — consumption of another's property. The one who devours the orphan's wealth does not simply commit a crime against property; he fills his body with the thing that will burn him from within. Ibn Kathir quotes Abu Hurayrah's report in the Two Sahihs: the Prophet said, "Avoid the seven great destructive sins" — associating partners with Allah, magic, killing the life Allah has forbidden except for just cause, consuming interest, consuming an orphan's property, turning the back and fleeing the battlefield, and accusing chaste believing women who never think of anything harmful to their chastity. The list places the consumption of an orphan's property alongside shirk, murder, and usury — among the seven that destroy the one who commits them.

The word "unjustly" is the verse's legal hinge. Not every consumption of property is forbidden: guardians may take what is owed to them; lawful debts may be collected; wages may be paid; and 4:6 allowed the poor guardian a reasonable provision from the orphan's estate. What is punished here is *ẓulm* — consuming the orphan's wealth without right, by false claim, by exchange of bad for good, by mixing and devouring, or by failing to hand it over at maturity. The verse's *ẓulm* carries the sense of placing a thing where it does not belong — the wealth that belonged to the orphan has been placed in the guardian's stomach.

Two readings of the following clause are recorded by Al-Jalalayn: *yāṣlawna* (active — they will enter) and *yuṣlawna* (passive — they will be made to enter); both lead to the same meaning, that they are delivered into the blaze and will burn. The word *saʿīr* — translated "blazing Hell" — is one of the Qur'an's words for the Fire and carries the sense of intense, sustained heat. The picture is doubled: what was swallowed in this life becomes the fire that consumes in the next, and the "eating" is matched by a "burning." In Ibn Kathir's framing, the verse warns that the consumed property "will kindle in your stomach on the Day of Resurrection," and the classical commentators expanded this from the hadiths that describe the punished person's food rising up and crying out.

The wider web of warnings in the Qur'an reinforces the severity. Zakat and inheritance law, honesty in trade, the prohibition of usury, and the repeated command to treat orphans with kindness all belong to a single system, because property in the Quranic view is an amanah laid upon human beings by the One who owns everything. The orphan, meanwhile, is singled out by the Qur'an more than any other vulnerable group — the surahs constantly join faith with the care of orphans (2:220, 4:6, 6:152, 17:34, 89:17–18, 93:9, 107:1–3), and the Prophet was himself an orphan, raised by his grandfather and uncle. The Qur'an's concern for the orphan is not sentimental; it is the test of whether a society's law protects those who have no one to protect them.

The verse also implies the rights of the estate beyond the literal orphan. The principle of "consuming what is not yours unjustly" extends to every form of wrongful appropriation: the trustee who dips into a trust, the administrator who charges what the law forbids, the relative who pressures a widow or a minor to surrender shares, the state that seizes property without right, the business partner who keeps silent while another's share is absorbed. In each case the Quranic category is the same: eating the property of another with no right, and the outcome — however delayed — is the fire.

Modern readers should feel the verse's force in two directions. For the one who holds another's property, it is a deterrent with a terrifying image. For the one whose property is held, it is assurance: the system is under divine supervision, and the unjust trustee does not escape. This is also why the verse closes the sequence that began at 4:2 — the commands to give, not to exchange, not to mix, not to consume, and to witness the hand-over, followed now by the verdict. Property law in the Qur'an is never abstract; it is a frame around the face of an orphan, and the fire that punishes the consumer is the measure of how seriously Allah takes that face.

## Verse 11

Verse 11 says: *Allah commands you regarding your children: the share of the male will be twice that of the female. If you leave only two ˹or more˺ females, their share is two-thirds of the estate. But if there is only one female, her share will be one-half. Each parent is entitled to one-sixth if you leave offspring. But if you are childless and your parents are the only heirs, then your mother will receive one-third. But if you leave siblings, then your mother will receive one-sixth—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. ˹Be fair to˺ your parents and children, as you do not ˹fully˺ know who is more beneficial to you. ˹This is˺ an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse lays down the law of inheritance for children and parents: a son receives twice the share of a daughter; two or more daughters together receive two-thirds of the estate; one daughter receives one-half; each parent receives one-sixth when there are children; if the deceased leaves no children and the parents are the only heirs, the mother receives one-third; and if the deceased leaves siblings, the mother receives one-sixth. All these shares apply after bequests and debts are fulfilled. The verse closes by reminding the heirs that they cannot fully judge who benefits them most, and that this allocation is an obligation from the All-Knowing, All-Wise Allah.

The verse is the foundation of the Islamic science of inheritance — the *farāʾiḍ* — and the classical scholars gave it enormous attention. Ibn Kathir notes that the fixed shares are derived from 4:11, 4:12, 4:176, and the hadiths that explain them, and he quotes Sufyan bin 'Uyaynah: "Knowledge of Al-Fara'id was called half of knowledge, because it affects all people." Every human being will die, and every family will face an estate; the law of inheritance is the rare legal science to which every household is a party.

The occasion of revelation is instructive. Ibn Kathir records two hadiths. The first, reported by Al-Bukhari through Jabir, concerns the death of Jabir's father: when Jabir was unconscious and the Prophet visited him with Abu Bakr and poured water on him, Jabir asked, "What do you command me to do with my money, O Allah's Messenger?" — though Ibn Kathir notes this report relates to 4:176, since Jabir had only sisters and no children or parents. The second, reported by Imam Ahmad through Jabir, is the famous case of Sa'd bin Ar-Rabi': his wife came with their two daughters after Sa'd was killed as a martyr at Uhud, complaining that their uncle had taken all the money and left nothing — "They will not be married unless they have money." The Prophet said, "Allah will decide," and after the inheritance verse was revealed he sent word: "Give two-thirds to Sa'd's two daughters and one-eighth for their mother, and whatever is left is yours" — recorded by Abu Dawud, At-Tirmidhi, and Ibn Majah. The case shows that the Quranic shares were applied directly to a real estate in the Prophet's presence.

The rule of the male receiving twice the female's share is often misunderstood. Ibn Kathir explains the underlying logic: men need money for dependants, commercial transactions, work, and obligations — the Quranic system allocates wealth according to responsibility, not dignity. The verse does not value a female at half a male; it assigns to the male twice the burden and twice the portion. Al-Jalalayn notes the practical arithmetic: if two women inherit with a male, half the property is his and half is theirs; if one female inherits with him, she has one-third and he two-thirds; if only one heir, he takes it all.

The verse also resolves the Jahiliyyah practice. Ibn Kathir records Al-Bukhari from Ibn 'Abbas outlining the old custom: property went to the offspring, and parents inherited only if named in the will — so a man's father and mother might receive nothing while a distant male cousin took everything. Allah ordained new limits: the male's double share, a sixth for each parent, an eighth or fourth for a wife, a half or fourth for a husband. Ibn 'Abbas notes that where daughters number two or more they receive two-thirds. Some early readers thought the phrase "two females" meant exactly two, but Ibn Kathir rejects this: the Sa'd hadith proves the two-thirds applies to two, and "more" extends it by the rule that what applies to two in this context also applies to more.

The parents' shares have detailed sub-rules. With children present, each parent receives one-sixth — but with a single daughter, she takes half, the parents take a sixth each, and (per Ibn Kathir's explanation of the standard ruling) the father takes an additional sixth, receiving the surplus. Where there are no children and only the parents, the mother takes one-third and the father two-thirds — twice the mother's share. Where a spouse is also present, the spouse takes half (husband) or a quarter (wife), and the mother receives one-third of the remainder; Ibn Kathir attributes this refined rule to the majority of the Companions. Where the deceased leaves siblings (brothers or sisters) at the mother's side, the mother is reduced to one-sixth, though Qatadah notes via Ibn Abi Hatim that the presence of the siblings reduces the mother while the siblings themselves do not inherit — the reduction is attributed to the father, who carries the financial responsibility of marrying them off from his own resources. Al-Jalalayn adds that *walad* includes a grandchild and *abb* a grandparent in the general reading.

The order of distribution is fixed: debts come before bequests, and bequests come before the fixed shares. Ibn Kathir states this sequence and says it is agreed upon by the predecessors and successors — the estate is first cleared of its debts, then the valid bequests are paid (within the one-third limit), then the appointed shares are distributed. The closing verse's words — "you do not fully know who is more beneficial to you" — are a rebuke to those who thought they could choose the allocation better than Allah, and a consolation to those who cannot see why a daughter receives less than a son: the wisdom is with the All-Knowing, All-Wise.

For modern readers, the verse establishes the central principle that inheritance is a divinely fixed right, not a family preference; that wealth follows responsibility rather than gender alone; that no heir may be disinherited by the deceased's will beyond the permitted third; and that the system, with its multiple rules for multiple family configurations, preserves both justice and family cohesion across generations.

## Verse 12

Verse 12 says: *You will inherit half of what your wives leave if they are childless. But if they have children, then ˹your share is˺ one-fourth of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And your wives will inherit one-fourth of what you leave if you are childless. But if you have children, then your wives will receive one-eighth of your estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts. And if a man or a woman leaves neither parents nor children but only a brother or a sister ˹from their mother’s side˺, they will each inherit one-sixth, but if they are more than one, they ˹all˺ will share one-third of the estate—after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm ˹to the heirs˺. ˹This is˺ a commandment from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.*

In simple terms, the verse completes the spouse shares and adds the rule for a person who dies without children or parents — the *kalālah* case: a single maternal brother or sister takes one-sixth; more than one share one-third equally. All shares again apply after bequests and debts, without any harm to the heirs, and the whole arrangement is declared a commandment from the All-Knowing, Most Forbearing Allah.

The spouse shares are simple but precise. A husband inherits half his wife's estate when she dies childless and one-fourth when she leaves a child. A wife (or wives, who divide the share together) inherits one-fourth of her husband's estate when he is childless and one-eighth when he leaves a child. Ibn Kathir notes the classical agreement that these rules apply to grandchildren as well as children — indeed, to descendants at every level — so *walad* in this verse is read in its comprehensive sense, a point the later jurists applied consistently to the whole inheritance system. Where there are multiple wives, the fourth or eighth is divided equally among them.

The *kalālah* case — the person who "leaves neither parents nor children" — is a genuinely technical term in Islamic inheritance law, and the verse handles it expressly. A man or woman who dies without parents or offspring has few close heirs; the verse specifies the maternal sibling shares: a single maternal brother or sister takes one-sixth; if there are more than one, they share one-third equally — and Ibn Kathir notes this concerns siblings from the same mother, corresponding to Ibn Mas'ud's reading of the verse. The maternal siblings' share — one-sixth each, one-third collective — is distinct from the shares of full and paternal siblings, which are treated separately in 4:176, where full or paternal siblings receive larger settled portions.

The phrase "after the fulfilment of bequests and debts without harm to the heirs" is one of the most important protective clauses in the entire inheritance passage. Ibn Kathir states flatly that "there is a consensus on this matter among the scholars": the debt is paid before the will, and the will is carried out before the fixed inheritance shares are distributed. The clause "without harm" (*ghayra mudārrin*) matters because the pre-Islamic and early juristic concern was that a dying man might issue a bequest that strips the heirs — or that a debt might be used as a device to defeat them. Al-Jalalayn explains "without causing any prejudice to the inheritors by bequeathing more than the third." A bequest to an heir, or a bequest exceeding one-third of the estate, is precisely the "harm" the verse excludes, and the Prophet's instruction to Sa'd bin Abi Waqqas — "one-third, and even one-third is too much" — was built on this principle. The clause is therefore both a procedural rule and a substantive limit.

The verse ends with *waṣiyyatan min Allah* — "a commandment from Allah" — emphasising that these entitlements are not judicial inventions but divine ordinance, and with two divine names: *ʿAlīm* (All-Knowing), because the shares are based on knowledge no family could assemble on its own — nearness, dependency, obligation, and need; and *Ḥalīm* (Most Forbearing), interpreted by Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn as deferring punishment while the people act. The Forbearance of Allah in the verse is connected to the fact that the law of inheritance is one the heirs might be tempted to manipulate, and Allah — who knows their motives and delays their reckoning — is nonetheless watching.

Ibn Kathir adds a crucial juristic note: the Sunna specifies that the individuals mentioned inherit provided they are not barred from inheritance by a legal impediment — murder, difference of religion, or slavery. These exclusions do not come from this verse; they come from the prophetic rulings and the consensus of the scholars, and they belong to the same system: inheritance is a right of relationship unless the relationship of the heir to the deceased has been broken by a prohibitively severe act.

For the modern reader, the verse teaches three things. First, the rights of spouses in inheritance are real and explicit — a wife is not left dependent on the goodwill of her husband's family, and a husband does not automatically take his wife's entire estate. Second, the family structure the verse contemplates is broad — it includes the childless, whose wealth must not be swallowed by relatives with no claim, and the sibling-only household, whose members receive defined rather than arbitrary shares. Third, the orderly processing of an estate matters more than its size: debts first, bequests within limits, then shares, with no harm to anyone. The All-Knowing, Most Forbearing Allah has arranged it all, and the believer's task is to carry out the arrangement rather than engineer around it.

## Verse 13

Verse 13 says: *These ˹entitlements˺ are the limits set by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger will be admitted into Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. That is the ultimate triumph!*

In simple terms, the verse declares that the inheritance rules just given — and, in the broader reading, the whole set of divine limits — are boundaries fixed by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger by respecting these limits is promised Gardens beneath which rivers flow, to remain there forever; that is the great success.

The word translated "limits" is *ḥudūd* — the plural of *ḥadd*, a boundary, limit, or demarcation. Ibn Kathir explains that the *farāʾiḍ* (the fixed inheritance shares) are Allah's set limits, allotted to heirs by degree of relation and dependency. The word *ḥudūd* is used across the Qur'an for the laws Allah has drawn around human conduct — marriage, divorce, inheritance, punishment — and the image is precise: law is a fence around what is permitted. The believer does not stand on one side of the fence and the other side open; every allocation is bounded, and every deviation — giving an heir more than his share by trickery, moving money to defeat another heir, inventing allocations to please one relative — is a transgression of the limit itself.

The verse then defines the path of obedience: *man yuṭiʿi Allāha wa-rasūlahu* — whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger. Ibn Kathir frames the obedience "regarding the inheritance" as not adding or decreasing the fixed shares by tricks and plots, but giving each heir his appointed share. The pairing of Allah and the Messenger is significant throughout the Qur'an: the Messenger is the one who explains, applies, and embodies the limits, so obeying the command is impossible in practice without the prophetic application — including the rules the revelations themselves either established or confirmed, such as the one-third bequest limit, the exclusion of murderers and disbelievers from inheritance, and the system of witness and arbitration that the Sunnah elaborated. Obedience is not a matter of preference; it is the only door the verse names.

The reward is described with the Qur'an's standard and richest imagery: *jannāt tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhār* — Gardens under which rivers flow. Ibn Kathir and the classical commentators read the rivers flowing beneath the Gardens, so that their banks and orchards are irrigated continually; the image combines rest, water, fruit, and permanence. The reward is qualified with *khālidīna fīhā* — "to stay there forever" — which is the point: the shares of this world are temporary and small, and the one who refuses to cheat an heir of a few dirhams is being offered a reward with no end. The verse calls the outcome *al-fawz al-ʿaẓīm* — "the ultimate triumph" or "the great success" — the word the Qur'an uses for the final, decisive victory, the one that cannot be reversed or contested.

The placement of the reward clause is not accidental. The inheritance verses are the most temptation-prone legislation a society has: every family estate is a miniature contest between greed and fairness, and the dying man, the heirs, the witnesses, and the executor each face an opportunity. The Qur'an counters the temptation by moving the reader's gaze from the estate to the eternal Gardens — the one who obeys here is admitted there, and the few dirhams at stake in a will become small beside rivers flowing under Paradise.

For the modern reader, the verse also clarifies the status of these rulings in the believer's framework. Inheritance law is not one of the "spiritual" teachings that may be followed in spirit while its details are negotiated away; it is *ḥudūd* — a limit with the same standing as the other divine boundaries. The family that quietly "fixes" the daughter's share downward, or that uses a debt claim to defeat an heir, is not merely being ungenerous; it is crossing a limit, and the verse's promise and threat both hang on that crossing. The believer who embraces the inheritance rules as divine limits has already chosen, in effect, between the estate and the Gardens.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 14

Verse 14 says: *But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and exceeds their limits will be cast into Hell, to stay there forever. And they will suffer a humiliating punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse sets the counterpart of the previous promise: whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and goes beyond the limits He fixed — including by manipulating, suppressing, or violating the inheritance shares — is cast into Hell forever and suffers a humiliating punishment.

The pairing with 4:13 is exact, and the structure of the two verses is a standard Quranic pattern: promise and warning, obedience and disobedience, Paradise and Hell. The verse names two failures: *yaʿṣi Allāha wa-rasūlahu* — disobedience of Allah and the Messenger — and *yataʿaddā ḥudūdahu* — exceeding His limits. Ibn Kathir notes that the one who instead changes what Allah ordained, and disputes His judgment, is punished with humiliation in the eternal, painful torment. The two clauses correspond to two possible failures: outright disobedience to the command itself, and the more subtle transgression of the boundary — giving an unapproved extra, forcing an unauthorized deduction, circumventing the shares through legal devices while outwardly performing the text.

The punishment is stated plainly: *yudkhilhu nāran khālidan fīhā* — He will admit him into a Fire, to remain there forever. Al-Jalalayn records the variant reading *nudkhilhu* ("We shall admit him") along with the standard *yudkhilhu* ("He shall admit him"), and in both cases the meaning is the same: the admittance is certain. The word *khālidan* — "eternal" — deserves care. The classical scholars understood the eternity of the Fire for those who die upon unrepented kufr and major sin and are judged accordingly; the verse's language is unconditional, and the believer's response to it is neither complacency nor despair but awe, obedience, and hope in Allah's mercy.

The verse's characterisation of the punishment as *ʿadhābun muhīn* — "a humiliating punishment" — is precise. *Muhīn* comes from the same root as *hāna* (to be lowly, contemptible); it is the punishment of being brought low, exposed, and disgraced. There is a subtle appropriateness in this for the inheritance context: the punisher of the one who humiliated the weak — the orphan whose wealth was consumed, the daughter whose share was suppressed, the widow whose rightful portion was seized — is himself humiliated. The Quranic principle of recompense is not merely retributive; it is descriptive — the one who was unjustly high in this life is brought low in the next.

The hadith of Abu Hurayrah, recorded by Imam Ahmad and cited by Ibn Kathir, gives the verse its most vivid application. The Prophet said: "A man might perform the actions of righteous people for seventy years, but when it is time to compile his will, he commits injustice. So his final work will be his worst, and he thus enters the Fire. A man might perform the deeds of evil people for seventy years, yet he is fair in his will. So his final work will be his best, and he thus enters Paradise" — and then he recited: "These are the limits (set by) Allah... a disgraceful torment." Abu Dawud records the same from Abu Hurayrah with "sixty years," and the recitation of 4:13–14 follows. At-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah also record it; At-Tirmidhi graded it Hasan Gharib. The hadith is a profound warning: the last act of a long life can undo it, and one's final decisions about a will are decisions about one's own destiny. This is exactly the verse's point — the inheritance is not merely a financial document; it is a moral event, and the hand that writes the will writes the record.

For the modern reader, the verse corrects a common slide in how inheritance is discussed. It is easy to treat family estate planning as a private matter of preference — who "deserves" more, who "needs" it, whose feelings must be managed. The Qur'an categorically refuses that framing: the shares are limits fixed by Allah, and the person who engineers an estate to defeat those limits is not managing a family but crossing a boundary. The verse also warns against the specialist's rationalization — the lawyer, accountant, or family elder who says "the law is one thing, but in practice we can arrange it differently." In the Quranic frame, there is no "in practice" beside the limit: the practice is the limit, and the outcome is the eternal Fire or the eternal Gardens.

## Verse 15

Verse 15 says: *˹As for˺ those of your women who commit illegal intercourse—call four witnesses from among yourselves. If they testify, confine the offenders to their homes until they die or Allah ordains a ˹different˺ way for them.*

In simple terms, the verse prescribes an early Islamic response to illicit intercourse by women: four witnesses from the community must testify, and if they do, the offenders are confined at home until death or until Allah provides a different ruling. That "different way" arrived with Surat An-Nur, which replaced house confinement with the prescribed punishments.

Understanding the verse requires seeing it historically. Ibn Kathir explains that at the beginning of Islam, if a woman committed adultery with sufficient proof, she was confined to her home until she died. Ibn 'Abbas states plainly: "The early ruling was confinement, until Allah sent down Surat An-Nur which abrogated that ruling with the ruling of flogging (for fornication) or stoning to death (for adultery)" — and Ibn Kathir lists similar statements from 'Ikrimah, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Al-Hasan, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani, Abu Salih, Qatadah, Zayd bin Aslam, and Ad-Dahhak, noting the matter is agreed upon among the commentators. The verses that follow (4:16) also belong to this interim period; both were superseded by the detailed rulings of Surat An-Nur.

The requirement of four witnesses is the verse's most enduring element, and it survived into the final law. The Qur'an repeatedly demands four witnesses for a charge of *zinā* (24:4 — "those who accuse chaste women then do not bring four witnesses, flog them eighty lashes"), and the point is the protection of the accused: a charge that can destroy a woman's life and a family's honour cannot rest on rumour, suspicion, or two idle observers. The witnesses must be from among the community — "from among yourselves" — meaning persons known, of upright character, and accountable in the social order, not strangers passing through. Al-Jalalayn notes the four must testify to the act itself, and the substantive law required the testimony to be direct, consistent, and unrefuted. The threshold was set deliberately high, both because of the gravity of the accusation and because the punishment, once stipulated, was itself severe.

The remedy of confinement is the interim regulation: the women who committed the offence were kept under house arrest, *ḥattā yatawaffāhunna al-mawt* — "until death overtakes them" — or *yajʿala Allāhu lahunna sabīlan* — "until Allah makes a way for them." The "way" the Qur'an leaves open is the promise of a new ruling, and the commentators identify it with the legislation of Surat An-Nur: the unmarried offender receives one hundred lashes and, in the Sunna's established practice, a year of banishment; the married offender receives one hundred lashes and stoning. Ibn Kathir records Imam Ahmad from 'Ubadah bin As-Samit, who said the Prophet declared: "Take from me: Allah has made some other way for them. The married with the married, the unmarried with the unmarried. The married gets a hundred lashes and stoning to death, while the unmarried gets a hundred lashes then banishment for a year" — recorded by Muslim and the collectors of the Sunan, with At-Tirmidhi grading it Hasan Sahih. Al-Jalalayn frames the same hadith: "Come listen to me! Come listen to me! God has now made a way out for them" (Muslim). The verse's phrase — "or Allah ordains a different way for them" — was thus fulfilled.

The verse also says "the offenders" — the plural in the translation reflecting that the confinement applied to the woman and, in the context, those involved with her; 4:16 develops the case of the two who commit the sin. The protection of the accused is reinforced by the fact that the offence described is one against both the individual and the social moral order — the Qur'an addresses it as a community matter, requiring public evidence and a public response rather than private revenge or vigilantism. The family that "handles it quietly" with harm is not the Quranic framework; the framework requires witnesses, process, and the appointed consequence.

For the modern reader, the verse carries three lasting lessons. First, the charge of immorality is a legal matter with evidentiary rules, not a social weapon — four upright witnesses, not suspicion, are the test. Second, the Qur'an's law is revealed progressively: rulings were given as the community matured, and the final law of Surat An-Nur supersedes the earlier confinement, a point the classical scholars followed in their understanding of abrogation. Third, the moral order is protected through due process: punishment follows proof, the accused is not destroyed by rumour, and the community's morality is maintained by public institutions rather than private vendettas. The verse's lasting command — four witnesses — remains the eternal standard of the law of slander that follows in Surat An-Nur.

## Verse 16

Verse 16 says: *And the two among you who commit this sin—discipline them. If they repent and mend their ways, relieve them. Surely Allah is ever Accepting of Repentance, Most Merciful.*

In simple terms, the verse continues the interim ruling of the previous verse: the two people who commit the offence are to be disciplined, but if they repent and reform their conduct, the punishment ends — because Allah accepts repentance and is most merciful.

The verse's first words are *wa-alladhāni yaʾtiyānihā minkum* — "and the two of you who commit it." The dual form indicates two persons, and the classical commentators debated the reference. Ibn Kathir reports that Mujahid said the verse was revealed about two men, "as if referring to the people of Lut" — the sin in the early Islamic period could include both heterosexual immorality and the same-sex acts of the people of Lut, which the Qur'an condemns elsewhere. Other authorities understand the verse as addressing the man and woman who commit the offence together, completing the case of 4:15. Ibn Kathir notes Ibn 'Abbas and Sa'id bin Jubayr held that the punishment at this stage included cursing, shaming, and beating with sandals — that is, a public, corrective discipline — and that this was the ruling until Allah abrogated it with the flogging or stoning of Surat An-Nur.

The word *faʾdhūhumā* — "discipline them" — is the command of the interim law. It does not mean judicial execution or imprisonment as an end in itself; the preceding verse prescribed confinement for the women, and this verse adds corrective punishment for the two offenders, with the goal of deterrence and reform. The classical discussion of *taʾdhīb* in this verse covers verbal rebuke, physical discipline within the limits the law allowed at the time, and isolation — all measures meant to restore the offender rather than to destroy him. This is why the verse's center of gravity is the next clause: *fa-in tābā wa-aṣlaḥā fa-aʿriḍū ʿanhumā* — "if they repent and mend their ways, then turn away from them."

The condition has two parts. *Tawba* — repentance — is the turning of the heart away from the sin and back to Allah, with remorse and the resolve not to return. *Iṣlāḥ* — "mending their ways" — is the outward, verifiable change: the conduct that follows the repentance, the restored behaviour in the community, the evidence that the sin is not merely regretted but abandoned. Ibn Kathir notes the verse's sequence: if the two repent and make good, leave them alone — that is, stop punishing them, stop shaming them, and let them rejoin the community. The relief is not a discount on punishment but the condition of the law: the Qur'an withholds punishment's continuation when the person who sinned has genuinely turned.

The verse closes with two of the most repeated divine names: *tawwāb* — Ever Accepting of Repentance — and *raḥīm* — Most Merciful. The pairing is consistent: the Qur'an repeatedly joins the two at the end of verses about sin and repentance, because the two names correspond to the two divine responses — the acceptance of the return and the mercy that covers the past. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn note the comfort in the verse: the repentance must be sincere and the way must change, but the door remains open, because Allah "is Ever-Accepting of repentance, Most Merciful."

For the modern reader, the verse establishes the Quranic philosophy of correction: punishment is remedial, not merely retributive; its end is the offender's reform; and the moment reform is real, the community's duty is to stop punishing. The verse also establishes a psychological and social principle: when a person repents and changes, the past is not to be endlessly wielded against him. Ibn Kathir notes the verse's mercy even amid the severity of the preceding verses — the law of the early community was stern enough to confine and discipline, yet the verse's conclusion is that the offender who returns is to be left alone. The verse also carries a warning to the self-righteous: the members of the community who punish are themselves standing where repentance is the only deliverance, and the names *tawwāb* and *raḥīm* belong to the One they are imitating in their own lives.

## Verse 17

Verse 17 says: *Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺ then repent soon after—Allah will pardon them. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.*

In simple terms, the verse sets the conditions for accepted repentance: those who sin out of ignorance or recklessness and then turn back to Allah quickly — before death — are pardoned. The verse also teaches that every sin is committed in some form of ignorance, and that the "soon after" means all of life before the soul reaches the throat.

The first condition is the state of the sinner: *alladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sūʾa bi-jahālah* — "those who commit evil in ignorance." The classical commentators are careful here, because the verse could be misread as excusing only the truly ignorant. Mujahid and others explain: "Every person who disobeys Allah by mistake, or intentionally is ignorant, until he refrains from the sin." Abu Al-'Aliyah reports that the Companions used to say: "Every sin that the servant commits, he commits out of ignorance." Ibn Kathir records the same from Qatadah, from 'Abdur-Razzaq through Ma'mar, and from Ibn Jurayj through Mujahid and 'Ata' bin Abi Rabah. Abu Salih reports from Ibn 'Abbas: "It is because of one's ignorance that he commits the error." The point is not that deliberate sin is excused — deliberate sin is also a form of ignorance, because the sinner is ignorant of the consequence and of the gravity of the act, or he knows the fact and not the meaning. The verse's *jahālah* covers both the one who does not know and the one who knows but proceeds as though he did not know.

The second condition is the timing of the repentance: *thumma yatūbūna min qarīb* — "then repent soon after." The commentators interpret "soon" in relation to death. Ibn Kathir records Ibn 'Abbas on "repent soon afterwards": "Until just before he looks at the angel of death." Ad-Dahhak says: "Everything before death is 'soon afterwards.'" Al-Hasan Al-Basri says: "Just before his last breath leaves his throat." 'Ikrimah says: "All of this life is 'soon afterwards.'" The meaning is that repentance is accepted throughout life, and the only boundary is the approach of death itself. This is why Ibn Kathir's summary of the verse is: Allah accepts the repentance of the servant who commits an error in ignorance then repents, "even just before he sees the angel who captures the soul, before his soul reaches his throat."

The prophetic hadith confirms the boundary. Ibn Kathir records Imam Ahmad from Ibn 'Umar: the Prophet said, "Allah accepts the repentance of the servant as long as the soul does not reach the throat" — collected by At-Tirmidhi, who graded it Hasan Gharib, and by Ibn Majah, who mistakenly mentioned 'Abdullah bin 'Amr; Ibn Kathir notes the correct narrator is 'Abdullah bin 'Umar bin Al-Khattab. The image is of a person in the last moments: the soul rising to the throat, the vision of the world receding, and the door of repentance closing with it. Before that moment, the door is open.

The verse then declares the outcome: *fa-ulāʾika yatūbu Allāhu ʿalayhim* — "then Allah will pardon them." The verb *yatūbu* in this construction is Allah's turning toward the servant, and it answers the servant's turning. The verse ends with *ʿAlīm* (All-Knowing) and *Ḥakīm* (All-Wise): Allah knows the sincerity of the repentance and the condition of the heart that made it, and He is wise in both accepting and deferring — no repentance is lost, no sincerity is measured by a human scale, and no one but Allah can judge the moment of the soul's return.

For the modern reader, the verse has a liberating and a sobering message. Liberating: the past does not disqualify. Every human being who has sinned — whether in ignorance, recklessness, or deliberate error — can return, and the return is accepted as long as there is life. Sobering: the boundary is real and named — death — and nobody knows when the soul will be at the throat. The verse is therefore both the greatest encouragement in the Qur'an on the subject of repentance and the clearest reminder that the time to return is now, not "later," because "later" has no name. The All-Knowing, All-Wise Allah is the One who both opens the door and knows the hour at which it closes.

## Verse 18

Verse 18 says: *However, repentance is not accepted from those who knowingly persist in sin until they start dying, and then cry, “Now I repent!” nor those who die as disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.*

In simple terms, the verse sets the two exceptions to the previous promise: the person who persists in sin until the moment of death and then repents at the brink, and the person who dies in unbelief. For both, repentance is not accepted, and a painful punishment is prepared.

The verse's first clause is *laysati al-tawbatu li-alladhīna yaʿmalūna al-sayyiʾāt ḥattā idhā ḥaḍara aḥadahum al-mawt* — "repentance is not for those who continue in evil until death comes to one of them" — and the second is "nor those who die as disbelievers." Ibn Kathir's framing: when the hope of continued living diminishes and the angel of death comes forth and the soul reaches the throat, there is no accepted repentance nor a way out. He cites the parallel verses the Qur'an uses to make the same point: 40:84 — "So when they saw Our punishment, they said: 'We believe in Allah Alone...'" — and 6:158 — "The day that some of the signs of your Lord do come, no good will it do to a person to believe then..." Ibn Kathir notes that Allah decided repentance is not accepted from the people of the earth when the sun rises from the west, the sign that brings the door closed for all.

The crucial distinction between 4:17 and 4:18 is the moment and the motive. In 4:17, the sinner repents "soon after" — while there is still life, still a future, still the possibility of proof that the turn is genuine. In 4:18, the person repents at the moment of death: no life remains to prove the turn, no future exists in which the repentance can take effect, and the confession of "Now I repent!" is not a return to Allah but a retreat from death. The classical commentators also note the element of choice: the dying person has no choice but to face the consequence, and the repentance that is forced by the approach of the inevitable is not the turning of a free heart. Al-Jalalayn explains that the repentance must come "before the last gasps of death" and that the one who repents only at death has no accepted repentance.

The second exception — "nor those who die as disbelievers" — is the verse's decisive line. Ibn Kathir notes that the person who dies upon kufr is not merely refused repentance; he is said to have died in a state that the Qur'an itself describes over and over as loss. The verse does not linger on the reasons — unbelief embraced, revelation denied, the mercy of Allah rejected — but states the result: *ulāʾika aʿtadnā lahum ʿadhāban alīman* — "for them We have prepared a painful punishment." The verb *aʿtadnā* — "We have prepared" — suggests something readied, waiting, decreed. The punishment is *alīm* — painful, severe — the same word used repeatedly in the Qur'an for the consequence of unrepented sin.

The pair of verses teaches the theology of the last moment with complete clarity. Allah's mercy is not limited by the number of sins; it is limited by life. The Quranic principle, confirmed in the hadith of Ibn 'Umar, is that the door of repentance remains open while the soul is in the body, and the door closes at the moment the soul reaches the throat. The person who has spent a lifetime sinning and returns in the last year, the last month, or the last moment before the angel comes is accepted — because Allah is *tawwāb*. But the person who waits until the deathbed and then professes is not, because the profession at that moment is not repentance; it is a reaction to the end. The difference is not measured by how long the sin lasted. It is measured by whether the turn was a return to Allah or an escape from death.

For the modern reader, the verse is a call to a practical discipline: do not postpone. The believer is to return while life is still open — today, not at death. The verse also safeguards the truthfulness of repentance: it can never be a transaction made in panic at the end. And the verse's second clause — "nor those who die as disbelievers" — is the Qur'an's warning that the mercy of Allah is not a force that operates against a person's own final choice. The door is open, and it closes at a moment nobody knows; the believer's task is to be inside while there is still time.

## Verse 19

Verse 19 says: *O believers! It is not permissible for you to inherit women against their will or mistreat them to make them return some of the dowry ˹as a ransom for divorce˺—unless they are found guilty of adultery. Treat them fairly. If you happen to dislike them, you may hate something which Allah turns into a great blessing.*

In simple terms, the verse abolishes the pre-Islamic custom whereby the male relatives of a deceased man inherited his widow as if she were property, and it forbids the husband who wishes to divorce from pressuring his wife to give back her dowry. The one permissible exception is adultery. The verse then commands fair treatment of wives and teaches that a husband may dislike something in which Allah has placed great good.

The first prohibition addresses a real historical institution. Ibn Kathir records Al-Bukhari from Ibn 'Abbas: "Before, the practice was that when a man dies, his male relatives used to have the right to do whatever they wanted with his wife. If one of them wants, he would marry her, give her in marriage, or prevent her from marriage, for they had more right to her than her own family. Thereafter, this Ayah was revealed about this practice." Al-Jalalayn gives the details of the Jahiliyyah practice: they would inherit women from kin, marry a woman without giving her a dowry, or marry her off and take the dowry for themselves, or prevent her from marrying until she gave up what she inherited or died. The verse's phrase "inherit women against their will" is therefore literal — the widow was inherited along with the estate, and the verse's opening words, *lā yaḥillu lakum* — "it is not permissible for you" — are a categorical abolition. The Quranic law returns the widow to her own person: she inherits from the estate (4:12), she chooses her own future, and her family decides for her, not the deceased's kinsmen.

The second prohibition concerns the dowry. The verse forbids a husband from mistreating his wife *li-tadhhabū bi-baʿḍi mā ātaytumūhunna* — "in order that you may take away part of what you gave her." Ibn Kathir explains: "Do not treat the woman harshly so that she gives back all or part of the dowry that she was given, or forfeits one of her rights by means of coercion and oppression." The goal is pressure — withholding affection, constant criticism, threats, neglect — until the wife, exhausted, offers to relinquish her mahr in exchange for release. The Qur'an permits a wife to give up part of her dowry voluntarily (4:4), but it forbids the husband from engineering that surrender. The recognized procedure for a wife who cannot live with her husband is *khulʿ* — a mutual, negotiated separation in which the wife redeems herself — and the verse sets its limits: the redemption must be her genuine choice, not the product of mistreatment.

The exception is stated in the translation as "unless they are found guilty of adultery." The Arabic is *illā an yaʾtīna bi-fāḥishatin mubayyina* — "unless they commit a clear immorality." Ibn Kathir lists the authorities who explain *fāḥishah* here as illicit sexual relations: Ibn Mas'ud, Ibn 'Abbas, Sa'id bin Al-Musayyib, Ash-Sha'bi, Al-Hasan Al-Basri, Muhammad bin Sirin, Sa'id bin Jubayr, Mujahid, 'Ikrimah, 'Ata' Al-Khurasani, Ad-Dahhak, Abu Qilabah, Abu Salih, As-Suddi, Zayd bin Aslam, and Sa'id bin Abi Hilal — in which case the husband may take back the dowry, and may also push for khul'. Other authorities — Ibn 'Abbas in one report, 'Ikrimah, and Ad-Dahhak — explain it as disobedience and defiance, and Ibn Jarir considers the general reading — covering adultery, disobedience, defiance, and rudeness — to be a good and reasonable interpretation. The verse's concern throughout is clear: a marriage is not a financial trap, and a woman's money and dignity are not forfeited because she is unhappy.

The verse then says *wa-ʿāshirūhunna bi-al-maʿrūf* — "and live with them honorably." Ibn Kathir explains the meaning as kind words, kindly treatment, and appealing appearance "as much as you can, just as you like the same from them," and he cites 2:228 — "they have rights similar over them to what is reasonable" — and the Prophet's words: "The best among you is he who is the best with his family. Verily, I am the best one among you with my family." The command is not sentimental; it is a full standard of marital conduct. And the verse ends with the famous counsel: *fa-in karihtumūhunna fa-ʿasā an takrahū shayʾan wa-yajʿala Allāhu fīhi khayran kathīran* — "if you dislike them, you may hate something in which Allah has placed much good." Al-Jalalayn suggests the good may be a righteous child granted through them; the broader meaning is that a husband's judgment of his wife is partial, the discomfort may conceal a blessing, and the believer is not to act on first emotions. For the modern reader, the verse is a complete charter: the wife is a person, not an inheritance; her property is hers; her release must be free; her treatment must be honourable; and the husband's feelings, however strong, are not the measure of the marriage's good.

## Verse 20

Verse 20 says: *If you desire to replace a wife with another and you have given the former ˹even˺ a stack of gold ˹as a dowry˺, do not take any of it back. Would you ˹still˺ take it unjustly and very sinfully?*

In simple terms, the verse closes the dowry legislation: a man who decides to divorce a wife and marry another may not take back anything he gave her — even if the dowry was a stack of gold. The verse ends with a rebuke: would you take it back unjustly and sinfully?

The verse begins with the situation: *wa-in aradtumū istibdāla zawjin makāna zawjin* — "and if you desire to replace a wife with another" — the case of a man who prefers a new wife and wishes to remarry. Ibn Kathir and Al-Jalalayn explain that the former wife may have been given a *qintār* — a large, abundant sum, an enormous hoard of gold — as her dowry; the verse says: take not the least bit of it back, and certainly not the whole. The Qur'an's use of the extreme example makes the point absolute: whatever was given, and however great it was, belongs permanently to the wife. The *qintār* is the largest unit of wealth the Arabic tradition named — the verse deliberately sets the bound at the furthest possible limit, so that no husband can plead "it was only a little" or "surely so much entitles me to some."

The core legal principle is established in 4:4 (the wife may waive) and 4:19 (the husband may not pressure her into waiving): the dowry belongs to the wife by right of the marriage contract. The verse before us adds the specific case of remarriage — the desire for a replacement is not a ground for reclaiming the first wife's property. The charge is stated as a rhetorical question: *a-taʾkhudhūnahu buhtānan wa-ithmnan mubīnan* — "would you take it wrongfully without a right and with a manifest sin?" Al-Jalalayn notes the interrogative "is meant as a rebuke and as a disavowal"; the verse is not asking information but condemning the act before it happens. The two words are precise. *Buhtānan* — calumny, or the wrongful taking that has no basis in right — describes the moral character of the act: a false claim, an unjust accusation, a taking that defames; *ithm* — sin — describes its religious character: it is disobedience to Allah. Al-Jalalayn notes both are accusative circumstantial qualifiers: the taking is wrongful, and the taking is sinful.

The verse's placement is strategic. The preceding verses dealt with marriage, inheritance, and the protection of women's property; this verse converges on the practical case of a husband who has given a large dowry and now wants a new wife. The message is unmistakable: a man's wish for a new marriage does not create any new right over the funds already given. The classical jurists applied this principle in detail — the husband cannot deduct from the mahr for "unpaid maintenance," cannot use the dowry as a bargaining chip in the khulʿ negotiations beyond the wife's own free offer, and cannot use the desire to remarry as a reason to pressure the current wife. Ibn Kathir frames the verse with the hadith-based rule that the dowry, once given, is the wife's property, unreachable except through her consent.

For the modern reader, the verse teaches the dignity of money given in marriage. The dowry is not a deposit to be recovered or a bride price to be refunded when the transaction changes; it is a gift with right attached — the wife owns it, controls it, and may not be stripped of it by the husband's change of heart. The verse also teaches the Quranic method of moral instruction: the rebuke is delivered as a question, so that the reader who is tempted to take back what he gave is confronted with the absurdity of his intention in his own conscience. And the verse closes the marriage and inheritance section of the surah's opening sequence with a principle that governs every financial exchange in it: what is given to a person is theirs; taking it back unjustly is both calumny and sin, and the heaviest punishment belongs to the practice of despising a woman's rights in order to fund a new preference.
