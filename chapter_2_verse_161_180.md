## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:161

> Surely those who disbelieve and die while they are disbelievers are under the curse of Allah, the angels, and all people.

**Commentary:**

### The Final Condition Is Death upon Disbelief

This āyah speaks about those who reject the truth and die while persisting in rejection. The phrase “while they are disbelievers” is decisive. It distinguishes the final state of a person from a temporary period of confusion, sin, or opposition during life. As long as a person lives, the door of repentance remains open. The verse describes the one who meets death without returning to Allah or accepting the truth that had become clear to him.

The Qur’an does not encourage Muslims to declare the final fate of particular individuals without revelation. Allah knows what knowledge reached each person, what obstacles surrounded him, what was in his heart, and how his life ended. The warning is firm as a principle, while judgement of a specific person belongs to the One who knows the unseen.

### The Meaning of the Curse

The “curse” of Allah—*laʿnah*—means exclusion from divine mercy and goodness because of deliberate rejection. It is not an emotional outburst or a human wish for harm. Allah is perfectly just, and His curse falls upon those who knowingly turn away from the purpose for which they were created and refuse the guidance that should have led them to worship Him.

The angels’ curse is their condemnation and supplication against the rejecters, while “all people” can refer to believers and to the moral witness of humanity against those who knowingly corrupt truth and spread oppression. Some exegetes also explain the expression as the condemnation of all creatures capable of expressing it, or of people in general when the truth of the matter is exposed. In every reading, the verse presents rejection of Allah as a public moral disaster, not a harmless private preference.

### Disbelief Is More Than a Moment of Doubt

The Qur’anic *kufr* literally carries the sense of covering or concealing. In theological usage it includes rejecting Allah, denying His signs, or refusing the messenger after truth has been established. It should not be carelessly applied to every person who asks a question, struggles with doubt, commits a grave sin, or belongs to a different community without having received the message in a clear and responsible way.

This distinction protects both truth and justice. Muslims affirm that faith and disbelief have real meanings, but they leave the detailed judgement of individuals to Allah and qualified scholarship. The verse warns the living to repent; it is not a licence for ordinary people to become judges of the hidden state of others.

### The Opposite of Concealed Guidance

The connection with the preceding verses is important. The prior passage condemned those who conceal clear proofs and guidance, then opened repentance to those who clarify what they had hidden. This verse shows the terrible end of a person who knowingly rejects and dies without repentance. The Qur’an thus balances warning with invitation: concealment is grave, but return is possible before death.

A Muslim should not read this only as a description of others. Every blessing of guidance creates a responsibility to preserve it, practise it, and communicate it honestly. A person may not have the authority to teach every detail, but he can avoid concealing what he knows out of embarrassment, worldly advantage, or fear of people.

### Hope before the End

The severity of the verse should produce urgency, not despair. The Prophet ﷺ sent people invitations to Islam and repentance until the end of their lives. A person who has spent years in sin can change through sincere *tawbah*. The Qur’an praises those who believe, repent, and act righteously after warning those who reject (25:70; 39:53).

For believers, the āyah is a reminder to ask for a sound ending—*ḥusn al-khātimah*. Faith is not something to take for granted while neglecting worship and repentance. The servant repeatedly renews the testimony, seeks forgiveness, and asks Allah to keep his heart firm.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:159–162 on concealed guidance, repentance, and the consequence of dying upon disbelief.
- Qur’an 3:85–91 and 4:18 on disbelief at death and the difference between immediate repentance and delayed regret.
- Qur’an 25:70 and 39:53 on the open door of repentance during life.
- Qur’an 16:106 and 49:14 on distinguishing inward faith, compelled speech, and the final knowledge of Allah.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the importance of the final deeds and seeking a good ending, while leaving individual judgement to Allah.
- For *kufr*, *laʿnah*, and the qualification “while they are disbelievers,” see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:161.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:162

> They will remain in it forever. The punishment will not be lightened for them, nor will they be given respite.

**Commentary:**

### The Consequence of Deliberate Rejection

The pronoun “it” refers to the curse and punishment described in the surrounding passage, especially the punishment of the Fire. The verse portrays the final consequence of a person who dies upon deliberate disbelief after the truth has been made clear. “Remaining forever” emphasizes that this is not a temporary purification followed by release for the one whose final state is rejection of Allah.

The Qur’an’s warning is meant to wake the living before the final decision is sealed. It is not revealed so that people may speculate about the unseen fate of individuals or take pleasure in punishment. The believer hears it with fear, humility, and concern for those who are still able to repent.

### No Lightening and No Respite

“Nor will the punishment be lightened” means that the pain will not be reduced to a tolerable inconvenience, and “nor will they be given respite” means that there will be no postponement or interval in which they can return to worldly life and repair what they rejected. The two phrases close two imagined escapes: perhaps the punishment will become bearable, or perhaps another opportunity will be granted.

In this world, Allah gives repeated opportunities through time, conscience, scripture, messengers, and reminders. At death, opportunity gives way to consequence. The Qur’an describes people asking to be returned so that they might act righteously, but their request will not be accepted (32:12; 35:37). The time to respond is now.

### Justice, Not Arbitrary Anger

The permanence of the warning must be read with the Qur’an’s insistence upon Allah’s justice. Allah does not punish people for what they could not know or for a message that never reached them in a meaningful form. He judges with perfect knowledge, and no injustice is done to anyone (18:49; 4:40). The verse concerns those who knowingly persist in kufr until death, not a simplistic category imposed without understanding circumstances.

The severity also reflects the seriousness of the object rejected. Disbelief is not merely failing a minor examination; it is refusing the Creator’s right to be worshipped and rejecting the purpose of revelation. The Qur’an joins warning with repeated evidence so that no one can claim that Allah deprived humanity of guidance.

### A Warning against Delay

People often postpone repentance by telling themselves that they will change later. The verse undermines this false security. No one controls the length of life, the circumstances of death, or the state of the heart at the final moment. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah accepts repentance until the soul reaches the throat, as reported in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī and other collections.

Delay is dangerous even when a person intends eventual reform. Sin shapes habits, and habits shape the heart. A person who repeatedly refuses a known truth may eventually lose the desire to return. The wise servant responds to the first clear reminder rather than negotiating with death.

### The Balance of Fear and Hope

Islamic teaching does not ask believers to live in terror without hope. The Qur’an places this warning beside verses about Allah’s forgiveness, mercy, and acceptance of repentance (2:160; 39:53). Fear protects against complacency; hope protects against despair. A believer fears dying upon rejection or hypocrisy and hopes that Allah will keep him firm, forgive his shortcomings, and accept his return.

The Prophet ﷺ said that no one should die except while having a good expectation of Allah, while also warning against relying upon wishful thinking without action. Hope is not an excuse to remain unchanged. It is confidence that motivates repentance and obedience.

### The Hereafter Gives Moral Weight to Life

Without final accountability, oppression and concealment could appear to succeed. The permanence of the Hereafter assures believers that reality is not measured by worldly comfort. A person may escape human courts or die honoured by society, but Allah knows the final truth. Conversely, a person who suffers for faith and dies unknown to people is not forgotten.

This belief should make Muslims more just in the present, not more eager to condemn. They leave final punishment to Allah and focus on warning with wisdom, protecting people from harm, and inviting them toward the mercy that remains available in life.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:159–162 on concealment, curse, permanence, and the urgency of repentance.
- Qur’an 4:40 and 18:49 on Allah’s perfect justice and the complete record of deeds.
- Qur’an 32:12 and 35:37 on the rejected request to return after death.
- Qur’an 39:53 and 2:160 on mercy and the acceptance of repentance before death.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī and Sunan Ibn Mājah, reports that Allah accepts repentance until the soul reaches the throat; Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on hope and fear of Allah.
- For the permanence of the punishment and the relationship between divine justice and final disbelief, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:162.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:163

> Your God is only One God. There is no deity except Him, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### The Heart of the Message

After warning about rejection, the Qur’an states the central truth upon which all guidance rests: *wa-ilāhukum ilāhun wāḥid*. Your object of worship is one God. The statement is both universal and personal. Allah is not merely one god among a collection of possible deities; He alone deserves worship, obedience, ultimate love, fear, and hope.

The declaration *lā ilāha illā Huwa* is the essence of *tawḥīd*. It negates every false object of worship and affirms Allah alone. The negation comes first—there is no deity worthy of worship—because the heart must be emptied of rivals before devotion can be purified for Allah. A person may acknowledge that Allah created the universe yet still give worship, ultimate obedience, or unseen reliance to others. Tawḥīd corrects both belief and practice.

### One Lord and One Human Purpose

Allah’s oneness means that creation has one source, one ultimate owner, and one final judge. Human beings do not belong to competing divine authorities. This truth gives moral unity to life. Prayer, charity, work, family responsibilities, public justice, and private intention can all become acts of worship when directed to Allah.

It also frees the servant from enslavement to created powers. Wealth, rulers, ancestors, social approval, ideology, and desire may influence people, but none has the right to receive the surrender owed to Allah. The believer respects people and uses worldly means without treating any of them as an independent source of benefit or harm.

### Mercy at the Centre of Divinity

The verse closes with *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*, the Entirely Merciful, the Especially Merciful. The One God is not presented as a remote abstract principle. He is the Lord whose mercy encompasses creation and whose special mercy reaches those who believe, repent, and obey. The pairing of *Raḥmān* and *Raḥīm* suggests the breadth and continuity of mercy: Allah’s general generosity sustains all, while His special mercy guides, forgives, and rewards His servants.

This is significant after the severe warnings of the preceding verses. Fear of the punishment must not lead to a distorted image of Allah as eager to condemn. His mercy is not opposed to justice. Mercy guides people before judgement, accepts repentance, multiplies good deeds, and gives every person opportunities to return. Justice places consequences where they belong; mercy opens the road to salvation.

### Tawḥīd and the Names of Allah

The Qur’an teaches Allah’s oneness through His names and attributes. He is not only the one who exists; He is the Merciful, the Knowing, the Wise, the Forgiving, and the Just. Knowing the names deepens worship. The servant calls upon *al-Raḥmān* in need, hopes in *al-Raḥīm* when repenting, and trusts that the One who commands is also the One whose wisdom and care encompass the command.

The names must be affirmed as revelation presents them, without comparing Allah to creation or denying what He has described of Himself. His mercy is real and perfect, but it is not identical to human emotion or weakness. It befits His majesty and does not make Him dependent upon anyone.

### The Declaration in Daily Life

The testimony of faith is not a phrase recited only at conversion. It is renewed whenever a Muslim resists showing off, refuses unlawful authority, abandons superstition, or chooses Allah’s pleasure over immediate gain. A person can say *lā ilāha illā Allāh* while still needing to identify hidden rivals in the heart: reputation, money, tribal pride, or the desire to control others.

The Prophet ﷺ taught that the best remembrance is *lā ilāha illā Allāh*, and he called people to make their worship sincere. The statement becomes living faith when it shapes priorities, ethics, and courage. It does not require abandoning lawful worldly responsibilities; it requires placing them beneath Allah’s authority.

### A Foundation for Human Dignity

If there is one God and all people are His servants, no human being is born with divine status and no class may claim ownership of another’s soul. Islam rejects the worship of rulers, the deification of ancestors, and the use of race or wealth as a basis for ultimate worth. Human beings differ in rights, duties, gifts, and social roles, but all stand before one Lord.

The mercy named in the verse also demands merciful conduct. Whoever claims tawḥīd while enjoying cruelty, fraud, or oppression has not understood the moral consequences of worshipping *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*. Mercy to creation does not replace devotion to Allah, but devotion to Allah must produce mercy where mercy is due.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:163–164 on Allah’s oneness, mercy, and the signs in creation.
- Qur’an 3:2, 20:8, and 59:22–24 on the testimony of divine oneness and Allah’s names.
- Qur’an 16:36 and 21:25 on the message of every messenger: worship Allah and avoid false deities.
- Qur’an 51:56 on the purpose of creation as worship.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, the hadith “The best remembrance is lā ilāha illā Allāh,” and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the rights of Allah over His servants.
- For *tawḥīd*, *ilāh*, and the meanings of *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:163.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:164

> Surely in the creation of the heavens and the earth, the alternation of night and day, the ships that sail through the sea with benefit for people, the rain Allah sends down from the sky—giving life to the earth after its death—and the creatures He has spread throughout it, and in the changing of the winds and the clouds controlled between heaven and earth are signs for people who use reason.

**Commentary:**

### Signs, Not Objects of Worship

The previous āyah declared Allah’s oneness, and this āyah presents signs of that oneness throughout creation. The word *āyāt* means signs, indications, and evidences. The heavens and earth do not merely exist as neutral scenery; their order, dependence, variety, and renewal point beyond themselves to the Creator. The Qur’an directs attention to the created world without asking people to worship nature or to treat natural processes as independent gods.

Reflection on creation is a form of *tafakkur*. It is not a replacement for revelation, nor is every personal impression a proof of doctrine. It is disciplined observation joined to humility. The more a person sees the complexity and interdependence of the world, the more he recognizes his own dependence and Allah’s wisdom.

### The Heavens and the Earth

The creation of the heavens and the earth encompasses realities far beyond human control. The vastness of the sky, the stability of the earth, and the conditions that make life possible all expose human limitation. People may discover laws and develop technologies, but they do not create the underlying existence of matter, time, life, or consciousness from nothing.

The Qur’an’s purpose is not to provide a technical textbook. It invites the mind to ask the ultimate questions: Who originated this order? Why are its patterns intelligible? Why does life depend upon such a delicate network of causes? Scientific investigation can describe mechanisms, and that description can itself become a means of appreciating the wisdom of the Creator. The verse resists both superstition and the arrogance that mistakes description for ultimate explanation.

### Night and Day

The alternation of night and day regulates rest, work, seasons, worship, and the rhythms of life. It is constant enough for human planning yet varied enough across regions and seasons to reveal the earth’s movement and changing conditions. The darkness of night can offer rest and reflection; the light of day enables activity and provision. Both are gifts, and neither should be treated as an accident without meaning.

The Qur’an uses this alternation elsewhere as a sign of Allah’s power over resurrection (36:37–40). A world that renews itself each morning after night makes the return of bodies less difficult to imagine. The believer does not confuse analogy with proof, but he recognizes that the One who brings light after darkness can bring life after death.

### Ships and Human Benefit

Ships sail through the sea carrying people, food, knowledge, and goods. Their movement depends on the properties of water, wind, buoyancy, human design, and Allah’s permission. The verse acknowledges human benefit and ingenuity without allowing people to forget the Creator of the materials, minds, and natural laws that make navigation possible.

The sea can also humble human beings. A vessel that seems powerful becomes vulnerable in a storm, and sailors instinctively call upon Allah when familiar means fail (17:66–69). Maritime trade should therefore be accompanied by gratitude and justice, not exploitation, piracy, or the enrichment of a few through the suffering of distant communities.

### Rain and the Revival of Earth

Rain falls, enters the soil, feeds rivers and roots, and brings dead-looking land back to life. The phrase “after its death” is both a description of the earth and a sign of resurrection. Seeds hidden in dry ground emerge, multiply, and produce nourishment. The process is regular enough to be studied but wondrous enough never to be exhausted by description.

Water is also a moral reminder. A blessing that sustains every creature should not be hoarded, polluted, or wasted. The Prophet ﷺ prohibited extravagance even while using water for ablution, as reported in Sunan Ibn Mājah and other collections. Stewardship of the earth is part of gratitude to the One who sends its rain.

### Living Creatures, Winds, and Clouds

Allah spread throughout the earth creatures of countless forms, capacities, and relationships. Their diversity invites knowledge and care. Humans are not permitted to treat animals as meaningless resources; the Qur’an describes animal communities as communities like humanity (6:38), and the Prophet ﷺ warned against cruelty and praised mercy to living beings.

The changing winds carry heat, cold, clouds, seeds, and rain. Clouds are controlled between heaven and earth, gathering and dispersing according to patterns that sustain life. The Qur’an draws attention to movement and balance: wind can bring mercy, but it can also become destructive. Human beings are reminded that power and benefit are in Allah’s hand and that creation is not theirs to abuse.

### Reason Guided by Revelation

The signs are “for people who use reason”—*yaʿqilūn*. The Qur’an honours intellect, but it calls for an intellect willing to follow evidence and moral truth. *ʿAql* is not merely cleverness or technical skill. It is the capacity to restrain the self, connect signs to their meaning, and act wisely. A person can be highly educated yet fail to understand if pride prevents him from recognizing the Creator.

The correct method joins observation, reflection, revelation, and gratitude. Creation points to Allah, and revelation teaches how to worship Him and use His creation justly. This produces a science of humility rather than a culture of exploitation.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:163–164 on divine oneness and the signs of the Creator.
- Qur’an 3:190–191, 16:10–13, and 30:20–25 on reflection upon creation and the signs in natural phenomena.
- Qur’an 17:66–69 on ships and Allah’s control of the sea; Qur’an 6:38 on animal communities.
- Qur’an 21:30 and 36:33–40 on water, revival, and the sign of resurrection.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the Prophet’s supplications during rain and wind; Sunan Ibn Mājah and other collections on avoiding extravagance with water.
- For *āyāt*, *tafakkur*, and the relationship between natural signs and the proof of tawḥīd, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:164.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:165

> Yet among the people are those who take rivals besides Allah, loving them as they should love Allah. But the believers are stronger in their love for Allah. If only the wrongdoers could see, when they see the punishment, that all power belongs to Allah and that Allah is severe in punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Rivals in Love and Worship

After displaying the signs of Allah’s oneness, the Qur’an describes people who still take *andād*, rivals or equals, besides Him. A rival may be a physical idol, a deified person, a spirit, an ideology, wealth, power, or any created thing given the devotion and obedience that belong exclusively to Allah. The central issue is not simply affection. It is a form of love that includes worship, ultimate submission, fear, hope, and the belief that the rival possesses independent power.

Human beings naturally love parents, spouses, children, friends, homeland, beauty, and knowledge. Islam does not demand that these loves disappear. It orders them beneath love of Allah. When a created love agrees with obedience to Allah, it becomes a blessing. When it makes a person reject Allah, violate rights, or sacrifice truth, it has become a rival.

### The Believers Are Stronger in Love

The believers are “stronger in love for Allah”—*ashaddu ḥubban lillāh*. Their love is not a momentary emotion. It is rooted in recognizing Allah’s perfection, mercy, lordship, and countless gifts. It appears in obedience when desire pulls in another direction, in gratitude during ease, patience during hardship, repentance after sin, and preference for truth over applause.

Love of Allah produces both intimacy and awe. The believer hopes in mercy, fears displeasing Him, longs to meet Him, and trusts His wisdom. The Qur’an describes love as a mark of faith and says that those who believe are more intense in love for Allah because no rival divides the centre of their devotion.

### The Test of Competing Loves

A person can examine his hierarchy of love through choices. What does he protect when truth and reputation collide? What does he obey when lawful income conflicts with profit? Whose approval determines his clothing, speech, friendships, or public positions? The answers reveal whether Allah is loved above all or merely included among other priorities.

The Prophet ﷺ said that none truly believes until he loves the Messenger more than his parent, child, and all people, and another authentic hadith describes the sweetness of faith when Allah and His Messenger are more beloved than everything else. This does not make the Prophet an object of worship. It means loving him as Allah’s messenger and following him because of Allah.

### The Vision of the Wrongdoers

The verse suddenly turns toward the Day of Punishment: “If only the wrongdoers could see.” Their false hierarchy will then be exposed. They will see that all power—*al-quwwah jamīʿan*—belongs to Allah, while the rivals they obeyed possess no independent control. The phrase is a picture of regret: the truth becomes undeniable only after the opportunity to act upon it has ended.

In this world, power may appear to belong to rulers, armies, money, or public opinion. These are created means, and Allah may allow them influence for a time. But no means operates outside His knowledge and permission. On the Day of Resurrection, the illusion of independent power disappears, and every false allegiance is seen in its true weakness.

### Love and Justice

The verse links shirk with wrongdoing—*al-ẓulm*. Giving worship to other than Allah is the greatest injustice because it places a created thing in the position of the Creator. It also creates secondary injustices: people may obey idols of tribe, race, nation, or leader and then oppress others to protect them.

Pure love of Allah, by contrast, disciplines power. The servant knows that he will answer for how he treats people and creation. He can love his family deeply without owning them, love his homeland without worshipping nationalism, and respect a leader without obeying him in sin.

### Cultivating Love of Allah

Love grows through knowing Allah’s names, reciting and reflecting upon the Qur’an, remembering blessings, performing obligatory worship, engaging in voluntary deeds, and observing the effects of obedience on the heart. The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah’s servant draws near through obligations and then through voluntary works until Allah loves him, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī.

Love also grows when a believer notices Allah’s mercy in forgiveness and guidance. It is not built only through fear. The names *al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm* in the preceding verse show that the One who deserves ultimate love is the One whose mercy surrounds His servants.

- Qur’an 9:24 on loving Allah and His Messenger above family, wealth, and other attachments.
- Qur’an 31:13 on shirk as the greatest injustice, and 18:42 on regret over misplaced reliance.
- Qur’an 39:67 and 40:16 on Allah’s absolute power on the Day of Resurrection.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on the sweetness of faith and the hadith qudsī on drawing near through obligations and voluntary deeds.
- For *andād*, *maḥabbah*, and the connection between shirk and *ẓulm*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:165.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:166

> When those who were followed disown those who followed, and they see the punishment, and all ties between them are cut off.

**Commentary:**

### The Collapse of False Leadership

The verse describes a scene from the Hereafter in which followers and leaders discover that their worldly relationship cannot save them. Those who were followed—rulers, religious figures, celebrities, ancestors, ideologues, or charismatic personalities—disown those who followed them. The followers had treated created beings as authorities deserving unquestioning loyalty, but the leaders had no power to carry their burdens before Allah.

The verse does not condemn every form of following. Muslims follow prophets, qualified scholars, parents in what is right, and legitimate authority within the limits of Allah’s law. The condemned following is blind allegiance that continues in falsehood after evidence has arrived, or obedience to a person in disobedience to Allah.

### Disowning at the Moment of Truth

The leaders’ disavowal is not necessarily evidence that every leader was innocent. Some were themselves deceivers who encouraged the following for status or gain. Others may have been worshipped or obeyed beyond what they claimed. The scene reveals that the relationship was based on mutual illusion: followers expected rescue from leaders, and leaders enjoyed influence without accepting responsibility for the consequences.

The Qur’an elsewhere records Iblīs saying that he had no authority except that he called and people responded (14:22). His statement does not absolve him from deception, but it exposes the followers’ responsibility to choose. No created being can force the heart to worship. Both the deceiver and the willing follower face the consequences of their own choices.

### When Punishment Is Seen

The phrase “and they see the punishment” marks the end of argument and denial. In this world, false alliances can be protected by wealth, propaganda, family pressure, and emotional attachment. In the Hereafter, the reality is visible. The followers see that the power they feared was temporary, the leader they praised was helpless, and the obedience they offered did not produce salvation.

The Qur’an repeatedly describes this moment so that people can break false allegiances before death. A servant should ask now whether his loyalty is based on truth or on the fear of losing a group. The ability to change one’s allegiance is a mercy available in the world; in the Hereafter, regret cannot create a new test.

### The Cutting of Ties

“All ties between them are cut off” refers to the causes of relationship that once united them—*asbāb*. It may include claims of kinship, political alliances, shared ideology, promises of intercession, and mutual support in wrongdoing. The Qur’an does not mean that every family relationship ceases to have any emotional memory; it means that no worldly tie can function as a means of rescue when it was not established upon faith and righteousness.

This is not a reason to neglect family or community. Islam commands maintaining kinship and honouring lawful commitments. It is a warning against assuming that blood, nationality, membership, or proximity to a famous person automatically transfers spiritual benefit. The useful relationship is one that encourages truth and obedience.

### The Responsibility of Followers

Followers often say, “I was only obeying my leader,” but the Qur’an rejects this as a complete excuse. The Prophet ﷺ taught that there is no obedience to a creature in disobedience to the Creator. A person must use discernment, ask for evidence, and refuse commands that clearly violate Allah’s law. Respect for elders or scholars does not mean treating them as infallible.

This responsibility is especially important in movements that demand absolute loyalty, silence criticism, or portray outsiders as less than human. A Muslim may cooperate in good but must retain conscience, justice, and accountability before Allah. No group identity can cancel individual responsibility.

### Leadership Is an Amānah

Those who lead others carry an even heavier trust. The Prophet ﷺ said that every person is a shepherd and every person is responsible for his flock, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. A leader cannot wait until the Hereafter to claim that followers misunderstood him if he knowingly encouraged falsehood or exploited their devotion.

Religious leaders should teach tawḥīd rather than make themselves indispensable. Political and community leaders should welcome accountability rather than demand worshipful obedience. The best leadership directs people to Allah; the worst leadership makes itself the centre of their fear and hope.

- Qur’an 28:63 and 33:66–68 on false leaders and those who obeyed them without discernment.
- Qur’an 43:67 on close friends becoming enemies except those united by taqwā.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith “Each of you is a shepherd and each of you is responsible for his flock,” and the principle that obedience is not given in sin.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:167

> Those who followed will say, “If only we had another chance, we would disown them as they have disowned us.” Thus Allah will show them their deeds as regrets, and they will never leave the Fire.

**Commentary:**

### Regret without a Second Trial

The followers respond to the leaders’ disavowal with a wish for another opportunity: “If only we had another chance.” They now recognize that the leaders could not help them and that their obedience was misplaced. They promise to reject those leaders just as the leaders have rejected them. But the wish comes after the test has ended.

The Qur’an repeatedly presents such regret to teach the living. Regret is useful when it leads to repentance and change, but regret after death cannot create new deeds. A wise person listens to the warning before the consequences are visible. He examines the people he follows, the assumptions he inherits, and the desires that govern his decisions while correction remains possible.

### The Consequence of Blind Following

The followers’ words imply an admission of responsibility. They do not simply say, “Our leaders ruined us”; they say that if returned, they would disown them. This shows that they know they participated in the alliance. They chose to follow, defended the leaders, and perhaps benefited from the false system. A person cannot surrender his moral judgement to another and then expect to be free of accountability.

This does not deny that leaders can manipulate, threaten, or deceive. Allah judges coercion, knowledge, capacity, and influence perfectly. But ordinary claims of “I was only following orders” do not excuse a person who could recognize and refuse wrongdoing. The Qur’an asks people to use hearing, sight, and reason rather than surrendering their conscience (67:10).

### Deeds Become Sources of Regret

“Allah will show them their deeds as regrets.” The phrase suggests that the very actions in which they once found pride, pleasure, or belonging will become objects of pain. Wealth gained through injustice, loyalty offered to falsehood, time wasted in mockery, and opportunities for good deliberately abandoned will all appear in their true form.

The Qur’an says that a person will see what he sent ahead (59:18; 81:14). Deeds are not erased by death or hidden behind public reputation. When the curtain lifts, the person sees how every choice shaped his final state. This is why sincere repentance is a gift: it allows a person to erase the path of regret before it is displayed.

### The Fire and the Finality of Consequence

“They will never leave the Fire” describes the final punishment of those who die upon disbelief and persist in the allegiance to falsehood described in the passage. The permanence is not a denial of Allah’s mercy; it is the consequence of meeting Allah in a state that rejects Him. Muslims affirm the warning while entrusting the detailed judgement of individuals to Allah’s perfect knowledge and justice.

The verse should produce seriousness about the Hereafter, not a culture of cruelty. The Prophet ﷺ grieved over people’s rejection and sought ways to guide them. Believers warn because they want people saved, and they invite through wisdom, mercy, and clear evidence rather than celebrating another person’s destruction.

### Returning to the Present

The phrase “if only we had another chance” should be transformed into action now. A person who recognizes a harmful influence can leave it. Someone who followed a false opinion can seek sound knowledge. A community that has defended injustice can acknowledge it, repair the harm, and change its structures. Repentance is not merely feeling ashamed; it is breaking the alliance with falsehood before it becomes permanent.

The believer should also remember that influence is reciprocal. A follower can strengthen a leader by praise, money, votes, silence, or repeated sharing of harmful speech. Refusing to participate in falsehood is a form of worship. Supporting a good leader is likewise a good deed when the support remains within truth and accountability.

- Qur’an 67:10 and 33:66–68 on reason, obedience, and regret for following people without discernment.
- Qur’an 18:49, 59:18, and 81:14 on deeds being displayed and their consequences becoming clear.
- Qur’an 39:58 and 63:10–11 on regret at death and the impossibility of delaying the appointed term.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on every person’s responsibility for those under his care and the hadith on helping or hindering others through established good or evil practices.
- For *ḥasrah*, *tabarruʾ*, and the meaning of deeds appearing as regret, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:167.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:168

> O humanity, eat from what is lawful and good on the earth, and do not follow the footsteps of Satan. Surely he is a clear enemy to you.

**Commentary:**

### A Universal Invitation

After describing false allegiance and the regret of those who followed leaders in error, Allah addresses all humanity. The command to eat *ḥalālan ṭayyiban*—what is lawful and wholesome—shows that Islam’s guidance concerns ordinary life as well as formal worship. Food sustains the body, shapes habits, affects family life and commerce, and becomes part of the servant’s relationship with Allah.

The address is universal, not limited to Muslims. Allah’s provision is made available to humanity, and everyone is called to recognize its lawful limits and its moral source. For believers, the command is also a test of gratitude: they do not consume whatever desire or market power can obtain, but ask whether it is lawful, wholesome, and acquired without injustice.

### Ḥalāl and Ṭayyib

*Ḥalāl* means permitted by the Sharīʿah, while *ṭayyib* includes what is wholesome, clean, beneficial, and suitable. The two terms are related but not identical. Food may be technically permissible in its ingredients yet harmful through contamination, abuse, or an unlawful method of acquisition. Wealth may purchase a lawful product while the money used to buy it came from theft, fraud, bribery, or exploitation.

The command therefore includes the source, substance, preparation, and effect of consumption. Muslims examine labels and ingredients where needed, avoid intoxicants and harmful substances, and seek trustworthy information without falling into obsessive doubt. They also remember that lawful food does not excuse waste. The Qur’an commands eating and drinking without extravagance (7:31).

### Do Not Follow Satan’s Footsteps

Allah does not merely say, “Do not follow Satan”; He says, “Do not follow his footsteps”—*khuṭuwāt al-shayṭān*. Evil often develops gradually. A person begins with a doubtful transaction, normalizes a small deception, accepts a gift that influences his judgement, and eventually consumes wealth or food known to be forbidden. Satan invites through incremental steps so that the destination is hidden behind ordinary choices.

The same pattern appears in speech, dress, relationships, and ideology. The believer watches the path, not only the final act. He avoids environments and habits that repeatedly pull him toward sin, and he seeks lawful alternatives before temptation becomes dominant.

### Satan’s Enmity Is Clear

Satan’s enmity is clear because he seeks to corrupt the human relationship with Allah. He wants people to treat blessings as independent possessions, to consume without gratitude, and to define right and wrong according to desire. His promises are decorated with convenience and freedom, but they end in enslavement to appetite, debt, addiction, and regret.

The Qur’an says Satan commands obscenity and false speech about Allah (2:169). The next verse explains how food restrictions and religious claims can be manipulated. People may declare lawful what Allah forbade or forbid what Allah allowed, then attribute their preferences to revelation. This is one of Satan’s most dangerous steps: turning desire into theology.

### Consumption and Spiritual Health

The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah is good and accepts only what is good, then mentioned the person who travels, raises his hands in supplication, but whose food, drink, clothing, and nourishment are unlawful; how can his supplication be answered? This hadith, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, connects lawful consumption with the health of worship.

The point is not that every hardship in provision is proof that a prayer is rejected. Allah answers supplication in His wisdom. It is a warning that unlawful income and consumption can place barriers before spiritual life. A believer who wants a responsive heart should care about contracts, wages, ingredients, and the rights of producers and workers.

### Gratitude and Responsible Stewardship

Food is a gift from Allah, even when it reaches the table through human labour. Gratitude includes saying Allah’s name, avoiding waste, sharing with others, and recognizing the people and land involved in production. The Prophet ﷺ taught moderation and encouraged sharing food. The Qur’an condemns those who hoard or consume wealth unjustly.

A community guided by this verse supports food security, honest trade, humane treatment of animals, and access to nutritious food for the poor. Individual piety cannot be separated from the economic conditions that determine whether people can obtain wholesome provision.

- Qur’an 2:168–173 on lawful provision, Satan’s steps, and specific dietary prohibitions.
- Qur’an 5:87–88 and 7:31 on enjoying lawful good things without inventing prohibitions or practising waste.
- Qur’an 16:114–116 on gratitude and not declaring lawful and unlawful without knowledge.
- Qur’an 23:51 on eating good things and acting righteously.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith “Allah is good and accepts only what is good,” and the report concerning the supplication of one whose food, drink, clothing, and nourishment are unlawful.
- For *ḥalāl*, *ṭayyib*, *khuṭuwāt*, and the moral scope of consumption, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:168.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:169

> He only commands you to evil and immorality, and to say about Allah what you do not know.

**Commentary:**

### Satan’s Two Great Invitations

This verse explains Satan’s footsteps. He commands *sūʾ*, evil in general, and *faḥshāʾ*, obscene or gross immorality. He also commands people to say about Allah what they do not know. The first corrupts conduct; the second corrupts belief and law. Together they destroy both the servant’s relationship with Allah and his relationships with people.

Satan’s command is an invitation, not a power that compels. He whispers, beautifies, exaggerates, and presents sin as freedom, but he cannot force the person who seeks refuge in Allah and uses his will responsibly. On the Day of Resurrection, Satan will say that he had no authority except that he called and people responded (14:22). This is why the Qur’an holds human beings accountable while warning them about the deceiver.

### Evil and Immorality

*Sūʾ* includes disbelief, injustice, betrayal, cruelty, and every action that Allah dislikes. *Faḥshāʾ* is more specific to shameless and indecent acts, especially those that violate sexual morality, family dignity, or the natural sense of modesty. The Qur’an does not treat immorality as a private matter without social consequences. It harms individuals, families, and communities, and it normalizes the humiliation or exploitation of the vulnerable.

The believer protects himself through modesty, lawful relationships, lowered gaze, truthful companionship, and distance from media or environments that repeatedly make indecency attractive. He also avoids becoming self-righteous. Allah commands opposition to sin with wisdom and justice, not with slander, spying, or cruelty.

### Speaking about Allah without Knowledge

The second command is especially dangerous: inventing claims about Allah without knowledge. This includes declaring something lawful or unlawful without evidence, attributing a false statement to revelation, explaining the Qur’an from personal whim, denying a divine attribute without understanding, or issuing confident religious rulings beyond one’s competence.

The Qur’an places “saying about Allah what you do not know” among the gravest prohibitions (7:33). A person may be physically abstinent while spiritually violating this command through misinformation. False theology can lead people into worship of other than Allah, unjust treatment of others, or despair of divine mercy.

### The Duty to Say “I Do Not Know”

The opposite of speaking without knowledge is intellectual humility. The Prophet ﷺ answered questions directly when revelation had clarified them and waited for revelation when it had not. His Companions and the classical scholars frequently said, “Allah knows best,” not as a performance of modesty but as recognition of responsibility.

A student should verify a hadith before sharing it, distinguish a scholar’s opinion from an explicit verse, and avoid presenting a disputed matter as unanimous. Social media makes the sin easier because a short statement can reach thousands before it is corrected. The believer slows down when the subject is Allah, His Book, and the rights of people.

### Desire Disguised as Religion

Satan may lead a person first through appetite and then through justification. Someone wants a forbidden profit, a relationship, or a ritual innovation, so he searches for a religious sentence that will approve it. Alternatively, someone dislikes a command and declares it irrational or obsolete without studying its evidence. In both cases, desire has become the hidden author of theology.

The cure is to return to the Qur’an, authentic Sunnah, Arabic meanings, the understanding of the Companions, and qualified scholarship. A believer does not treat every inherited custom as revelation or every personal preference as a principle of Islam.

### Hope and Protection

Satan’s clear enmity does not mean that a believer is helpless. The Qur’an teaches seeking refuge in Allah, reciting the last two sūrahs, maintaining remembrance, and filling the heart with beneficial knowledge and good action. When a whisper appears, the servant does not debate it endlessly; he seeks refuge, abandons the path, and turns to a lawful alternative.

Repentance also breaks Satan’s strategy. If a person falls, he should not conclude that he is permanently corrupt. Allah loves those who repent and purify themselves (2:222). The enemy wants both the sin and the despair that follows it; sincere *tawbah* denies him both victories.

- Qur’an 7:33 and 16:116 on the gravity of inventing lawful and unlawful claims.
- Qur’an 14:22 and 35:6 on Satan’s limited power and declared enmity.
- Qur’an 24:30–31 and 17:32 on modesty and avoiding the paths that lead to indecency.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith on Satan’s whispering and the command to seek refuge; the hadith “Whoever lies upon me deliberately, let him take his seat in the Fire” also warns against careless attribution to the Prophet ﷺ.
- For *sūʾ*, *faḥshāʾ*, and the prohibition of speaking about Allah without knowledge, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:169.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:170

> When it is said to them, “Follow what Allah has sent down,” they say, “Rather, we will follow what we found our forefathers doing.” What! Even if their forefathers understood nothing and were not guided?

**Commentary:**

### Inherited Practice and Revealed Guidance

The verse describes people who answer a call to follow revelation with an appeal to ancestral custom. They do not say, “We examined the evidence and found it unconvincing.” They say, “We will follow what we found our forefathers doing.” Their argument is social rather than intellectual: the practice is old, familiar, and attached to respected names, so it must be preserved.

Islam honours righteous ancestors, family ties, and beneficial tradition. The problem is not that a practice has been inherited. Much of religious knowledge is transmitted through trustworthy teachers and communities. The problem is treating ancestry as a proof that can overrule Allah’s revelation. A custom is evaluated by truth; truth is not evaluated merely by age.

### “Even If They Understood Nothing?”

The rhetorical question exposes the weakness of blind imitation. What if the ancestors did not understand, investigate, or receive guidance? Reverence for parents cannot require following them into shirk or injustice. The Qur’an commands kindness to parents while stating that they must not be obeyed in associating partners with Allah (31:14–15). Good family relations and independent moral responsibility coexist.

The verse is not an insult to every forefather. Some ancestors were knowledgeable and guided, and following their sound teaching can be praiseworthy. It is directed at people who use ancestry as a shield against clear evidence, even when the inherited practice is demonstrably false or harmful.

### Taqlīd and Responsible Learning

The Arabic concept of *taqlīd* can mean following a qualified authority without personally deriving every ruling from its evidence. For a non-specialist, asking scholars is necessary and reasonable: “Ask the people of knowledge if you do not know” (16:43). The condemned imitation is not all reliance on scholarship; it is stubborn adherence to an inherited position after the truth has become clear, or following an unqualified person simply because he is famous or familiar.

Responsible learning combines trust with discernment. The ordinary believer consults reliable scholars, compares claims with the Qur’an and authentic Sunnah, and avoids making every personal preference into an independent legal school. The scholar, in turn, does not demand loyalty to himself in place of loyalty to Allah.

### Culture Can Hide Falsehood

Every society has customs that appear natural because they are repeated. Some are harmless or beneficial; others preserve discrimination, exploitation, superstition, or family abuse. The verse asks Muslims to examine inherited practices through the principles of revelation. A tradition that strengthens honesty, hospitality, kinship, and justice may be retained. A tradition that violates tawḥīd or human rights must be corrected, even if correction is unpopular.

The same warning applies to modern customs. People sometimes reject revelation while claiming to follow “what everyone does.” Modernity is not automatically guidance, just as antiquity is not automatically truth. The Qur’an gives a criterion independent of fashion and ancestry.

### The Courage to Break a Harmful Pattern

Following revelation can require a person to disappoint relatives or leave a familiar environment. This must be done with wisdom, patience, and good character. The believer does not humiliate parents or insult a culture when rejecting a false practice. He explains the reason, preserves lawful ties, and refuses obedience only where obedience would violate Allah’s command.

Prophets repeatedly stood against the inherited religion of their people. Ibrāhīm confronted the worship of idols while speaking with clarity and courage (21:51–70). His example shows that loyalty to truth is not disrespect for family; it is the highest form of concern when a family is travelling toward harm.

- Qur’an 5:104, 31:14–15, and 43:22–24 on inherited custom, parental kindness, and obedience within truth.
- Qur’an 16:43 and 39:18 on consulting people of knowledge and listening critically to guidance.
- Qur’an 21:51–70 and 26:69–82 on Ibrāhīm’s challenge to inherited idolatry.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on obedience to parents being bounded by what is right, and the hadith that whoever intends good learning should seek understanding of the religion.
- For the distinction between blameworthy blind imitation and following qualified knowledge, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:170.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:171

> The example of those who disbelieve is like that of someone who calls out to what hears nothing except a cry and a shout. Deaf, dumb, and blind, they do not understand.

**Commentary:**

### A Picture of Unreceived Guidance

The verse gives a parable of people who hear the sound of revelation but do not grasp its meaning. A shepherd may call animals that hear a noise without understanding the words or the danger being addressed. In the same way, a person may listen to recitation, arguments, warnings, and reminders while allowing none of them to reach the heart.

The comparison is not saying that all disbelievers lack physical hearing, speech, or sight. The Qur’an immediately uses “deaf, dumb, and blind” in a moral and spiritual sense. Their faculties function biologically, but they refuse to use them for guidance. The Qur’an elsewhere speaks of hearts that do not understand and ears that do not hear the truth despite being physically sound (7:179; 22:46).

### Sound without Understanding

A cry or shout can alert an animal without conveying a reasoned message. It may produce movement, fear, or temporary attention, but it does not produce insight. The parable describes people whose response to revelation is similarly shallow. They may react to a loud speaker, a social identity, or an emotional atmosphere without understanding what Allah is asking of them.

This is a warning to Muslims as well. Recitation can become sound without reflection, sermons can become familiar background, and religious language can be repeated without repentance. The believer asks whether the Qur’an has changed his choices, not merely whether he has heard it many times.

### The Three Blocked Faculties

Hearing represents receiving evidence, speech represents acknowledging and responding to truth, and sight represents recognizing the signs that lead to Allah. When the three are described as blocked, the verse portrays a person who refuses to listen, will not confess what he knows, and does not see the meaning of the world around him.

The blockage is not Allah’s injustice. It is connected to persistent rejection. When a person repeatedly turns away, his choices harden into a veil. The Qur’an says that when they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate (61:5). Divine judgement and human choice are both affirmed: people choose refusal, and Allah allows the consequences of their chosen path to settle upon them.

### Understanding Requires Humility

The final phrase, “they do not understand”—*lā yaʿqilūn*, does not deny their ability to solve worldly problems. A person can be clever in business, science, or politics while failing to understand the purpose of life. Qur’anic understanding is *ʿaql*: connecting signs to their Creator, controlling desire, and accepting moral consequence.

Pride is one of the greatest barriers. The person who approaches revelation only to defeat it may notice details but miss its direction. The person who asks sincerely, “What does my Lord want from me?” can be guided even when he begins with limited knowledge. Understanding is therefore both an intellectual and moral act.

### The Messenger’s Compassion

The image also explains why prophets repeatedly called people despite rejection. They were not shouting meaningless sounds; they were conveying a clear message with patience. The Prophet ﷺ recited, explained, warned, encouraged, and answered questions. When people refused to hear, he grieved for them, but he did not force faith. Guidance belongs to Allah, while the messenger’s duty is clear conveyance (16:82).

Muslim educators should follow this model. Teaching should be understandable, truthful, and connected to real life. A speaker should not rely only on volume, fear, or performance. The goal is for the listener to comprehend, reflect, and choose obedience.

### Restoring the Faculties

Spiritual deafness is not necessarily permanent while a person lives. The Qur’an invites people to listen attentively, reflect, and return. Turning off distractions, reading a translation with the Arabic, asking qualified questions, keeping good company, and practising what one learns can reopen the heart.

The believer should make the supplication taught in the Qur’an: “Our Lord, grant us from our spouses and offspring comfort to our eyes and make us an example for the mindful” (25:74). A receptive heart is a gift, so one asks Allah for it while taking the means of receptivity.

- Qur’an 7:179, 22:46, and 63:3 on spiritual deafness, blindness, and blocked understanding.
- Qur’an 16:82 and 24:54 on the messenger’s duty of clear conveyance and the human duty to obey.
- Qur’an 61:5 and 47:24 on deviation, reflection, and the heart’s response to revelation.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the Prophet’s recitation, teaching, and grief for those who refused guidance; the hadith on a good companion and a bad companion also illustrates how receptivity is shaped by environment.
- For the parable, the moral meanings of “deaf, dumb, and blind,” and *ʿaql*, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:171.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:172

> O believers, eat from the good things We have provided for you and be grateful to Allah, if it is truly Him that you worship.

**Commentary:**

### Lawful Provision as a Sign of Worship

The preceding verse addressed all humanity; this one addresses believers specifically. Faith changes the way a person receives provision. Food is not merely fuel or pleasure. It is a gift from Allah, obtained through a chain of created means, and it should be consumed within the limits He has set. The command combines eating good things with gratitude, showing that bodily nourishment and spiritual worship are not enemies.

“Good things”—*ṭayyibāt*—means wholesome and beneficial provision. Believers may enjoy lawful food without treating enjoyment as shameful. The Qur’an rejects ascetic inventions that declare Allah’s lawful adornments forbidden (7:32). At the same time, enjoyment must remain disciplined by moderation, health, justice, and gratitude.

### Gratitude in Three Places

*Shukr* begins in the heart by recognizing Allah as the giver. It appears on the tongue through praise and the mention of His name, and it appears in action by consuming responsibly and using strength in obedience. Saying *al-ḥamdulillāh* after a meal is beautiful, but gratitude is incomplete if a person wastes food, hoards it while neighbours are hungry, or uses the resulting energy for oppression.

The believer also thanks the people through whom provision arrives. Islam commands gratitude to parents, workers, hosts, farmers, and all who provide lawful benefit, while remembering that their ability is itself from Allah. Appreciation of created means does not compete with tawḥīd; it is part of good character.

### “If You Worship Him”

The closing condition connects dietary practice to exclusive worship. If Allah alone is worshipped, then His servant accepts His guidance about what to consume rather than allowing appetite, fashion, or commercial pressure to define every limit. Worship is not confined to prayer in a mosque. Choosing lawful food, earning lawful income, and refusing exploitation can all become acts of ʿibādah when done for Allah.

The phrase also prevents ritual from becoming disconnected from ethics. A person cannot claim that he worships Allah while deliberately consuming stolen wealth or supporting cruelty when lawful alternatives are available. What enters the body affects the heart, and the heart’s state affects worship.

### The Prophet’s Teaching on Good Provision

The Prophet ﷺ taught that Allah is good and accepts only what is good, then described a traveller whose supplication is hindered by unlawful food, drink, clothing, and nourishment, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. The hadith places a high value on lawful provision. It does not mean that every poor person’s prayer is rejected or that every affluent person’s prayer is accepted. It warns that deliberate unlawful consumption can become a barrier to spiritual receptivity.

The Prophet also taught moderation. The stomach should not be filled without restraint, and the believer should leave room for food, drink, and breath, according to the well-known report in Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī. Moderation protects health and helps a person remain energetic for worship and service.

### Food, Justice, and Community

The word “our” in “Our provision” reminds believers that provision is entrusted to a community as well as individuals. Those with abundance must help those facing hunger. Zakah, voluntary charity, fair wages, honest trade, and refusing food waste are expressions of gratitude. A society cannot praise *shukr* while allowing preventable hunger through greed or corruption.

The Prophet ﷺ said that the one who is not concerned with the affairs of Muslims is not following the proper spirit of the community, and he encouraged feeding the hungry. The exact legal duties vary with circumstances, but the moral direction is clear: Allah’s gifts should circulate with mercy.

### Freedom from Food Superstition

The verse prepares for the specific prohibitions in the next āyah. Muslims do not invent dietary restrictions from superstition, caste, or religious competition. They follow what Allah has made lawful and unlawful, and they avoid both excess and negligence. A food is not spiritually powerful merely because a culture labels it so, nor is a lawful blessing made impure by baseless fear.

- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, the hadith “Allah is good and accepts only what is good,” and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī on the Prophet’s supplication after eating.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, the hadith on moderation in the stomach and the report on the virtue of feeding others.
- For *ṭayyib*, *shukr*, lawful earnings, and the link between provision and worship, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:172.
## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:173

> He has only forbidden you carrion, blood, the flesh of swine, and what has been dedicated to other than Allah. But whoever is forced by necessity, neither desiring it nor exceeding the need, there is no sin upon him. Surely Allah is Forgiving and Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Clear Prohibitions

This verse lists four primary categories of forbidden food: *maytah*, an animal that dies without lawful slaughter; flowing blood; swine; and an animal dedicated to other than Allah. The list establishes that dietary law is determined by revelation, not by superstition, ethnic pride, or personal taste. The phrase “He has only forbidden” also corrects people who invent unnecessary restrictions and then attribute them to Allah.

The Qur’an gives related lists in 5:3, 6:145, and 16:115. The passages complement one another, while the Sunnah and juristic reasoning explain details such as lawful slaughter, hunting, impurities, and circumstances of necessity. A Muslim should learn from reliable scholarship rather than making confident rulings from a single translation.

### Carrion and Blood

*Maytah* is an animal that dies without the prescribed process, though the jurists discuss exceptions such as certain sea animals and locusts based on authentic hadith. Flowing blood—*dam masfūḥ* in 6:145—is prohibited, while blood that remains in the veins after proper slaughter is not treated in the same way. These distinctions show why Arabic wording, prophetic explanation, and legal method matter.

The prohibition protects health, cultivates respect for life, and separates Muslim worship from pre-Islamic sacrificial customs. Slaughter is not a licence for cruelty. The Prophet ﷺ commanded excellence in slaughter, sharpening the blade, and sparing the animal unnecessary suffering, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

### Swine and Sacrificial Dedication

The flesh of swine is explicitly prohibited. Muslims accept the command as an act of obedience even where they do not know every wisdom. They may reflect upon hygiene, disease, and the moral symbolism of the prohibition, but they do not claim that an unproven explanation is the whole reason. Revelation is authoritative because Allah knows what His servants do not know.

“What has been dedicated to other than Allah” concerns food offered as a sacrifice in the name of an idol, person, spirit, or other created object. The issue is theological as well as dietary. An act of sacrifice is worship, and worship belongs to Allah alone. Eating such a sacrifice would participate in or approve a rival allegiance, which contradicts *tawḥīd*.

### Necessity Is Not Desire

Allah then gives an important concession: whoever is compelled by necessity may eat the forbidden food without sin, provided he neither desires it nor exceeds the need. *Iḍṭirār* occurs when a person faces a real danger to life or serious health and cannot find lawful food. Mere convenience, curiosity, social pressure, or a preference for forbidden taste is not necessity.

The concession demonstrates that the Sharīʿah protects life. A person who would die or suffer severe harm without food may take enough to survive. He does not continue eating for pleasure once the danger is removed, and he does not turn the exception into a routine. The maxim “necessities permit the prohibited” is tied to genuine necessity and measured by its extent.

### Neither Desiring Nor Transgressing

“Neither desiring it nor exceeding the need” is often explained as not seeking the forbidden item while lawful alternatives exist and not eating beyond what necessity requires. The person’s heart remains unwilling to violate the rule, and his action is restricted to preservation of life. If someone can obtain lawful food through asking, purchasing, travelling, or accepting help, he should do so.

The concession also applies in a way that avoids harm to others. A person may not use emergency conditions as a licence to steal from someone more vulnerable, endanger another life, or profit from scarcity. Necessity removes the sin of the narrowly defined consumption; it does not suspend all other obligations.

### Mercy in the Law

The verse ends with Allah’s forgiveness and mercy. The Lawgiver knows human weakness, accidents, travel, famine, coercion, and illness. Legal commands are not designed to trap a servant who is genuinely unable. The concession is not a loophole against obedience; it is part of obedience because Allah Himself revealed it.

This principle extends across worship and social law. A sick person may pray sitting, a traveller may shorten prayer, and a person facing compulsion is judged according to what he could control. Mercy is embedded in the law while the normal rule remains clear.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:174

> Surely those who conceal what Allah has sent down of the Scripture and exchange it for a small price consume nothing but fire in their bellies. Allah will not speak to them on the Day of Resurrection, nor purify them, and they will have a painful punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Concealment for Worldly Gain

The passage returns to the betrayal of revealed knowledge. These people conceal what Allah sent down and exchange it for a small price. The “price” can be money, office, influence, political protection, social honour, or the preservation of an institution’s reputation. It is called small not because worldly wealth has no value at all, but because no temporary gain can compare with the truth and the eternal consequences of betraying it.

The concealment is deliberate. The previous passages have distinguished sincere uncertainty from knowing refusal. Here, people possess revelation and hide it when making it known would cost them something. Religious authority becomes a marketplace, and the scripture is treated as an instrument for securing advantage rather than as a trust that judges the holder.

### Consuming Fire

“Consume nothing but fire in their bellies” is a severe image. The wealth acquired through the concealment may look like food, salary, or comfort in this life, but its unseen reality is fire because it was gained by selling guidance. The image also reverses the purpose of food in the preceding verses. Lawful provision nourishes a servant for worship; unlawful gain acquired through betrayal becomes a source of punishment.

This does not mean that every teacher who receives a salary has sold religion. Scholars, educators, translators, and institutions may lawfully receive payment for their time and work. The warning concerns making revelation serve a dishonest bargain or suppressing truth specifically to obtain worldly advantage. The intention, knowledge, and conduct must be examined with justice.

### Allah Will Not Speak to Them

The statement that Allah will not speak to them is a declaration of humiliation and deprivation, not a denial that Allah knows or judges them. On the Day of Resurrection, the honour of divine address and acceptance is withheld from those who treated His words as commodities. The Qur’an elsewhere describes Allah speaking to believers with peace and honour, making the contrast profound.

The verse should make people value truthful speech in the present. Those who manipulate scripture may receive applause from audiences, but they will not be able to purchase Allah’s approval. The servant should prefer an honest admission of limited knowledge to a confident answer designed to retain status.

### Nor Will He Purify Them

Purification—*tazkiyah*—can mean cleansing from sin and granting praise, acceptance, and growth. The concealment of guidance corrupts both the teacher and the community. The person may retain an outward religious identity, but the deliberate betrayal prevents him from receiving the purification that comes through sincere faith and repentance.

Yet the next verse in this passage explains that repentance remains possible before death. The warning is not a barrier against return; it is a description of the unrepentant concealer. A person who recognizes his failure should expose the truth, repair the harm, and seek Allah’s forgiveness rather than defending the old falsehood.

### The Responsibility of Religious Speech

This āyah applies to every field in which people claim Allah’s authority. A preacher may conceal a command because it would offend donors. A leader may hide a legal principle because it threatens his political alliance. A community may suppress abuse to protect its image. A writer may quote scripture selectively to sell a message. These are all forms of the danger described, though the precise judgement of each case belongs to Allah.

The solution is *amānah*: honest transmission, transparent sources, correction of mistakes, and willingness to say what is true even when it reduces worldly advantage. The Qur’an commands people to fulfil trusts and judge with justice (4:58). Religious knowledge is among the greatest trusts.

### A Warning against Cynicism

The verse should not lead people to distrust every scholar or religious institution. Most knowledge is transmitted through human effort, and trustworthy teachers are necessary. Muslims should neither idolize teachers nor assume corruption without evidence. They evaluate claims, ask for proof, recognize expertise, and preserve good manners.

A teacher’s livelihood can be lawful when it supports sincere service. The danger arises when money controls the conclusion or when the teacher knowingly hides a clear truth. The distinction protects both scholarship and the public from cynical suspicion.

- Qur’an 4:58 and 16:43 on trusts, justice, and referring matters to qualified people.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:175

> They are the ones who have exchanged guidance for misguidance and forgiveness for punishment. How patient they are in pursuing the Fire!

**Commentary:**

### A Terrible Exchange

The verse describes the people who concealed revelation as merchants making a disastrous exchange. They give up *hudā*, guidance, and take *ḍalāl*, misguidance. They abandon the possibility of Allah’s forgiveness and choose punishment. The language of trade continues from the “small price” in the preceding āyah: they believe they are gaining something, but the transaction is spiritual ruin.

Guidance is not an abstract possession. It is the light by which a person knows his Lord, distinguishes right from wrong, and finds the path to forgiveness. Misguidance may offer immediate comfort, status, or profit, but it leaves the person unable to see the consequences of his choices. The exchange is therefore irrational when measured by the Hereafter, even if it looks profitable in the marketplace of this world.

### Forgiveness and Punishment

The contrast is exact. Allah’s forgiveness is available through faith, repentance, and righteous action. Punishment is the result of refusing that mercy and persisting in betrayal. A person who hides truth for a temporary advantage does not merely lose information; he turns away from the means by which he could be purified.

The Qur’an never presents Allah’s forgiveness as scarce. It repeatedly calls people to repent and return (39:53; 66:8). The tragedy described here is that the concealers knowingly exchange an open gift for a consequence they could have avoided. Their sin is not a lack of access to mercy but a preference for something lesser.

### “How Patient They Are!”

The final expression—*fa-mā aṣbarahum ʿalā al-nār*—is an Arabic rhetorical exclamation. It does not praise them for admirable patience. It expresses astonishment and rebuke: how can they endure or persist in the path that leads to the Fire? Some early exegetes explain it as amazement at their audacity in committing the acts that make punishment their outcome.

The wording is powerful because people are often patient for worldly aims. They endure long hours for money, status, or entertainment, but may not endure the short discomfort of honesty, prayer, or repentance. The verse asks the heart to compare its endurance: what hardship is it willing to accept now, and what consequence is it purchasing for later?

### The Psychology of Self-Deception

A person rarely announces, “I choose punishment.” He tells himself that the compromise is small, the audience will never know, or the truth can be addressed later. Satan makes the immediate price of integrity seem larger than the eternal price of betrayal. Repeated choices then make the false path feel normal.

The remedy is honest accounting. Before accepting a benefit, the believer asks what he is being asked to conceal. Before repeating a religious claim, he asks whether it is supported. Before obeying an influential person, he asks whether the command agrees with Allah’s guidance. Regular repentance interrupts self-deception before it becomes a settled identity.

### A Warning to Knowledge-Bearers

The immediate audience includes people entrusted with scripture, but the warning reaches every Muslim who knows a truth and chooses to distort it. A student may hide a fact to win a debate; a businessperson may use religious language to justify fraud; a parent may teach children a convenient custom while ignoring a clear obligation. The danger is not limited to scholars.

Conversely, knowledge should not be shared irresponsibly. The one who speaks without competence can mislead even while believing he is spreading guidance. The safest path is to learn, verify, state the evidence accurately, and refer complex matters to those qualified.

### The Choice Still Exists

The exclamation is addressed to the living. The person who has taken a wrong path can still exchange misguidance for guidance through sincere repentance. He may have to surrender money, status, or the approval of a group, but Allah’s forgiveness is worth more than every worldly price. The Qur’an describes repentance as a return to the best trade, not a humiliation.

A community should make such return possible by welcoming truthful correction, protecting those who expose wrongdoing, and refusing to make a person’s past the sole definition of his future. Accountability and mercy are not opposites when both are directed toward truth and reform.

- Qur’an 14:22 and 35:6 on Satan’s deception and the need to treat him as an enemy.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:176

> That is because Allah has sent down the Book in truth. Surely those who differ over the Book are far gone in extreme opposition.

**Commentary:**

### Revelation Is Truth

The verse gives the reason for the punishment described above: Allah sent down the Book in truth—*bi-l-ḥaqq*. The revelation is true in its source, content, purpose, and judgement. It comes from the One who knows the human condition and shows people the way to worship, justice, and salvation. Because the Book is true, concealing or distorting it is not a minor intellectual error; it is a betrayal of reality.

The Qur’an is also described as confirming earlier revelation and serving as a criterion over it (5:48). This means Muslims honour the original messages given to the prophets while recognizing the Qur’an as the final preserved revelation. “In truth” therefore includes continuity with the prophetic call and correction of human additions and distortions.

### Difference over the Book

“Those who differ over the Book” can refer to the People of the Book who disputed about the Qur’an, concealed its signs, or altered the meaning of their scripture. It can also include anyone who divides over revelation through denial, selective acceptance, invented interpretation, or conflict driven by worldly interest. Not every scholarly difference is condemned. The Qur’an itself recognizes sincere people who differ while seeking evidence, and the early jurists had legitimate disagreements in matters open to interpretation.

The blameworthy *ikhtilāf* is rooted in *shiqāq*, extreme opposition and hostility. It is not simply that two people reach different conclusions; it is that one side turns away from truth, breaks away from guidance, or uses disagreement to protect pride and power. A person can be wrong without being malicious, and he can be technically learned while being spiritually rebellious.

### The Book as a Criterion

The Qur’an does not merely settle arguments after they become public. It provides a criterion before the argument begins. Muslims return to it and the Sunnah to distinguish principle from custom, revelation from speculation, and genuine disagreement from factional rivalry. The Book also corrects the tendency to select isolated passages while ignoring the whole message of justice, mercy, worship, and accountability.

A sound interpretation respects the Arabic text, the context of revelation, the Prophet’s explanation, the understanding of the Companions, and the principles of the religion. It avoids forcing the Qur’an to say what a political movement, personal desire, or inherited prejudice already decided. The one who approaches the Book with humility is more likely to benefit from its clarity.

### Truth and Human Freedom

The fact that Allah sent the Book in truth does not mean that every person will accept it. Allah allows people a genuine moral response, and the Qur’an records those who believe, those who deny, and those who conceal what they know. Revelation is a proof and guidance; it does not remove the responsibility of choosing.

This is why a messenger’s task is clear conveyance. Muslims invite without coercion, explain without distortion, and leave hearts to Allah. They should not manipulate evidence to force agreement, because the truth does not need dishonesty to support it.

### Unity around Revelation

The community’s unity is not achieved by suppressing every difference or demanding loyalty to a personality. It is achieved by returning together to Allah’s Book and the authentic Sunnah, maintaining justice and good manners when interpretation differs. The Qur’an commands holding fast collectively to Allah’s rope and not dividing into hostile factions (3:103).

Where a matter is clear, believers should not manufacture controversy. Where a matter is genuinely interpretive, they should preserve brotherhood and avoid accusing one another of disbelief or corruption without valid grounds. The extreme opposition condemned in the verse is not the careful work of scholarship; it is the will to fight the Book rather than be corrected by it.

### A Lesson for the Present

Modern disputes can repeat the old pattern. A person may reject revelation because it challenges a social ideology, or quote it selectively to sanctify a political interest. Another may abandon the Qur’an’s ethical balance by turning one passage into a weapon against the rest. The remedy is to read the Book as a coherent revelation and to ask what Allah is requiring of the heart and society.

The final authority belongs to Allah, not to the loudest faction. Every interpreter remains a servant who can be corrected. This humility is the opposite of *shiqāq*.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:177

> Righteousness is not merely that you turn your faces toward the east or the west. Rather, righteousness is the way of one who believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets; who gives wealth, despite loving it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveller, those who ask, and for freeing captives; who establishes prayer and gives zakāh; who fulfils promises; and who is patient in poverty, hardship, and at times of battle. Those are the ones who are true, and those are the mindful.

**Commentary:**

### The Meaning of Birr

This āyah gives one of the Qur’an’s most comprehensive definitions of *birr*, righteousness and expansive goodness. It comes in the middle of the qiblah discussion, where people had argued about direction. Allah does not dismiss the qiblah; facing the commanded direction is part of obedience. He corrects the reduction of religion to an outward symbol while faith, charity, justice, and endurance are neglected.

The verse begins with negation—righteousness is not “that you turn your faces”—then gives the complete positive definition. *Birr* includes sound belief, worship, social generosity, moral reliability, and steadfastness under pressure. A Muslim cannot choose one dimension and abandon the others. Ritual without mercy is hollow, and good intentions without worship are incomplete.

### Faith in the Unseen Foundations

The righteous person believes in Allah, the Last Day, the angels, the Book, and the prophets. These are foundations of *īmān*. Belief in Allah gives life its purpose; belief in the Last Day gives actions consequence; belief in angels affirms an unseen order and accountability; belief in the revealed Books establishes guidance; and belief in all the prophets protects the unity of the prophetic mission.

The list joins the final revelation to the whole history of revelation. Muslims do not believe in Muhammad ﷺ while rejecting Mūsā, ʿĪsā, Ibrāhīm, or the other messengers. They affirm the original messages given to them and accept the Qur’an as the final criterion. This faith is not a vague spirituality. It shapes how a person sees time, responsibility, suffering, and the rights of others.

### Giving Wealth Despite Love of It

The righteous give wealth—*ātā al-māl ʿalā ḥubbih*—despite loving it. The phrase recognizes that wealth is naturally attractive. Islam does not demand that people pretend money has no utility or that they have no affection for what they earned. It asks them to overcome possessiveness when another person has a legitimate need.

The recipients are listed broadly. Relatives have a claim because generosity should begin with family without becoming tribal favoritism. Orphans need protection because they have lost parental care. The needy may lack sufficient provision. The traveller may be stranded far from resources. Those who ask should be treated with dignity, even when a person cannot give. Freeing captives points to liberation from unjust bondage and the restoration of human dignity.

This spending includes obligatory zakāh and voluntary charity, though the verse’s wording is broader than zakāh alone. It can include food, medical assistance, debt relief, education, shelter, and advocacy for the oppressed. The aim is not only to transfer money but to remove vulnerability and restore people’s ability to live with dignity.

### Prayer and Zakāh Together

The verse joins *ṣalāh* and *zakāh*, as the Qur’an often does. Prayer repairs the servant’s relationship with Allah, while zakāh purifies wealth and repairs social relations. Establishing prayer—*aqāma al-ṣalāh*—means guarding its times, conditions, humility, and communal purpose, not merely performing occasional movements. Giving zakāh means calculating it accurately, paying it to eligible recipients, and refusing to treat wealth as absolute ownership.

The pairing prevents two distortions. A person cannot claim spirituality while neglecting the poor, and he cannot claim social activism while refusing the worship that gives justice its ultimate foundation. The body turns toward the qiblah, and wealth turns toward those whom Allah has made deserving.

### Fulfilling Promises

“Those who fulfil their promises”—*al-mūfūna bi-ʿahdihim*—are righteous. Trustworthiness includes personal promises, contracts, debts, covenants, employment agreements, marriage responsibilities, and public commitments. A believer does not use religious language to escape a written obligation or exploit a technical loophole against the purpose of an agreement.

The Qur’an commands fulfilment of covenants (16:91) and describes breaking promises as a sign of hypocrisy. The Prophet ﷺ identified breaking a promise as one of the signs of a hypocrite, while the hadith does not mean that every broken promise under every circumstance makes a person a hypocrite. It warns against making commitments carelessly or breaking them deliberately.

### Patience in Three Hardships

The verse ends by naming patience in poverty, hardship, and battle. Poverty tests basic provision; hardship includes illness, fear, grief, and oppressive circumstances; battle tests courage, discipline, and moral restraint. The list shows that righteousness is proven when values are costly.

Patience—*ṣabr*—does not mean accepting preventable oppression without action. The poor should be supported, the sick treated, and the oppressed defended. The patient person seeks lawful relief without abandoning faith, justice, or self-control. In war, patience includes refusing aggression, protecting non-combatants, honouring agreements, and stopping when justice requires it. Courage without restraint is not Qur’anic righteousness.

### Truthfulness and Taqwā

“Those are the ones who are true”—*ṣadaqū*—means that their claim to faith is confirmed by action. The verse does not demand sinlessness. It describes a direction of life in which belief, worship, generosity, reliability, and patience support one another. A believer can fail and repent; he cannot make hypocrisy his settled method while claiming that external symbols alone prove righteousness.

“They are the mindful”—*al-muttaqūn*. Taqwā is awareness and protective obedience to Allah. It guards the believer from showing off in charity, abandoning prayer in private, breaking promises when no one can enforce them, or using hardship as an excuse for injustice. The final measure is not public appearance but sincerity before Allah.

### A Complete Religious Vision

The verse is a charter of integrated faith. It includes creed without abstraction, ritual without formalism, charity without vanity, social duty without secular emptiness, and patience without passivity. The qiblah matters because Allah commands it, but it becomes spiritually meaningful when the person who faces it also faces poverty, injustice, temptation, and responsibility with truth.

The Prophet ﷺ embodied this integration. He worshipped at night, fulfilled covenants, fed the poor, protected orphans, forgave when justice allowed, and stood firm under persecution. His Sunnah shows how the list becomes a life rather than a slogan.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:178

> O believers, legal retribution has been prescribed for you concerning those killed: the free for the free, the enslaved for the enslaved, and the female for the female. But if something is pardoned by the victim’s brother, then the appropriate follow-up and payment must be made with good conduct. This is an alleviation and mercy from your Lord. Whoever transgresses after that will have a painful punishment.

**Commentary:**

### Qisās and the Protection of Life

This āyah establishes *qiṣāṣ*, measured legal retribution for intentional murder. It does not invite private revenge or give individuals permission to kill. It places the response within lawful authority, evidence, due process, and the rights of the victim’s family. The purpose is to protect life by making murder a grave offence and by preventing cycles of tribal vengeance in which an entire clan could be attacked for one death.

The Qur’an’s wording is addressed to believers as a community because justice must be administered publicly and responsibly. A person who kills cannot be punished merely on rumour, anger, or accusation. Judges must establish the facts, distinguish intentional murder from accident and other categories, and consider the applicable rules of evidence. Vigilantism is not qiṣāṣ; it is another form of unlawful violence.

### Equality before the Law

“The free for the free, the enslaved for the enslaved, and the female for the female” confronted pre-Islamic practices in which powerful tribes demanded that several people be killed for one victim or refused any equivalent accountability when the victim came from a weaker group. The verse limits retribution to the actual offender and establishes that status cannot turn a murder into a licence for collective revenge.

The wording is not a statement that one human life is inherently worth more because of social status or sex. It is a prohibition of substituting one person for another and of allowing tribal power to determine whose blood is protected. The Qur’an elsewhere affirms the sanctity of every life and the equal moral accountability of men and women. Classical jurists discussed the detailed conditions of qiṣāṣ, and those discussions must be read in their legal framework rather than extracted as slogans.

### Mercy through Forgiveness

The verse immediately opens a path beyond retribution: if the victim’s right-holder pardons something, then the matter proceeds through appropriate follow-up and payment with good conduct. The “brother” language is striking. Even after murder, the Qur’an calls the parties brothers in humanity and community, encouraging the possibility of mercy without denying the horror of the crime.

The victim’s heirs may have the right to seek qiṣāṣ, accept *diyah*—financial compensation—or forgive without compensation, according to the law and the circumstances. Pardoning is praised as a noble option, but it must be genuinely voluntary. No family should be threatened, bribed, or socially coerced into surrendering a right. Reconciliation is meaningful only when truth, safety, and consent are present.

### Follow-Up and Payment with Good Conduct

The phrase *ittibāʿun bi-l-maʿrūf* requires the parties to handle the settlement properly. The killer or his representatives should not delay, insult, manipulate, or reduce the agreed compensation. The victim’s family should not demand what exceeds the lawful agreement or use forgiveness as a reason for humiliation. *Adāʾun ilayhi bi-iḥsān* requires payment graciously, promptly, and without resentment.

This ethical detail shows that the law is not only a calculation of punishment. It seeks to prevent a second round of harm. Even where rights are contested, speech and conduct must remain within justice. The state must protect both the family’s entitlement and the accused person’s due process.

### An Alleviation and Mercy

Allah calls qiṣāṣ an alleviation and mercy. It is an alleviation compared with unlimited revenge, because it sets a measured boundary. It is mercy because it protects potential victims, restrains anger, and leaves room for forgiveness and compensation. A legal system that allows murderers to escape with impunity endangers society; a system that permits unbounded revenge multiplies victims. Qiṣāṣ stands between negligence and excess.

Mercy here does not mean sentimental refusal to confront serious crime. Mercy to the victim’s family includes recognizing their loss and giving them a lawful right. Mercy to society includes deterrence and protection. Mercy to the offender includes due process and the possibility of repentance, forgiveness, and reform where the law permits.

### “Whoever Transgresses after That”

The warning against transgression after the settlement protects the agreement. If the family pardons or accepts compensation, it may not continue unlawful revenge. If the killer’s side accepts the settlement, it may not retaliate against the heirs. No party may use the pardon as a pretext for further harm. The boundaries of Allah’s law remain binding after emotion has cooled.

This also warns against exceeding the limits during enforcement. Punishment must not become torture, mutilation, humiliation, or collective retaliation. The Prophet ﷺ forbade transgression and commanded excellence even in difficult legal circumstances. The state and its agents are accountable to Allah for how they administer justice.

### Relationship to the Wider Law

Later verses 5:32 and 17:33 reinforce the sanctity of life, while 4:92 distinguishes accidental killing and its expiations. The jurists developed detailed rules for intentional murder, proof, retaliation, compensation, pardon, and the rights of heirs. The Qur’an’s objective remains clear even where legal details require expertise: preserve life, block vengeance, establish accountability, and open the door to mercy.

The verse should not be used to justify modern individuals taking punishment into their own hands. Application of criminal law belongs to legitimate judicial authority. Where such authority is absent or unjust, individuals still may not create chaos; they pursue lawful protection, evidence, mediation, and reform while leaving final judgement to Allah.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:179

> There is life for you in legal retribution, O people of understanding, so that you may become mindful.

**Commentary:**

### The Paradox of Qisās

The statement “there is life for you in legal retribution” is concise and profound. At first, people may imagine that responding to death with a death only multiplies loss. The Qur’an explains that measured, lawful accountability protects the living. When society knows that intentional murder has a serious and proportionate consequence, potential killers are restrained, victims’ families are given a recognized path to justice, and private revenge is less likely to spread.

*Qiṣāṣ* comes from a root associated with following and matching. It means a proportionate response, not uncontrolled vengeance. The law follows the act without exceeding it. This balance protects the sanctity of life while acknowledging that a community that refuses to confront murder may allow more lives to be destroyed.

### Deterrence and Due Process

The verse’s public benefit depends upon justice in application. A punishment imposed on the wrong person, on weak evidence, or through a corrupt process does not produce life; it produces another injustice. The accused has rights, the victim’s family has rights, and society has a right to safety. Qualified judges must establish guilt and distinguish intentional killing from accident, self-defence, coercion, and other circumstances.

The verse cannot be used as permission for mobs, families, militias, or individuals to execute people. Private retaliation often kills the innocent and turns one crime into a feud. Islamic legal authority exists precisely to prevent emotions and tribal power from controlling life and death.

### Mercy and Prevention

Qiṣāṣ protects life in more than one way. It restrains the murderer, discourages future violence, prevents the victim’s family from carrying out broader revenge, and allows a lawful choice of forgiveness or compensation. The previous āyah calls this an alleviation and mercy. The Qur’an’s objective is not a culture of death; it is a society in which life is so protected that murder carries a measured and unavoidable accountability.

Modern discussions sometimes focus only on the punishment and overlook prevention. The verse also requires addressing conditions that contribute to violence: injustice, impunity, corruption, lack of protection, and the easy availability of means of harm. A community that seeks “life” must protect the vulnerable before a murder occurs and support families afterward.

### “O People of Understanding”

The appeal to *ūlī al-albāb*—people possessing sound understanding—shows that the wisdom of the law requires reflection. Superficial emotion may see only revenge; deeper understanding sees protection and mercy. The phrase *lubb* refers to the core or sound kernel of the mind. These people look beyond immediate sentiment to consequences, rights, and the preservation of social order.

Sound understanding also avoids the opposite superficiality: using the verse as a slogan without learning its conditions. A person who cites qiṣāṣ to justify personal violence has not understood the verse. The people of understanding study the whole Qur’an, the Sunnah, the legal system, and the moral objectives of justice.

### Taqwā as the Goal

The purpose is “so that you may become mindful”—*laʿallakum tattaqūn*. Legal accountability should cultivate *taqwā*, awareness of Allah that restrains wrongdoing even when human law cannot see. A society needs courts, evidence, and enforcement, but it also needs hearts that fear Allah, value life, and refuse violence in private.

Taqwā affects every stage: the potential killer restrains himself; witnesses tell the truth; investigators refuse torture; judges resist bribery; heirs do not demand unlawful revenge; officials protect the accused from abuse; and the public does not celebrate cruelty. Without taqwā, even a formally correct rule can be corrupted by power.

### The Value of Forgiveness

The existence of qiṣāṣ does not eliminate forgiveness. In many cases, a victim’s family may pardon or accept compensation, and such mercy can heal wounds when it is voluntary and safe. The law protects the right to seek retribution while also honouring the right to forgive. Life is served by both accountability and reconciliation, according to the circumstances.

Forgiveness should not be forced upon families or used to protect powerful offenders. Nor should a family that seeks justice be shamed as lacking mercy. The verse presents a lawful balance in which the rights of all parties are recognized and Allah remains the final judge of intentions.

- Qur’an 5:32 and 17:33 on the value and protection of human life.
- Qur’an 4:58 and 5:8 on trustworthy judgement and justice even toward opponents.
- Qur’an 16:126 and 42:40 on proportionate response and forgiveness.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reports on the sanctity of Muslim blood, due process in disputes, and the excellence of pardon without denying justice.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:180

> Prescribed for you, when death approaches one of you and he leaves wealth, is a bequest for parents and close relatives according to what is acceptable—a duty upon the mindful.

**Commentary:**

### Wealth and the Approaching End

This āyah turns from criminal justice to the justice of family wealth. When death approaches and a person leaves property, Allah prescribes a *waṣiyyah*, a bequest, for parents and close relatives in a fair manner. Death exposes the temporary nature of ownership. What a person gathered must be transferred without greed, revenge, or neglect of those who depended upon him.

The command to make a bequest also encourages preparation. A Muslim should not leave debts, trusts, family responsibilities, or charitable commitments hidden. The Prophet ﷺ taught that a person who has something to bequeath should not allow many nights to pass without having his bequest written, as recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim. Writing protects memory and prevents disputes, especially when illness or sudden death makes speech difficult.

### Parents and Close Relatives

Parents and relatives are mentioned because family ties create rights and responsibilities. Wealth should not be treated as an instrument for rewarding favourites or punishing relatives after death. A bequest should be *bi-l-maʿrūf*, according to what is recognized as fair, lawful, and socially reasonable. It should account for genuine need, prior support, debts, and the rights established by revelation.

The verse is not a permission to deprive a spouse, daughter, orphan, or weaker relative through clever documents. Justice in inheritance protects people who might otherwise be overridden by the strongest members of a family. A testator’s affection is respected, but it is governed by Allah’s limits.

### The Relationship with Fixed Inheritance

The inheritance shares are detailed later in Sūrah al-Nisāʾ (4:11–12, 4:176). Classical scholars discussed how those later verses relate to this early command. The accepted legal framework is that Allah’s fixed shares must be observed, and a bequest cannot be used to alter them. The well-known hadith states, “Allah has given each person entitled to a share his share, so there is no bequest for an heir,” recorded in Sunan Abī Dāwūd, Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, and other collections.

Thus a person may bequeath up to one-third of the estate to non-heirs or charitable purposes, while heirs receive the shares Allah assigned. A bequest to an heir may be valid only with the consent of the other heirs after death, according to the details of the schools. These rules prevent the living from using a last testament to override the divine distribution.

Some exegetes described the early general command as qualified or superseded in its application to fixed heirs by the later inheritance verses. Others emphasized that the principle of making a fair bequest remains for relatives who do not inherit under the fixed shares. In either case, the Qur’an’s enduring aims are clear: protect family rights, prevent arbitrary exclusion, and preserve lawful charity.

### Debts and Trusts Come First

Before distributing an estate, debts and valid trusts must be settled. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ repeatedly mentions inheritance after a bequest or debt (4:11–12). A person should record loans, unpaid wages, entrusted property, mahr, zakāh obligations, and other liabilities. Concealing a debt so that heirs can enjoy the estate is a grave betrayal.

The executor must act as an *amīn*, a trustworthy administrator. He may not take hidden fees, favour one heir, delay distribution to control relatives, or pressure vulnerable heirs into surrendering their rights. The Prophet ﷺ warned against consuming the wealth of orphans and taught that trusts must be returned to their owners.

### “According to What Is Acceptable”

The phrase *bi-l-maʿrūf* places moral limits on the bequest. It requires moderation and fairness, not extravagant gifts that harm obligatory heirs or negligible gifts that mock a dependent relative. The testator considers actual need and the wider obligations of the estate. The bequest should not become a tool for continuing family conflict after death.

The Prophet ﷺ advised Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ that leaving heirs financially secure is better than leaving them dependent upon people, while allowing a charitable bequest within the lawful limit. This hadith, recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, balances generosity with responsibility toward dependants.

### A Duty upon the Mindful

The final phrase calls the practice a duty upon the *muttaqūn*. Taqwā is necessary because wills are often executed when the person who wrote them can no longer defend himself. The mindful person knows that Allah sees hidden arrangements, verbal promises, and the treatment of the weak. He writes honestly, seeks qualified legal and religious advice, and tells his family where the document can be found.

The heirs also need taqwā. They must not manipulate the dying, forge documents, conceal assets, or consume another heir’s share. A person who receives less in the worldly division but remains patient and lawful has not lost before Allah. A person who takes more through deception has not truly gained.

### Mercy and Family Continuity

Islamic inheritance law is not merely a system of arithmetic. It is designed to distribute wealth, protect kinship, support dependants, and prevent accumulation in one powerful hand. A bequest can address needs that fixed shares cannot cover: a relative in hardship, a non-heir who cared for the deceased, a charitable project, or a public benefit.

The law also recognizes that grief can make families vulnerable. Clear writing, witnesses, transparent debts, and fair administration reduce conflict. The best legacy is not only property; it is a family taught to fear Allah and respect one another’s rights.
