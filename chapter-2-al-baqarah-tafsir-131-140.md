# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 131–140

This is the climax of the passage that opened at verse 124, and it is where the surah's argument about Abraham is finally cashed in. The preceding batch established that the covenant of leadership is not inherited and that the way of Abraham is the religion. These ten verses now produce the evidence and make the argument explicit.

First the patriarchs' own testimony: Abraham is commanded to submit and submits to the Lord of all worlds (131); he and Jacob bequeath the religion to their sons with the charge not to die except as submitters (132); and Jacob on his deathbed asks his sons what they will worship, and they name the God of Abraham, Ishmael and Isaac — one God — and declare their submission (133). Then the principle that settles it: the past community has passed, every generation earns its own, and no one is answerable for another (134). Then the argument itself: they say be Jews or Christians; the answer is the religion of Abraham, a *hanif*, who was not a polytheist (135), followed by the creed that makes no distinction between any messenger (136). Then the terms of the dispute: if they believe the same they are guided, and if they turn away the matter belongs to Allah (137). Then the image that resolves the whole passage — the *sibghah*, the colouring of Allah, which is an inner change and not an external mark (138). Then the manner of the dispute: share the premise, assign accountability, and stop (139). And finally the decisive chronological argument: Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and the tribes were not Jews or Christians, because those names postdate them by centuries — and who conceals a testimony he has from Allah (140)?

## Verse 131

**"When his Lord ordered him, “Submit ˹to My Will˺,” he responded, “I submit to the Lord of all worlds.”"**

In simple terms: when his Lord said to Abraham "Submit," he answered at once, "I submit to the Lord of all the worlds."

*Idh qala lahu rabbuhu aslim* — "when his Lord said to him: submit." *Aslim* is a bare imperative from the same root as *islam* and *muslim*: to hand over, to yield, to be safe by being surrendered. Two letters of command, with no explanation attached and no negotiation offered.

*Qala aslamtu li-rabbi al-'alamin* — "he said: I submit to the Lord of the worlds." Two things in the answer deserve attention.

The first is its tense. He says *aslamtu* — "I **have** submitted" — in the perfect, as though the matter were already settled in the instant of the command. There is no "I will think about it," no "on condition that," no "after I have finished what I am doing." The gap between the command and the compliance is one clause long. This is the same quality the Qur'an records of him elsewhere: when the command came to sacrifice his son, he said "O my son, indeed I have seen in a dream that I [must] sacrifice you, so see what you think" — and the son answered "O my father, do as you are commanded" (37:102) — and "when they had both submitted and he put him down upon his forehead" (37:103). That is what submission looks like when it is real, and it is why he is called the *imam*.

The second is what he added. He was told "submit," and he could have answered "I submit." Instead he answered "I submit **to the Lord of the worlds**." The commentators did not pass over this. The command named the act; the answer named the one to whom the act is directed. Submission is never a posture in the abstract. It is always submission *to* someone, and the whole question is who.

And then *al-'alamin* — the worlds, everything that exists other than God. By naming the object of his surrender as the Lord of all of it, Abraham removes everything from the exception. There is no department of his life reserved: not his family, not his property, not his body, not his reasoning, not his community. The one commanding him is the Lord of the lot, so the surrender is total or it is not surrender.

This is the verse the surah has been building toward since verse 112, where the condition of the one whose religion counts was *man aslama wajhahu lillahi wa huwa muhsin* — the one who submits his face to Allah *and* does good. Here is that submission in its purest instance, in a man with nothing held back.

For practice, the verse supplies a test that is unusually direct.

When something is asked of you — by the religion, by a person you owe, by a situation that has become clear — how long is the interval between hearing it and yielding to it? Most of us do not refuse. We defer. We say "yes, but first," or "yes, when I understand why," or "yes, in principle." Abraham's answer contains none of that, and it is recorded as the model.

And there is a second, quieter application in the object-clause. When you submit to something, name what you are actually submitting to. A great deal of what passes for submission in religious life is submission to a community's expectations, to a family's reputation, to a teacher's temperament, or to the fear of what people will say. Those are real submissions, and they are total — which is exactly the problem. The verse asks, in effect: to whose lordship is this surrender actually made? If the answer is not "the Lord of the worlds," then it is being made to something inside the worlds, and something inside the worlds cannot hold what you have given it.

## Verse 132

**"This was the advice of Abraham—as well as Jacob—to his children, ˹saying˺, “Indeed, Allah has chosen for you this faith; so do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission.”"**

In simple terms: Abraham charged his sons with this religion, and so did Jacob: "My sons, Allah has chosen this religion for you, so do not let death come upon you in any state other than submission."

*Wa wassa biha Ibrahima banihi wa Ya'qub* — "and Abraham enjoined it upon his sons, and Jacob [did too]." *Wassa* is from the root of *wasiyyah*, a will and bequest: to charge someone solemnly with something, to hand it over deliberately. The pronoun *-ha* refers back to the religion — the *millah* named at 130, the submission enacted at 131.

The grammar joins Jacob to Abraham as a second subject: Abraham bequeathed it and Jacob bequeathed it. The reported words that follow are understood by the majority as Jacob's charge to his sons, which is why verse 133 then shows the scene. Some grammarians discussed whether the saying belongs to both or to Jacob alone; the difference does not change the substance, since verse 133 records Jacob saying the same thing in his own voice.

Note what this establishes about how religion is transmitted. It is not absorbed from the atmosphere. It is *bequeathed* — deliberately, by name, by one generation to the next. And note who does the bequeathing: not a class of specialists but fathers. The first institution of religious transmission in this passage is the family.

And note the name. Jacob *is* Israel. The "children of Israel" are precisely the descendants of this man, and this verse shows him charging them with submission. The argument is already half-made before verse 135 states it: the founder of the people who claim him was himself one who submitted.

*Ya baniyya inna Allaha istafa lakum al-dina* — "O my sons, indeed Allah has chosen for you the religion." *Istafa* is the same verb used at verse 130 for the choosing of Abraham: He chose, He elected, He selected out.

And note the preposition: *lakum* — **for you**. Allah has chosen the religion *for* them, not *from* them. This is the single most important word in the verse for anyone who has to hand the religion to another person. It is presented not as a burden imposed but as a gift selected on their behalf. The parent who communicates obligation without communicating gift has misrepresented the thing he is trying to transmit.

Note too that he says *al-din* — **the** religion, with the definite article. Not "a path among paths" but the one religion.

*Fa-la tamutunna illa wa antum muslimun* — "so do not die except while you are submitters." The verb carries the emphatic *nun* of a firm prohibition: *do not*, ever, die in any other state.

And here is the crux, which the commentators all paused over: dying is not something a person controls. How can it be prohibited?

The answer they gave is the point of the verse. The prohibition is not of the event but of the state in which the event finds you. "Do not die except as submitters" means: live in submission, so that whenever death arrives — and it will arrive without consulting you — it finds you already submitted. This is the same petition Yusuf makes at 12:101, "Cause me to die as one who submits and join me with the righteous," and the same charge given to the believers at 3:102: "O you who have believed, fear Allah as He should be feared, and do not die except as Muslims [submitters]."

The supporting hadith is recorded by Muslim from Jabir: the Prophet ﷺ said, "Every servant is resurrected upon that upon which he died." And al-Bukhari records from Sahl ibn Sa'd that the Prophet ﷺ said that a man may do the deeds of the people of Paradise until there is nothing between him and it but an arm's length, and then what is written overtakes him and he does a deed of the people of the Fire and enters it — with the reverse case stated in the same narration, and the comment that deeds are [judged] by their endings.

For practice, this is one of the most practically useful verses in the Qur'an, because it takes something that appears to be outside your control and places it inside it.

You cannot choose the hour of your death. You can choose, with near-total reliability, the state in which it arrives — because the state in which people die is overwhelmingly the state in which they lived. The verse therefore redirects the whole of religious effort: not toward engineering a good final moment, but toward being the kind of person whom death finds already submitted. That is why the tradition treats the habitual *dhikr*, the maintained prayer, the settled repentance and the regular charity as protections for the end — not because they are magic at the final second, but because they are what the final second is made of.

There is a second application for anyone with children or students. The religion is transmitted the way a will is: named, deliberate, spoken. Abraham did not assume his sons would pick it up. He told them. And he told them what it *is* — something chosen for them — before he told them what to *do*. Children who receive the religion only as a set of prohibitions have been given half of what Jacob gave.

And the third is the content of the charge itself, which is worth saying plainly to a child or a student at least once: the goal of the religion is not that you should be impressive, or correct, or win arguments. The goal is that you should meet your death in a state of surrender to God. Everything else in the religion is in service of that.

## Verse 133

**"Or did you witness when death came to Jacob? He asked his children, “Who will you worship after my passing?” They replied, “We will ˹continue to˺ worship your God, the God of your forefathers— Abraham, Ishmael, and Isaac—the One God. And to Him we ˹all˺ submit.”"**

In simple terms: you were not present when Jacob was dying and asked his sons what they would worship after him. They answered: we worship your God and the God of your forefathers, Abraham, Ishmael and Isaac — one God — and to Him we submit.

*Am kuntum shuhada'a idh hadara Ya'quba al-mawtu* — "or were you witnesses when death approached Jacob?" *Shuhada'* means those present, those who saw. The question is rhetorical and the expected answer is no: you were not there. So on what basis do you make claims about what Jacob's religion was? The one reporting it is Allah, who was. This is prepared for at verse 140, where the question becomes explicit: "Are you more knowing, or is Allah?"

*Idh qala li-banihi ma ta'buduna min ba'di* — "when he said to his sons: what will you worship after me?"

This is the verse's centre of gravity, and it is one of the most searching scenes in the Qur'an.

Consider what a dying man has available to think about. His estate, his arrangements, his regrets, the people he is leaving, the pain, the fear. And the thing this man asks about is: what will you worship when I am gone?

And note the form. He does not deliver a lecture and he does not extract a promise. He asks a question. That is the model. A question asked at the right moment obliges the one answering to produce the commitment from himself, and a commitment a person has spoken in his own voice holds him in a way that an imposed instruction does not. Jacob had already instructed them at verse 132; here, at the end, he asks — and the asking is what converts instruction into confession.

*Qalu na'budu ilahaka wa ilaha aba'ika Ibrahima wa Isma'ila wa Ishaqa ilahan wahidan* — "they said: we worship your God and the God of your fathers, Abraham, Ishmael and Isaac — one God."

Now, the crux, which the commentators addressed directly: **Ishmael is named among the "fathers" of Jacob, but Ishmael was Jacob's uncle, not his father.** Ishmael and Isaac were brothers, sons of Abraham; Jacob was Isaac's son. So on the strict sense of *ab*, the listing is wrong.

Two things resolve it, and both are established. First, in Arabic *ab* is used of the paternal uncle as well as the father, and of forefathers generally. The Qur'an uses the same word this way at 12:38, where Yusuf says: "And I have followed the religion of my fathers [*aba'i*], Abraham, Isaac and Jacob" — and Abraham is Yusuf's great-grandfather, and Jacob his father. So *aba'* there plainly means forefathers, and it is the same word used the same way here.

Second — and this is the argumentative payoff — the inclusion of Ishmael is deliberate and it is doing work. Ishmael is the ancestor of the northern Arabs; Isaac is the ancestor of the Israelites. By naming both in the single creed of the dying patriarch, the verse places both branches of Abraham's family under one God and one religion. The descendants of Ishmael and the descendants of Isaac are here confessing the same *ilah*. That is the argument of verse 135 stated in advance, by the patriarch himself rather than by the Qur'an.

Note also the order. Ishmael is named before Isaac, as he is throughout the Qur'an (2:136, 2:140, 3:84, 4:163) — by birth order, since Ishmael was the firstborn. And note the answer's own grammar: *na'budu ilahaka* — "we worship **your** God." Not a new God, not our own God, not a God appropriate to our generation. The same God, carried forward. Continuity is the content of their answer.

*Ilahan wahidan* — one God. The phrase is placed at the end as a climax and as a qualifier on everything before it: the God of Abraham, Ishmael and Isaac is *one* God, so the plurality of names does not imply a plurality of objects.

*Wa nahnu lahu muslimun* — "and to Him we submit." Note that *lahu* — to Him — comes before the verb's complement, which in Arabic gives restriction: to **Him** we submit, and to no other. This is the third time this exact clause has closed a statement in five verses (133, and in the believers' creed at 136; and at 3:84), and it is the thread that ties the patriarchs to the Muslim community: the same verb, the same preposition, the same restriction.

For practice, three things.

The first is the deathbed question, and it is the kind of thing a person can act on today. What do you ask the people you are responsible for, when it matters most? The question "what will you worship after me?" is not morbid; it is the most accurate summary of what a parent is for. Jacob did not ask "will you remember me?" or "will you keep the property?" or "will you be successful?" He asked about worship — because everything else follows from whatever the answer to that is.

The second is the *method* of the question. Ask rather than announce. Most parents and teachers announce, and are baffled when the announcement does not take. Jacob instructed at verse 132 and asked at verse 133, and it is the asking that produced the confession recorded here. A question makes the other person say it, and what a person says himself becomes his.

And the third is the Ishmael point, which is far from academic. Both branches of Abraham's family are named in one creed on the lips of the dying patriarch. Every later attempt to make the God of Abraham the property of one lineage, one people or one community is answered by this verse — because the man being claimed named both branches himself.

## Verse 134

**"That was a community that had already gone before. For them is what they earned and for you is what you have earned. And you will not be accountable for what they have done."**

In simple terms: that was a community that has gone by. It has what it earned and you have what you have earned, and you will not be questioned about what they did.

*Tilka ummatun qad khalat* — "that was a community that has passed." *Ummah* here means a generation, a community of a time. *Khalat* is to pass away, to be bygone, to have gone before.

This verse is repeated verbatim at verse 141, and the repetition should be noticed: the surah states it once after the patriarchs' testimony and once after the mention of the earlier communities, closing both halves of the argument with the same principle.

*Laha ma kasabat wa lakum ma kasabtum* — "for it is what it earned, and for you is what you earned." *Kasabat* is from the root meaning to earn or acquire by one's own effort — the same word used at verse 79 for what the forgers of scripture earned, and at verse 81 for what a person's own hands have earned.

*Wa la tus'aluna 'amma kanu ya'malun* — "and you will not be asked about what they used to do."

**And here is the principle, which is one of the load-bearing beams of the whole religion: no inherited guilt and no inherited merit.**

The Qur'an states it in this form repeatedly and without exception: "And no bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another" (6:164, 35:18, 17:15, 53:38); "And that there is not for man except that [good] for which he strives" (53:39); "Every soul, for what it has earned, is retained" (52:21, 74:38). This is the verse that rules out the doctrine of inherited sin — a point already established in this surah's opening passage on Adam — but it is equally and more immediately the verse that rules out inherited *credit*.

And that is exactly how the surah is using it here. The Children of Israel claimed standing by descent from the prophets. This verse says: what your ancestors earned is theirs. It is not on your account and it is not in your favour. Every soul is credited with its own earnings and nothing else.

For practice, this verse cuts in two directions, and most people use it in only one.

The direction people know is the comfort: you are not answerable for what your people did. Your family's failures, your community's history, your nation's crimes, your ancestors' errors — none of it is charged to you. That is a genuine and enormous relief, and it is the answer to a great deal of inherited shame, including the specifically religious shame carried by people whose families lapsed or who come from communities with hard histories. The account is individual.

The direction people forget is the harder one: **you are not credited with what your people did either.** Your grandfather's scholarship is not on your account. Your community's golden age is not on your account. Being born into the ummah is not on your account, any more than being born into the people of the Book was on theirs. And this is precisely the claim the surah has been dismantling since verse 111 — the claim to Paradise by belonging — and it is the same claim verse 124 disposed of in its own terms: the covenant is not reached by wrongdoers.

So the two verses belong together and should be read as one statement. Verse 124 says the covenant is not inherited. Verse 134 says neither is anything else.

There is a third application that is worth naming because it is uncomfortable. The early generations of this community are not on our account either. The first three generations are extolled in the authentic Sunnah and no Muslim disputes their station, but their standing does not transfer. A community that has inherited a magnificent history and produces nothing is in the position this verse describes: it has what it earned, and we have what we have earned.

And the fourth is the release in the last clause. *Wa la tus'aluna* — you will not be asked about them. That is not only a statement about the Hereafter; it is a statement about this life. You are not required to answer for your predecessors, defend them, or explain them. You are required to answer for what is in front of you. That is a lighter burden than most religious people carry, and carrying less of it is not disloyalty.

## Verse 135

**"The Jews and Christians each say, “Follow our faith to be ˹rightly˺ guided.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “No! We follow the faith of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.”"**

In simple terms: they say, become Jews or become Christians and you will be guided. Say: no — rather, the religion of Abraham, who inclined wholly to the truth, and who was not one of those who associate partners with God.

This is the verse the whole passage has been moving toward, and it is one of the most consequential in the Qur'an.

*Wa qalu kunu hudan aw nasara tahtadu* — "and they say: be Jews or Christians, and you will be guided." On the two names: *hud* is commonly derived from Judah, and the Arabic lexicographers also connect it to the verb *hada*, to turn back or return in repentance. *Nasara* is connected to *nasr*, help and support — and the Qur'an supplies the occasion for that name at 61:14, where Jesus asks "Who are my supporters [*ansari*] unto Allah?" and the disciples answer "We are the supporters [*ansar*] of Allah."

Note that neither name began as a term of abuse. On the transmitted derivations they mean "those who turned back" and "the helpers." The question the verse raises is not about the names but about the claim attached to them — that belonging to one of these named communities is the condition of being guided.

*Qul bal millata Ibrahima* — "Say: rather, the religion of Abraham." *Bal* is a reversal, not a refinement: it cancels what came before and substitutes. Not this, not that — but the *millah* of Abraham, which precedes both.

***Hanifan*** — the word I introduced at verse 130, and it deserves full treatment here because it carries the verse.

*Hanif* is from a root meaning to incline, to turn aside. The lexicographers note that it was used of a physical turning — of the feet — before it was applied to religion. In the Qur'an it denotes the one who turns away from every false object of worship and inclines wholly to the pure worship of the One God. It occurs twelve times, and the two decisive instances are these:

3:67: "Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian, but he was one inclining toward truth [*hanifan*], a submitter [*musliman*], and he was not of the polytheists." Note that 3:67 closes with the identical clause that closes this verse — *wa ma kana mina al-mushrikin*. The two verses are a matched pair, and together they are the definitive Qur'anic statement on Abraham's religion.

30:30: "So direct your face toward the religion, inclining to truth [*hanifan*] — the *fitrah* of Allah upon which He has created [all] people. No change should there be in the creation of Allah. That is the correct religion." This is the verse that connects the *hanifiyyah* to the *fitrah* — the natural disposition. The religion of Abraham is not an innovation or a sect; it is the religion of the human being as created.

And 6:79 gives it in Abraham's own voice: "Indeed, I have turned my face toward He who created the heavens and the earth, inclining toward truth, and I am not of those who associate others with Allah."

*Wa ma kana mina al-mushrikin* — "and he was not of the polytheists." And here is the clause that makes the verse a threefold argument rather than a twofold one.

**All three groups claimed Abraham.** The Jews claimed him as their ancestor and as the first of their line. The Christians claimed him, as Paul's argument in the New Testament makes explicit — that the promise to Abraham is inherited by faith, not by law. And the pagan Arabs of Makkah claimed him too: they were the custodians of his House, they traced their descent to Ishmael, and they described their own religion as the religion of Abraham while worshipping idols around the Ka'bah.

And this verse answers all three at once. "Be Jews or Christians" — no. And to the third claim, which is not addressed in the first clause at all: *wa ma kana mina al-mushrikin* — and he was never a polytheist. The Quraysh are refuted by a clause they were not expecting, in a verse that appears to be about other people.

That is the structure of the argument and it is worth stating plainly, because it is the pattern for how the Qur'an handles contested inheritance:

**When several parties claim a common source, the source is the criterion and the parties are not.** Abraham predates Judaism, Christianity and the idolatry of Quraysh. He therefore cannot be owned by any of them, and the thing he actually was — one who turned from all falsehood to the one God — is the measure by which all three claims are found wanting.

For practice, this gives two things, one large and one immediate.

The large one is a method, and it is transferable. Whenever two or more parties claim a common founding figure — and this happens constantly, in religion and well outside it — the correct question is not "which party has the better claim?" but "what was the founder actually?" The founder is prior, the parties are derivative, and the derivative cannot be the measure of the prior.

The immediate one is the application to Muslim sectarianism, and it is exact. Every group in this community claims the Prophet ﷺ. The claim is worthless in itself, for the same reason the Jewish, Christian and Qurayshi claims to Abraham were worthless: the claimant is derivative and the one claimed is prior. The measure is the Prophet ﷺ himself, as he is actually known — through the Qur'an he brought and the Sunnah that is established from him — and not any party's account of him. The verse does not say "follow the party that quotes Abraham best." It says: *millata Ibrahima hanifan*. Go to the man.

And there is a caution that must be stated alongside this, exactly as it was stated at verse 111. The verse objects to a claim of exclusive guidance made without proof; it does not object to the existence of distinct communities, and it is not a dismissal of the Jewish and Christian traditions or of their attachment to Abraham. What it rejects is the proposition that belonging to the named community is what constitutes guidance. Guidance is constituted by the *hanifiyyah* — the turn to the one God — which is available to anyone and which no community can monopolise.

## Verse 136

**"Say, ˹O believers,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us; and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord. We make no distinction between any of them. And to Allah we all submit.”"**

In simple terms: say — we believe in Allah and in what was sent down to us, and in what was sent down to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and the tribes, and in what was given to Moses and Jesus, and in what was given to the prophets from their Lord. We separate none of them from another, and to Him we submit.

This is the creed of the Muslim community, and along with 3:84 (which is nearly identical) it is the most important single statement of what a Muslim must believe about revelation.

*Qulu amanna billahi* — "say: we believe in Allah." Note that the imperative is plural: **you all say**. This is not a confession the Prophet ﷺ makes on the community's behalf; it is the community's own confession, in its own voice. The creed belongs to every Muslim and every Muslim is required to be able to say it.

*Wa ma unzila ilayna* — "and what has been sent down to us" — the Qur'an.

*Wa ma unzila ila Ibrahima wa Isma'ila wa Ishaqa wa Ya'quba wa al-asbat* — "and what was sent down to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and the *asbat*."

*Al-asbat* is the crux of this list. The word is from a root meaning branches or offshoots, and it denotes the tribes — specifically the twelve tribes descended from the sons of Jacob, and by extension the grandsons. In Arabic *sibt* is used for a grandson, and it is the word applied in Islamic usage to al-Hasan and al-Husayn, the grandsons of the Prophet ﷺ.

The commentators differed on whether the *asbat* were themselves prophets. Some held that each of Jacob's twelve sons was a prophet, and read the revelation "to the *asbat*" accordingly. Others held that the *asbat* are the tribes and that what was "sent down to" them means what was sent down to the prophets among them. Both readings are transmitted; the disagreement does not affect the creed, which is belief in whatever Allah sent to them.

*Wa ma utiya Musa wa 'Isa* — "and what was given to Moses and Jesus." **Note that the verb changes**, from *unzila* (was sent down) to *utiya* (was given). The rhetoricians discussed this at length. The dominant explanation is variation for eloquence — a known and valued feature of Arabic style, avoiding the repetition of the same verb five times in one sentence. Some commentators looked for a distinction, suggesting that *inzal* (sending down) is used of what came as a complete revealed book whereas *itiya'* (being given) is broader. That distinction is not established, and the safer reading is the rhetorical one: the change carries no difference in what is required to believe.

What *is* required, and this is a point of creed that is often muddled, needs stating precisely. The Muslim is required to believe that Allah revealed to Moses the Torah and to Jesus the Injil, as genuine revelation from Him. What the Muslim is *not* required to believe is that every statement in the texts currently held by Jews and Christians is that original revelation. The surah has already established why: verse 75 spoke of a party who heard the word of Allah and then distorted it after understanding it, and verse 79 spoke of those who wrote the book with their own hands and then said "this is from Allah." So the belief is in the revelation as it came — *min rabbihim*, "from their Lord," as this verse specifies — and not in every subsequent claim about it.

*La nufarriqu bayna ahadin minhum* — "we make no distinction between any of them." This is the operative clause, and the Qur'an states its meaning unmistakably at 4:150–152:

"Indeed, those who disbelieve in Allah and His messengers and wish to discriminate between Allah and His messengers and say, 'We believe in some and disbelieve in others,' and wish to adopt a way in between — those are the disbelievers, truly... But they who believe in Allah and His messengers and do not discriminate between any of them — to those He is going to give their rewards."

That is the commentary on this clause, and it names the error exactly: "we believe in some and disbelieve in others." The creed requires accepting all of them, and accepting them as messengers.

*Wa nahnu lahu muslimun* — "and to Him we submit." The same closing the sons of Jacob used at verse 133.

And note the structure of the surah: this verse states the creed at its centre and 2:285 states it at its close — "We make no distinction between any of His messengers." The surah opens the confession here, at the climax of the argument about who owns Abraham, and seals it at the end. That is deliberate composition, and it means the verse should be read as one of the pillars of the whole surah.

For practice, three things.

The first is the test the verse supplies, and it is the sharpest available for the claim made in verse 135. You cannot claim Abraham while rejecting any of the messengers who came through his line. The claim to the founder is validated only by accepting everything the founder's line brought. The People of the Book failed it by rejecting Muhammad ﷺ; and the Muslim community fails it wherever it treats any messenger as optional.

The second is the precision about the earlier scriptures, which prevents two opposite errors. The error of suspicion — treating the Torah and the Injil as fabrications with no revealed origin — is a failure of this creed. And the error of indiscriminate acceptance — treating every present text as untouched revelation — is not required by it, and the surah itself rules it out. The position is narrower than both: belief in the revelation as it came from their Lord.

And the third is the plural imperative. *Qulu* — you say it. This is not the creed of the specialists; it is what every Muslim says, and a Muslim who cannot say it in his own words has something to learn. It is worth memorising and it is worth being able to recite in a sentence.

## Verse 137

**"So if they believe in what you believe, then they will indeed be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, they are simply opposed ˹to the truth˺. But Allah will spare you their evil. For He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: if they come to believe in the same way you believe, then they are guided. And if they turn away, then they are only in schism — and Allah will be enough for you against them. He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

*Fa-in amanu bi-mithli ma amantum bihi fa-qadi ihtadaw* — "so if they believe in the like of what you have believed in, then they are guided."

The crux is *bi-mithli* — "in the **like** of." Why not simply "in what you have believed"?

The commentators and grammarians gave two answers. Some treated *mithl* as an emphatic insertion with no independent meaning, so that the sense is simply "if they believe in what you believe." Others — and this is the reading that accounts better for the word's presence — took it as specifying *kind* rather than identity: if they believe with a belief corresponding to yours in its foundation, meaning the oneness of God and the acceptance of all the messengers without distinction. The second reading has the advantage of explaining why the word is there at all, and of being consistent with the rest of the Qur'an, which holds that communities differ in their laws while sharing their foundation (5:48: "To each of you We prescribed a law and a method").

Either way the practical criterion is the same and it is stated in the preceding verse: one God, and no distinction between the messengers.

*Wa in tawallaw fa-innama hum fi shiqaq* — "and if they turn away, then they are only in *shiqaq*." *Shiqaq* is from the root meaning to split, to be in two halves. It denotes opposition, schism, being ranged in a camp against you.

The point of the word is that turning away is not presented as neutrality. Once the creed has been stated, there is no third position of simple non-involvement. A person either holds it or is ranged apart from it, and the verse names that ranging honestly.

But — and this must be said firmly, because the verse is regularly misread — *shiqaq* here describes their stance toward the message. It is not a ruling on how they are to be treated. The law of civil conduct toward those who do not fight you is set out at 60:8: "Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, Allah loves those who act justly." That verse was noted at 2:105 and it governs here too. Being told that someone is in opposition to your creed is not permission to treat him unjustly.

*Fa-sayakfikahumu Allahu* — "and Allah will suffice you against them." And note carefully what the verse does **not** say. It does not say to fight them, or to coerce them, or to punish them, or to argue them into submission. It says: Allah will be enough for you against them.

That is a significant statement in a passage that has been stern, and it is the same principle stated at verse 120 in the previous batch and at 2:272 and 88:21–22: the outcome belongs to Allah, not to you. The Prophet ﷺ is not instructed to compel acceptance. He is instructed to state the creed and to leave the consequence to God.

*Wa huwa al-sami'u al-'alim* — "and He is the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing." And the two Names are chosen for the situation. *Al-Sami'*, the All-Hearing: He hears every argument made against you and every word you say in response, and He hears what you cannot answer. *Al-'Alim*, the All-Knowing: He knows what is in their hearts and what is in yours, and He knows how it ends.

For practice, this verse is a manual for the person who has stated the truth and been refused.

The first thing it gives is the criterion, which prevents both laxity and severity. Laxity is prevented by *bi-mithli ma amantum* — the faith has content, and content can be failed. Severity is prevented by *fa-sayakfikahum Allah* — the consequence is not yours to execute. Hold both.

The second is the release in the sufficiency clause, and it is the direct cure for the exhaustion described at verse 119 in the previous batch, where the Prophet ﷺ was told that he is only a bearer of good news and a warner and is not accountable for whether people accept. This verse gives the same release in stronger terms: not only are you not accountable, you are not even required to manage the outcome. Allah suffices.

And the third is the naming of the two attributes as an actual practice. When someone argues with you about the religion and will not be moved, the verse's answer is to say: *al-Sami', al-'Alim*. He heard it. He knows. That is not a pious evasion; it is the recognition that there is a record being kept by someone competent, and that your job was the conveying.

## Verse 138

**"This is the ˹natural˺ Way of Allah. And who is better than Allah in ordaining a way? And we worship ˹none but˺ Him."**

In simple terms: the colouring of Allah — and who gives a better colouring than Allah? And it is Him we worship.

*Sibghata Allahi* — "the *sibghah* of Allah." *Sibghah* is from a root meaning to dye, to dip, to immerse something in colour so that it takes the colour through.

And the background the commentators transmitted for this verse matters. The Christians of the time practised baptism — immersion in water — and it is reported that they would immerse their children and say that the child had thereby become a true Christian. The word *sibghah* is precisely a dip or a dye, so the Qur'an's answer lands on their own term: you have a *sibghah*, and it is a physical one performed on the body. The real *sibghah* is the *sibghah* of Allah.

That transmitted background should be held alongside a second reading, which some commentators gave: that *sibghah* here simply means the religion, so that the sense is "the religion of Allah, and who is better than Allah in religion?" The two readings converge rather than compete. The *sibghah* is the faith Allah gives, and what it does is colour the person. And some commentators connected it to the *fitrah* of 30:30 — the natural disposition upon which Allah created people — which fits: the *sibghah* is what is there by creation and restored by faith, not what is added by a rite.

**And the image is the whole point, and it is one of the most powerful in the Qur'an.**

A dye is not a coating. Paint sits on a surface and can flake. A dye penetrates the fibre — the colour goes through the material, and once it has gone through, there is no separating the colour from the thing coloured. That is what the verse claims about real religion: it is not a mark you carry, it is what you have become.

And that is the answer to everything the surah has been arguing since verse 111. Every claim in this passage has been external: "we are the people of the Book," "we are the children of Abraham," "none will enter Paradise except...," "be Jews or Christians," "we are baptised." Every one of them is a claim about a mark. And the Qur'an has answered each by demanding the *burhan* (111), by pointing to the heart's actual state (88, 93), by naming the founder's actual religion (135), and — here, in one word — by relocating the whole question from the mark to the fibre.

*Wa man ahsanu mina Allahi sibghata* — "and who is better than Allah in *sibghah*?" The question is comparative and the answer is contained in it. If religion is a colouring, then the colours available are the ones the world offers and the one Allah gives, and the comparison is not close.

*Wa nahnu lahu 'abidun* — "and we are worshippers of Him." *'Abidun* is an intensive form: those whose worship is their defining activity. And again the preposition comes first: *lahu* — Him, and no other.

For practice, this short verse carries more than almost any other in the batch, and it should be used as a diagnostic.

The first application is the straightforward one. Religion is what has penetrated, not what has been applied. A person can carry every external marker — the name, the family, the community, the attendance, the dress, the vocabulary — and be uncoloured. And a person can carry very little of the external and be thoroughly dyed. The verse's question is not "what are you called?" but "what colour are you?"

And here is the test, which is concrete and hard to argue with: **what do you revert to?** A dyed fabric does not revert. Take it into a different light, wash it, wear it, stress it — the colour is the colour. So: what do you revert to under pressure, when you are angry, when no one is watching, when it costs you money, when it costs you a relationship, when you are frightened? That is the colour. Not what you say at your best.

The second application is to how we understand religious rites — and this is where the verse cuts against a misuse in both directions.

Against the misuse of treating a rite as automatic: no rite saves anyone by having been performed. The verse's whole thrust is that the physical act is not the reality.

But equally, and this must be said just as plainly: the verse is not a dismissal of rites. The *sibghah* of Allah is given, and it is given through the means He appointed — the prayer, the fasting, the charity, the pilgrimage, the remembrance, the whole apparatus. The verse is a warning against treating the means as though the end were automatic, not a licence to dispense with the means. A dye requires immersion. The critique is of those who think that being dipped once settles the matter regardless of what happens afterwards.

And the third is the beauty of the image as a description of grace. You do not dye yourself. The dyer dyes. *Sibghata Allahi* — the colouring **of Allah**, attributed to Him. This is why the verse is recited with a particular tenderness in the tradition: what is being described is something done to you, by the One who does it better than anyone.

## Verse 139

**"Say, “Would you dispute with us about Allah, while He is our Lord and your Lord? We are accountable for our deeds and you for yours. And we are devoted to Him ˹alone˺."**

In simple terms: say — do you dispute with us about Allah, when He is our Lord and your Lord? For us are our deeds and for you are your deeds, and we are sincere to Him alone.

*Qul a-tuhajjuna fi Allahi* — "say: do you argue with us concerning Allah?" The verb is from the root of *hujjah*, a proof, and of *hajj*, the pilgrimage: to dispute with arguments, to plead a case. And note what the dispute is *about*: *fi Allahi* — concerning God. Not about a law, not about a custom, not about a tribe. About God Himself — who He is, what He has said, who belongs to Him.

*Wa huwa rabbuna wa rabbukum* — "while He is our Lord and your Lord." And here is the verse's method, and it is worth learning as a method.

Before disputing the conclusion, the verse identifies the shared premise. He is the Lord of us *and* the Lord of you. That is agreed. Both parties affirm the one God who is Lord of all. The disagreement is therefore not about the premise; it is about what follows from it.

This is the same move the Qur'an makes at 3:64: "Say: O People of the Scripture, come to a word common between us and you [*sawa'in baynana wa baynakum*]: that we worship none but Allah and associate nothing with Him and not take one another as lords besides Allah." Find the common word first. Dispute from it, not around it.

*Wa lana a'maluna wa lakum a'malukum* — "and for us are our deeds, and for you are your deeds." This is the formula of mutual disavowal, and it appears in several forms in the Qur'an: 10:41 — "And if they deny you, then say: for me are my deeds and for you are your deeds. You are disassociated from what I do, and I am disassociated from what you do"; 109:6 — "For you is your religion, and for me is my religion"; 34:25; 42:15.

**And this clause is the most misused verse-form in the Qur'an, so it needs stating precisely, in both directions.**

What it means: each of us is accountable for his own deeds, and I am not answerable for yours. That is a statement about **accountability**, and it is the same principle as verse 134.

What it does **not** mean: that the claims are equally valid. This verse cannot be read as religious relativism, because the surah has just spent four verses establishing that the truth is one (135–137), that turning away is *shiqaq* (137), and that the creed has content which can be failed (136). A verse that says "our deeds are ours and yours are yours" in a passage that has just said "if they turn away they are in opposition" is not saying the options are equivalent. It is saying: I am not going to be responsible for your choice, and I am not going to compel you out of it.

The clearest case of the misuse is 109:6, "for you is your religion and for me is my religion," which is routinely quoted as a charter of pluralism. In its own surah it is a declaration of dissociation from *shirk* specifically, addressed to the Quraysh — and the transmitted occasion is that they proposed a compromise: worship our gods for a year and we will worship yours for a year. The verse rejects the compromise. It does not bless the alternative religion; it declines the trade.

*Wa nahnu lahu mukhlisun* — "and we are sincere to Him." *Mukhlisun* is from the root of *ikhlas*: purity, exclusivity, making the religion for God alone.

And note where the verse **ends**. Not with the argument, and not with the disavowal, but with *ikhlas*. That is the model for how a religious dispute is closed: state the shared premise, assign accountability, and then turn back to sincerity to God. The argument is over. What remains is the worship.

For practice, this verse is the most immediately usable thing in the batch, because religious argument is constant and almost nobody knows how to end one.

The first thing it teaches is to locate the shared premise before disputing. Most religious arguments — with family, online, across communities — proceed as though everything were in dispute. Very little usually is. Both parties in the original dispute affirmed that God is one and is the Lord of all; the disagreement was downstream of that. Naming what is actually agreed is not a concession; it is the only way to find out where the real disagreement is, and it very often turns out to be smaller than the noise.

The second is the accountability formula used correctly. "For you your deeds and for me mine" is a legitimate and dignified way to end a dispute, and it is Qur'anic. But it must be used in its meaning — a statement of responsibility and a refusal to coerce — and not smuggled in as a claim that the other person's position is as good as yours. Those are different claims, and conflating them is how people end up holding two incompatible beliefs at once.

And the third is the ending. The last word in the verse is *mukhlisun* — sincere to Him. There is a point at which the argument stops and the worship continues, and the verse places that point before the dispute has been resolved rather than after. Most of us argue past it. The cost of arguing past it is not usually that we lose the argument; it is that the argument becomes the thing we are doing instead of the religion. Ending in *ikhlas* is a way of refusing that exchange.

## Verse 140

**"Or do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants were all Jews or Christians?” Say, “Who is more knowledgeable: you or Allah?” Who does more wrong than those who hide the testimony they received from Allah? And Allah is never unaware of what you do."**

In simple terms: or do you claim that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and the tribes were Jews or Christians? Say: is it you who know better, or Allah? And who does greater wrong than someone who conceals a testimony he holds from Allah? And Allah is not unaware of what you do.

*Am taquluna inna Ibrahima wa Isma'ila wa Ishaqa wa Ya'quba wa al-asbata kanu hudan aw nasara* — "or do you say that Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob and the tribes were Jews or Christians?"

The *am* — "or" — joins this to the preceding verse: do you merely dispute about God (139), or do you go further and make this claim (140)? The second is the stronger charge and it is what the whole passage has been driving at.

**And the argument is chronological, and it is decisive.** Judaism takes its name from Judah — the son of Jacob — or, in the view of many historians, as a religious category it crystallises considerably later still, in the Persian and post-exilic periods. Christianity takes its name from Christ, more than a millennium and a half after Abraham. Both named communities therefore postdate every person in this list. To say that Abraham was a Jew or a Christian is to assign him to a category that did not exist in his lifetime.

This is not a rhetorical flourish. It is a simple argument about time, and it cannot be answered. The claimants are later than the one they claim, so the claim cannot be a description of him; it can only be a retrojection of themselves onto him.

*Qul a-antum a'lamu ami Allahu* — "say: are you more knowing, or is Allah?" The *am* here is the "equal" *am*, the one that follows an interrogative and offers an alternative: is it you, or is it Allah? The question does not request information; it exposes the structure of the claim. Allah has stated, at 133 and 135, what the patriarchs' religion was. To assert otherwise is not to differ with an argument; it is to prefer your own knowledge to His.

The question is therefore one of the most useful instruments in the Qur'an, and it applies far beyond its original addressees. Whenever a human judgement is set above a clear text — whenever someone says, in effect, "I know this is what the revelation says, but I know better" — this is the question that meets him.

*Wa man azlamu mimman katama shahadatan 'indahu mina Allahi* — "and who does more wrong than one who conceals a testimony he has from Allah?"

*Man azlamu* — "who is more wrongful" — is the superlative of censure, and the surah used the identical form at verse 114: "And who is more unjust than one who prevents the mosques of Allah from His name being mentioned therein." The parallel is telling: the gravest wrongs in this surah are those that interpose between people and the truth about God.

*Katama* is to conceal, to hide. *Shahadah* is a testimony — knowledge one has witnessed and can bear witness to. *'Indahu min Allah*: in his possession, from Allah.

So the accusation is precise: **they possess knowledge, from their own scripture, and they suppress it.** And that knowledge is the thing the surah has been naming — that the patriarchs were submitters to one God (133, 135), and that the Prophet ﷺ is described in their Books (2:146: "Those to whom We gave the Scripture know him as they know their own sons"). The general obligation to transmit rather than suppress is stated at 3:187: "And when Allah took a covenant from those who were given the Scripture: 'You must make it clear to the people and not conceal it.'"

And the warning is severe, as it was at 2:159: "Indeed, those who conceal what We sent down of clear proofs and guidance after We made it clear for the people in the Scripture — those are cursed by Allah and cursed by those who curse."

**Now the guardrail, which is essential and must not be skipped.** This verse is Allah's accusation, in His knowledge, against specific people who possess a specific testimony and suppress it. It is not a licence for Muslims to level the charge at Jews and Christians generally, and still less at any individual. The verse's own wording requires knowledge actually possessed — *shahadatan 'indahu* — so it cannot be applied to a person who genuinely does not know. And this surah has repeatedly forbidden declaring any particular individual's destination. The accusation belongs to the One who knows what is concealed; our business is the conveying, not the verdict.

*Wa ma Allahu bi-ghafilin 'amma ta'malun* — "and Allah is not unaware of what you do." The negation with *bi-* is emphatic: He is not, at all, heedless. The same closing appears at 2:85 in this surah.

And note that it cuts both ways, which is why the Qur'an closes with it rather than with the threat. To the concealer it is a warning: nothing you suppress is lost to Him. And to the person who has been wronged by concealment, by distortion, by a testimony suppressed — it is a comfort. The record is complete. It always was.

For practice, three things.

The first is the chronological argument, and its application to Muslims is direct and uncomfortable. We retroject too, constantly. "The Prophet ﷺ was a nationalist." "The early community were socialists." "The Salaf were of my school." "The companions were really proto-modern." Every one of these is the same error as saying Abraham was a Jew or a Christian: assigning a later category to a prior figure. The figures are prior; the categories are ours. And the question that meets it is the verse's own: are you more knowing, or is Allah?

The second is the question itself as a standing instrument. "Are you more knowing, or is Allah?" is the correct response — inwardly, and sometimes outwardly — to any position that requires setting aside a clear text in favour of a judgement. It is not a conversation-ender to be deployed aggressively; it is a way of making the structure of the claim visible, because most people who override a text have not noticed that overriding is what they are doing.

And the third is the concealment clause turned inward, which is its proper use for a Muslim. The testimony in your possession is not only a matter for others. Every Muslim who knows something of the religion and stays silent when it is needed is holding a *shahadah*. The verse's severity is a warning about that, and it is a warning that most readers need far more than the outward-facing accusation does. And the closing clause is the companion to it: nothing is hidden from Him. Which means the silence is recorded, and so is the speaking.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

This batch completes the argument that began at verse 124 and it does so in three movements.

**The patriarchs' own testimony (131–133).** Abraham was commanded to submit and submitted to the Lord of all the worlds (131). He and Jacob bequeathed the religion to their sons, charging them not to meet death in any state but submission (132). And Jacob, dying, asked his sons what they would worship after him, and they named the God of Abraham, Ishmael and Isaac — one God — and declared their submission (133). The people who claim Abraham are here shown his religion in his own family's words, and it is submission to the One God, with both branches of his descendants named in a single creed.

**The principle that settles it (134).** The past community has passed. Every generation earns its own. No one is credited with his ancestors' merit and no one is charged with their guilt. This is the verse that removes the ground from the claim of inherited standing, and it states as a general principle what verse 124 stated about the covenant specifically.

**The argument with the claimants (135–140).** "Be Jews or Christians" — no: the religion of Abraham, a *hanif*, who was not a polytheist, which answers all three claimants at once (135). The positive creed: belief in all revelation and no distinction between any messenger (136). The terms: if they believe the same they are guided, and if they turn away the consequence belongs to Allah, who suffices (137). The nature of the thing disputed: not a mark but a colouring, the *sibghah* of Allah, which penetrates rather than coats (138). The manner of the dispute: share the premise, assign accountability, and end in sincerity (139). And the closing case: the patriarchs were not Jews or Christians, because those names postdate them — and who is more wrongful than one who conceals a testimony he holds from Allah? (140)

Three threads run through the whole. First, **the claim is not the reality** — not the claim to Abraham by descent (124, 134), not the claim of a named community (135), not the claim of a performed rite (138). Second, **the prior measures the derivative** — Abraham measures the Jews, Christians and Quraysh; the Prophet ﷺ measures every group that claims him; a founder measures his successors. And third, **the outcome is not yours** — not for the Prophet ﷺ (137), not for anyone who argues and is refused (139), and the record is kept regardless (140).

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them, which is worse than omitting them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: whether the saying in verse 132 belongs to Jacob alone or to both patriarchs; whether the *asbat* were themselves prophets; whether *mithl* in verse 137 carries independent meaning or is emphatic; whether the shift from *unzila* to *utiya* in verse 136 is rhetorical or semantic; and whether *sibghah* in verse 138 means the religion or the act of colouring.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the background of Christian baptism as the occasion of verse 138, the occasion of 109:6 in the Quraysh's proposed compromise, and the derivations of *hud* and *nasara*. The derivation of "Jew" from Judah has been given as the common etymology, and the late crystallisation of Judaism as a religious category has been stated as the view of many historians rather than as a settled fact, since the dating is itself debated among specialists.

Four boundaries have been observed. On verse 134: the principle has been stated in both directions — no inherited guilt *and* no inherited merit — because stating only the comfortable half is how the verse is usually misused. On verse 137: the promise that Allah suffices has been kept distinct from any instruction to coerce, and 60:8 has been restated as the governing rule for civil conduct. On verse 139: the disavowal formula has been explicitly distinguished from relativism, and 109:6 has been given in its own context rather than in its popular misreading. And on verse 140: the charge of concealing the testimony has been confined to Allah's knowledge of those who actually possess it, with the standing rule maintained that no individual's destination may be declared.

The chronological argument of verse 140 has also been turned inward on the Muslim community as well as outward on its original addressees, because the error it identifies — retrojecting a later category onto a prior figure — is one that every community, including this one, commits.
