# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 81–90

These ten verses answer the claim that closed the last batch — "the Fire will never touch us except for a few days" — with the reality of two destinations and the two conditions that lead to them (81–82). Then the surah returns to the covenant and spells out its contents in full: worship of one God, kindness to parents and kin and orphans and the poor, good speech, the prayer and the zakah (83), and the two prohibitions against bloodshed and expulsion (84). Then the breach, in detail, and one of the most quoted charges in the whole Qur'an: do you believe in part of the Book and deny part of it? (85–86). Then the history — messenger after messenger, Jesus and the clear signs, and the recurring response of arrogance (87). And finally the diagnosis of the present: they claim their hearts are sealed, they rejected the very Book they had been praying for, and the real motive is named in a single word — envy that Allah's bounty went to someone else (88–90). The passage moves from the criterion to the covenant to the breach to the motive, and the motive it lands on is not intellectual at all.

## Verse 81

**"But no! Those who commit evil and are engrossed in sin will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."**

In simple terms: their claim in verse 80 is refuted outright. Those who commit evil and are completely enveloped by it belong to the Fire, and remain there.

*Bala* — "But no!" The word is a particle of reversal, used to affirm a positive after a negation. It opens the verse with a single syllable that demolishes the whole of verse 80. They had said the Fire would barely graze them, briefly. *Bala*: it is not as you say. Here is the truth.

*Man kasaba sayyi'atan* — "whoever earns an evil." *Kasaba* means to acquire by one's own action, and the Qur'an uses it consistently for what a person brings on himself. The same root closed verse 79 — "woe to them for what they earn" — and the same root appears in the principle at 2:134: "It will have what it earned, and you will have what you have earned." Nothing here is imposed.

*Wa ahatat bihi khati'atuhu* — "and his sin envelops him." This is the crux of the verse, and the whole of its difficulty and its comfort live in it. The verb *ahata* means to surround, to encompass from every side, so that nothing remains outside. What does it mean for a sin to surround a person?

The commentators gave two readings, and they converge.

The first is that *al-sayyi'ah* here means *shirk* — associating partners with Allah. A number of early authorities read the word this way, on the grounds that this is the one sin the Qur'an places beyond the others and the one for which the threat is stated unconditionally. On this reading the verse is about the person who dies upon shirk.

The second is that the envelopment is the point: the sin has closed in from every side, meaning the person persisted in it, made no opening for repentance, and died in that state. On this reading the verse is about the totality of the condition rather than the specific category of the sin.

The two readings meet in the same place: the subject of this verse is the person whose evil has become total, not the person who sinned. That distinction is not a softening. It is the whole difference between the religion of the Qur'an and a terror cult, and it is worth setting out carefully because this verse is quoted constantly by people who want it to mean something it does not.

Three errors have historically been built on verses like this one, and each has an answer.

The Kharijites held that anyone who commits a major sin leaves the religion and abides in the Fire forever. That reading is rejected by Ahl al-Sunnah on the plainest possible evidence: "Indeed, Allah does not forgive association with Him, but He forgives what is less than that for whom He wills" (4:48; repeated at 4:116). If every major sin were unforgivable, the second half of that verse would be meaningless. The Prophet ﷺ said, in the hadith recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim: "Whoever has an atom's weight of faith in his heart will be brought out of the Fire." And the hadith of intercession, mass-transmitted, states that people who knew Allah will be taken out of the Fire after having been in it.

The Murji'ites held the opposite error — that sin does not affect a person's faith at all, so that the believer need not fear. That reading is rejected because it evacuates the warnings in this very surah of content, and because the Companions and the early generations held fear and hope together without collapsing either.

The Mu'tazilites held an intermediate position — that the grave sinner is in a station between the two stations, neither believer nor disbeliever. That too is rejected by Ahl al-Sunnah, who hold that such a person is a believer deficient in his faith: *mu'min bi-imanihi, fasiq bi-kabiratihi* — a believer by virtue of his faith, a sinner by virtue of his major sin.

The Ahl al-Sunnah position, then: the person who dies upon faith bearing major sins that he did not repent of is under the will of Allah. If He wills, He punishes him for a time; if He wills, He forgives him outright. He does not leave the religion by sinning, and he does not abide in the Fire forever. What does put a person beyond forgiveness is dying upon *shirk* without repentance, and that is what this verse, read carefully, is describing.

One further detail is worth noting in the Arabic. The verse uses two different words for the wrong: *sayyi'ah* in "whoever earns an evil," and *khati'ah* in "his sin envelops him." The commentators discussed why. Some said they are synonyms used for variation; some said *khati'ah* is the graver of the two; some said the shift from one to the other tracks the movement from the act to the state the act produces. The last is the most useful: a person commits an evil deed, and if he keeps committing it, the deed becomes a condition, and the condition closes around him.

*Fa-ula'ika ashabu al-nari hum fiha khalidun* — "those are the companions of the Fire, abiding therein forever." *Ashab* means companions, those who belong to and keep company with something. The word is deliberately relational: they are not visitors to the Fire but its people.

Two applications carry this verse.

The first is the answer to cheap grace, which is what the verse is for. Verse 80 had shrunk the reckoning to a few days. Verse 81 restores it. There is a real destination, it is real, and the condition for it is real. A religion that cannot say this has nothing to offer anybody, because it has nothing to save anybody from.

The second is the pastoral one, and it is the one most often neglected. Read this verse carefully and it does not say "whoever sins." It says whoever is *enveloped*. The difference is the difference between a person who fell and got up, and a person who fell and built a house there. The image of the envelopment is the more searching of the two for most readers, because it describes a process rather than an event. Sin accumulates. A person who keeps deferring repentance is being encircled a little more each time, and the tragedy of the image is that the encirclement is invisible from the inside — he experiences his own closure as normal life. The way out is always the same and always available, and the Qur'an says so in the same breath as its severest warnings: "Say: O My servants who have transgressed against themselves, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Indeed, Allah forgives all sins" (39:53).

## Verse 82

**"And those who believe and do good will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever."**

In simple terms: those who believe and do righteous deeds belong to Paradise, and remain in it forever.

*Wa alladhina amanu wa 'amilu al-salihati ula'ika ashabu al-jannati hum fiha khalidun.* The verse is constructed as a perfect mirror of verse 81. *Ashab al-nar* against *ashab al-jannah*; *khalidun* against *khalidun*. Every element is matched except the destination and the condition.

This pairing is one of the Qur'an's most characteristic rhetorical forms, and it appears throughout the Book — the two parties of 82:13–14 and 83:18–22, the two paths of 90:10–20, the two outcomes of 92:5–10, the two records of 84:7–12, the two ends of 95:6. The form itself carries a meaning: the choices human beings face are genuinely binary at the level that matters, and the Qur'an will not blur the line between them.

*Amanu wa 'amilu al-salihat* — "believed and did righteous deeds." This is the pairing that appears more often than any other in the Qur'an — dozens and dozens of times, from 2:25 to 103:3. It is worth stating plainly what the repetition establishes: in the Qur'an's own vocabulary, faith is never asserted as sufficient by itself. Belief and deed are named together so consistently that separating them is separating what the text has joined. *Salihat* means deeds that are sound, whole, fitting — from a root meaning to be right and sound.

Now, an important observation about what this verse does *not* say. It says nothing about the believer who dies bearing unrepented major sins. The verse describes those who believe and do good, and promises them Paradise; it does not address the case in between. That silence is filled elsewhere — by 4:48, by the hadith of intercession, by the hadith of the atom's weight of faith — and the filling is what produces the Ahl al-Sunnah position described in the previous verse. A person who reads 2:82 as excluding every flawed believer from Paradise has made the same error as the Kharijites, and has put himself in a position where no one, including himself, can hope.

*Hum fiha khalidun* — "abiding therein forever." The permanence of Paradise for its people is a matter of consensus in Islam, and it stands in deliberate contrast to the discussion about the permanence of the Fire. The mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah, by consensus, holds that the disbelievers abide in the Fire forever. A minority position — attributed to some scholars, including in various forms to Ibn Taymiyyah and Ibn al-Qayyim, though the attribution itself is contested — held that the punishment of the Fire eventually comes to an end. That view is rejected by the overwhelming majority, and the relevant texts were noted at verse 24. I mention it here only so that a reader who encounters it elsewhere knows that it exists, that it is a minority view, and that it is not the position of the majority of the community.

For practice, two things.

The first is the symmetry as a tool. The surah has just given the reader two destinations and two conditions, side by side, in the plainest language available. That is not an accident of style; it is an invitation to self-examination. Which of these two descriptions applies to me? The question is not "am I perfect?" — the verse does not require perfection, and the tradition nowhere teaches that a believer is sinless. The question is about direction: is my life oriented toward belief expressed in deed, or toward evil accumulated into a condition? That question has an answer, and most people know it.

And the second is the permanence, which is the thing that makes the brevity of this life intelligible. Every difficulty a person endures has an expiry date; the two destinations named in these verses do not. The Qur'an states this pairing so often — the finite against the infinite — precisely because a human being who has internalised it lives differently from one who has not. Patience, generosity, restraint, the willingness to lose something now: all of these become rational the moment the two durations are actually compared, and all of them become irrational the moment they are not.

## Verse 83

**"And ˹remember˺ when We took a covenant from the children of Israel ˹stating˺, “Worship none but Allah; be kind to parents, relatives, orphans and the needy; speak kindly to people; establish prayer; and pay alms-tax.” But you ˹Israelites˺ turned away—except for a few of you—and were indifferent."**

In simple terms: Allah took a solemn covenant from the Children of Israel binding them to worship Him alone, to treat parents, relatives, orphans and the poor with excellence, to speak kindly to people, and to establish the prayer and give the zakah — and they turned away from it, all but a few.

The covenant contains eight items, and the order is not random.

**1. *La ta'buduna illa Allah*** — "worship none but Allah." A grammatical note: the prohibition is expressed with the indicative *la ta'buduna* ("you do not worship") rather than the jussive *la ta'budu* ("do not worship"). The commentators observed that the indicative gives it the force of a binding declaration of what they are held to — "you shall not worship any but Allah" — rather than a bare command, and some grammarians said the form stands in the position of the imperative. *'Ibadah* comes from a root meaning to be lowly and submissive; a *'abd* is a slave, and a *ma'bad* is a road made smooth by being trodden. Worship is therefore both the posture of total submission and the walking of a path already made level.

**2. *Wa bi-al-walidayni ihsanan*** — "and to parents, excellence." The placement is significant and should be noticed: kindness to parents is listed immediately after the worship of Allah. This is the most frequent pairing in the Qur'an after the pairing of belief and good deeds, appearing at 2:83, 4:36, 6:151, 17:23, 29:8, 31:14 and 46:15. That repetition means something: the obligation to one's parents is not a cultural sentiment the Qur'an happens to mention, it is structurally tied to the obligation to God.

The hadith states it just as plainly. In the narration recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim, the Prophet ﷺ was asked, "Which deed is most beloved to Allah?" He said, "The prayer at its proper time." "Then which?" "Kindness to parents." "Then which?" "Striving in the cause of Allah." And in the hadith recorded in both Sahihs, a man asked, "Who is most deserving of my good company?" He said, "Your mother." "Then who?" "Your mother." "Then who?" "Your mother." "Then who?" "Your father." The threefold repetition of the mother is one of the most famous numerical emphases in the hadith literature.

**3. *Wa dhi al-qurba*** — "and the near of kin." The maintenance of kinship ties, *silat al-rahim*, is among the most emphasised duties in Islam. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him maintain the ties of kinship" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). And in a hadith qudsi recorded by al-Bukhari, Allah said of the womb: "Whoever maintains you, I will maintain him, and whoever severs you, I will sever him." The hadith "The one who severs [the ties of kinship] will not enter Paradise" is recorded in both Sahihs.

**4. *Wa al-yatama*** — "and the orphans." *Yatim* in Arabic means specifically a child who has lost his father, because the father was the provider; the term is not used for a motherless child in its technical sense. The Qur'an's concern for orphans is one of its most striking and most repeated features, appearing at 2:177, 2:215, 2:220, 4:2, 4:6, 4:8, 4:10, 4:36, 6:152, 17:34, 89:17, 93:6, 93:9 and 107:2, among others. The Prophet ﷺ said: "I and the guardian of the orphan will be like this in Paradise," and he indicated his index and middle fingers with a slight gap between them (al-Bukhari). And the Qur'an's warning on the subject is among its severest: "Indeed, those who devour the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming into their bellies fire, and they will be burned in a Blaze" (4:10).

**5. *Wa al-masakin*** — "and the needy." *Miskin* comes from a root meaning to be still, to be motionless; the *miskin* is the one whose poverty has stilled him, brought him to a halt. The jurists discussed at length whether the *faqir* or the *miskin* is in the worse condition, and the schools differed; the difference affects the distribution of zakah and is one of those technical questions where no single answer should be dogmatically asserted outside its school.

**6. *Wa qulu lil-nasi husnan*** — "and speak to people good." Two things about this clause.

The first is a variant reading. The transmitted text is *husnan* (حُسْنًا), and a variant vocalisation with *hasanan* (حَسَنًا) is also transmitted. Both mean "good," with a subtle difference in intensity; the obligation is unaffected either way.

The second is the more consequential question: who are *al-nas*, "the people"? The majority of commentators read it in the most general sense — speak good to all people, with no restriction. A second reading, transmitted from some early authorities, takes it as referring specifically to Muhammad ﷺ: speak to him with fairness and do not be harsh with him. Both are held. The general reading is the dominant one and is the one the verse has always been used to support — and its placement in the list is the thing to notice.

Because look where it sits. Good speech is listed in the same covenant as the worship of Allah, the prayer, and the zakah. The surah is elevating a quotidian, easily-discounted obligation — how you talk to people — to the level of the pillars of the religion. That elevation is deliberate, and it should unsettle anyone who is scrupulous about the prayer and careless with his tongue. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day, let him speak good or remain silent" (al-Bukhari and Muslim), and he listed "a good word" among the daily charities every person owes for every joint of his body (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

**7. *Wa aqimu al-salah*** — "and establish the prayer." As noted at verse 3, *aqimu* means to set it up properly, to perform it as it should be performed, not merely to go through it.

**8. *Wa atu al-zakah*** — "and give the zakah." Noted at verse 43, with the caveat recorded there that a single verse never carries an entire ruling: the thresholds, rates, recipients and conditions are established from the Sunnah and the consensus of the jurists, not from any one verse alone.

Now, the single most important thing about this list for a reader of any background: **it is the same list the Qur'an gives the Muslims.** Monotheism, parents, kin, orphans, the poor, good speech, prayer, zakah — the moral core is identical across the revelations. The Qur'an says this explicitly: "He has ordained for you of religion what He enjoined upon Noah and that which We have revealed to you, and what We enjoined upon Abraham and Moses and Jesus — to establish the religion and not be divided therein" (42:13). The differences between the revealed laws are in their particulars; the substance is one religion given by one God to all of them.

*Thumma tawallaytum illa qalilan minkum wa antum mu'ridun* — "then you turned away, except a few of you, and you were averse." Note *illa qalilan minkum*, "except a few of you." This is now the fifth time in this passage that the surah has qualified a condemnation rather than applying it wholesale — "those who wronged" (59), "those among you who transgressed" (65), "a party of them" (75), "except a few of you" (83). The consistency is the point, and it is being modelled for the reader in the middle of an indictment. *Mu'ridun* is the active participle: you are people who habitually turn aside.

For practice, three things.

Take the list as an examination. Eight items, all of them binding in one form or another on every community that received revelation, including the Muslim one. Most people will find one or two they have never considered religious obligations at all. "Speak to people good" is usually the one.

Take the pairing of worship and parents seriously. The person who prays five times a day and speaks to his mother with contempt has a structural problem in his religion that no amount of extra prayer will fix, because the Qur'an tied the two together and did not permit one to substitute for the other.

And take the "except a few" as a discipline. Even here, in the act of recording a collective turning away, the text carves out the minority who did not. Whatever else this surah teaches about how to speak about communities, it does not teach generalisation.

## Verse 84

**"And ˹remember˺ when We took your covenant that you would neither shed each other’s blood nor expel each other from their homes, you gave your pledge and bore witness."**

In simple terms: they were bound by covenant not to kill one another and not to drive one another out of their homes, and they formally acknowledged this and stand as witnesses to it.

*La tasfikuna dima'akum* — "do not shed your blood." *Safaka* is the specific verb for spilling blood. Note the construction *dima'akum*, "your blood," meaning one another's — the same idiom as verse 54's *uqtulu anfusakum* and 4:29's *la taqtulu anfusakum*, where "yourselves" means "one another." The grammar itself encodes the point: the blood of your brother is your own blood.

*Wa la tukhrijuna anfusakum min diyarikum* — "and do not expel one another from your homes." Note that this is given equal weight with the killing. Expulsion from home is named alongside bloodshed as a capital breach. That equation is not incidental and it is not softened anywhere in the Qur'an: to drive a people from their homes is treated as a crime of the same order.

*Thumma aqrartum wa antum tashhadun* — "then you acknowledged, and you bear witness." *Aqrartum* is from a root meaning to be settled and fixed, and *iqrar* is the legal term for a formal affirmation or confession. *Wa antum tashhadun* — and you are witnesses. The commentators noted the force: they affirmed it with their own mouths, so they are witnesses against themselves. This is the basis of accountability throughout the Qur'an — a person who has acknowledged a commitment cannot afterwards plead that he did not know. The same principle appears at 7:172, where humanity attests to Allah's lordship before birth: "[Lest] you should say on the Day of Resurrection, 'Indeed, we were unaware of this.'"

The sanctity of blood is one of the great constants of the Qur'an, and the fullest statement of it is itself addressed to this same people: "Because of that, We decreed upon the Children of Israel that whoever kills a soul unless for a soul or for corruption [done] in the land — it is as if he had slain mankind entirely. And whoever saves one — it is as if he had saved mankind entirely" (5:32). And: "And do not kill the soul which Allah has forbidden, except by right" (17:33).

The Sunnah states the priority with equal force. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The first matter to be judged between people on the Day of Resurrection is blood" (al-Bukhari and Muslim, from Ibn Mas'ud). And he said: "It is not lawful to shed the blood of a Muslim who bears witness that there is no god but Allah and that I am the Messenger of Allah, except for one of three: a life for a life, the married adulterer, and the one who leaves his religion and separates from the community" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). And in a narration recorded by al-Bukhari: "Whoever kills a person under a covenant of protection without right will not smell the fragrance of Paradise, though its fragrance is found at a distance of forty years' journey."

For practice, this verse is unusually direct and has three layers.

The first is the gravity of what is normally treated lightly. Two things are named as capital breaches: taking life, and taking home. The second of those is treated with enormous casualness in the modern world. Eviction, displacement, the bureaucratic machinery that removes a family from the place it lives: the Qur'an places this in the same covenant as murder and does not grade it downwards. A reader who takes this verse seriously will find his politics changed by it.

The second is the internal application. The covenant was against shedding *each other's* blood. This is not a prohibition against foreign enemies; it is a prohibition against intra-community violence — the blood of the people who share your scripture, your tribe, your family, your sect. That is the killing the Qur'an most often condemns, because it is the killing most often committed. Sectarian violence, tribal bloodshed, the family feud that outlives the memory of its cause: all of it is the exact thing this covenant forbade, and all of it has been done by people who would have insisted they were religious.

And the third is the affirmation. *Thumma aqrartum wa antum tashhadun* — you acknowledged, and you are witnesses. Every person reading this surah has acknowledged things he knows to be true and is answerable for them. The verse's quiet point is that the problem is almost never that a person does not know. It is that he knows, has said he knows, and is now living as though he had not.

## Verse 85

**"But here you are, killing each other and expelling some of your people from their homes, aiding one another in sin and aggression; and when those ˹expelled˺ come to you as captives, you still ransom them—though expelling them was unlawful for you. Do you believe in some of the Scripture and reject the rest? Is there any reward for those who do so among you other than disgrace in this worldly life and being subjected to the harshest punishment on the Day of Judgment? For Allah is never unaware of what you do."**

In simple terms: having covenanted not to kill or expel one another, they did exactly that — backing each other up in it — and then ransomed their own people when they were taken captive, which was the one part of the law they were willing to observe. The verse asks how they can accept part of the scripture and reject part of it, and answers that the reward is disgrace now and the severest punishment hereafter.

*Thumma antum ha'ula'i taqtuluna anfusakum* — "then there you are, killing one another." The Arabic construction *thumma antum ha'ula'i* is an expression of amazed censure: after everything, here you are doing the very thing. The surah does not report the breach flatly; it reports it with astonishment.

*Wa tukhrijuna fariqan minkum min diyarihim* — "and expelling a party of you from their homes." Again the qualifier: *fariqan*, a party. And note the verb — *tukhrijuna*, you expel, present tense, ongoing. The breach was not a single episode but a practice.

*Tazaharuna 'alayhim bi-al-ithmi wa al-'udwan* — "backing one another against them in sin and transgression." *Tazaharuna* is from the root for the back: you put your back to your brother's back, you support one another. The evil here is collaborative, and that matters. Collective wrongdoing has a property individual wrongdoing does not: it reinforces itself. Each participant is confirmed by the others, the act acquires a rationale from its prevalence, and the person who wants to stop finds that he is not merely resisting an impulse but opposing a group. The Qur'an names this structure and treats it as an aggravating factor, not a mitigating one.

Note also the two nouns. *Ithm* comes from a root meaning to fall short, to lag behind. *'Udwan* comes from a root meaning to run beyond, to exceed. Between them they cover both directions of failure — falling short of what was required, and going past what was permitted. The pairing recurs in the Qur'an and is worth noticing whenever it appears, because human wrongdoing almost always takes one of those two shapes.

Now the historical setting, which is transmitted in the commentaries and should be cited as such. The report, given on the authority of Ibn 'Abbas and others, is that the Jewish tribes of Madinah were allied with the Arab tribes of Aws and Khazraj. When those Arab tribes went to war, a Jew would fight and kill a Jew, because his ally was fighting the other. Then, when captives were taken, they would ransom their co-religionists, citing the Torah's command to ransom captives. So they killed Jews in alliance with polytheists, then ransomed Jews in obedience to the Torah — obeying the part of the scripture that was visible and honourable, and violating the part that was inconvenient to their alliances.

That report is transmitted material rather than Qur'anic text. But the verse's own internal logic corroborates it precisely, and the accusation the verse levels — *a-fa-tu'minuna bi-ba'di al-kitabi wa takfuruna bi-ba'din* — is exactly the accusation the report describes. The two fit together, which is why the commentators have transmitted it without objection.

*Wa in ya'tukum usara tufaduhum wa huwa muharramun 'alaykum ikhrajuhum* — "and if they come to you as captives, you ransom them, though their expulsion was forbidden to you." Here is the devastating detail. They were performing the remedy for a crime they had committed. The ransom was obedience; the expulsion that made the ransom necessary was disobedience; and both were being done at the same time by the same people, with the second financing the first. The charity and the crime were one operation.

*A-fa-tu'minuna bi-ba'di al-kitabi wa takfuruna bi-ba'din* — "do you believe in part of the Book and deny part of it?" This is one of the most quoted phrases in the Qur'an, and it should be, because it names the most common religious failure there is. Note that the question implies its own answer: believing in part is not believing. The scripture is not a menu. The Qur'an states the principle even more explicitly elsewhere: "Indeed, those who disbelieve in Allah and His messengers and wish to discriminate between Allah and His messengers and say, 'We believe in some and disbelieve in others,' and wish to adopt a way in between — it is they who are the disbelievers, truly" (4:150–151). There is no station in between.

*Fa-ma jaza'u man yaf'alu dhalika minkum illa khizyun fi al-hayati al-dunya* — "so what is the reward of whoever does that among you but disgrace in the life of this world?" *Khizy* is disgrace, shame, exposure. Note that the consequence begins here. The partial observance is not merely punished later; it tends to become visible and shameful now. A person who holds loudly to the parts of the religion that cost him nothing and quietly drops the parts that do will eventually be seen doing it, and the seeing is part of the punishment.

*Wa yawma al-qiyamati yuradduna ila ashaddi al-'adhab* — "and on the Day of Resurrection they will be returned to the severest punishment." *Yuradduna*, they will be consigned, handed over. And *wa ma Allahu bi-ghafilin 'amma ta'malun* — "and Allah is not unaware of what you do" — the same closing as verse 74, which ties the two verses together: the hardening and the selective obedience are the same disease, and neither is unseen.

For practice, this is the most transferable verse in the batch and perhaps in the whole passage.

The first application is the obvious one and it is inexhaustible. Partial adherence is the default condition of religious people. The believer scrupulous about the prayer and casual about his financial dealings. The community that enforces certain rules rigorously and treats others as optional. The person who quotes the verses about mercy and has never read the verses about justice, or vice versa. The Qur'an's answer to all of it is a question with no third option: do you take some of the Book and leave some?

The second is more searching, because it is the specific mechanism the verse exposes. They observed the part of the law that was public, communal and brought them credit — the ransoming — and violated the part that was costly to their alliances — the killing. That is how selection almost always works. Nobody picks and chooses randomly. People keep the obligations that are visible, rewarded, and cheap, and drop the ones that would cost them a relationship, a position, or an income. The test for anyone wondering whether he is doing this is simple and uncomfortable: which of my religious observances would I lose money, status, or a friendship by keeping? The ones that cost nothing are not evidence of anything.

And the third is the ransom for the crime you committed. Philanthropy funded by harm. The public generosity that runs on a private exploitation. The apology offered repeatedly for a thing that is not being stopped. The verse dismantles this in a single clause: it was unlawful for you to expel them in the first place. Repairing the symptom of an ongoing wrong is not obedience. It is the appearance of obedience, and the difference is visible to everyone except the person paying.

## Verse 86

**"These are the ones who trade the Hereafter for the life of this world. So their punishment will not be reduced, nor will they be helped."**

In simple terms: these are the people who bought this life by paying with the next, so their punishment will not be reduced and no one will rescue them.

*Ula'ika alladhina ishtaraw al-hayata al-dunya bi-al-akhirati* — "those are the ones who purchased the life of this world with the Hereafter." Watch the grammar carefully, because it is the whole point. In the construction *ishtara X bi-Y*, X is what was bought and Y is what was paid. So the Hereafter is the currency and this world is the purchase. They paid their afterlife and received their present life. The transaction is not a metaphor for a loss; it is a description of a real exchange in which they got what they wanted.

The same construction appears at 2:16 — "those who purchased error with guidance" — where guidance was the price and error the acquisition. And the concept is threaded through the Qur'an at 2:102, 2:175, 3:77, 5:44, 9:9 and 16:95.

There is a counterpart that makes the point by contrast, and it is one of the most beautiful verses in the Book: "Indeed, Allah has purchased from the believers their lives and their properties [in exchange] for that they will have Paradise" (9:111). Two transactions, moving in opposite directions. In 9:111 the believer sells his self and his wealth and receives Paradise. In 2:86 the seller sells his Hereafter and receives the world. Both are real. Both succeed. The difference is only in what was handed over and what was taken.

*Fa-la yukhaffafu 'anhum al-'adhabu wa la hum yunsarun* — "so the punishment will not be lightened for them, nor will they be helped." Two negations: no reduction, and no rescue. The first forecloses mitigation, the second forecloses help from outside — no intercessor, no ally, no arrangement. That second clause is pointed, because the alliances they had built were exactly the things they were protecting when they broke the covenant. What they bought cannot help them.

The language of the punishment not being lightened recurs for the inhabitants of the Fire: "And those who disbelieve will have the fire of Hell; it will not be decreed for them that they die, nor will its punishment be lightened for them" (35:36). This is among the texts on the permanence of the punishment for those who die in disbelief. The mainstream of Ahl al-Sunnah holds this permanence by consensus; a minority view holding that the punishment eventually ends has been attributed to a small number of scholars, and the attribution and the view itself are both contested. It was noted at verse 24 and need not be reopened here except to say that the majority position is what is stated above.

For practice, the transaction is the whole verse.

The first thing to see is that the purchase succeeded. This is the part people misread. The people in this verse were not fools who got nothing. They got the alliance, the standing, the money, the reputation, the life. The transaction completed. What is tragic about it is not that it failed but that it worked — and that the price comes due later, at a point when the purchase cannot be returned. Every bad bargain in human life has this shape: the goods are delivered immediately and the invoice arrives afterwards.

The second is to notice that everyone is making this transaction. Nobody is outside it. Every person is handing over something for something, and the only question is the direction of the exchange. The career built on a compromised foundation. The wealth acquired by a route that will not survive scrutiny. The popularity bought with a silence. The comfort bought with a truth withheld. All of these are purchases, and all of them deliver. The Qur'an's question is never "did you get what you wanted?" It is "what did you pay?"

And the third is the second negation: *la hum yunsarun*, they will not be helped. The alliances they were protecting are the very things that cannot rescue them. This is worth sitting with, because it is the structural flaw in every compromise. A person compromises in order to keep something — a relationship, a position, a group. And the thing he compromised to keep is, by its nature, unable to help him at the point where help would matter. The protection he bought is not convertible into the currency he will need.

## Verse 87

**"Indeed, We gave Moses the Book and sent after him successive messengers. And We gave Jesus, son of Mary, clear proofs and supported him with the holy spirit. Why is it that every time a messenger comes to you ˹Israelites˺ with something you do not like, you become arrogant, rejecting some and killing others?"**

In simple terms: Moses was given the scripture and followed by a succession of messengers; Jesus the son of Mary was given clear proofs and strengthened by the Holy Spirit. And every time a messenger came with something they did not want to hear, they responded with arrogance — denying some and killing others.

*Wa la-qad atayna Musa al-kitaba wa qaffayna min ba'dihi bi-al-rusuli* — "and We certainly gave Moses the Book and sent messengers in succession after him." *Qaffayna* is from the root for the back of the neck; the verb means to follow at someone's heels, to send one after another in a train. The image is of messengers arriving in an unbroken line, each one at the heels of the one before. This affirms the long succession of Israelite prophets, and it means the rejection was not a single refusal but a settled response renewed across generations.

*Wa atayna 'Isa ibn Maryam al-bayyinati* — "and We gave Jesus, son of Mary, the clear proofs." *Bayyinat* are evident signs — the miracles the Qur'an attributes to him in abundance: healing the blind and the leper and raising the dead by Allah's permission, forming a bird from clay and breathing into it (3:49, 5:110); speaking in the cradle (3:46, 19:29–33); the table sent down from heaven (5:112–115).

It is worth stating plainly, because it is widely unknown on both sides, what Islam actually holds about Jesus. A Muslim is **required** to believe in him: his virginal conception (3:47, 19:16–22, 21:91, 66:12), his status as the Messiah, his being a word from God and a spirit from Him (4:171), the Gospel revealed to him (5:46), his miracles, his ascension (4:158), and his return before the Hour. To deny any of this is to leave the religion. He is named *'Isa ibn Maryam* — Jesus, son of Mary — in every one of his appearances in the Qur'an, always by his mother's name, which is both an honour to her and a refusal of the title "son of God."

And the difference from Christianity must be stated as honestly as the agreement, because softening it serves no one. The Qur'an explicitly rejects the incarnation and the Trinity: "O People of the Scripture, do not commit excess in your religion and do not say about Allah except the truth. The Messiah, Jesus the son of Mary, was but a messenger of Allah and His word which He directed to Mary and a spirit from Him. So believe in Allah and His messengers. And do not say, 'Three' — desist; it is better for you. Indeed, Allah is but one God. Exalted is He above having a son" (4:171). And: "It is not [befitting] for Allah to take a son; exalted is He!" (19:35). And the whole of Surah al-Ikhlas (112).

On the crucifixion, the majority position of the Muslim tradition is that Jesus was not killed or crucified but that it was made to appear so, and that Allah raised him up (4:157–158). A minority of modern Muslim readers have argued that the verse denies the claim of his killers rather than the event itself. This is a genuinely contested area within contemporary Islamic thought, and it is an irreconcilable difference with Christianity. It should be stated as a difference rather than explained away.

*Wa ayyadnahu bi-ruh al-qudus* — "and We supported him with the Holy Spirit." Who or what is *ruh al-qudus*? The majority of commentators identified it with Jibril (Gabriel), and the internal evidence of the Qur'an supports that strongly: this very surah identifies the one who brings the revelation down as Jibril (2:97), and 16:102 says "the Holy Spirit has brought it down from your Lord in truth," and 26:193 calls him "the Trustworthy Spirit." Other readings transmitted in the commentaries take it as the Gospel, or as a divine strengthening granted to Jesus, or as the greatest name of Allah by which he raised the dead. What should be said firmly is that it is not, in any Islamic reading, a divine person in a Trinity: the Qur'an rejects tritheism explicitly at 4:171 and 5:73. *Qudus* means purity and holiness; *ruh al-qudus* is the Spirit of Holiness.

*A-fa-kullama ja'akum rasulun bima la tahwa anfusukum istakbartum* — "so is it that whenever a messenger came to you with what your souls did not desire, you became arrogant?" Here is the diagnosis, and it is the heart of the verse.

The word *tahwa* deserves attention. It comes from a root that means both to desire passionately and to fall — *hawa* is desire, and it is also the pit you fall into. The Arabic itself carries the connection: the thing you crave is the thing that drops you. They did not reject the messengers because the evidence was insufficient. They rejected them because the message contradicted what they wanted.

And the response is named as *istakbartum* — you were arrogant, you considered yourselves too great. This is the same root as Iblis's refusal, and the same diagnosis the Qur'an gives for rejection throughout: "I will turn away from My signs those who are arrogant upon the earth without right" (7:146). The Prophet ﷺ said: "No one who has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will enter Paradise" (Muslim).

*Fa-fariqan kadhdhabtum wa fariqan taqtulun* — "and a party you denied, and a party you kill." Note the two verbs: *kadhdhabtum* in the past, "you denied," and *taqtulun* in the present, "you kill." The commentators discussed the shift and generally took the present tense as indicating a continuing disposition rather than a single past event — killing the messengers is described as their habit. Note also *fariqan* again: a party, not all.

For practice, the diagnosis is the whole value of this verse, and it works in two directions.

The first is as a self-examination. When a truth comes to you and it happens to be one you did not want, what happens next? The honest answer for most people is that the objection is produced after the rejection, not before it. The argument arrives assembled and confident, and it arrived suspiciously fast. The test the verse suggests is a simple one: did I find this unconvincing because of the evidence, or because of what it would cost me if it were true? The method of checking is to notice what you do with a message you *did* want — how much less evidence you required.

And the second direction is the one that matters for anyone who speaks on behalf of the religion. The messengers came with things people did not want to hear, and they were killed and denied for it. They did not adjust the message to improve its reception. That is not a licence for harshness — the Qur'an commands the opposite at 3:159 and 16:125, and "speak to people good" is in the covenant of verse 83. It is a warning against a different failure: the reformulation of the religion into whatever the audience will accept. A message that never contradicts anyone is not being delivered. It is being sold.

## Verse 88

**"They say, “Our hearts are unreceptive!” In fact, Allah has condemned them for their disbelief. They have but little faith."**

In simple terms: they claimed their hearts were closed and unreceptive. The Qur'an reverses this: it is not that they are structurally unable to believe; Allah has cursed them *because of* their own denial, and so their faith is negligible.

*Wa qalu qulubuna ghulfun* — "and they said: our hearts are *ghulf*." The word is disputed and the possibilities matter.

The dominant reading takes *ghulf* as the plural of *aghlf*, from the root meaning a covering, a sheath, a wrapping. On this reading they are saying: our hearts are covered over, wrapped, insulated against your message — nothing you say can get in. And crucially, this is a boast rather than a complaint. They are not lamenting an incapacity; they are asserting an immunity. Some of the transmitted reports describe them saying it in that spirit: our hearts are encased, so do not trouble yourself.

A second early reading takes *ghulf* as vessels — "our hearts are receptacles," meaning containers already full of knowledge, so they had no need of what Muhammad ﷺ brought. Both readings are transmitted.

There is also a textual note: two vocalisations are transmitted, *ghulfun* and *ghulufun*, with the same range of meaning. The difference is one of form rather than substance.

*Bal la'nahumu Allahu bi-kufrihim* — "rather, Allah has cursed them because of their disbelief." This is the reversal, and it is one of the most important theological sentences in the passage.

They claimed a structural incapacity: we are made in such a way that this cannot reach us. The Qur'an answers that the condition is acquired, and that they acquired it. *Bi-kufrihim* — because of their disbelief. The curse is a consequence of their own act, not a prior decree that produced it. The causal direction matters enormously here, and the commentators were precise about it: the sealing is not God's arbitrary act imposed on an unwilling person, it is the condition a person produces in himself by persistently refusing what he knows.

This is the same principle the surah established at verse 7, where the sealing is described, and it is what makes the condition reversible. A structural incapacity could not be cured and its possessor could not be blamed. An acquired condition can be repented of, and its possessor is fully responsible for it. The Qur'an consistently chooses the second framing, and it does so because the first would make the entire moral life meaningless.

The theological position, stated carefully: Ahl al-Sunnah hold that everything occurs by Allah's decree and creation, and that human beings have a real capacity and a real choice for which they are accountable; and they hold that Allah's decree is never an excuse a person may produce for himself, because he does not know it and cannot use it as a defence. The curse follows the denial. That is the order this verse states, and it is the order the tradition maintains.

*Fa-qalilan ma yu'minun* — "so little is what they believe." Read as a statement of fact: what they believe amounts to very little. The grammarians discussed the construction, with some taking the *ma* as extra for emphasis and paraphrasing it as "they believe not except a little." The sense is not in doubt: whatever faith is present in them is negligible.

For practice, this verse addresses one of the most common and most protected defences a human being has.

The defence is: I am not wired for this. Religion doesn't speak to me. I've never felt anything. I'm too rational, or too damaged, or too set in my ways. The defence is usually offered as a description of a fact about the speaker, and the Qur'an's answer is that the fact is real but the description is wrong. The closure is real. It is not structural. It was acquired — through refusal, through neglect, through years of small decisions not to look — and it is experienced as a limitation precisely because the process was gradual enough to be invisible.

The hope in that is enormous, and it is the reason this verse should be preached as often as it is. A person who has been closed for thirty years is not closed in the way a stone is closed. He is closed in the way a door is closed. He produced the condition, which means he can, with the grace of Allah, undo it. No one reading this is beyond the reach of that, and no one should be told that he is.

The second application is more uncomfortable and is for those who already believe. The boast embedded in *qulubuna ghulfun* — we are immune — has a religious version. It is the person who has heard so much that he has stopped being able to hear; who can sit through a reminder that would have moved him once and feel precisely nothing, and who has come to experience that numbness as maturity. It is not maturity. It is the condition this verse describes, arrived at by a slower route, and it is named as a curse rather than as a stage of development.

## Verse 89

**"Although they used to pray for victory ˹by means of the Prophet˺ over the polytheists, when there came to them a Book from Allah which they recognized, confirming the Scripture they had ˹in their hands˺, they rejected it. So may Allah’s condemnation be upon the disbelievers."**

In simple terms: the Book they had been invoking to pray for victory over the idolaters finally arrived, confirming the scriptures they already held — and when it came, they denied it, even though they recognised it.

*Wa lamma ja'ahum kitabun min 'indi Allahi musaddiqun lima ma'ahum* — "and when there came to them a Book from Allah, confirming what was with them." *Musaddiq* is the same word used at verse 41, and the argument is the same: the Qur'an confirms the Torah rather than replacing it wholesale, which means its possessors should be the first to recognise it. That is precisely the point the surah is about to make against them.

*Wa kanu min qablu yastaftihuna 'ala alladhina kafaru* — "and they used to, before it, seek victory over those who disbelieved." *Istiftah* means to seek *fath* — an opening, a decisive judgement, a victory.

The historical setting is transmitted in the commentaries on the authority of Ibn 'Abbas and others, and should be cited as a report. Before the Prophet's mission, the Jewish tribes of Madinah, when they were at odds with the polytheistic Arabs around them, used to pray for victory by invoking the coming of the promised prophet — saying, in effect, that when he came they would follow him and defeat their enemies with him. Then he came, from among the Arabs rather than from among them, and they rejected him.

That report is transmitted material rather than Qur'anic text, but the verse's own structure depends on exactly that situation, and the two fit together without strain. The indictment is specific and it is devastating: you used the coming of this prophet as a weapon against your enemies, and when he arrived you denied him. The denial cannot have been about the evidence, because you had been citing the evidence in your own prayers.

*Fa-lamma ja'ahum ma 'arafu kafaru bihi* — "but when there came to them what they recognised, they disbelieved in it." The word is *'arafu*: they knew it, they recognised it. This is the fourth time in this passage that the surah has made the same point — they mix truth with falsehood *while knowing* (2:42), they distort the word *after understanding it* (2:75), they suppress the proof *knowing* it will be used against them (2:76), and now they deny what they *recognised*. The surah's thesis is consistent and it is not intellectual. The rejection of the truth is a knowing act.

The same point is made later in this surah in unforgettable language: "Those to whom We gave the Scripture know him as they know their own sons. But indeed, a party of them conceal the truth while they know" (2:146).

*Fa-la'natu Allahi 'ala al-kafirin* — "so the curse of Allah is upon the disbelievers." *La'nah* is being distanced from mercy, driven away. Note the grammatical shift: the verse moves from "you" to the third person, "upon the disbelievers." The commentators observed that the shift generalises the statement — it is not only they, but anyone who does what they did.

For practice, the most painful and most useful application is the one that is hardest to hear.

How many people pray for something and then reject it when it arrives in a form they did not expect? The prayer for guidance, answered with a guidance that requires leaving something. The prayer for a spouse, answered with a person who is not the one you had in mind. The prayer for reform, answered by a reformer you do not like. The prayer for help, answered through a channel you would never have chosen. The pattern of this verse is one of the most reliable and least examined in human life: we ask for the thing, and then refuse it because of the packaging.

And the more specific version, which is the one this verse is actually about: rejecting a truth because of the person who brings it. They knew the Book was from Allah. What they could not accept was that it came through an Arab rather than through one of them. That is the oldest and most durable form of rejection there is — and it is worth naming, because it operates constantly and invisibly. The colleague whose idea you dismiss and then adopt when someone else says it. The scholar whose work you reject because of his school, his country, or his background. The family member whose correction you cannot hear because of who he is.

The test the verse suggests is not complicated: would you accept this if it came from someone else? If the answer is yes, the objection is not about the message, and it never was.

## Verse 90

**"Miserable is the price they have sold their souls for—denying Allah’s revelation and resenting Allah for granting His grace to whoever He wills of His servants! They have earned wrath upon wrath. And such disbelievers will suffer a humiliating punishment."**

In simple terms: how evil is what they sold themselves for — rejecting what Allah revealed, out of resentful envy that Allah should give His bounty to whomever He chooses. So they incurred wrath upon accumulated wrath, and a humiliating punishment awaits those who disbelieve.

*Bi'sama ishtaraw bihi anfusahum* — "miserable is that for which they sold their souls." *Bi'sa* is the verb of censure: how evil is it. And note what the price was: *anfusahum*, their own selves. The transaction described in verse 86 — buying the world with the Hereafter — is restated here from the other side. They handed over themselves.

*An yakfuru bima anzala Allahu* — "that they disbelieve in what Allah sent down." And here is the sting: the thing they bought, the thing for which they paid their own selves, was disbelief. They gave everything and received nothing but a refusal. There is no transaction in human experience that loses more completely.

*Baghyan an yunazzila Allahu min fadlihi 'ala man yasha'u min 'ibadihi* — "out of envy that Allah should send down of His bounty upon whom He wills of His servants." This is the motive, and the surah has saved it for last because it is the real one.

*Baghy* is envy laced with transgression — the resentment of another's good combined with the desire to overturn it. And what was the object of the envy? That Allah should send down *fadl*, bounty, upon *whomever He wills*. In this case: upon a messenger from the descendants of Ishmael rather than from the descendants of Isaac and Jacob. The resentment was that the bounty had gone to the wrong lineage.

The Qur'an answers this with total clarity and returns to it repeatedly: "Say: Indeed, [all] bounty is in the hand of Allah; He gives it to whom He wills" (3:73). "That is the bounty of Allah which He gives to whom He wills" (57:29, 62:4). And the verse that settles the whole question of human ranking: "O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" (49:13).

That principle was stated at verse 47 of the earlier batch and it is worth repeating here, because this verse is where the surah exposes what lies underneath tribal and racial religious pride. The rejection was not theological. It was that the favour had gone to someone else's people. And the Qur'an's answer is that bounty is not property, that lineage confers nothing, and that what Allah gives to another person is not taken from you.

*Fa-ba'u bi-ghadabin 'ala ghadabin* — "so they brought upon themselves wrath upon wrath." *Ba'u* is the same verb used at verse 61: they returned bearing it, they incurred it. What are the two wraths? The commentators differed. Some said the first was for their earlier rejection of the messengers and killing of them, and the second for their rejection of Muhammad ﷺ. Some said the first was for their disobedience to Moses and the second for their denial of Jesus and Muhammad. Some said the doubling is simply emphatic — accumulated, compounded wrath. All of these are transmitted, and they are compatible: the point is that the wrath is layered, and that each rejection added to the one before it.

*Wa lil-kafirina 'adhabun muhin* — "and for the disbelievers is a humiliating punishment." *Muhin*, from a root meaning to be lowly and contemptible. Note the fit: the sin was *baghy*, arrogant resentment, and the punishment is *muhin*, abasement. The Qur'an states this correspondence explicitly elsewhere: "Indeed, those who are arrogant toward My worship will enter Hell in humiliation" (40:60). Arrogance is answered by its own shape. There is no verse in which the proud are honoured.

For practice, this verse is the most searching in the batch because it names a motive that almost nobody confesses.

The first application is envy as the hidden engine of religious and intellectual disagreement. How much of what presents as a principled objection is actually *baghy* — resentment that the bounty went to someone else? Not all of it; genuine disagreement exists and the tradition has always honoured it. But a great deal of it. The scholar who rejects the work of a rival. The community that cannot accept a truth carried by another community. The person who cannot learn from someone he considers beneath him. The test is the same as in verse 89: would I receive this if it came from my own side?

The second is the tribal version, which is the one this verse is literally about and which has never gone away. Refusing a truth because of the group, nation, race, class or school it came from. The Qur'an's answer is 49:13 — nobility is by *taqwa*, full stop — and it is worth observing that the Prophet ﷺ said this in his Farewell Sermon, in the largest gathering of his life, as one of the last things he told the community: an Arab has no superiority over a non-Arab, nor a non-Arab over an Arab, nor a white over a black, nor a black over a white, except by righteousness.

And the third is the cure, which is stated inside the verse itself. The resentment is that Allah should give *of His bounty* to *whomever He wills*. The cure is to grasp what bounty means. Bounty is not a fixed quantity distributed by desert, such that another person's share diminishes yours. It is *fadl* — pure gift, from a source that is not depleted by giving. The person who understands that cannot be envious, because envy requires a scarcity that does not exist here. That is why the tradition treats envy as a spiritual disease rather than a character flaw: it rests on a false belief about how the world is supplied, and it is cured by correcting the belief.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses dismantle the last verse of the previous batch piece by piece. They had said the Fire would barely touch them, and briefly (80). Verses 81 and 82 restore the two destinations and the two conditions, and they do it with a symmetry that leaves no middle station to stand in: those whose evil has enveloped them belong to the Fire, and those who believe and do good belong to the Garden.

Then the surah returns to the covenant and shows why the claim was false. The covenant was specific and its terms were moral before they were ritual — one God, then parents, kin, orphans, the poor, kind speech, the prayer, the zakah (83) — and it forbade shedding one another's blood and driving one another from their homes (84). They broke both, collaboratively, and then ransomed the captives their own expulsion had produced, which was the one part of the law they were willing to keep (85). That is the transaction they made: they bought this life and paid with the next, and no one will lighten it or rescue them from it (86).

Then the surah widens the lens to the whole history. Messenger after messenger was sent, each at the heels of the one before; Jesus was given clear signs and strengthened by the Holy Spirit; and the response, every single time, was the same — the message contradicted what they wanted, and they responded with arrogance, denying some and killing others (87). And the present is the same as the past. They say their hearts are sealed, when the sealing is their own work (88). They rejected the very Book they had been invoking against their enemies, knowing it was true (89). And the motive, named at last, was not doubt: it was envy that Allah's bounty had gone to someone else (90).

Three things hold the passage together. First, the criterion in verses 81 and 82 is stated in the plainest language in the Qur'an and admits of no exception for lineage, membership or claim. Second, the breach is consistently described as *knowing* — they knew, they understood, they recognised, and they refused anyway. And third, the sin the passage finally lands on is not intellectual error but *baghy*: the refusal to accept that Allah gives His bounty where He wills. That is the disease this surah has been tracking since the angels were told that man would shed blood and spread corruption while claiming to set things right, and it is the disease it will continue to track for the rest of its length.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators genuinely differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: whether *ghulf* means covered or receptive, whether *ruh al-qudus* is Gabriel or something else, what the two wraths in verse 90 refer to, whether *qulu lil-nasi husnan* is general or specific, and what *al-sayyi'ah* in verse 81 denotes.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the historical setting of the Madinan alliances and the ransoming in verse 85, and the report of the pre-Islamic invocations of the coming prophet in verse 89. Both are transmitted on the authority of Ibn 'Abbas and others and sit consistently with the verses' own internal logic, but they are not Qur'anic text.

Five boundaries have been observed. On verse 81: the Kharijite, Murji'ite and Mu'tazili readings have each been named and answered, and the Ahl al-Sunnah position on the grave sinner has been stated with its evidence, so that the verse is not used to terrify anyone beyond what the text supports. On verse 82: the minority view on the ending of the Fire's punishment has been noted as a contested minority view and not as the position of the majority. On verse 87: what Islam requires Muslims to believe about Jesus has been stated in full, the difference with Christianity on the incarnation and Trinity has been stated as a difference rather than softened, and the contested minority reading of the crucifixion has been identified as contested. On verses 89 and 90: the rejection has been attributed to knowing refusal and to envy rather than to a people's nature, and the anti-tribal principle of 49:13 and the Farewell Sermon has been stated as the Qur'an's answer to it. And throughout: no individual person, living or dead, may be declared among the inhabitants of the Fire on the basis of any verse in this passage, and no reading of these verses may be used to justify hostility toward any community.
