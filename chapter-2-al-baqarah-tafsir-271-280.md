# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 271–280

This batch closes the long passage on charity and then turns to the subject the Qur'an legislates more severely than almost any other.

Four verses finish the spending passage (271–274). **Then 275–280 prohibit *ribā* — and they do it in the strongest language the Qur'an ever uses about a financial matter**, culminating in a declaration of "war from God and His Messenger" (2:279). The passage closes, at 2:280, not with a threat but with a command to grant the struggling debtor more time — **and with a note that forgiving the debt altogether is better.**

**Three cautions at the outset, because they govern how 275–280 should be read.**

**First: the reported occasions differ, and they differ substantially.** Some reports name Banū 'Amr ibn 'Umayr of Thaqīf and Banū al-Mughīrah of Makhzūm; others name al-'Abbās and Khālid ibn al-Walīd. **Both are transmitted, and nothing here should be asserted on a single report.**

**Second: the prohibition of *ribā* was not a single event.** It was legislated in stages, and the stages matter — **a Makkan verse (30:39) had already condemned it before the Madīnan legislation**. Anyone who reads 2:275 as the first word on the subject has the chronology wrong.

**Third, and most important: how the prohibition applies to modern banking is a live, contested question, and I will present it as one.** I will state clearly where the overwhelming weight of the tradition lies, and I will name the minority positions and the necessity-based exceptions that are argued today — **but I am not issuing a fatwa, and no reader should take this commentary as one.** On a matter this consequential, the honest thing is to say what the sources say, mark the disagreements, and send the reader to a qualified scholar for his own case.

---

## Verse 271

**"To give charity publicly is good, but to give to the poor privately is better for you, and will absolve you of your sins. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do."**

In simple terms: if you give charity openly, excellent it is; but if you conceal it and give it to the poor, that is better for you — and We will efface from you some of your evil deeds. And God is Acquainted with what you do.

*In tubdū al-ṣadaqāti fa-ni'immāhiya, wa in tukhfūhā wa tu'tūhā al-fuqarā'a **fa-huwa khayrun lakum**, wa nukaffiru 'ankum min sayyi'ātikum, wa Allāhu bi-mā ta'malūna khabīr.*

**This verse is a ranking, and it is more balanced than it is usually quoted as being.**

*In tubdū al-ṣadaqāti **fa-ni'immā hiya*** — "if you give charity openly, **excellent it is**."

**Note that open giving is not criticised. It is praised.** *Ni'immā* is strong commendation. **So the verse does not begin from suspicion of public charity.**

**And the tradition has always preserved the function of open giving:** it encourages others, it refutes the suspicion that a wealthy person is stingy, it makes the obligation visible and therefore normative. **The Prophet ﷺ himself gave publicly on many occasions.**

*Wa in **tukhfūhā** wa tu'tūhā al-fuqarā'a **fa-huwa khayrun lakum*** — "but if you **conceal** it and give it to the poor, **that is better for you**."

**And the ranking is explicit: concealment is *khayr*, better.**

**But note the two words that qualify the ranking, and both matter.**

**1. *Fa-huwa khayrun **lakum*** — "better **for you**."** **The benefit named is to the giver, not the recipient.** Which means the verse is protecting your motive, not maximising the gift's efficiency. **The reason concealment is better is that concealment is safe from *riyā'* — and 2:264 has just spent a whole verse explaining what *riyā'* does to a deed.** So 2:271 is not a general rule about method. **It is the protective measure that follows the diagnosis of 2:264.**

**2. *Wa tu'tūhā al-fuqarā'a*** — "and give it to the poor." **The concealment is paired with getting it to the right people.** Which excludes the other kind of secrecy — the quiet giving that is really about avoiding awkwardness, or the anonymous gift that never reaches anyone. **It must be hidden *and* delivered.**

**And the hadith that belongs here, in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s from Abū Hurayrah:** among the seven whom God will shade on the Day when there is no shade but His is **"a man who gives charity and conceals it, such that his left hand does not know what his right hand spends."**

**Note the image: the left hand does not know.** Not "no one knows." **The concealment asked for is so complete that the part of you that keeps accounts is not informed.** That is 2:271 taken to its conclusion.

**And now the balance, which the verse itself invites and which the jurists draw.** The Schools differ over whether the **obligatory zakāt** should be given openly or secretly — with many jurists preferring openness for the obligatory portion (to avoid suspicion and to encourage others) and secrecy for the voluntary. **The disagreement is real and both positions are held.** What is not disputed: **the voluntary gift is better hidden, unless there is a reason for it to be seen.**

*Wa **nukaffiru 'ankum min sayyi'ātikum*** — "and We will **efface from you some of your evil deeds**."

***Nukaffiru*** — from *kaffara*, to cover, to expiate, to wipe out. **And note: *min* — *some* of your evil deeds.** Not all. **The verse is precise and does not over-promise.**

**And the sequence is worth holding: giving expiates.** Which is why the whole passage is ordered as it is — **charity is not only a social transfer. It is an eraser.**

*Wa Allāhu bi-mā ta'malūna **khabīr*** — "and God is **Acquainted** with what you do."

***Khabīr*** — the one who is inwardly aware of the fine detail of a thing, who has the *khabar*, the report, of it. **Which is exactly the right closing for a verse about hidden charity: the thing nobody else saw is the thing He is *khabīr* about.** And it is also the closing for a verse about *public* charity: **He knows which of the two you chose and why.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 272

**"You are not responsible for people’s guidance ˹O Prophet˺—it is Allah Who guides whoever He wills. Whatever you ˹believers˺ spend in charity, it is for your own good—as long as you do so seeking the pleasure of Allah. Whatever you donate will be paid back to you in full, and you will not be wronged."**

In simple terms: not upon you is their guidance, but God guides whom He wills. And whatever good you spend is for yourselves; and you do not spend except seeking the face of God. And whatever good you spend will be repaid to you in full, and you will not be wronged.

*Laysa 'alayka hudāhum wa lākinna Allāha yahdī man yashā'u, wa mā tunfiqū min khayrin fa-li-anfusikum, wa mā tunfiqūna illā ibtighā'a wajhi Allāhi, wa mā tunfiqū min khayrin yuwaffa ilaykum wa antum lā tuẓlamūn.*

**This verse is addressed to the Prophet ﷺ in the second person, and it is consolation before it is legislation.**

*Laysa 'alayka **hudāhum*** — "**not upon you** is their guidance."

**Read this with 28:56, which is its twin: "You do not guide whom you love, but God guides whom He wills."**

**And the context the commentators place it in is the Prophet's ﷺ grief over his uncle Abū Ṭālib.** The man who raised him, protected him, and stood by him through the boycott did not accept Islam, and the Prophet ﷺ wanted it more than he had wanted anything. **This verse tells him: that was never in your charge.**

**And the consequence drawn here is startling, because it is drawn immediately:**

*Wa mā tunfiqū min khayrin **fa-li-anfusikum*** — "and whatever good you spend, **it is for yourselves**."

**The logic runs: your job is the gift, not the outcome.** You are not responsible for whether they believe. **You are responsible for whether you gave.**

**Which produces the ruling that follows — and it is one of the most generous in the Qur'an:**

**Give to people who may never believe, and give anyway.**

**The reported occasion, from Ibn 'Abbās (by way of al-Kalbī from Abū Ṣāliḥ, recorded by al-Wāḥidī):** the Prophet ﷺ went for the make-up pilgrimage and had with him **Asmā' bint Abī Bakr**. Her mother — named in the reports as **Qutaylah** — and her grandmother, **both idolatresses at the time**, came to ask her for help. Asmā' said: **"I will not give you anything until I consult the Messenger of God — for you do not follow my religion."** She consulted him, and this verse was revealed. **And the Prophet ﷺ commanded her to give to both of them.**

**And the ḥadīth in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s, in Asmā''s own words:** *"My mother came to me during the lifetime of the Messenger of God ﷺ, and she was a polytheist. I asked the Messenger of God ﷺ: 'My mother has come to me, desiring — shall I maintain ties with her?' He said: '**Yes, maintain ties with your mother.**'"*

**A second report** (from Ibn al-Ḥanafiyyah): the Muslims used to dislike giving charity to the poor among the idolaters, **until they were ordered to give it to them.** And the Prophet ﷺ said, on this verse: **"Give charity to the people of the religions."** Both are transmitted reports and the wording varies between them.

**Two things about this must be said plainly.**

**First: the verse separates giving from converting.** The motive for the gift is *wajh Allāh*, not the recipient's response to it. **A person who gives in order to be thanked, or admired, or — worse — in order to create an opening for proselytism, has changed the motive the verse requires.** That is not what 2:272 legislates. **It legislates: give because you were told to, and leave the outcome with the One who owns it.**

**Second: this is the verse to hold against every form of conditional aid.** The Muslim who says "I'll help them when they become like me" has the order reversed. **The verse was revealed precisely to permit the gift to someone who has not and may never change.**

*Wa mā tunfiqūna illā **ibtighā'a wajhi Allāh*** — "and you do not spend except **seeking the face of God**."

**Two readings are held, and both should be given:**
- **As a description:** you do not spend for any other reason. **This is the state of the sincere giver, stated as a fact about him.**
- **As a stipulation:** your spending only counts if this is what you are seeking. **On this reading it is the condition the whole passage has been building toward since 2:262.**

**Both are held, and they converge on the same requirement.**

***Wajh Allāh*** — the Face of God. **An attribute, and it is handled exactly as 2:115 handled it: affirmed as it came, without likening it to the faces of creatures and without denying it.** The phrase means His Self, His pleasure, His countenance — **the direction you are facing when you give.**

*Wa mā tunfiqū min khayrin **yuwaffa ilaykum*** — "and whatever good you spend, **it will be repaid to you in full**."

***Yuwaffa*** — from *waffā*, to pay in full, to complete the measure, leaving nothing short. **Not "you will be rewarded." *Repaid in full.*** The language is the language of a settled account.

*Wa antum **lā tuẓlamūn*** — "and you will not be wronged."

**Nothing will be deducted.** Which, after 2:264's warning that a deed can be nullified, is the reassurance the reader needs: **the one that survives is the one that is paid in full, down to the last measure.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 273

**"˹Charity is˺ for the needy who are too engaged in the cause of Allah to move about in the land ˹for work˺. Those unfamiliar with their situation will think they are not in need ˹of charity˺ because they do not beg. You can recognize them by their appearance. They do not beg people persistently. Whatever you give in charity is certainly well known to Allah."**

In simple terms: for the poor who have been constrained in the path of God, unable to travel about in the land — the ignorant one supposes them wealthy because of their restraint; you will know them by their mark; they do not ask the people importunately. And whatever good you spend, God knows it.

*Li-al-fuqarā'i alladhīna uḥṣirū fī sabīli Allāhi lā yastaṭī'ūna ḍarban fī al-arḍi, yaḥsabuhumu al-jāhilu aghniyā'a mina al-ta'affufi, ta'rifuhum bi-sīmāhum, lā yas'alūna al-nāsa ilḥāfā, wa mā tunfiqū min khayrin fa-inna Allāha bihi 'alīm.*

*Li-al-fuqarā'i alladhīna **uḥṣirū fī sabīli Allāh*** — "for the poor who have been **constrained in the path of God**."

**The commentators identify these as the *Ahl al-Ṣuffah*** — the poor Emigrants who lived at the back of the Prophet's mosque, who had no trade and no home, and who were occupied with learning and with the community's affairs. **Flag the identification as a report; the description in the verse is what is textual.**

*Lā yastaṭī'ūna **ḍarban fī al-arḍ*** — "unable to **travel about** in the land."

***Ḍarb fī al-arḍ*** — striking out into the land, journeying to trade. **So these are people who cannot earn, not because they will not but because they are held.** Their occupation is the community's need. **Which is why they have a claim on it.**

*Yaḥsabuhumu al-jāhilu **aghniyā'a mina al-ta'affuf*** — "the ignorant one supposes them **wealthy because of their restraint**."

**This is one of the most precise psychological observations in the Qur'an, and it is a warning to anyone who distributes charity.**

***Al-ta'affuf*** — from *'iffah*, chastity, self-restraint, dignity, the refusal to extend the hand. **They hold themselves in.** And the consequence is: **they look fine.**

**The person who does not know them reads the dignity as sufficiency.** He sees clean clothes and a quiet manner and no complaint, and he concludes there is no need here. **And the verse says: that is ignorance.** *Al-jāhil* — the one who does not know. **The supposition of wealth is named as a failure of knowledge, not as a reasonable inference.**

**This is the verse for everyone who has ever said "but they didn't look poor."** The Qur'an's answer is that **the poor who most deserve your attention are precisely the ones who will not look it.**

*Ta'rifuhum **bi-sīmāhum*** — "you will know them **by their mark**."

***Sīmā*** — a mark, a sign, the outward trace of an inward state. **And note what the verse does with it: it tells you they *can* be known — but not by their asking.**

**Which makes this an instruction to the community, and it is the practical heart of the verse: you have to look.** The people described here will never present themselves. **So the obligation is on the giver to learn the signs.**

**And that is a real, concrete duty for any community.** It is why the tradition insists on local knowledge of who is struggling — **the neighbour who knows, the mosque that knows, the family that knows.** A system that only responds to applications will systematically miss the people this verse describes.

*Lā yas'alūna al-nāsa **ilḥāfā*** — "they do not ask the people **importunately**."

**And here is the precision that is usually lost: the verse does not say they do not ask. It says they do not ask *ilḥāfā* — persistently, pressingly, with badgering.**

***Ilḥāf*** is from *laḥafa*, to wrap, to insist, to press. **The blameworthy thing named here is not the request. It is the hounding request.** The tradition is consistent on this: asking when one is in need is permitted; **what is rebuked is the persistent, humiliating ask that wears the other person down.** The verse praises these people for the dignity of their asking, not for the impossibility of it.

**And the ḥadīth that is the commentary on this verse, in the two *Ṣaḥīḥ*s from Abū Hurayrah:** the Prophet ﷺ said — **"The *miskīn* is not the one who goes around asking the people for a date or two, or a mouthful or two. The *miskīn* is the one who has nothing to suffice him, whose condition is not known so that charity might be given to him, and who does not ask the people."**

**Note the definition: the truly poor person is the invisible one.** Which is this verse in a sentence.

*Wa mā tunfiqū min khayrin fa-inna Allāha bihi **'alīm*** — "and whatever good you spend, God **knows** it."

**The third time in three verses that the passage closes on God's knowledge** (2:270 *ya'lamuh*, 2:271 *khabīr*, 2:273 *'alīm*). **Which is the point: the people this verse describes will not be advertised. God sees the gift anyway.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 274

**"Those who spend their wealth in charity day and night, secretly and openly—their reward is with their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."**

In simple terms: those who spend their wealth by night and by day, secretly and openly — their reward is with their Lord, and no fear upon them, nor do they grieve.

*Alladhīna yunfiqūna amwālahum bi-al-layli wa-al-nahāri sirran wa 'alāniyatan, lahum ajruhum 'inda rabbihim wa lā khawfun 'alayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn.*

**This verse closes the spending passage that opened at 2:261 — and it does so by covering every case.**

*Bi-al-layli wa-al-nahāri **sirran wa 'alāniyatan*** — "by night and by day, **secretly and openly**."

**Four terms, and together they exhaust the possibilities. Night or day, hidden or seen — there is no combination left out.**

**Which is the verse's first point: there is no time at which giving is not accepted and no manner in which it is refused.** The person who can only give at night is included. The person who can only give in public is included. **The passage that opened with a seed and went through nullification, quality, and motive, now closes by leaving nobody out.**

**And note the deliberate echo of 2:271.** There, concealment was declared *better for you*. Here, **both are named in the same breath and given the same reward.** Which resolves the tension: **concealment is safer for the motive, but openness is not disqualified — and the reward is *'inda rabbihim* either way.**

*Lahum ajruhum **'inda rabbihim*** — "their reward is **with their Lord**."

The same phrase as 2:262. **Lodged with God, not paid out by the recipient.**

*Wa lā khawfun 'alayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn* — "and no fear upon them, nor do they grieve."

The same closing as 2:262 and 2:38, 2:62, 2:112, and — in the next batch — 2:277. **Nothing ahead to dread, nothing behind to regret.**

**A report that must be flagged.** Some tafsir works connect this verse to a narration that Abū Bakr gave forty thousand dīnārs in charity — ten by night, ten by day, ten secretly and ten openly — and that the verse was revealed about him. **The narration is weak: al-Suyūṭī noted that it is transmitted only by Ibn 'Asākir in his *History*, from 'Ā'ishah, and that this report contains no mention of the verse's revelation.** So the story is transmitted without an established chain, **and the connection to this verse is not established.** I flag it so that it is not repeated as though it were.

**What the verse does not need that report to establish is already clear: the four-fold description is a promise to every giver, and the passage closes by opening the door as wide as it goes.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 275

**"Those who consume interest will stand ˹on Judgment Day˺ like those driven to madness by Satan’s touch. That is because they say, “Trade is no different than interest.” But Allah has permitted trading and forbidden interest. Whoever refrains—after having received warning from their Lord —may keep their previous gains, and their case is left to Allah. As for those who persist, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever."**

In simple terms: those who consume *ribā* will not stand except as one stands whom the Shayṭān has driven to madness by his touch. That is because they say, "trade is only like *ribā*" — while God has permitted trade and forbidden *ribā*. So whoever receives an admonition from his Lord and desists — for him is what has passed, and his affair is with God. And whoever returns — those are the companions of the Fire; they will abide in it.

*Alladhīna ya'kulūna al-ribā lā yaqūmūna illā kamā yaqūmu alladhī yatakhabbaṭuhu al-shayṭānu mina al-massi, dhālika bi-annahum qālū innamā al-bay'u mithlu al-ribā, wa aḥalla Allāhu al-bay'a wa ḥarrama al-ribā, fa-man jā'ahu maw'īẓatun min rabbihi fa-ntahā fa-lahu mā salafa wa amruhu ilā Allāhi, wa man 'āda fa-ulā'ika aṣḥābu al-nāri hum fīhā khālidūn.*

*Alladhīna **ya'kulūna al-ribā*** — "those who **consume** *ribā*."

**Note the verb: *ya'kulūna* — they eat it.** Not "take," not "charge," not "earn." **Eat.**

**The commentators give the reason, and it is worth having: what is eaten cannot be retrieved.** You can return a coat, a house, a tool. **You cannot return a meal.** Ma'arif al-Qur'an notes this directly — the word was chosen because **eating expresses total consumption, irrevocable possession.** The money has gone into you.

**And there is a second resonance the word carries: eating is what sustains you.** To say a person "eats *ribā*" is to say **this is what he is living on.** It is not a transaction he occasionally makes. **It is his income.**

*Lā yaqūmūna illā kamā yaqūmu alladhī **yatakhabbaṭuhu al-shayṭānu mina al-mass*** — "they will not stand except as one stands whom the Shayṭān **drives to madness by his touch**."

**And here is the image, and it is the most discussed and most disputed description in the whole passage.**

***Yatakhabbaṭuhu*** — from *khabṭ*, which is the word for a camel walking unevenly, groping, striking out blindly, stumbling and thrashing. **And *al-shayṭān mina al-mass* — the Shayṭān by/through the touch (*mass*).**

**There are two readings of this clause, and the tradition holds both:**

**1. Literal / eschatological:** on the Day of Resurrection they will be raised **in this state** — stumbling, thrashing, not upright, like one possessed. **The standing (*qiyām*) is the standing before God, and it is described as the standing of a man who cannot control his own limbs.** The commentators who hold this reading connect it to the principle that **a person is raised in the state in which he died.** The inner disorder is made visible on the outside.

**2. Moral / this-worldly:** the description applies to **the state of a person consumed by *ribā* in this life** — the restlessness, the inability to be still, the compulsion. Maududi argues this reading, pointing out that the consumer of *ribā* is heedless of what *ribā* actually does to human brotherhood, and **that his enrichment is at the expense of the well-being of many others — which is his "insanity" here, and he will be raised in the state he died in.**

**Both readings are held and they are not exclusive.** On either reading the claim is the same: **the outward posture reveals an inward disorder.** Whether that disorder is made visible at the Resurrection or is already visible now, **the verse is saying that this is what the practice does to a person.**

**And note the word *mass* — touch.** This is the same root that appears in the commonly held belief about jinn "touching" a person. **I will state the caution plainly: this verse is not a proof-text for popular theories of demonic possession, and it should not be read as an aetiology of mental illness.** The tradition's own reading is about the *likeness* (*kamā* — "as one whom..."), and **mental illness is not in this verse attributed to jinn in any way that should govern how a Muslim treats a sick person.** That is a point worth making firmly, because this verse is abused in that direction.

*Dhālika bi-annahum qālū **innamā al-bay'u mithlu al-ribā*** — "that is because they say: **trade is only like *ribā***."

**And here is the argument they made, and it is the same argument made today.**

**Their claim: a loan at interest and a sale are the same kind of thing — two parties agree, both gain, so what is the difference?**

**And note the direction of the comparison in the Arabic.** They said *al-bay'u mithlu al-ribā* — **trade is like *ribā*** — not the other way round. **They took the permitted thing and measured it by the forbidden one.** Which is the standard move: **start from what you want to do, and find the permitted thing that resembles it.**

*Wa **aḥalla Allāhu al-bay'a wa ḥarrama al-ribā*** — "and **God has permitted trade and forbidden *ribā***."

**The answer is six words, and it is one of the most consequential sentences in the Qur'an.**

**God does not explain why.** He states: **I permitted this, and I forbade that.** The difference between them is not a matter for the parties to negotiate, because the difference is not in the form. **It is in the ruling.**

**And that is the right place to state the definition, which the tradition extracted from this verse and the Sunnah.** Ibn al-'Arabī's formulation in *Aḥkām al-Qur'ān* is the one usually quoted: **lexically, *ribā* means increase; and in this verse it means the increase for which nothing is given in exchange apart from the loan and its time.**

**That is the definition, and it is exact: *ribā* is increase with no countervalue.**

**Which is why trade is different.** In a sale, each side gives something. **In a loan at interest, one side gives money and the other gives time — and the increment attaches to the time, not to any good or service or risk borne.** The prohibition is not of profit. **It is of profit that is attached to nothing but the passage of time on a debt.**

**And this is the place to state the chronology, because it is usually got wrong.**

*Ribā* was **not** first prohibited here. It was legislated in stages:

- **30:39 (Makkan):** *"And whatever you give for increase through the property of the people, it will not increase with God — but what you give in zakāh, seeking the face of God: those are the ones who will have manifold."* **A Makkan verse, and it already condemns it.**
- **3:130:** *"O you who believe, do not consume *ribā*, doubled and multiplied."* **This prohibits the compounded form.**
- **2:275–280:** the comprehensive, final prohibition.

**Which means the practice was already morally condemned in Makkah, was partially restricted in Madīnah, and was closed entirely here.** Anyone presenting 2:275 as the first word on the subject has the history wrong — **and the staging matters, because it shows the legislation was neither sudden nor arbitrary.**

*Fa-man jā'ahu **maw'īẓatun** min rabbihi fa-ntahā **fa-lahu mā salafa*** — "so whoever receives an **admonition** from his Lord and desists — **for him is what has passed**."

**Two things here, and both are mercy.**

**1. *Maw'iẓah* — admonition, exhortation.** Not a threat, not a punishment. **The word is the word of gentle counsel.** The verse describes the prohibition reaching a person as a *maw'īẓah* — as something meant for his good.

**2. *Fa-lahu mā salafa*** — "for him is what has passed." **The prohibition is not retroactive.** Interest already collected before the ruling reached him is not to be disgorged. **This is a major principle of the law — no retrospective criminalisation — stated in four words.**

*Wa amruhu ilā Allāh* — "and his affair is with God."

**Which leaves the inner question where it belongs.** Whether the desisting was sincere is between him and God. **No one else is authorised to investigate it.**

*Wa man 'āda fa-ulā'ika aṣḥābu al-nāri **hum fīhā khālidūn*** — "and whoever **returns** — those are the companions of the Fire; they will abide in it."

**And now the boundary, which must be stated with precision because this clause is the one most often used to frighten people and the one most often quoted by those who want to declare Muslims outside the fold.**

***'Āda* — returns.** The verb is about **going back to it after the admonition reached him.** This is not the description of a person who fell into it; **it is the description of a person who was warned and went back.**

**And on the eternity clause, the positions must be given rather than flattened:**

- **The Khārijī and Mu'tazilī reading** takes this verse — and similar ones — to establish that **the Muslim who commits a major sin abides in the Fire eternally.**
- **The position of Ahl al-Sunnah**, and the one Ṭabāṭabā'ī explicitly argues here against that inference, is that **this is not what the verse establishes.** The Sunni resolutions given are: the eternity clause applies to **the one who deems *ribā* lawful** (*mustaḥill*) — which is a rejection of the ruling itself and therefore a different matter altogether; or that the threat is **real but its execution is subject to God's will**, who may punish, forgive, or accept repentance; or that *khulūd* here is a lengthy but finite abiding, a reading also applied to similar texts.

**I am not going to resolve that debate, and I am going to state the practical consequence clearly, because it is a boundary I have held throughout this commentary:**

**A Muslim who consumes *ribā* has committed a major sin, and the threat against it is the most severe the Qur'an attaches to any financial matter. He is not thereby outside Islam, and no one may declare him an inhabitant of the Fire.** **The door of repentance is open to him, and the verses four verses later are addressed precisely to him — *O you who believe... if you repent, you may have your principal* (2:278–279).**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 276

**"Allah has made interest fruitless and charity fruitful. And Allah does not like any ungrateful evildoer."**

In simple terms: God destroys *ribā* and gives increase to charities. And God does not love any ingrate, sinner.

*Yamḥaqu Allāhu al-ribā wa yurbī al-ṣadaqāti, wa Allāhu lā yuḥibbu kulla kaffārin athīm.*

*Yamḥaqu Allāhu **al-ribā*** — "God **destroys** *ribā*."

***Yamḥaq*** — from *maḥaqa*, to efface, to wipe out, to cause to dwindle until nothing is left. **It is not "God will punish it." It is: God removes the increase from it.**

**And that is the plain claim of the verse, and it should be read as a claim about how the world works rather than as a threat held over the next life only.** The *ribā* economy produces wealth that does not last. **It is effaced.** The commentators read this both of the Hereafter — **no reward remains for it** — and of this world, where wealth acquired this way is observed to be stripped of blessing.

***Wa yurbī al-ṣadaqāt*** — "and He **gives increase** to charities."

***Yurbī*** — from *rabā*, to grow, to swell. **Note the wordplay, which is deliberate and which the Arabic makes unmissable: *al-ribā* and *yurbī* share a root.** Both mean increase.

**Which is the verse's whole point, and it is a devastating piece of rhetoric:**

**You want *ribā* — increase? Then take it by the route that actually produces increase.** The name of the thing you were chasing is being applied to charity instead. **God has taken the word for growth and attached it to the thing you give away.**

**The same root-play appears at 30:39**, which is the Makkan precursor of this verse: *"whatever you give for increase through the property of the people, it will not increase with God; but whatever you give in zakāh seeking the face of God — those are the ones who will have manifold."*

*Wa Allāhu lā yuḥibbu kulla **kaffārin athīm*** — "and God does not love any **ingrate, sinner**."

***Kaffār*** — from *kufr*, which at root means **covering**. A *kāfir* in this intensive form is one who covers over the favour — **who receives and does not acknowledge.** ***Athīm*** — one who persists in sin.

**And note which two words close the verse: the one who covers the favour, and the one who sins persistently.** Which is the exact description of the person who has just been told that the increase he has was given to him. **The sin and the ingratitude are the same motion here: taking what was given and declining to acknowledge the Giver.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 277

**"Indeed, those who believe, do good, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax will receive their reward from their Lord, and there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."**

In simple terms: indeed those who believe and do righteous deeds and establish the prayer and give the zakāt — their reward is with their Lord, and no fear upon them, nor do they grieve.

*Inna alladhīna āmanū wa 'amilū al-ṣāliḥāti wa aqāmū al-ṣalāta wa ātawu al-zakāta lahum ajruhum 'inda rabbihim wa lā khawfun 'alayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūn.*

**Note where this verse is placed: between the threat of 2:275–276 and the command of 2:278.**

**And note what it does: it restates 2:274's reward, word for word, and attaches it to a different set of people.**

2:274: **those who spend** by night and day — *lahum ajruhum 'inda rabbihim wa lā khawfun 'alayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūna.*
2:277: **those who believe, do good, pray and give zakāt** — *lahum ajruhum 'inda rabbihim wa lā khawfun 'alayhim wa lā hum yaḥzanūna.*

**Identical closing.** Which is the surah's way of saying: **these are the same people.** The one who gives properly is the one who believes and prays. **And the one who prays and pays zakāt is the one who has escaped what was just described.**

*Āmanū wa 'amilū al-ṣāliḥāti* — "believed and did righteous deeds."

**The recurring pair. Belief, then works.** The surah has used it since 2:25 and 2:82. **Neither alone.**

*Wa **aqāmū al-ṣalāta*** — "and **established** the prayer."

**Note the verb: *aqāmū* — they *established* it, set it up, kept it standing.** Not *ṣallaw* — they performed it. **The distinction the tradition draws is between performing the prayer and establishing it** — the second includes regularity, the congregation where required, and the prayer's outward and inward form. **And note: the prayer is named here, in the middle of the *ribā* passage.** Which is the surah's constant method — **the financial ruling is bracketed by the worship ruling**, as it was at 2:238–239 in the middle of the divorce passage. **Money is never treated as a separate department.**

*Wa **ātawu al-zakāta*** — "and **gave the zakāt**."

**And note that this is *zakāt*, not *ṣadaqah*.** The passage from 2:261 has been about voluntary charity. **Here, at the turning point, the obligatory due is named.** Which is the verse's structure: **voluntary giving is praiseworthy; the zakāt is not optional and is listed with the prayer.**

**And the pairing of *ṣalāh* and *zakāt* is the single most frequent pairing in the Qur'an** — over eighty occurrences. **The two are named together so consistently that the tradition treats them as one institution.**

**The point for the reader: 2:277 is the alternative economy.** It is placed here to answer 2:275. **You want to know what replaces *ribā*? Belief, works, prayer, and the zakāt.** The verses around it destroy a system; this one names the system that stands in its place.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 278

**"O believers! Fear Allah, and give up outstanding interest if you are ˹true˺ believers."**

In simple terms: O you who believe, fear God and give up what remains of *ribā*, if you are believers.

*Yā ayyuhā alladhīna āmanū ittaqū Allāha wa dharū mā baqiya mina al-ribā in kuntum mu'minīn.*

**Note that this is a direct command, in the second person plural, to the community — and note the two things it commands.**

*Ittaqū Allāha* — "fear God."

**The command opens with *taqwā*, as the serious commands in this surah do** (2:189, 2:194, 2:196, 2:231, 2:233). **And the placement is exact: the prohibition is framed as *taqwā* before it is framed as law.** You are being asked to be conscious of God, and then told what that consciousness requires.

*Wa dharū **mā baqiya** mina al-ribā* — "and give up **what remains** of *ribā*."

**Note the phrase: *mā baqiya* — what remains, what is outstanding, the balance still owed.**

**Which is a precise legal term and it settles a question.** The command is not "return everything you ever took." **It is: write off what is still outstanding on your books.** That is consistent with 2:275's *fa-lahu mā salafa* — what has passed has passed. **The obligation is prospective and immediate: stop, and cancel the balances.**

*In kuntum **mu'minīn*** — "**if you are believers**."

**And this closing is the verse's real weight, and it should not be read as mere emphasis.**

**The verse makes the abandonment of *ribā* a condition of the claim to faith.** Not "if you are pious." Not "if you want extra reward." **If you are believers.** The commentators note this pointedly: **desisting from *ribā* is stated as an inseparable property of faith**, which is why it is followed immediately by the declaration of war in the next verse.

**Now the reported occasions, and they differ, and the difference matters.**

**Report One (from Ibn 'Abbās, by way of al-Kalbī from Abū Ṣāliḥ, recorded by al-Wāḥidī; and in the reports of Zayd ibn Aslam, Ibn Jurayj, Muqātil ibn Ḥayyān and al-Suddī):** the verse was revealed about **Banū 'Amr ibn 'Umayr** (a sub-tribe of Thaqīf) and **Banū al-Mughīrah** (of Banū Makhzūm). Banū al-Mughīrah used to lend money with *ribā* to the people of Thaqīf in the Jāhiliyyah. When Islam came, Thaqīf demanded the *ribā*, and Banū al-Mughīrah said: **"We do not pay *ribā* in Islam."** Thaqīf said: **"The treaty we have stipulates that *ribā* is paid to us."** **'Attāb ibn Usayd**, the Prophet's ﷺ deputy in Makkah, wrote to the Messenger of God ﷺ about it. **And these verses were revealed.**

**Report Two (from al-Suddī):** the verse was revealed about **al-'Abbās ibn 'Abd al-Muṭṭalib and Khālid ibn al-Walīd**, who had been partners in the pre-Islamic period and lent money at interest. When Islam came, people owed them large sums.

**Both are transmitted. Neither should be asserted to the exclusion of the other.** And the substance they share is what matters: **the first dispute the new community had to settle was whether a contract written under the old order survived the new one.** Thaqīf's argument was that the treaty protected it. **The answer was that it did not.**

**And the compliance, which is recorded in both reports:** **al-'Abbās and 'Uthmān ibn 'Affān took back only the capital they had originally lent.** Not the increase. **The ruling was applied to the Prophet's own uncle.**

**And the enactment, in the Farewell Sermon** (in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, from Jābir): the Prophet ﷺ said — **"All of the *ribā* of the Jāhiliyyah is annulled, and the first *ribā* I annul is the *ribā* of al-'Abbās ibn 'Abd al-Muṭṭalib."**

**That sentence is 2:278 being carried out in public, on the Prophet's own family, at the largest gathering of his life.** It is one of the most striking moments in the *sīrah*, and it is the proof that the ruling was not theoretical.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 279

**"If you do not, then beware of a war with Allah and His Messenger! But if you repent, you may retain your principal—neither inflicting nor suffering harm."**

In simple terms: and if you do not, then be informed of a war from God and His Messenger. But if you repent, then for you is your principal — you do not wrong, and you are not wronged.

*Fa-in lam taf'alū fa-'dhanū bi-ḥarbin mina Allāhi wa rasūlihi, wa in tubtum fa-lakum ru'ūsu amwālikum lā taẓlimūna wa lā tuẓlamūn.*

*Fa-in lam taf'alū fa-'dhanū bi-**ḥarbin mina Allāhi wa rasūlih*** — "and if you do not, then **be informed of a war from God and His Messenger**."

**This is the strongest formulation in the Qur'an on any financial matter, and I do not think that can be overstated.**

**"A war from God and His Messenger."** The Qur'an uses severe language about many things. **It does not use this formulation about any other economic sin.** Not about hoarding, not about fraud in the marketplace, not about withholding zakāt. **This is the one.**

**And two words in the clause are worth their weight:**

***Ādhanū*** — from * adhina*, to give notice, to announce formally. **The imperative is: *take notice*.** It is the language of a formal declaration. **God is not sneaking up on anyone. He is serving notice.**

***Bi-ḥarbin*** — "of a war." Indefinite, which in this position is intensifying: **a war, and what a war.**

**And note whose war it is: *mina Allāhi wa rasūlihi* — from God and His Messenger.** Not from the community. Not from the state, primarily. **A declaration of war from God is not something a person can negotiate with, outrun, or wait out.** The commentators have always read this as the ultimate threat and the ultimate mercy at once — **because it is addressed to the person while he is still alive to hear it.**

**Now the enforcement question, which must be stated and then fenced immediately.**

The commentators record a disagreement: **Ibn 'Abbās, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Ibn Sīrīn and Rabī' ibn Anas held** that one who persists in consuming *ribā* within the territory of the Islamic state should be pressed to recant, **and if he persists should be put to death**; others held that **imprisonment until he repents** is sufficient; and others hold that the matter is left to the discretion of the ruler (*ta'zīr*).

**And here is the fence, which is essential: this is a question of state enforcement under a legitimate Islamic judiciary, with due process, in a polity where the law is Islam. It is emphatically not a warrant for any individual, group, or vigilante to punish anyone.** No private person has ever been authorised by this verse to do anything. **The verse's function for you and me is the threat, the warning, and the call to repent — not a penal code to administer.**

*Wa in **tubtum** fa-lakum **ru'ūsu amwālikum*** — "but if you **repent**, then for you is **your principal**."

**And now the turn, and it comes in the same breath as the declaration of war.**

**Note what is restored: *ru'ūs amwālikum* — the heads of your wealth, the capital, the original sum.** **Not the increase. The principal.**

**Which means: the lender loses the profit but not the loan.** That is the settlement. **He is not expropriated. He is returned exactly what he parted with, and no more.**

**And this is the economic logic of the whole prohibition, stated in one clause.** Under *ribā*, the lender is guaranteed a return **whether or not the borrower's venture succeeds.** The risk is entirely on one side. **The ruling restores the symmetry: you get your money back. You do not get a guaranteed return on someone else's risk.**

*Lā taẓlimūna wa lā tuẓlamūn* — "you do not wrong, and you are not wronged."

**Two negations, and they cover both directions:**
- ***Lā taẓlimūna*** — you do not wrong: **do not seek more than your principal, and do not add penalties or compound the delay.**
- ***Lā tuẓlamūn*** — you are not wronged: **you are not required to forfeit what you actually lent.**

**And that double protection is the verse's fairness, and it is worth dwelling on.** The Qur'an is not confiscating the lender's property. **It is placing both parties on the same footing: the lender bears no loss of capital and claims no guaranteed gain. The borrower returns what he took.**

**And note: this is the second time the passage has said "you will not be wronged"** (2:272 *wa antum lā tuẓlamūn*, and 2:275's closing bookend at 2:281: *wa hum lā yuẓlamūn*). **The prohibition of *ribā* is framed, three times, as a refusal to let anyone be wronged.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 280

**"If it is difficult for someone to repay a debt, postpone it until a time of ease. And if you waive it as an act of charity, it will be better for you, if only you knew."**

In simple terms: and if he is in difficulty, then [grant] respite until ease. And that you give charity [by waiving it] is better for you, if only you knew.

*Wa in kāna dhū 'usratin fa-naẓiratun ilā maysaratin, wa an taṣaddaqū khayrun lakum in kuntum ta'lamūn.*

**The most severe passage in the Qur'an on money closes with an instruction to be kind to the person who owes you.**

**Let that ordering be felt.** 2:275 declared what *ribā* does to a person. 2:279 declared war. **And 2:280 immediately says: and if the debtor is struggling, give him time.**

*Wa in kāna **dhū 'usratin*** — "and if he is **in difficulty**."

***'Usrah*** — straitened circumstances, hardship, the opposite of *yusr*, ease. **The term is general and the jurists have always read it broadly: genuine inability to pay, not mere inconvenience.**

*Fa-**naẓiratun ilā maysaratin*** — "then **respite until ease**."

***Naẓirah*** — a deferral, a postponement, a looking-ahead. ***Maysarah*** — ease, the state of being able.

**And note what is commanded: an open-ended extension until the person is able.** Not a fixed second term. Not a penalty. **Until ease.** The creditor does not get to set the date.

**And the jurists derived from this that the extension is obligatory, not optional** — the majority holding that a judge may compel the respite for a debtor established to be insolvent, **and that adding any increase for the delay is itself *ribā*.** That last point is the crucial one, and it closes the circle: **charging more because the debtor is late is exactly the practice the passage prohibits.** The pre-Islamic formula — recorded in the reports — was **"either you pay, or you increase"** (*immā an taqḍiya wa immā an tarbū*). **That is the thing being abolished.**

*Wa an **taṣaddaqū** khayrun lakum* — "and that you **give charity** is better for you."

**And now the verse goes further than the law requires.**

**Having commanded the respite, it recommends the remission.** *Taṣaddaqū* — give it as charity. **Write the debt off.**

**And note the phrasing again: *khayrun lakum* — better for you.** The same construction as 2:271's "better for you." **The benefit is named to the creditor.** Which is the Qur'an's consistent method in this passage: **it never asks someone to be generous by telling him generosity is a loss. It tells him what he gains.**

*In kuntum **ta'lamūn*** — "**if only you knew**."

**A closing that appears at 2:103 and 2:232 and here: *if you knew.*** Which is the verse's acknowledgement that **the recommendation looks like a loss and isn't.** You would do it instantly if you could see what it does. **The obstacle is not unwillingness. It is not knowing.**

**And this is the note the whole *ribā* passage is allowed to end on.** Not the declaration of war. **Not the description of the Fire. A command to give the debtor time, and a suggestion that you might forgive the debt altogether.**

**Which tells you what the passage is for.** It is not a set of financial regulations with a penalty attached. **It is a description of an economy in which the person who has money does not get to convert having it into a guaranteed return extracted from the person who does not.**

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis: Verses 271–280

**The arc.** Four verses close the charity passage (271–274): the ranking of concealment, the separation of giving from converting, the invisible poor, and the four-fold promise that closes it. **Then 275–280 prohibit *ribā***: the description of what it does (275), the root-play of increase (276), the alternative economy named in its place (277), the command to cancel outstanding balances (278), the declaration of war and the restoration of the principal (279), and the command to give the struggling debtor time (280).

**The single most important thing to see is where the passage ends.** The most severe financial legislation in the Qur'an — including a declaration of war from God — **closes with an instruction to extend the debtor's time and a suggestion that you forgive the debt.** The threat is real and it is not softened. **But the last word is mercy, and the last word is addressed to the creditor.**

**And the second thing to see is the root-play at 2:276.** *Yamḥaqu Allāhu al-ribā wa yurbī al-ṣadaqāt* — **God effaces *ribā* and gives increase to charity.** The word for increase is taken from the thing you were chasing and attached to the thing you were avoiding. **The verse is not merely prohibiting a practice. It is relocating the desire.**

**Three threads connect back across the surah.** First: **the closing "no fear upon them, nor do they grieve" is repeated identically at 2:274 and 2:277**, which is the surah's way of identifying the giver with the believer who prays and pays zakāt — **they are the same people.** Second: **2:277 names the prayer in the middle of the financial passage**, exactly as 2:238–239 interrupted the divorce passage — **the surah never treats money as a separate department.** Third: **the passage is framed three times by "you will not be wronged"** (2:272, 2:279, and 2:281 ahead) — **the prohibition of *ribā* is presented as a refusal to let either party be wronged, not as a confiscation.**

**Where the supplied translation resolves an ambiguity, it has been marked.** At 2:275, "driven to madness by Satan's touch" follows the reading of *yatakhabbaṭuhu al-shayṭānu mina al-mass* as a description of a possessed or thrashing state; **both the eschatological and the this-worldly readings have been given.** At 2:279, "you may retain your principal" renders *ru'ūsu amwālikum* — the heads of your wealth, the capital — and the echo of that phrase in 2:280's deferral has been drawn out.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. **Where the scholars differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved:** whether 2:275's description is eschatological or this-worldly (**both readings held**); what the reported occasion of 2:278 was (**two competing reports named**); whether the one who persists in *ribā* is to be executed, imprisoned, or left to discretionary punishment by the ruler; whether a Muslim who commits a major sin abides eternally in the Fire; and what *mūkil al-ribā* means in the ḥadīth of the curse — the majority render it "the one who pays it," while some scholars argue it means the one who *enables or brokers* it, a reading with significant consequences for borrowers.

**Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout** — the identification of the poor of 2:273 with the *Ahl al-Ṣuffah*; the narration of Abū Bakr's forty thousand dīnārs, **which is weak and whose connection to 2:274 is not established**; the seventy-types narration, **whose grading is disputed**; and the competing occasions of 2:278.

**Four boundaries have been observed.** On 2:275: **the eternal-abiding clause has been read within the Sunni hermeneutic and not as a licence to declare any individual Muslim an inhabitant of the Fire** — the threat is real, the sin is major, and the door of repentance is open, as 2:278–279 itself proves. On 2:275: **this verse is not a proof-text for popular theories of jinn possession, and mental illness is not attributed to jinn here in any way that should govern how a Muslim treats a sick person.** On 2:279: **the question of capital punishment is a matter of state enforcement under due process in an Islamic polity and is emphatically not a warrant for any individual to punish anyone** — the verse functions for the reader as threat, warning and call to repent. And on the contemporary application: **the question of how these verses bear on modern banking and interest has been left open and contested, with the weight of the tradition stated and the minority and necessity-based positions named — but no fatwa issued, because this commentary is not one.**

The most important sentence in the batch is the one that ends it, and it is addressed to the person holding the debt: ***wa an taṣaddaqū khayrun lakum in kuntum ta'lamūn* — and that you forgive it is better for you, if only you knew.** The passage that declares war on *ribā* closes by telling the creditor that mercy is the better deal, **and that the only reason he cannot see it is that he does not know.**
