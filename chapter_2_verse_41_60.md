# Tafsir — Surah Al-Baqarah (Chapter 2), Verses 41–60

## Verse 41

Verse 41 says: *"Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures. Do not be the first to deny them or trade them for a fleeting gain. And be mindful of Me."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now speaks directly to the Children of Israel with the central command: believe in the revelation that has come — the Qur'an — which confirms the Scriptures they already possess. They are told not to be the first to deny it, not to trade the revealed signs for a fleeting worldly gain, and to remain mindful of Allah. The verse is addressed to the people who had the greatest reason to believe and the greatest warning against rejecting.

The phrase "which confirm your Scriptures" is crucial for understanding the Qur'an's self-presentation. The Qur'an does not call itself a new religion that replaced what came before; it presents itself as the confirmation of the earlier divine messages — the Torah and the Gospel — in their original, uncorrupted essence. The Jewish community of Madinah had texts that contained descriptions of a final prophet and references to him (the Qur'an says in 2:146 that they recognized him as they recognized their own children). The command is therefore not to accept something alien but to recognize the fulfillment of what they already had. The believer's relationship to earlier revelation is not one of denial but of recognition: whatever was true in the Torah and the Gospel is confirmed by the Qur'an, and whatever was added or distorted is corrected by it.

"Do not be the first to deny them" carries a specific historical warning. The Jews of Madinah were in a position of knowledge: they had the scripture, they had the expectations, and they had the scholars who could read the signs. If they rejected the Prophet ﷺ, not only would they be wrong, but they would set the pattern for others to follow — becoming the first people of knowledge to turn away. Ibn Kathir and the classical commentators explain that the phrase addresses the leaders and scholars of the Jewish community, who understood the prophecies but chose to suppress them. The warning is against being the first among those who know to reject — because such rejection has an outsized influence. When the educated and the religious lead rejection, the rejection becomes respectable, and the whole community follows into error.

"Or trade them for a fleeting gain" is the second warning. The "fleeting gain" refers to the worldly benefits the scholars of the People of the Book received — bribes, positions, status, protection from the powerful — in exchange for hiding what they knew to be true about the Prophet ﷺ. The Qur'an returns to this accusation repeatedly (2:79, 2:174, 3:187, 5:44): knowledge of the divine message was bought and sold for a small price. The phrase is a warning that applies universally to every person who knows the truth and refuses it for advantage: the truth cannot be sold, because the price is always too low.

"And be mindful of Me" — *wa iyyāya fattaqūn* — again carries the emphatic construction: fear Me, and Me alone. The verse ends with the single orientation that would have protected the Children of Israel through every test: reverent awareness of Allah. The person who is mindful of Allah does not sell his convictions for gain, does not follow the crowd into rejection, and does not allow knowledge to become a commodity. In modern life this verse speaks to every truth-teller: the scholar, the journalist, the lawyer, the teacher, the religious leader, and the ordinary person, who is tempted to soften, conceal, or trade the truth for comfort, approval, or profit.

## Verse 42

Verse 42 says: *"Do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly."*

In simple terms, this verse gives two prohibitions that define the ethics of those who possess knowledge. Do not mix truth with falsehood — do not blend the two so that people cannot distinguish them. And do not hide the truth — do not conceal it once you know it. The verse is addressed to the People of the Book, but its principle is universal: truth is a trust, and the person who corrupts or conceals it has betrayed the trust.

The first prohibition — *lā talbisū al-haqqa bil-bātil* — uses the verb *labasa*, which means to dress, to clothe, to mix. The image is of clothing truth in falsehood, draping one over the other so that they are confused. This was exactly what some Jewish scholars of Madinah were doing: they mixed the authentic teachings of the Torah with their own interpretations, and they mixed the descriptions of the coming Prophet with distortions and interpretations designed to conceal him. The method is ancient and universal: take a grain of truth, surround it with falsehood, and the mixture becomes more convincing than pure falsehood would be. Classic examples in Islamic literature include the practice of writing one's own words and attributing them to Allah (2:79), and of interpreting scripture in ways that serve private interests (5:13, 5:41). The verse also attacks the softer forms of this practice: cherry-picking evidence, quoting out of context, using the right words for the wrong purpose, and presenting opinion as revelation.

The second prohibition — *wa lā taktumū al-haqqa wa antum ta'lamūn* — "do not conceal the truth while you know." The addition of "while you know" makes the sin unmistakable. Hiding what one does not know is not hiding; it is ignorance, and it is forgivable. Hiding what one knows to be true is a deliberate act of betrayal. The Prophet ﷺ warned that whoever hides knowledge that people need will be bridled with fire on the Day of Judgment (a narration recorded by al-Tirmidhi and others, and the Qur'an itself says in 2:159 that those who hide the clear proofs after We made them clear are cursed by Allah and all who curse). The scholars of the Children of Israel knew the descriptions of the final Prophet; they hid them because of jealousy, fear, and worldly gain. The verse names both sins — mixing and concealing — because they are the two ways truth is destroyed: the active corruption of the message, and the passive silence of those who know.

These two prohibitions also define an ethic of communication that is profoundly relevant to the modern age. Every field — journalism, science, law, medicine, religious teaching, business, social media — is filled with truth that gets mixed with falsehood and knowledge that gets concealed. The Qur'anic standard for the person of knowledge is the opposite of both: present truth clearly, distinguish it from error, and never withhold what people need to know for personal advantage. This verse is one of the strongest statements in the Qur'an on intellectual honesty, and it begins with the word "do not" — a prohibition, not a recommendation. Truthfulness is not a virtue to be practiced when convenient; it is a boundary that must never be crossed.

## Verse 43

Verse 43 says: *"Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and bow down with those who bow down."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now commands the Children of Israel to do three things that are, in fact, the pillars of Islamic practice: establish prayer, pay the alms-tax, and bow with those who bow. The verse shows that the fundamental worship of the earlier communities was the same worship Islam commands: prayer, purification of wealth, and communal devotion. The revelation is not introducing a new religion of ritual but calling the people back to the rites that had been theirs all along.

"Establish prayer" — *wa aqīmū al-salāh* — we discussed this phrase under verse 3: it means to set prayer firmly on its foundations — at its times, with its conditions, with presence of heart. The Qur'an mentions the one who "neglects" or "forgets" prayer, and it describes prayer as the connection between the servant and the Lord: "Prayer restrains from immorality and evil" (29:45). For the Children of Israel, prayer was part of their inheritance, but they had neglected its spirit even when they maintained its form. The command to "establish" it is a call to both the outward observance and the inward presence.

"Pay alms-tax" — *ātu al-zakāh* — is purification of wealth: the fixed obligation that takes a portion of what one owns and gives it to those in need. Zakah is not charity as a favor; it is a right of the poor in the wealth of the rich (70:24-25). The effect of zakah is both social and spiritual: it circulates wealth, prevents the concentration of riches in a few hands, and purifies the giver of greed and attachment. The pairing of prayer and zakah is one of the constants of the Qur'an — the two are mentioned together more than twenty times — because worship of the Lord and service to His creation are inseparable. A person who prays but does not give has not understood his prayer.

"Bow down with those who bow down" — *wa irka'ū ma'a al-rāki'īn* — is the third command, and it adds an element that the first two do not: community. The command is not merely to bow but to bow *with those who bow* — with the congregation, in the company of those who worship. This is one of the Qur'an's foundational statements of the communal nature of worship: prayer is not a private act of self-improvement but a collective action of the community. The verse also implied the command to join the new community of believers — the *rāki'ūn* — when it was revealed in Madinah. Classical commentators saw in this phrase a call to the Muslims' congregational prayer, and by extension to the ummah: the believer worships Allah *with* the believers, not merely alongside them.

This verse has a profound message for the modern reader. The spiritual life is not self-contained; it requires the anchor of ritual, the discipline of communal worship, and the habit of giving from one's wealth. A person who says he has faith but does not pray, does not give, and does not join the community of worshippers is missing the whole structure of the practice the Qur'an describes. The verse also speaks to the value of the congregation: the believer who prays alone may drift, but the believer who bows with those who bow is held by the row, by the imam, by the others — and is reminded, five times a day, that he is part of a people of worship.

## Verse 44

Verse 44 says: *"Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?"*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now delivers one of its most famous rebukes: you command people to do good and forget your own selves, while you recite the Book. Do you not reason? The verse addresses the scholars of the Jewish community who urged people toward faith and righteousness but refused to act on what they preached. The rebuke, however, is universal, and it has become the Qur'an's classic condemnation of hypocrisy in the religious life.

The context, reported by Ibn Abbas and other early authorities through Ibn Ishaq and others, is that the Jewish scholars of Madinah were urging the common people to accept the Prophet ﷺ and to practice righteousness — while they themselves did not accept him, because accepting him would cost them their positions of leadership and their worldly privileges. Ibn Kathir records the interpretation of Qatadah: these self-regarding preachers "used to command people to pray and fast, but they did not practice what they commanded others." Ibn Abbas is also reported as saying the verse means: you forbid people from rejecting the prophethood that you yourselves testified to in the Torah, yet you forgot your own covenant and rejected it. The verse, then, is not a criticism of commanding good — commanding good is itself an obligation for the scholar — but of failing to be the first among those who follow it. The Prophet Shu'ayb gave the model: "I do not wish to contradict you by doing what I forbid you" (11:88). The true teacher is the first doer.

The Arabic word *al-birr* is even broader than "righteousness": it includes every act of obedience to Allah, every kindness, faith, truthfulness, and devotion. The verse's structure is devastating: "Do you command people to al-birr and forget yourselves?" The word "forget" (*tansawn*) is precise. It is not "you knowingly rebel" but "you forget" — the preacher's hypocrisy is a form of forgetting oneself, of becoming so absorbed in directing others that one loses sight of one's own soul. The one who preaches continuously without practicing is, in the deepest sense, absent from his own life.

The verb *tatlūn* — "you recite" — is the most damning detail. The very book they were quoting contained the warnings they were violating. They were not ignorant of the demands; they were readers of the demands. A person who recites verses about prayer and charity while neglecting both is not merely inconsistent; he has made the text a hobby rather than a command. The verse ends with *afalā ta'qilūn* — "do you not reason?" — an appeal to the very faculty that should have caught the contradiction. The contradiction is so obvious that only deliberate self-deception can sustain it.

The contemporary application is direct and uncomfortable. This verse is a mirror for every religious teacher, every leader, every parent, every mentor, and every person on social media who urges others toward virtue. The Qur'an's standard is that the preacher must live the sermon, that the teacher must be the first student, and that the one who commands good must command himself first. The person who cannot practice what he preaches does not become a better preacher; he becomes a warning. And the Qur'an's question — do you not understand? — is addressed to the intelligence that knows the truth and chooses to ignore its own application. For the believer, this verse is not for measuring others but for self-audit: do I do what I tell others to do? Am I the first of those I call?

## Verse 45

Verse 45 says: *"And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble—"*

In simple terms, after the rebuke to those who preach without practicing, the Qur'an now gives the remedy. Seek help through patience and prayer. The command is addressed to the believer who is being tested — and it acknowledges, honestly, that this help is heavy: prayer and patience are a burden, except for those who are humble. The verse does not pretend that worship is easy; it says what makes it easy.

The phrase *ista'īnū bi al-sabr wa al-salāh* — "seek help through patience and prayer" — presents the two supports of the believer in distress. Patience (*sabr*) is the soul's endurance: the ability to remain firm under trial, to control the impulse to despair or to strike out in anger. Prayer (*salāh*) is the soul's connection: the act that turns the burdened heart toward its Lord. The two are paired because they work together: prayer gives patience its strength, and patience gives prayer its sincerity. The believer who is overwhelmed can do one of two things — or rather, one thing with two sides: be patient, and pray. This is the Qur'an's answer to anxiety, loss, injustice, and fear.

Why is prayer called "help"? Because prayer is not merely a ritual performed for Allah; it is a means by which the believer is helped. The Prophet ﷺ made this practical reality famous: Bilal would be told to "call the prayer, and give us comfort by it" (recorded by Abu Dawud and others). When the Prophet ﷺ faced hardship, his answer was the prayer — and this verse is the Qur'anic ground for that practice. Prayer does not remove the problem, but it changes the person facing the problem: it replaces confusion with direction, fear with trust, and isolation with communion with Allah.

The verse then makes a remarkable admission: *wa innahā la-kabīratun* — "and indeed it is a great thing." The reference is to prayer — or, in the most natural reading, to the whole life of faith described in these verses. The Qur'an does not say that prayer is easy. It is hard: to pray at the prescribed times, in the middle of work, in the middle of despair, in the middle of distraction, with the full presence of the heart. The Qur'an says so directly, and then says what makes it otherwise: "except for the humble" — *illā 'alā al-khāshi'īn*. The word *khushū'* is the specific state of humility, submission, and stillness of heart before Allah. It is the opposite of arrogance, of the self that believes it can handle everything alone. The person who is humble finds prayer light, because he knows he needs it. The person who is proud finds prayer heavy, because he believes he does not.

This distinction explains one of the most common spiritual experiences: two people pray the same prayer, at the same time, in the same masjid, and one finds it a burden while the other finds it a relief. The difference is not the ritual; it is the heart. Prayer becomes heavy when it is an obligation performed for an audience; it becomes light when it is a lifeline grasped by a humble soul. The cure for the heaviness of worship is therefore not to abandon worship but to change the heart — to approach Allah with humility, to admit one's need, and to let the prayer be what it always was: a conversation between the needy and the Merciful.

## Verse 46

Verse 46 says: *"those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now explains who the "humble" ones are, whose prayer and patience are light: those who are certain that they will meet their Lord, and that to Him they will return. The verse defines the deepest source of the believer's strength: the certainty of the meeting with Allah. A person who is certain of the meeting has a different relationship with hardship, with worship, and with life itself.

The word *yaqūnūn* — "are certain" — comes from *yaqīn*, the settled conviction we met in verse 4. The humble are not merely hopeful that they will meet Allah; they are certain. This certainty is an intellectual state built on evidence — the signs of creation, the testimony of the prophets, the coherence of revelation — and it is also a spiritual state, a settled assurance that pervades the heart. The Qur'an describes the believers as those who have "certainty of the meeting with their Lord" in several places (32:21, 6:31), and it identifies this certainty as one of the things that distinguishes the one who is guided from the one who is lost.

"Meet their Lord" — *liqā' rabbihim* — is one of the most debated phrases in the Qur'an. Does it mean the Day of Judgment, the resurrection, or the actual vision of Allah? The classical scholars held a range of views. The majority read "the meeting" as encompassing the resurrection, the standing before Allah, the accounting, and the final encounter — and the strongest view within the tradition, represented by many of the early authorities and affirmed by later scholars, is that the believers will see Allah in Paradise, as the Qur'an says: "Faces on that Day will be radiant, looking upon their Lord" (75:22-23), and as the Prophet ﷺ taught in the famous hadith: "You will see your Lord on the Day of Judgment as you see the moon on the night it is full" (Sahih al-Bukhari, Sahih Muslim — the vision of Allah is a matter of settled belief in the Sunnah, though how it happens is beyond human comprehension). The verse does not require the reader to resolve every detail: it states the reality — there is a meeting — and the believer trusts its reality even while not grasping its mode. What matters is the effect: the certainty of the meeting shapes the life.

"To Him they will return" — *wa annahum ilayhi rāji'ūn* — is the second half of the conviction. The human being is not lost in the universe but is on a journey toward a destination: Allah. Every experience, good and bad, is part of that journey. The person who is certain of the return does not treat life as a random accident or death as final annihilation; he treats life as a trust and death as the passage to the meeting. This is the ground of the believer's patience in the previous verse: it is easy to endure when one knows where one is going. The certainty of the return is the spiritual engine of all the virtues: patience, gratitude, courage, repentance, and hope.

In modern psychological terms, the verse speaks to the need for meaning. Anxiety and despair in human life are often rooted not in immediate circumstances but in the sense that nothing ultimately matters — that all effort ends in nothing. The Qur'anic answer is the opposite: everything matters, because everything is heading toward a meeting with the One who made it. The humble believer does not carry the burden of life alone; he holds it with the certainty that it has a destination. This is why the verse says the humble find prayer light: the one who is certain of the meeting with his Lord knows that the prayer connects him to the very One he is about to meet — and that the connection is already real.

## Verse 47

Verse 47 says: *"O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an begins the detailed recounting of the favours with a direct address: O Children of Israel, remember the favours I granted you, and remember that I honoured you above the others. The verse establishes the frame for the whole following section: the history of the Children of Israel is a history of divine favour, and the correct response to favour is gratitude and obedience.

The phrase *udhkurū ni'matiya* — "remember My favours" — is the same command that opened the address in verse 40, now expanded. The favour (*ni'mah*) is comprehensive: the deliverance from bondage, the parting of the sea, the manna and quails, the shade, the water, the Torah, the covenant, the prophets, and the place of honour in the plan of revelation. The command to "remember" is not a request for nostalgia; it is a call to moral awakening. In the Qur'anic worldview, forgetfulness (*nisyān*) is the root of disobedience: the person who forgets the favours of Allah becomes ungrateful, and the ungrateful person drifts into rebellion. Remembering, by contrast, is the beginning of gratitude, and gratitude is the beginning of obedience.

"How I honoured you above the others" — *wa annī faddaltukum 'alā al-'ālamīn* — is the phrase that requires the most careful reading. The *'ālamīn* here means the people of the world of that time — the contemporaries of the Children of Israel, the other nations of Moses' era. The Qur'an does not say the Children of Israel are superior to all humanity for all time; it says that within their historical context, they were granted a unique honour: they were the people who received the Torah, who were delivered from Pharaoh, who witnessed the miracles, and who were chosen as the line through which the great prophets came. The same phrase recurs in 2:122 and 7:140 ("Allah has chosen you above the worlds"), and it is always understood in this temporal and covenantal sense. The Qur'an repeatedly makes clear that this honour was conditional and that the later generations of the Children of Israel forfeited it through their disobedience (2:87, 2:91, 3:110-112 — the latter describing how the People of the Book are no longer above the worlds).

The theological point is important for the modern reader. The verses are not an endorsement of ethnic or religious superiority. They are a historical acknowledgment of a role: the Children of Israel were the bearers of divine revelation for a period, and they were honoured because of that role, not because of their own merit or race. The same pattern applies to the ummah of Muhammad ﷺ: Allah calls the Muslims "the best community brought forth for humanity" (3:110) — but again, the honour is tied to the function: commanding good, forbidding evil, and believing in Allah. Honour in the Qur'an is functional, not racial. A community that abandons its function abandons its honour.

The practical lesson of the verse is the discipline of gratitude. Every community — and every individual — has received favours that, when arranged, form a history of divine care. The person who remembers his own history honestly — the difficulties he survived, the blessings he received, the protection he did not deserve — is moved toward gratitude and humility. The person who forgets it becomes entitled and arrogant. The Qur'an's command "Remember My favours" is a command to every generation: tell the story of mercy, learn from it, and let it shape the present.

## Verse 48

Verse 48 says: *"Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now commands the Children of Israel to fear the Day of Judgment — and it describes that Day in terms of its absolute isolation. No soul will be able to help another. No intercession will be accepted. No ransom will be taken. No help will be given. The verse is one of the Qur'an's most powerful statements about the final Day, and it sets a critical frame for understanding the People of the Book's false confidence in their ancestors, their scriptures, or their special status.

The central teaching of the verse is *individual accountability*. On the Day of Judgment, every soul stands alone before Allah. The parent cannot help the child. The prophet cannot help his follower beyond what Allah permits. The leader cannot answer for the follower. The verse is a reply to the Jewish claim that they would be saved because they are descendants of prophets — a claim the Qur'an dismantles repeatedly through this section: "Neither your blood nor theirs will benefit you" (and the direct challenge in 2:94-96). The Day of accounting is not determined by ancestry, by group membership, by religious labels, or by the merits of others. It is determined by what each soul did with its life, knowledge, and opportunities.

The three negations — no intercession accepted, no ransom taken, no help given — are the complete picture of the Day's severity. "No intercession" is a phrase that must be understood carefully, because later verses of the Qur'an affirm that intercession will occur: "Who is there to intercede with Him except with His permission?" (2:255), and "They cannot intercede except for those Allah is pleased with" (21:28). The resolution, as the classical commentators explain, is that intercession is not a natural right of any creature or any special group; it arises only by Allah's permission and for those He approves. The verse here is stating that intercession without Allah's permission — the kind the Jews believed their fathers or the prophets would secure for them automatically — will not be accepted. The whole account belongs to Allah; no one can obtain anything from Him except through what He has granted. The believer does not rely on the intercession of any being; he relies on Allah's mercy and his own sincere deeds.

"No ransom taken" is the second negation. In pre-Islamic and biblical law, a ransom could sometimes substitute for a penalty. The Qur'an says the Day of Judgment is not like that: no one can buy his way out, no payment of wealth, no sacrifice, no substitute will be accepted. The one who sows must reap. The third negation, "no help," completes the picture: on that Day, the sources of help that people ordinarily rely on — wealth, status, power, allies, connections — are utterly useless. Only the mercy of Allah and the weight of one's own deeds remain.

The psychological effect of this verse is intended: it replaces false security with honest preparation. The Children of Israel needed to stop relying on ancestry and to start relying on their own faith and deeds. Every reader of the verse faces the same question: what am I relying on? If the answer is "my family," "my community," "my organization," "my reputation," "my religious label," or "my ancestors' merits," the verse is a warning. If the answer is "my faith, my good deeds, and the mercy of Allah," the verse is a call to strengthen that reliance. The Day is certain; the preparation begins now.

## Verse 49

Verse 49 says: *"˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now begins the list of historical favours, and it begins with the greatest of them: the deliverance from Pharaoh. The Children of Israel were enslaved in Egypt, subjected to brutal torment — their sons were slaughtered and their women kept alive — and Allah delivered them from the people of Pharaoh. The verse ends with an important observation: that deliverance was itself a severe test from the Lord.

The story is one of the most repeated in the Qur'an, appearing in more than twenty surahs. The details here are compressed but historically and biblically consistent: Pharaoh's rule subjected the Israelites to forced labour (28:14-15, 40:30), and his policy of slaughtering male children and keeping the females was a method of destroying the people as a viable community (28:4: "He slaughtered their sons and kept their women alive"). The Qur'an presents this as the height of tyranny: the systematic destruction of a people, justified by the fear that they would rise. The deliverance from it was not merely political freedom; it was rescue from genocide, and it is always described by the Qur'an as divine mercy.

"That was a severe test from your Lord" — *wa fī dhālikum balā'un min rabbikum 'aẓīm* — is the decisive line of the verse. The word *balā'* means trial, test, affliction — and its presence here is the key to the Qur'an's reading of history. The ordeal was not a random misfortune; it was a test. And the test had two levels. For Pharaoh, the test was exposure: he was shown the miracles, refused, and was destroyed. For the Children of Israel, the test was endurance under tyranny and gratitude after deliverance. The Qur'an is always interested in the moral meaning of events, and it frames history as a series of tests for both oppressors and oppressed. The people who suffered were being tested with patience and faith; the people who were delivered were being tested with gratitude.

The verse also establishes a pattern that will run through the entire section: divine favour, human response, and the consequences of the response. The Children of Israel were delivered, and they should have responded with gratitude, obedience, and faith. Instead, as the following verses show, they complained, they doubted, they worshipped the calf, they demanded to see Allah, they asked for different food — a whole history of ingratitude. The Qur'an is not narrating this history to condemn the Children of Israel as a people; it is narrating it as a lesson for the believers of Madinah and for all humanity: the same pattern of favour and response, gratitude and ingratitude, is the pattern of every human life.

In modern terms, the verse speaks to the problem of interpreting suffering. People often ask, "Why did this happen to me?" The Qur'an's answer is not fatalistic; it is moral. Suffering can be a test of patience, a purification of faith, a call to remember. And deliverance, the Qur'an says, is likewise a test: the test of gratitude. Both the one who suffers and the one who is rescued are being examined — and the examination is what the believer calls *balā'*, a trial from the Lord, whether the difficulty or the relief. The person who understands this is freed from both bitterness in suffering and arrogance in success, because both are part of the same test.

## Verse 50

Verse 50 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh's people before your very eyes."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now recounts the scene of the exodus: the sea was parted, the Children of Israel were rescued, and the people of Pharaoh were drowned before their very eyes. The verse is short but contains one of the most dramatic moments of salvation in the Qur'an, and it is told with a deliberate emphasis: the Israelites were not merely told about the rescue; they witnessed it.

The story is the crossing of the Red Sea, told in detail in Surah Al-Shu'ara' (26:61-68) and Surah Al-A'raf (7:136-138): when Pharaoh's army pursued the Israelites to the edge of the water, Allah commanded Moses to strike the sea with his staff, and the sea split into two great walls of water, with a dry path in between. The Israelites crossed. When Pharaoh and his army followed, the waters closed upon them and they drowned. The Qur'an describes the event as one of the greatest of Allah's signs, and it uses it as proof of divine power: the Creator who splits the sea is the One who is able to raise the dead.

"Before your very eyes" — *wa antum tanzurūn* — "while you were looking." This is the emphasis of the verse. The children of Israel saw the sea part. They saw the dry path. They saw the army of Pharaoh enter after them. And they saw the waters close upon their enemies. This was not hearsay, not legend, not a distant story: it was witnessed. The Qur'an's point is that the deliverance was visible, undeniable, and still they would later say to Moses, "we will not believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes" (2:55) — as though the miracles they had witnessed were not enough. The history of the Children of Israel is, in this sense, a history of witnessed evidence and insufficient response. The believers of Madinah, and every reader of this verse, are being asked: what more evidence do you need?

The verse also carries the Qur'an's central theological argument for resurrection built on the deliverance: the God who parted the sea and drowned the pursuing army also has power over life and death. The same event that saved a nation and destroyed a tyrant is an evidence of the final Day. This is why the Qur'an repeats: "In this there is a sign" (26:67, 26:103, 26:121). The exodus is not only history; it is a sign of the power that will bring every soul to account.

There is also a justice dimension to the verse. Pharaoh's crime had been the slaughter of the Israelite sons; his punishment was the drowning of his own army in the sea that had been his instrument of pursuit. The Qur'an presents this as the pattern of divine justice: the oppressions of the oppressor return upon him. The same Pharaoh who had said, "I will slaughter their sons and keep their women" (28:4; the sequence summarized) was destroyed in the way he had sought to destroy. The believer reads this and understands that injustice is not unaccounted; history, seen through the eyes of revelation, is full of divine compensation.

In modern life, this verse speaks to hope in the face of overwhelming power. The Israelites were a powerless, enslaved people; Pharaoh had the army, the chariots, the state. From every human calculation, they were doomed. The verse is the Qur'an's witness that human calculation is not the final calculation: the sea can part. The believer who is facing a seemingly impossible situation — an oppressive system, an illness, a debt, a broken family — is given the image of the parted sea: Allah's power is not limited to what the oppressor can imagine. And the believers' responsibility is the same as that of the Israelites: walk through the path when it opens, with faith, gratitude, and witness.

===VERSE-END===
## Verse 51

Verse 51 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights for Moses, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully."*

In simple terms, the next favour is introduced by its failure. Allah appointed forty nights for Moses, during which he was to receive the Torah from his Lord. In his absence, the Children of Israel made a calf and worshipped it. The verse says the two facts side by side with a harsh moral judgment: you acted wrongfully.

The "forty nights" refer to the period of Moses' retreat on Mount Sinai, where he was summoned to receive the revelation. The Qur'an mentions both forty nights (2:51, 7:142) and the broader appointment; the number is consistent in the Islamic text and has a parallel in the biblical account of Moses' forty days on the mountain (Exodus 24:18). The period was a covenant appointment: God had summoned His prophet, and the people were expected to remain in patience and faith, awaiting his return with the scripture.

Instead, the people broke. The account is given elsewhere: a man called the Samiri (as in 20:85-97) took the people's gold ornaments, made a calf with a lowing sound, and called upon the people to worship it, saying "This is your god and the god of Moses." Some of the people obeyed. Moses' own brother, Harun, had remained behind and had warned them, saying, "O my people, you are being tested by it; your Lord is the Most Merciful, so follow me and obey my command" (20:90). But most of them refused: "We will not cease worshipping it until Moses returns" (20:91). The Qur'an is the one who records this story, and it uses it to draw a double lesson: the test of absence and the speed of fall.

The first lesson is about distance from the prophet and the revelation. Moses was present, and the people were contained. Moses was absent for forty nights, and within that absence they made an idol. The absence of the guide is the moment when the heart reveals itself. The same pattern applies to every community: the presence of prophets, scholars, and reminders keeps people anchored; the absence of guidance is the opening through which every falsehood enters. The "children of Israel" did not need a long time to fall; they fell as soon as the anchor was removed. The Qur'an's audience — the believers of Madinah — is being warned: do not rely on the presence of others for your own faith. Change of heart must be genuine.

The second lesson is about the nature of the sin. The verse says *fa ittakhadhtum al-'ijl* — "you took the calf" — and the verb carries the sense of adoption: they adopted it as a god. The act is described as *zālimūn* — wrongdoers. The severity is not merely that they made a statue; it is that they associated a created thing with the Creator. This is *shirk*, the sin the Qur'an calls unforgivable if one dies upon it (4:48, 4:116) — and the irony is that they had been led away from a universe full of signs, a sea that had just been split, by the voice of a man who cast gold into a fire. The lesson is that faith must be based on what is real, and the first test of reality is the temptation to follow the spectacular and the immediate — the shiny calf — rather than the commanded truth, which in the moment seems invisible.

The verse also teaches about the danger of idolatry in the modern world. The calf is not a primitive superstition; it is the symbol of a fabricated deity constructed to satisfy people's desires. Every generation makes its own calves: money, power, ideology, technology, status, nationalism, and the approval of others. The "calf" of the modern age is anything that people gather around, worship through their attention, and sacrifice their obligations to. The Qur'anic warning is that the community which abandons its covenant and its guide in search of a visible, immediate, constructed god becomes, in the language of the verse, a community of wrongdoers.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 52

Verse 52 says: *"Even then We ˹still˺ forgave you so perhaps you would be grateful."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an states the mercy that followed the sin: even after the calf-worship, Allah forgave them. The verse is short but it is one of the most astonishing statements of divine mercy in the whole narrative: the people who made an idol, in the presence of the evidence, in the absence of the prophet, were forgiven. The purpose is stated: so perhaps you would be grateful.

The word *thumma* — "then, even after that" — connects the forgiveness to the crime. The forgiveness did not come because the crime was small; it came despite the crime. This is a masterstroke of the Qur'an's narrative structure: the section of favours is not a celebration of the Children of Israel's obedience; it is a testimony of Allah's mercy in the face of their repeated failure. The verses are arranged so that every human failure is followed by divine mercy: the calf-worship is followed by forgiveness; the demand to see Allah is followed by restoration; the complaints about food are followed by further provision. The Qur'an's history of the Children of Israel is, in the end, a history of mercy more than of sin.

This pattern of mercy contains the central theological insight of the narrative: Allah's mercy is not dependent on human performance; but the response to mercy is expected to be gratitude. The phrase *la'allakum tashkurūn* — "so perhaps you would be grateful" — is the conditional purpose. The forgiveness did not merely cancel a debt; it was given in order to create gratitude. The one who is forgiven and does not become grateful has not received the purpose of the forgiveness; he has only received the cancellation. The Qur'an's whole ethic of worship is built on this: gratitude is the response of the creature to the favour of the Creator, and ingratitude (*kufr*) is the refusal of that response — which is why the Qur'an uses the same root for "disbelief" and "ingratitude."

The sequence of the verse is also a model of repentance and mercy. Notice the order: (1) the people sinned; (2) Allah forgave them; (3) the forgiveness was designed to produce gratitude. There is no recorded repentance before the forgiveness in this verse — the repentance comes later (verse 54), when Moses returns and commands them to kill themselves as the specific form of repentance. But the forgiveness in verse 52 was already given, and the later command in verse 54 was the discipline of that mercy. The Qur'an is teaching that mercy and discipline can coexist: Allah forgave them, and He also prescribed for them a severe act of cleansing.

For the modern reader, this verse is the answer to despair. A person who has committed a grave sin — who has, in a sense, made a "calf" of some desire — may believe he has gone beyond forgiveness. The verse is a direct contradiction of that belief: the people who worshipped a calf, at the very origin of their prophetic history, were forgiven. If they were forgiven, then no one is beyond the reach of Allah's mercy. The only condition is the one stated in the purposes of the verse: gratitude — the acknowledgment of the sin, the turn back to Allah, and the life that shows thankfulness. The believer who has failed is not invited to live in the shame of the failure; he is invited to live in the gratitude of the forgiveness.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 53

Verse 53 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We gave Moses the Scripture—the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺ that perhaps you would be ˹rightly˺ guided."*

In simple terms, the next favour is the greatest of the physical one in the section: the Scripture — the Torah — given to Moses. The verse describes it with a single key word: *al-furqān*, "the criterion" or "the standard that distinguishes between right and wrong." And the purpose is stated: so that perhaps you would be rightly guided.

The word *furqān* comes from the root *farq*, meaning "to separate, to distinguish." The Scripture, as described by the Qur'an, is the great separator: it separates truth from falsehood, the lawful from the unlawful, guidance from misguidance, in matters of belief, law, and conduct. The word is used in the Qur'an for the Torah (2:53, 21:48), for the Qur'an (25:1: "Blessed is He who sent down the Furqan to His servant"), and for the moment at which truth and falsehood were separated at the Battle of Badr (8:41). The essential meaning is the same throughout: the revelation is the standard by which all claims are judged.

The historical context is critical: the Torah was given to Moses at the conclusion of the forty nights, at the very moment the people had fallen into the calf-worship. The Scripture was given against the backdrop of the community's failure — and the point is that the scripture itself was the remedy for the failure. The people had followed a visible miracle — the calf — without a standard; the Torah gave them the standard. The same pattern applies to every community: people who lack an authoritative criterion find themselves at the mercy of their desires, their leaders, and their cultural suggestions. The revelation is the criterion that stands above all of them.

"That perhaps you would be rightly guided" — *la'allakum tahtadūn* — states the purpose of the scripture and its condition. The scripture is guidance; the guidance is received by those who seek it. The phrase is one of the Qur'an's recurring formulas: the scripture is not magic; it is a guide, and it must be followed. The Children of Israel were given the Torah, and they were expected to be guided by it — to distinguish right from wrong, to follow the commands, to accept the prophets. The same purpose is stated for the Qur'an itself.

There is also a subtlety in the verse that modern readers should appreciate: the word *furqān* is a reminder that religion is not merely about devotion but about discrimination. The person who is guided knows the difference between what is true and what is false, between what is commanded and what is forbidden, between the message of Allah and the invention of people. This is a critical function for every generation, because falsehood always arrives dressed as truth, and confusion always accompanies innovation. The scripture is the school of discrimination.

In modern life, the verse speaks to the need for a standard in a time of information saturation. The internet, the media, and the marketplace of ideas present thousands of claims; without a criterion, people simply absorb what is repeated most loudly, or what aligns with their desires. The believer's criterion is the revelation: it is the *furqān* that governs what he accepts, what he rejects, and how he decides. The person who leaves the scripture aside to follow the trend is precisely the person the verse warns about; the person who holds it as the standard is the one who is "rightly guided."

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 54

Verse 54 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, "O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf, so turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves. That is best for you in the sight of your Creator." Then He accepted your repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now describes the specific repentance required of the Children of Israel after the calf-worship. Moses tells them: you have wronged yourselves; turn to your Creator in repentance; and execute the calf-worshippers among yourselves. That was best for them in the sight of their Creator. Then Allah accepted the repentance. The verse is the most dramatic statement of repentance in the whole narrative, and it has been misunderstood more often than almost anything else in the surah.

The first misunderstanding is the literal reading of "kill yourselves" as suicide. This is not what the verse means, and the classical exegetes are agreed that the command is not suicide. The Arabic phrase *uqtulū anfusakum* literally reads "kill yourselves," but in the Qur'anic usage it is addressed to a group, and the meaning is "kill one another" — that is, let the innocent among you execute the guilty among you. Ibn Abbas, as recorded by al-Nasa'i, Ibn Jarir, and Ibn Abi Hatim, explained that the command meant: slay by the sword every person you meet, be he father or son — those who did not worship the calf killing those who did. The translation supplied by the user itself shows the interpretation: "execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves." The Muslim scholars have always understood it this way, and the verse should never be read as a divine command to commit suicide.

The repentance itself was extraordinary: the people were separated into those who had worshipped the calf and those who had not; the latter were commanded to kill the former with the sword. Ibn Abbas relates that Allah ordered Moses to command the people to kill each other — the innocent killing the guilty — and that when they began, a great darkness fell upon them; when it lifted, about seventy thousand had been killed. Those killed were forgiven, and those who remained were also forgiven. The accounts of the precise number come from early reports and should be handled with care, but the account of the punishment as the prescribed form of repentance is well established in the classical tafsir. The severity of the repentance reflects the severity of the sin: idolatry at the origin of the community's faith did not have a trivial remedy.

Why did Allah require such a severe act? Classical commentators explain several reasons: the punishment was a purification of the community — the sin of idolatry could not be carried forward unaddressed; it was a deterrent — the visible consequence of turning from the Torah to an idol; and it was an act of justice carried out by those who remained faithful. But the deepest reason is stated in the verse itself: *dhālikum khayrun lakum* — "that is better for you" — better than living with the sin unaddressed, better than the compromise that lets falsehood survive. The repentance was the community's choice to purify itself, and the acceptance of the repentance is the divine mercy that follows the sincere act.

The modern application is in the sphere of the heart, not the sword. The believer who has committed a grave sin is being taught the standard of genuine repentance: the sin must be eradicated, not merely regretted. Mere verbal regret is not enough; the repentance takes the form of real action — removing the cause of the sin, cutting off the sources, making the correction publicly where needed, and restoring what was harmed. The Prophet ﷺ taught, "Follow a bad deed with a good deed and it will erase it" (Tirmidhi 1987) — the bad deed is erased by the good, not by the good intention. The verse's final words — "Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful" — the same names that closed the Adam narrative in verse 37 — close this one too, showing that the mercy of Allah is constant across every failure, if only the repentance is sincere.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 55

Verse 55 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when you said, "O Moses! We will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes," so a thunderbolt struck you while you were looking on."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now recounts another failure of the Children of Israel in Moses' time: they demanded to see Allah with their own eyes, refusing to believe Moses' testimony without a direct vision. The demand was so presumptuous that a thunderbolt seized them while they looked on. The verse is the Qur'an's record of the human refusal to accept revelation on the basis of evidence, and the demand for impossible direct certainty.

The demand is recorded in the same surah in the context of Moses' miracles: the sea had been split, the manna and quails had descended, the mountain was about to shake, and yet the people said, "O Moses, we will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes." The words *jahratan* — "with our own eyes," literally "openly," "clearly" — emphasize the demand for a visible, unmediated encounter. The Qur'an elsewhere describes the same people: "We will not believe you until we see Allah plainly" (4:153). The demand is the perfect picture of the demand for compulsion in faith.

The rabbis of the Jews taught that no human being could see God and live; the demand was therefore impossible by nature, and the children of Israel are often regarded as having been punished for its imperiousness. The Qur'an confirms the impossibility of the vision in this world — "Sight cannot grasp Him" (6:103), and Moses himself could not bear the direct manifestation (7:143: when he asked to see Allah, he was told to look at the mountain; when it was exposed, he collapsed). The thunderbolt that struck them was both a punishment and a revelation of the abyss between the creature and the Creator: the demand to see the Divine without the divine permission was an act of spiritual presumption. The verse says the thunderbolt struck them "while you were looking on" — they did not even receive the secret; they were destroyed in the very act of demanding.

The classical commentators offer the interpretation that the death was temporary — the thunderbolt struck them and they died, and then they were resurrected (as in the next verse, "Then We brought you back to life after your death"). This is also the meaning of the second half of the verse in 4:153, which says "and the thunderbolt struck them for their wrongdoing" and immediately adds that Allah then "resurrected them" (in the sense of reviving them) so that they would reflect. The narrative is a story of punishment followed by mercy, the pattern of the whole surah.

The lesson is profound about the nature of faith. Faith is not a mathematical certainty that removes all risk of being wrong; it is a trust in the evidence of revelation, in the testimony of the prophets, and the coherence of the signs. The Children of Israel demanded a certainty beyond evidence — and the certainty they demanded, if they had received it, would not have been faith. The moment certainty is forced, the freedom to believe is gone. This is one of the reasons the Qur'an's evidence is sufficient but not coercive: the believer sees, the doubter asks for more. The person who says "I will believe only when I see" has already misunderstood what belief is.

The modern reader knows this pattern well: the skeptic who says he will believe only if God appears before him, only if the miracle happens on camera, only if the scientific proof arrives. The Qur'anic answer is that the request itself is the problem — it is a demand for compulsion, and it treats the Creator as if He were the one on trial before the creature. The correct stance is the one Moses himself taught: "My Lord, show me" was only his request after he had already believed; the Children of Israel's demand was made *before* believing, and it was made with insolence. The difference between the seeker and the arrogant is the willingness to submit to the evidence that has already been provided.

===VERSE-END===
## Verse 56

Verse 56 says: *"Then We brought you back to life after your death, so that perhaps you would be grateful."*

In simple terms, the verse that follows the thunderbolt records the mercy: after the people who demanded to see Allah were struck dead, Allah brought them back to life. The purpose of the resurrection of that generation is stated with the same formula as the previous mercy: so that perhaps you would be grateful.

The sequence is extraordinary and deserves to be read slowly. The Children of Israel made a demand that was an act of presumption; the thunderbolt struck them; and then, instead of leaving them in the punishment, Allah revived them. The pattern of the whole narrative is visible in these two verses: sin → punishment → mercy → the call to gratitude. The Qur'an is not narrating a story of a cruel God who punishes and destroys; it is narrating the story of a Merciful God who disciplines, then restores, in order to teach.

The revival is directly connected to the purpose in the previous verse and this one: the people who had demanded to see Allah with their own eyes were given a vision of something else — they experienced death and return. If they had truly seen the punishment and then been returned, they were the perfect witnesses of the reality of the afterlife, of the resurrection, and of the divine power. In the classical accounts, the event is seen as one of the signs of the resurrection, and it is the reason the Qur'an can use this episode as proof of the final Day: the God who struck these people dead and revived them before their eyes has the power to do the same to the whole of humanity.

The phrase "after your death" (*min ba'di mawtikum*) is unambiguous: they were dead, and then they were brought back. This is one of the Qur'an's clearest statements that resurrection is not merely a future hope but has been shown in the past, in the history of the Children of Israel. The same power is referenced in the story of the man who passed by a ruined city (2:259) and the story of the young men of the Cave, but this event is the first and most direct. The believer reads the verse and finds the pattern: the Creator who gives life and death can give it again — and the purpose of the demonstration is the creation of gratitude in those who witness it.

"Gratitude" here is the spiritual response to an overwhelming favour. The people had not merely been forgiven; they had been struck dead and brought back. A person who has been given his life back has been given the most direct lesson in the value of existence. And the purpose of the lesson is the same as all the favours: the heart that has been revived should be a grateful heart, a heart that does not repeat the demand for signs and does not slide back into certainty through compulsion. The Qur'an is teaching that the reward of witnessing mercy is thanksgiving, and thanksgiving is the beginning of every virtue.

In modern life, this verse speaks to the experience of being brought back: the person who recovers from a life-threatening illness, who survives an accident, who escapes a disaster, who is saved from an addiction, who comes back from a collapse — all of these are moments of "revival," and the Qur'anic purpose for them is the same: gratitude. The natural response to a second chance is not to return to the same life as before but to thank the Giver of the second chance. Every morning, in a real sense, is a revival, and every believer wakes with the opportunity to live the gratitude that the verse calls for.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 57

Verse 57 says: *"And ˹remember when˺ We shaded you with clouds and sent down to you manna and quails, ˹saying˺, "Eat from the good things We have provided for you." The evildoers ˹certainly˺ did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an continues the list of favours with the provision in the wilderness. After the deliverance from Egypt, the Children of Israel wandered in the desert, where no crops grew and no water was available. Allah provided for them: clouds that shaded them from the sun, and food that descended from the sky. The manna and quails are the two famous foods of the wilderness account.

The manna (*al-mann*), according to the classical commentators and the hadith, refers to a sweet substance that descended like dew or snow — whiter than milk, sweeter than honey, and easily gathered. Ibn Abbas expressed this view, as did al-Hasan, Qatadah, and others. It is recorded in a hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim that the Prophet ﷺ said, "The truffle [al-ka'ma] is a kind of manna, and its liquid is a healing for the eye" — which shows that "manna" in the Islamic understanding refers primarily to a sweet, heavenly provision. The quails (*al-salwa*) are birds that the Qur'an describes as a type of bird, most commonly identified with the quail, that arrived in great numbers and were easily caught. Ibn Abbas identified the salwa as a bird resembling the quail; this is the view recorded from Mujahid, al-Sha'bi, al-Dahhak, al-Hasan, Ikrimah, and al-Rabi' ibn Anas. The exact identity of the manna — a gum, a sweet dew, or some other substance — is a matter of scholarly discussion; the point of the verse is not the taxonomy of the food but the divine provision, and the texts show that the provision was abundant, fresh, and without the usual effort of agriculture.

The favour is described in the verse with the command: "Eat from the good things We have provided for you." The phrase *tayyibāt* — "good things" — is important. It does not mean merely edible; it means pleasant, wholesome, lawful, and pure. The Qur'an repeatedly commands the believers to eat from the *tayyibāt* and to give thanks (2:168, 2:172, 5:88, 16:114). The word combines the material and the moral: the provision of Allah is good in its source, its nature, and its effect.

The final sentence is the moral of the whole favour: "The evildoers did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves." The Children of Israel later complained about the manna and asked for different food (as we will see in verses 61); they grumbled, they were ungrateful, and the Qur'an says clearly: they did not harm Allah by their ingratitude — Allah is unaffected, self-sufficient, and their complaining harmed only themselves. The verse contains a profound theological statement: human ingratitude does not diminish the Giver; it damages the taker. The person who complains about provision does not reduce the supply; he reduces his own capacity for joy. The person who rejects the gift does not harm the Giver; he harms himself.

The modern application is direct: gratitude for provision is a matter of soul-health, not merely a matter of obligation. The person who habitually complains about his food, his income, his housing, his circumstances — even when provision is adequate — is poisoning himself, not Allah. The person who receives provision with gratitude finds contentment, and the person who receives provision with complaint finds only dissatisfaction. The verse calls the believer to feed on the *tayyibāt* — the good things — with a grateful heart, and to know that the cure for dissatisfaction is not more provision but more thankfulness.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 58

Verse 58 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when We said, "Enter this city and eat freely from wherever you please; enter the gate with humility, saying, 'Absolve us.' We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers.""*

In simple terms, the next favour is the conquest of the city — Jerusalem — which entered the Israelite story after the wandering. Allah told the Children of Israel: enter this city, eat freely from it, but enter the gate with humility and say, "Absolve us." And the promise was two-fold: forgiveness of sins and multiplied reward for the good-doers. The favour was the entry into the land; the condition was humility.

The city is Jerusalem (*Iliya'* or *Bayt al-Maqdis*), as the classical commentators — Ibn Abbas, Mujahid, al-Suddi, Qatadah, and al-Dahhak — consistently identify it. The Children of Israel had been wandering for decades, and the promised entry into the land was the culmination of the deliverance from Egypt. The verse describes the conditions that were given to them at the moment of entry: enter the gate of the city while bowing in humility — the word *sujjadan* means prostrating or bowing down — and say the word *hittah*, which means "remission, forgiveness — absolve us, remove our sins."

The instruction combines three elements: the physical posture (bowing), the verbal formula (*hittah*), and the promise (forgiveness and multiplied reward). The classical commentators explain that the prostration was an act of humility and gratitude for the victory, a physical acknowledgment that the entry into the land was a divine gift, not a human achievement. The word *hittah* was a specific prayer of remission — "absolve us" — a request for the removal of sins. Together, the body and the tongue were to perform the same act: submission.

The promise is precise: forgiveness of sins for all who entered rightly, and multiplied reward for those who added good deeds. The verse shows the Qur'an's explanation of the distinction between the general acceptance and the special favour: everyone who follows the command is forgiven; those who go beyond the minimum and do good are rewarded more. The same pattern runs through the whole of the Qur'an: salvation through submission, excellence through deeds.

The modern application of this verse is a lesson about the posture of receiving. The Children of Israel were being given the land they had been promised; the condition of receiving it was humility and the prayer "absolve us." When a person receives a great blessing — a promotion, a home, a marriage, a recovery of health, an achievement — the Qur'anic pattern is to enter it with humility, to acknowledge the Giver, and to ask for the removal of one's own sins. The person who enters blessing with arrogance turns the blessing into a curse; the person who enters it with humility and the prayer "absolve us" turns both the blessing and the self into something pure. The verse is thus one of the most practical instructions in the whole Qur'an: the first act upon receiving a favour is not self-congratulation but praise and humility.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 59

Verse 59 says: *"But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say. So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness."*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now records the failure that followed the command to enter the city. The wrongdoers among them changed the words that they had been commanded to say. In response, Allah sent a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness. The verse is the Qur'an's account of the moment the entry into the land was spoiled.

The account is recorded in the Sunnah with remarkable detail. The Prophet ﷺ explained that the Children of Israel were commanded to enter the gate bowing and to say *hittah* — "absolve us" — but they deliberately distorted it, saying *hintah* — "a grain" — and entered dragging themselves on their rear ends. Ibn Abbas is also reported to have said that they went through the door backwards, and Ibn Mas'ud that they entered with their heads raised in defiance. The variants agree on the essential: the people mocked the divine command by changing the word and the posture. The commanded *hittah* (remission) became *hintah* (a grain of wheat) — a change that looks like a phonetic corruption but was in fact a deliberate mockery: they were saying, "a grain in a barley — a seed in a hair" instead of asking for the removal of their sins.

The change of words was perfectly calculated. The word *hittah* was a request for the removal of sins and a posture of humility; the distortion *hintah* was a request for a worldly thing, a grain of wheat — the very symbol of the material life. The act was rebellion not merely against a word but against the whole meaning of worship: they preferred the symbol of the world to the removal of the sins. The physical act of entering on their rear ends, dragging, with heads raised, completed the mockery: they inverted the posture of humility into a posture of contempt.

The punishment is described as *rijzan* — a punishment — sent down "from the heavens upon them." The classical commentaries describe a plague or a torment that was sent upon the wrongdoers among them; the Qur'an elsewhere says that the punishment killed those who had changed the words (7:162). The details of the plague are not the point; the point is that divine commands are not suggestions. The Qur'an's history of the Children of Israel is full of this pattern, and the primary lesson is the severity of treating the command of Allah as a joke.

The contemporary application is powerful in the age of distortion. The "wrongdoers changed the words" — *baddala alladhīna zalamū qawlan ghayra alladhī qīla lahum* — is the Qur'an's phrase for religious corruption. It applies to every person who takes God's message and changes it for his own purposes: the translator who alters the meaning, the preacher who quotes out of context, the religious leader who trades the command for convenience, the person who replaces the prayer of repentance with the symbol of the material. The verse says that this is not a minor act — it is the act of the *zālimīn*, the wrongdoers, and its consequence is the punishment of Allah. The believer who reads this verse is being taught the sanctity of revelation: the words of Allah are not to be twisted, mocked, or traded. They are to be received with humility, and the first act of the receiver is the prayer of *hittah*: "Absolve us."

## Verse 60

Verse 60 says: *"And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, "Strike the rock with your staff." Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place. ˹We then said,˺ "Eat and drink of Allah's provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.""*

In simple terms, the Qur'an now records another favour of the wilderness: the water from the rock. Moses' people were in the desert, thirsting, and Moses prayed to his Lord. Allah said: strike the rock with your staff. And from the rock twelve springs gushed out, each tribe knowing its own drinking place. The command accompanying the provision was clear: eat and drink from Allah's provisions, and do not spread corruption in the land.

The account is one of the most famous miracles of Moses. The event is described in the Qur'an in several places (2:60, 7:160, 26:63, 20:77), and the biblical parallel is in the Pentateuch. The details are consistent: Moses was commanded to strike the rock; the rock produced water; and the twelve springs correspond to the twelve tribes of Israel, with each tribe knowing its own spring. The provision was organized: not one spring for all, but twelve distinct springs — a provision that was both miraculous and orderly, meeting the needs of each tribe without conflict or crowding.

"Each tribe knew its drinking place" is a detail of mercy and justice. In the desert, water is the most precious resource, and the most common cause of conflict. The divine provision not only supplied the water but pre-assigned it: each tribe knew its own place, and no tribe had to fight another for it. The arrangement was a lesson in the manner of divine provision: Allah provides enough, and He also provides the order that prevents conflict over what is provided. The twelve springs were also a sign of the unity in diversity of the community: twelve tribes, one water, one Lord.

The command that accompanies the provision — "Eat and drink of Allah's provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land" — is the moral of the miracle. The one who has received the provision must not use it to spread corruption. The phrase *wa lā ta'thaw fī al-ard mufsidīn* — "do not spread corruption in the land" — is the same phrase used for the hypocrites and the rebellious in earlier verses (2:11, 2:27) and appears again in the story of the manna (2:61). The instruction is a reminder that provisions from Allah are not licences for abuse: the person who has been given water, food, or land is given a trust, not a right to exploit. The verse thus joins the pattern of the whole section: the favours of Allah are accompanied by the commands of Allah, and the correct response to the favour is the execution of the command.

The modern reader of this verse is given a double lesson. First, the atmosphere of scarcity is not the final reality: Allah can provide from the rock, from the desert, from what seems impossible. The believer who is facing scarcity of water, food, shelter, or opportunity is not facing a closed universe; he is facing a test that the Lord of the rock can resolve. Second, the believer who has been provided with abundance must use it without corruption: the water of the world, the wealth, the land, the resources are provisions to be used in obedience, not instruments of corruption, exploitation, and conflict. The verse calls for provision with gratitude, and gratitude with responsibility.
