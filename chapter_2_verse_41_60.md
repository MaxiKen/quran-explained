## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:41

> Believe in My revelations which confirm your Scriptures. Do not be the first to deny them or trade them for a fleeting gain. And be mindful of Me.

**Commentary:**

### The Qur’an and the Earlier Scriptures

Allah calls the Children of Israel to believe in the Qur’an because it confirms the revelation they were entrusted with. The Qur’an does not present itself as a religion unrelated to Moses, Abraham, and the earlier prophets. It confirms the original divine source of the Torah and the other revelations, affirms their essential call to worship Allah, and corrects the alterations, concealments, and interpretations that had entered human religious practice.

“Confirmation” does not mean that the Qur’an approves every sentence or doctrine attributed to earlier scripture without examination. Allah describes the Qur’an as *muhaymin*—a guardian, witness, and criterion over previous scripture (5:48). It confirms what remains true and exposes what has been changed or misrepresented. The Muslim position is therefore neither to reject the earlier prophets nor to place later textual claims beyond the judgment of the final revelation.

The command to believe in “My revelations” also includes belief in the Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ), who brought and explained them. A person cannot claim to honour divine scripture while rejecting the messenger whom Allah sent to complete its guidance. The Qur’an says that those who were given earlier scripture recognize the Prophet as they recognize their own children, yet some conceal the truth knowingly (2:146). Recognition creates responsibility; it is not enough to possess information about a messenger if pride prevents submission to him.

### Do Not Be the First to Reject

“Do not be the first to deny” has been understood in more than one related way. It may mean: do not be the first of the People of the Book to reject the Qur’an when others look to you for religious judgment. It may also mean: do not lead the recipients of earlier scripture in rejecting a revelation that confirms the truth you already possess. In either case, the warning concerns the added responsibility of influence. A person who rejects privately harms himself; a teacher, scholar, or leader who rejects publicly may encourage many others to reject with him.

Leadership in denial is not a distinction to be desired. The first person to popularize a falsehood often carries the burden of the people who follow it, just as the one who establishes a good practice receives a share of the reward of those who follow it (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1017). Religious authority makes honesty more important, not less. The fear of losing status must never become a reason to conceal what one knows to be true.

### Do Not Sell Revelation Cheaply

The command not to trade Allah’s revelations for a fleeting gain exposes the economics of religious corruption. The “price” may be money, office, patronage, social approval, institutional survival, or the comfort of not offending a powerful audience. The gain appears substantial to the person who wants it immediately, but it is *thaman qalīl*—a small price—when compared with the truth surrendered and the eternal accountability that follows.

This does not forbid scholars from earning a lawful livelihood, nor does it mean that every paid teacher has sold religion. The issue is whether a person changes, conceals, or selectively presents revelation in exchange for advantage. Allah condemns those who write claims with their own hands and then say, “This is from Allah,” seeking a small gain (2:79). Honest teaching and lawful compensation are different from manipulating scripture for profit.

### Fear Allah Rather Than People

The verse ends with the command to be mindful of Allah. Taqwā is the protection that allows a person to remain truthful when truth is costly. It places the approval of Allah above the approval of a community, institution, family, or ruler. It also reminds the bearer of scripture that revelation is a trust: it must be transmitted faithfully, understood responsibly, and obeyed even when it challenges inherited habits.

The warning is not limited to the Children of Israel. Muslims who inherit the Qur’an can repeat the same failure by hiding uncomfortable commands, bending religious language to serve political power, or treating revelation as a tool for winning arguments. Every community should ask whether it is following the Book or merely using the Book to defend what it had already decided.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:89 and 2:146 on the earlier communities’ recognition of the confirming revelation and their concealment of truth.
- Qur’an 3:3–4 and 5:44–48 on the Qur’an confirming earlier scripture and acting as a criterion over it.
- Qur’an 2:79 and 3:187 on concealing or altering scripture for worldly gain.
- Qur’an 16:95 on not exchanging Allah’s covenant for a small price.
- Qur’an 49:13 on honour being based on taqwā rather than lineage or communal claim.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 1017, on the reward or burden attached to initiating a practice that others follow.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:42

> Do not mix truth with falsehood or hide the truth knowingly.

**Commentary:**

### Truth Dressed in Falsehood

Allah forbids two related forms of dishonesty: mixing truth with falsehood and knowingly hiding the truth. The Arabic expression *lā talbisū al-ḥaqqa bil-bāṭil* evokes clothing: falsehood is placed over truth like a garment, making the genuine difficult to recognize. This can happen through a half-truth, a selective quotation, an accurate fact placed in a misleading context, or a true religious statement used to support an unrelated false conclusion.

This is often more dangerous than an obvious lie. A plainly false claim may be rejected, but a distortion that contains enough truth to appear credible can mislead thoughtful people. A person may quote a verse while concealing the verses that qualify it, cite a historical event while removing its context, or use a correct legal principle to justify an unjust application. The Qur’an condemns the method, not merely the final sentence: truth must not be used as camouflage for falsehood.

The command is addressed to people who possess scripture and knowledge, but its lesson is universal. Journalists, teachers, religious speakers, political leaders, researchers, and ordinary users of social media all carry the responsibility not to frame information deceptively. Accuracy is not achieved by avoiding literal lies alone. It requires fair context, honest uncertainty, and the courage to include evidence that weakens one’s preferred argument.

### Concealing What One Knows

The second prohibition is *wa-lā taktumū al-ḥaqqa wa-antum taʿlamūn*—do not hide the truth while you know it. Ignorance can explain a mistake, but knowledge turns deliberate concealment into betrayal. The Qur’an later describes a covenant with the People of the Book to make revelation clear and not conceal it (3:187). Knowledge is a trust; its possessor is answerable for what he suppresses as well as for what he says.

Concealment can take different forms. A person may omit a command that threatens his status, hide evidence from those making an important decision, refuse to answer a genuine question in order to preserve control, or keep a person ignorant of a right owed to him. Silence is not always sinful—confidentiality, discretion, and avoiding harmful speech are legitimate—but silence becomes blameworthy when it deliberately allows falsehood or injustice to prevail while the knower could clarify the matter responsibly.

The Prophet (ﷺ) warned that whoever is asked about knowledge and conceals it will be bridled with a bridle of fire on the Day of Resurrection (Sunan Abī Dāwūd, 3658; Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2649). This warning concerns beneficial knowledge that a person is qualified and obligated to convey, not every question on every subject. A responsible scholar may say “I do not know,” qualify an answer, or refer a matter to someone more qualified. That is honesty, not concealment.

### The Obligation of Intellectual Integrity

The verse asks the reader to hold truth and communication together. It is not enough to possess the right belief inwardly while presenting a distorted version of it publicly. Nor is it enough to speak accurately while intending to confuse. The Qur’an commands believers to be upright in speech (33:70), verify reports before acting on them (49:6), and avoid following what they do not know (17:36).

This integrity is especially important in religious interpretation. A commentator should distinguish between the clear meaning of an āyah, a probable inference, a classical disagreement, and a personal reflection. Presenting speculation as revelation is a form of mixing truth with falsehood. The believer honours the text by being precise about the limits of his claim.

The verse also protects people from sectarian manipulation. Communities can be taught to fear one another through selected evidence, caricatures, and suppressed facts. A person who wants reconciliation must tell the truth about wrongdoing even when it implicates his own group. Justice cannot be built upon a curated memory that celebrates one’s own virtues and erases one’s own crimes.

### Truth as a Moral Trust

Truth is not merely an abstract proposition; it has consequences for people’s worship, rights, safety, and dignity. Concealing a clear command can deprive someone of guidance. Mixing truth with falsehood can make injustice appear religiously legitimate. The Qur’an’s command therefore joins theology with public ethics. The faithful person refuses both the lie that is easy to detect and the sophisticated distortion that hides behind correct vocabulary.

The cure is taqwā: remembering that Allah hears every statement and knows every motive. A person who fears Allah does not need to win every debate or preserve every reputation. He can acknowledge what he does not know, correct what he misstated, and disclose truth with wisdom. Such honesty is a form of worship and a protection for the community.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 3:71 and 3:187 on mixing truth with falsehood and concealing revealed knowledge.
- Qur’an 2:159, 16:43, and 17:36 on clarifying guidance, asking those who know, and not following unsupported claims.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 4:135 on truth and justice even when they oppose one’s own interests.
- Qur’an 33:70 and 49:6 on upright speech and verifying reports.
- Sunan Abī Dāwūd, 3658, and Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2649, on concealing beneficial knowledge.
- For the imagery of clothing truth in falsehood, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:42.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:43

> Establish prayer, pay alms-tax, and bow down with those who bow down.

**Commentary:**

### Worship Must Be Established

The Children of Israel are commanded to establish prayer and pay the alms-tax, then to bow with those who bow. The Arabic wording is not simply “perform prayer” but *aqīmū al-ṣalāh*: establish it, maintain it, and give it its proper standing in personal and communal life. Prayer has appointed times, conditions, movements, recitations, and inward aims. It must be protected from being reduced to an occasional expression of emotion or a symbol of religious identity.

The command also shows that prayer is not a private invention. It is an instituted act of worship shared across the prophetic tradition, even though its details and forms may differ between laws. Moses and his people were instructed to establish prayer and give alms (5:12), and Jesus was commanded to pray and give alms as long as he lived (19:31). The continuity confirms that human beings have always needed a structured return to Allah.

Prayer disciplines time and attention. It interrupts the illusion that work, wealth, family, or political struggle is the centre of existence. The worshipper stands before Allah at regular intervals, recites revelation, bows, and prostrates. It is through this repeated surrender that the heart is trained to remember who is Lord. The Qur’an says, “Establish prayer for My remembrance” (20:14), and states that prayer restrains shameful and unjust deeds when it is established with understanding and sincerity (29:45).

### Zakāh and the Social Meaning of Worship

The second command is to pay *zakāh*. Zakāh is not merely voluntary generosity or a fee paid to make wealth religiously respectable. It is a divinely defined obligation upon qualifying wealth, directed to eligible recipients and intended to purify both wealth and the giver. The word is associated with purification and growth: giving a due portion cleanses the heart from possessiveness and causes blessing to grow through obedience.

The pairing of ṣalāh and zakāh appears repeatedly in the Qur’an. Prayer establishes the vertical relationship with Allah; zakāh demonstrates that this relationship changes the way a person handles resources. A worshipper cannot claim deep devotion while treating the needs of the poor, indebted, stranded, and vulnerable as irrelevant. The Qur’an identifies a recognized right for the beggar and deprived in the wealth of the righteous (70:24–25), and warns those who pray while refusing ordinary assistance (107:4–7).

Zakāh also protects society from the concentration of wealth as a measure of human worth. It does not abolish private property or lawful earning, but it makes wealth answerable to social obligation. The owner is reminded that provision is a trust, and the recipient is protected from being treated as a favour-seeker. Voluntary charity extends this mercy beyond the formal obligation, but the presence of an obligation ensures that generosity is not left entirely to mood.

### “Bow with Those Who Bow”

The final command is *irkaʿū maʿa al-rākiʿīn*: bow with those who bow. Bowing is one of the defining bodily acts of ṣalāh, but the phrase also emphasizes communal worship. The believer does not stand before Allah as a self-appointed spiritual authority. He joins a row in which social rank, wealth, ethnicity, and occupation do not determine who stands near whom.

The communal form of prayer cultivates humility and solidarity. The person who is powerful outside the mosque bows beside the person who has little worldly influence. The rows teach equality before Allah while preserving the rights and responsibilities of each individual. The Prophet (ﷺ) warned against neglecting the congregational spirit of worship and taught that prayer in congregation is superior to prayer performed alone by many degrees (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

“Bow with” also suggests that faith should be joined to a community of obedience. Religious life can be weakened by isolation, where a person answers only to his own preferences. The community is not infallible, and congregations must correct injustice rather than sanctify it, but sincere companionship helps a believer remember, learn, and persevere. A person who worships alone in principle may become increasingly confident in his own excuses.

### An Integrated Religious Life

The three commands form a coherent programme. Prayer purifies attention and establishes remembrance; zakāh purifies wealth and social relations; bowing with the worshippers purifies pride and restores communal belonging. Faith is thus not reduced to private belief, economic redistribution, or public ceremony alone. It joins the heart, the body, the wealth, and the community under Allah’s guidance.

The command is addressed to a people who possessed scripture, showing that inherited revelation is not enough. A community must keep worship alive in practice. Muslims likewise recite this āyah and are responsible for the same principles: guarding prayer, paying zakāh, and joining the believers in humility rather than using religion to elevate the ego.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:83, 5:12, and 19:31 on prayer and alms in the prophetic tradition.
- Qur’an 20:14 and 29:45 on prayer as remembrance and a restraint from wrongdoing.
- Qur’an 9:5 and 9:71 on prayer and zakāh as marks of the believing community.
- Qur’an 70:24–25 and 107:4–7 on wealth, the rights of the needy, and the danger of prayer without social responsibility.
- Qur’an 22:77 and 48:29 on bowing, prostration, and communal worship.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the excellence of congregational prayer and the five pillars of Islam.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:44

> Do you preach righteousness and fail to practice it yourselves, although you read the Scripture? Do you not understand?

**Commentary:**

### Knowledge Must Be Lived

This āyah exposes the contradiction of teaching righteousness while deliberately refusing to practise it. The question is not a prohibition against advising others until one becomes perfect; if that were the rule, no one could command good or forbid wrong. The criticism is aimed at people who make religious instruction a public duty for others while treating themselves as exempt, especially when they know the scripture and use their authority to direct people but do not submit to its demands.

The Arabic question—*ataʾmurūna al-nāsa bil-birri wa-tansawna anfusakum*—places the two actions side by side: commanding goodness and forgetting oneself. The problem is not that they encourage righteousness; it is that they forget that they are among those addressed by the command. A teacher may sincerely teach a duty while struggling to fulfil it, but he must not make the struggle an excuse to stop repenting or to pretend that his own conduct does not matter.

The Qur’an makes the danger sharper in Sūrah al-Ṣaff: “O believers, why do you say what you do not do? It is most hateful in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do” (61:2–3). The wording condemns a deliberate gap between speech and action. It does not demand flawless performance before speech; it demands sincerity, effort, and repentance when one falls short.

### Scripture Increases Responsibility

The phrase “although you read the Scripture” means that their failure cannot be blamed on ignorance. Revelation had made the standard clear. Knowledge is a blessing, but it also creates accountability. The person who knows that justice, honesty, prayer, charity, and kindness are obligatory cannot treat that knowledge as decoration. The Qur’an warns that the most severe regret is to have possessed guidance and then refused to follow it.

This principle applies to religious teachers and to every believer. Parents who teach honesty must practise it with their children. Leaders who speak about justice must apply it to their allies and opponents. A person who advises patience must examine his own anger; one who speaks about charity must examine his relationship with wealth. The more publicly a person represents a value, the more important it is that he pursue it privately.

Yet the verse should not be turned into a weapon that silences moral counsel. A sinner may still be obligated to tell the truth about a sin, and a person struggling with prayer may still be required to encourage prayer. The correct response to inconsistency is to repent and improve, not to declare the command invalid. Scholars often distinguish between two wrongs: abandoning a good deed oneself and discouraging others from doing it. Both require correction, but the first does not make the second a virtue.

### “Do You Not Understand?”

The closing question points to a failure of practical reason. Understanding in the Qur’an is not simply memorizing words; it is grasping their consequence. If a person knows that revelation is from Allah, he should realize that its commands apply to him before they become material for judging others. Knowledge that never reaches conduct has been blocked by pride, appetite, or love of reputation.

This is why the Prophet (ﷺ) warned of a person who will be brought on the Day of Resurrection and whose intestines will spill out as he turns around them in the Fire. The people will ask whether he did not command good and forbid evil, and he will say that he commanded it while failing to do it and forbade evil while committing it (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The image is a warning against becoming a public moral authority whose private life refutes his message.

The cure is not silence but congruence. The teacher says, “This is what Allah commands, and I am in need of His help to obey it.” The believer gives counsel while remembering that the same counsel is addressed to him. He does not use another person’s failure to hide his own, and he does not use his own struggle as an excuse to surrender. In this way, knowledge becomes understanding and understanding becomes action.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 61:2–3 on saying what one does not do.
- Qur’an 66:6 and 20:132 on beginning responsibility with oneself and one’s household.
- Qur’an 5:44 and 35:28 on scripture, knowledge, and accountability.
- Qur’an 16:125 and 103:1–3 on teaching with wisdom and combining truth with righteous action.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the person who commanded good but did not practise it.
- For the distinction between advising others and excusing one’s own disobedience, see Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr and al-Nawawī’s commentary on the relevant ḥadīth.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:45

> And seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, it is a burden except for the humble—

**Commentary:**

### Two Means of Endurance

Allah instructs the Children of Israel—and, through them, all believers—to seek help through patience and prayer. The two are paired because patience gives a person the strength to remain obedient through difficulty, while prayer continually reconnects him to the One who provides that strength. *Ṣabr* is not passive resignation. It includes restraining the soul from disobedience, persevering in worship, and remaining steady when hardship arrives.

The Qur’an describes patience in three related areas: patience in obeying Allah, patience in avoiding sin, and patience in enduring painful decree. A person may need patience to establish prayer, patience to resist unlawful gain, patience to forgive, and patience to continue acting justly when injustice appears to prosper. Without patience, faith becomes dependent upon comfort. Without prayer, patience can become mere self-reliance or suppression rather than reliance upon Allah.

Prayer is a source of help because it restores the correct perspective. The worshipper brings fear, grief, anger, and confusion before Allah, then stands, bows, and prostrates as a servant. The world may not change immediately, but the heart is reminded that it is not alone, and that no hardship lies outside Allah’s knowledge. The Prophet (ﷺ) would turn to prayer when a matter troubled him, demonstrating that prayer is not an escape from responsibility but the foundation from which responsible action begins.

### Why It Feels Heavy

The verse says that this path is heavy—*kabīrah*—except for the humble. Prayer can feel heavy when the ego dislikes interruption, when desire resists discipline, or when a person has not yet learned to value nearness to Allah. Patience can feel heavy because it requires delaying an immediate response and accepting that a good outcome may not appear at once. The burden is not in Allah’s guidance being unjust; it is in the human self resisting what would purify it.

The next verse identifies the humble as those who are certain they will meet their Lord and return to Him. Certainty in the Hereafter changes the weight of present obedience. A short act of restraint becomes meaningful when it is connected to an eternal outcome. A difficult prayer becomes an appointment with the Lord who will judge every effort. A wrong that is endured without retaliation is not wasted because the final Judge sees it fully.

Humility—*khushūʿ*—is not humiliation before people. It is the heart’s quiet awareness of Allah’s greatness and one’s own need. It appears in the body through stillness and reverence, but it begins with knowledge. The humble person does not assume that his own preference is the measure of right and wrong. He listens, obeys, asks forgiveness, and accepts correction.

### Patience Does Not Mean Accepting Injustice

Seeking help through patience must not be used to silence victims or preserve oppression. The Qur’an commands patience, but it also commands justice, defence of the oppressed, and speaking truth. Patience can mean refusing to respond to abuse with forbidden abuse while taking lawful steps to stop the harm. It can mean enduring the cost of justice rather than accepting the comfort of complicity.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that the strong person is not the one who overpowers others, but the one who controls himself when angry (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). This is a form of patience. It does not require abandoning justified action; it requires that action remain governed by principle rather than rage. Prayer helps the believer maintain that distinction.

### Turning to Allah Before Acting

The order of the verse is practical. When facing a task, temptation, conflict, grief, or responsibility, the believer does not rely solely on planning or emotion. He turns to Allah in prayer and prepares himself to persevere. The Qur’an says, “O believers, seek help through patience and prayer; surely Allah is with those who are patient” (2:153). Allah’s companionship here is a special companionship of support, care, and reward—not a claim that He is physically confined to a place.

The verse is also a correction to the illusion of self-sufficiency. Human beings need food, rest, medicine, advice, and material means, but they also need spiritual strength. Prayer and patience do not replace practical effort; they purify its intention and sustain it when results are delayed. The humble person works, seeks lawful means, and entrusts the outcome to Allah.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:153 and 3:200 on patience, prayer, steadfastness, and Allah’s companionship.
- Qur’an 29:45 on prayer restraining shameful and unjust deeds.
- Qur’an 70:19–35 on human weakness and the qualities that protect the worshipper.
- Qur’an 41:30 and 46:13 on remaining steadfast after affirming Allah as Lord.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on controlling anger as a sign of true strength.
- For *khushūʿ* and the heaviness of worship upon the insincere, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Saʿdī on 2:45.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:46

> those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and to Him they will return.

**Commentary:**

### Humility Grows from Certainty

The previous verse said that patience and prayer are heavy except for the humble. This verse explains who the humble are: those who are certain that they will meet their Lord and return to Him. The Arabic *yaẓunnūna* can sometimes mean “they think” or “suppose,” but in this context it carries the meaning of firm conviction. Their certainty is not a vague possibility. It is confidence grounded in Allah’s revelation and the signs of creation.

The phrase “meet their Lord” does not mean that Allah is absent from them in this life or that He is confined to a physical location. It describes the future encounter with Allah in resurrection, judgment, and the final disclosure of deeds. Every person will stand before the One who created him, and the veil between worldly life and the Hereafter will be removed. For believers, the meeting includes Allah’s mercy and the reward of seeing Him in the Hereafter; for those who rejected, it includes exposure and judgment.

### Return Gives Life Moral Direction

“To Him they will return” establishes the final direction of human existence. People may travel through the world, build institutions, form relationships, and pursue ambitions, but the journey ends with Allah. The knowledge of return prevents the present world from becoming the sole measure of success. If the only standard is immediate comfort, patience seems foolish and prayer seems burdensome. If the final return is certain, every act can be evaluated by its lasting consequence.

Certainty in the return also explains why the humble can endure injustice without becoming unjust themselves. They know that no hidden injury is lost and no oppressor escapes the final account. They can seek lawful justice without allowing revenge to govern the heart. They can forgive when forgiveness is wise because they do not believe that the wrong has disappeared; it has been transferred to the judgment of Allah.

The same certainty makes prayer more than a routine. The worshipper is not reciting words into emptiness. He is preparing to meet the Lord to whom he bows. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that when a person prays, he is privately conversing with his Lord (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in the discussion of recitation during prayer). The anticipation of the final meeting gives seriousness to the present meeting of prayer.

### The Hereafter and the Cure for Hypocrisy

Hypocrisy flourishes when public appearance is treated as the final reality. Certainty in the Hereafter breaks that illusion. The hypocrite works for what people see; the sincere believer works for the day when motives, hidden deeds, and neglected obligations will be exposed. The Qur’an says, “The Day the secrets will be tested” (86:9), and “that Day people will come forth in separate groups to be shown their deeds” (99:6).

This certainty is not meant to remove joy from the world. The Qur’an permits lawful enjoyment and asks people to seek the Hereafter without forgetting their share of worldly life (28:77). It teaches proportion. Food, family, work, beauty, and achievement can be blessings when they are received gratefully and used within Allah’s limits. They become distractions when they cause a person to forget the return.

### A Living Awareness

Certainty is strengthened through reflection upon creation, recitation of revelation, remembrance, and honest observation of death’s inevitability. A person does not need to see the Hereafter physically in order to live according to it. He trusts the One who informed him, just as he trusts many realities that cannot be seen directly. This trust produces calm, but not carelessness; humility, but not weakness; patience, but not passivity.

The two verses together define the spiritual posture of the believer. When life becomes difficult, he seeks help through patience and prayer. He is able to do so because he knows that the present hardship is temporary and that the final return is certain. The Hereafter is not an escape from moral responsibility; it is what makes responsibility meaningful.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:28, 23:115–116, and 29:57 on death, resurrection, and return to Allah.
- Qur’an 75:22–25 and 86:9 on the meeting, vision, and disclosure of hidden realities in the Hereafter.
- Qur’an 28:77 on seeking the Hereafter without neglecting lawful worldly life.
- Qur’an 99:6–8 on every person being shown the smallest good and evil he has done.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on prayer as intimate conversation with Allah.
- For the meaning of *yaẓunnūna* as certainty in this context, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:46.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:47

> O Children of Israel! Remember ˹all˺ the favours I granted you and how I honoured you above the others.

**Commentary:**

### Favour Is a Responsibility

Allah again calls the Children of Israel to remember His favours. Repetition is purposeful. Human beings forget blessings quickly, especially when a favour becomes familiar. The command recalls a people whose history was marked by rescue from oppression, revelation, prophets, provision, and opportunities to lead humanity toward worship of Allah. Their honour was not an ornament without responsibility; it was a trust that required gratitude and obedience.

The expression “I honoured you above the others” refers to the favour granted to the Children of Israel in the period of their prophetic history. Allah chose many prophets from among them, delivered them from Pharaoh, gave them scripture, and made them a community through which His guidance was known. The Qur’an uses similar language elsewhere: “I favoured you over the worlds” (44:32; 45:16). The immediate context indicates a historical and religious distinction, not an unconditional racial superiority over every human being for all time.

### Chosen for Service, Not Privilege

The Qur’an never presents divine selection as a licence for arrogance. Allah chooses people and communities for a mission, and the honour remains linked to fulfilling that mission. Abraham was made an exemplar after he completed his tests, but Allah declared that His covenant does not include the wrongdoers (2:124). The Children of Israel were given revelation, but they were also held accountable for concealing, distorting, and rejecting it. Privilege without obedience becomes a proof against the privileged person.

This principle applies to Muslims as well. The Qur’an calls the Muslim community a middle or just community made a witness over humanity (2:143), but it also warns that honour is lost through disobedience, injustice, and hypocrisy. Being born into a Muslim family or belonging to a religious community does not substitute for faith and righteous action. The proper response to a divine favour is humility and service, not claims of automatic superiority.

### Remembering History Correctly

To remember the favour is to remember the Giver, not merely to celebrate the recipient. A community that remembers only that it was chosen may turn history into a doctrine of entitlement. A community that remembers that it was rescued, taught, forgiven, and entrusted becomes grateful. The Qur’an’s historical retelling therefore seeks moral memory: Pharaoh’s oppression should produce opposition to tyranny, the gift of scripture should produce fidelity to truth, and repeated forgiveness should produce repentance rather than repeated defiance.

Historical memory must also remain truthful. It should not erase the suffering of others or become an excuse for present injustice. Allah’s favour upon one community does not authorize that community to wrong another. The Qur’an commands justice even toward those one dislikes (5:8), and it rejects the idea that ancestry guarantees nearness to Allah. “The most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most mindful of Him” (49:13).

### The Personal Application

Every believer has reasons to remember divine favour: existence, health, family, guidance, opportunities to repent, protection from sins, and the ability to do good. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that whoever awakens secure in his community, healthy in body, and possessing his day’s food has been given the world’s essential blessings (Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2346; Sunan Ibn Mājah, 4141). Remembering these gifts should produce gratitude, not comparison with those who possess more.

The verse asks the reader to consider whether he uses favour as Allah intends. Knowledge should produce teaching and humility; wealth should produce generosity; authority should produce justice; safety should produce worship; and historical honour should produce service. A blessing becomes dangerous when it is treated as evidence that its recipient can do no wrong.

- Qur’an 2:40, 2:122, 2:124, and 5:12 on the Children of Israel’s favours, covenant, and responsibility.
- Qur’an 3:110 and 2:143 on communal honour being tied to moral witness and enjoining what is right.
- Qur’an 44:32 and 45:16 on the historical favour granted to the Children of Israel.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 49:13 on justice and taqwā overriding tribal or communal claims.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī, 2346, and Sunan Ibn Mājah, 4141, on security, health, and daily provision as major blessings.
- For the conditional meaning of honour and selection, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:47.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:48

> Guard yourselves against the Day on which no soul will be of help to another. No intercession will be accepted, no ransom taken, and no help will be given.

**Commentary:**

### The Day When Social Protection Ends

After reminding the Children of Israel of their honour and favour, Allah warns them against the Day of Judgment. Historical privilege will not protect a person then. No soul will carry the burden of another, no family name will purchase safety, and no political or religious affiliation will substitute for personal faith and obedience. The warning exposes the false security that comes from belonging to a respected community while neglecting the covenant that gave the community its honour.

The Qur’an repeatedly states that each person will meet the consequences of his own deeds: “Every soul is held in pledge for what it has earned” (74:38), and “No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” (6:164). This does not deny that people can influence one another or share in the reward or sin of what they initiate. It means that no one can transfer his personal accountability to a parent, prophet, scholar, ruler, or group.

The Day described here is not merely a larger version of worldly courts. In this life, a person may escape through wealth, connections, legal skill, intimidation, or public sympathy. In the Hereafter, every hidden deed is known and the Judge needs no witness who can be bribed. The Qur’an says that the Day will be one in which no soul can help another and the command belongs entirely to Allah (82:19).

### Intercession Is Not Independent Power

The verse says that no intercession will be accepted, while other Qur’anic verses affirm that intercession exists by Allah’s permission. There is no contradiction. The intercession rejected here is the pagan idea that idols, saints, angels, ancestors, or religious figures possess an independent power to force Allah to forgive whoever they choose. Allah says, “Who is it that can intercede with Him except by His permission?” (2:255), and “On that Day no intercession will benefit except from one to whom the Most Compassionate has given permission and whose word He accepts” (20:109).

The Prophet (ﷺ) will be granted the great intercession, but it will occur only after Allah permits it and honours the one who intercedes. Intercession is therefore a manifestation of Allah’s mercy, not a rival authority alongside Him. It cannot be purchased through charms, offerings, lineage, or the mere claim that a person belongs to a favoured group. The Qur’an says that even the angels’ intercession cannot benefit unless Allah permits it for whom He wills and is pleased (53:26).

This doctrine protects pure worship. If people believe that a created being can hear every unseen plea, remove sins independently, or guarantee salvation regardless of repentance, they have given that being a right that belongs to Allah. The believer may ask Allah to grant intercession and may honour His prophets and righteous servants, but worship, ultimate reliance, and fear of final judgment belong to Allah alone.

### No Ransom and No External Rescue

No ransom will be taken. The word evokes a payment that releases a prisoner or compensates for a crime. On the Day of Judgment, wealth cannot buy forgiveness, and a person cannot offer another life, possession, or worldly service in exchange for his own salvation. The Qur’an says that even if the wrongdoer possessed everything on earth and more, he would offer it to ransom himself, but it would not be accepted (39:47; 70:11–14).

“No help will be given” does not mean that believers will lack Allah’s mercy or that righteous deeds have no value. It means that no independent helper can rescue a person against Allah’s judgment. Salvation comes through Allah’s grace, faith, repentance, and righteous action—not through a worldly substitute that allows a person to continue rejecting Allah while expecting someone else to absorb the consequence.

### Preparing for the Day

The command “guard yourselves” is *ittaqu*: build a protection through faith, repentance, obedience, justice, and good deeds. Taqwā is preparation for a day when appearances are removed. The person who guards himself does not rely on inherited honour; he fulfils the covenant personally. He seeks Allah’s forgiveness, repairs harm done to people, and does not postpone repentance on the assumption that status or association will save him.

The verse is severe because false security is spiritually dangerous. It can make a person proud of a favour he did not earn and careless about the responsibility attached to it. Yet the warning is also merciful: while life remains, a person can change the account. The next verses will recount the concrete deliverance and forgiveness granted to the Children of Israel, showing both the greatness of Allah’s favour and the seriousness of their response.

- Qur’an 2:123, 2:254, 6:164, and 74:38 on individual accountability and the limits of worldly relationships.
- Qur’an 2:255, 20:109, 34:23, and 53:26 on intercession requiring Allah’s permission and approval.
- Qur’an 39:47 and 70:11–14 on ransom being useless on the Day of Judgment.
- Qur’an 82:19 on the Day when no soul can help another and judgment belongs to Allah.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the Prophet’s intercession occurring by Allah’s permission.
- For the relation between communal honour and personal accountability, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī and Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr on 2:48.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:49

> ˹Remember˺ how We delivered you from the people of Pharaoh, who afflicted you with dreadful torment, slaughtering your sons and keeping your women. That was a severe test from your Lord.

**Commentary:**

### Deliverance from Pharaoh’s Tyranny

Allah recalls one of the greatest favours granted to the Children of Israel: deliverance from Pharaoh and his regime. Pharaoh’s power was not merely political; it was built upon dehumanization, class hierarchy, forced labour, terror, and the claim to ultimate authority. The Qur’an describes him as exalting himself in the land, dividing its people into groups, weakening one group, slaughtering their sons, and keeping their women alive (28:4).

The slaughter of the sons was a policy of fear and demographic control. Pharaoh sought to destroy the vulnerable population while preserving the women for servitude and exploitation. The Qur’an presents this as the behaviour of a tyrant who treats human beings as instruments of state power. It is a warning that oppression often begins by classifying people as less human, then makes violence appear administratively necessary.

The passage is also a reminder that liberation is a divine favour, but it normally arrives through human courage and action. Moses (ʿalayhi al-salām) confronted Pharaoh, the believing family protected the infant Moses, and the Children of Israel were required to leave under dangerous circumstances. Trust in Allah did not mean refusing every material means; it gave them the courage to use those means without worshipping Pharaoh’s power.

### The Test of Suffering

Allah calls the ordeal a “severe test.” The Arabic *balāʾ* can refer to hardship that tests a person, but also to a favour that tests gratitude. The suffering under Pharaoh tested their patience, courage, and faith; their later freedom tested whether they would remain grateful and obey the revelation brought through Moses. Hardship and ease are both examinations. A people may endure oppression and then fail when abundance and autonomy arrive.

The Qur’an records Moses reminding them that Allah saved them from Pharaoh’s people, who subjected them to terrible torment, and that in this was a great trial from their Lord (14:6). The reminder is meant to prevent historical amnesia. A community that forgets the structure of its oppression may reproduce it against others. A community that remembers only its suffering may treat deliverance as permission to become unjust. True remembrance produces a commitment to justice for all vulnerable people.

The verse also teaches that divine favour does not mean that a people will never face another test. Rescue from Pharaoh did not remove the need for obedience, patience, and moral discipline. Freedom is a responsibility. It creates the opportunity to build a just society, but it does not automatically create one. The Children of Israel would be tested in the wilderness, with provision, law, fear, and temptation.

### The Politics of Dehumanization

The phrase “slaughtering your sons and keeping your women” is deliberately concrete. It does not speak about oppression as an abstract concept; it names the bodies, families, and futures damaged by tyranny. The Qur’an’s moral vocabulary refuses to hide violence behind official language. Pharaoh’s policy may have been presented as security or necessity, but Allah identifies it as dreadful torment.

The verse is relevant to every society. Governments, movements, and majorities can repeat Pharaoh’s pattern when they divide people into superior and inferior classes, use collective punishment, destroy children to weaken a population, or keep women and families under coercive control. Remembering the Qur’anic account requires rejecting such practices regardless of which group commits them. Justice is not tribal, and the rescue of one oppressed community cannot justify the oppression of another.

### Favour Requires Gratitude

Deliverance should lead to gratitude, and gratitude should appear as worship and justice. The Children of Israel were not saved merely to exchange one ruler for another or to preserve a memory of escape. They were saved so that they could worship Allah, receive revelation, and become a community ordered by justice. This is why the Qur’an repeatedly links the memory of Pharaoh with commands to obey, spend, and avoid corruption.

For believers today, the verse encourages solidarity with the oppressed and humility about safety. Security, citizenship, family protection, and freedom from persecution are favours that can disappear. They should be used to protect others rather than to make the protected group indifferent to suffering. Allah’s rescue of the weak from Pharaoh is a promise that tyranny is not ultimate, but it is also a command to resist the habits that make tyranny possible.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:141, 14:6, and 28:4–6 on Pharaoh’s oppression and Allah’s deliverance of the Children of Israel.
- Qur’an 20:38–40 on the protection and upbringing of Moses under Allah’s care.
- Qur’an 26:10–68 on Moses’ confrontation with Pharaoh and the deliverance through the sea.
- Qur’an 14:6 on the ordeal as a test and reminder of divine favour.
- Qur’an 5:8 and 4:135 on justice regardless of group identity.
- For the meaning of *balāʾ* as both hardship and favour, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:49.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:50

> And ˹remember˺ when We parted the sea, rescued you, and drowned Pharaoh’s people before your very eyes.

**Commentary:**

### The Sea Opens by Allah’s Command

The Qur’an recalls the moment when Allah parted the sea for Moses and the Children of Israel. The event is presented as a real act of divine intervention, not merely a poetic description of political escape. Moses was commanded to strike the sea with his staff, and the sea became parted, with each portion like a great mountain (26:63). The people passed through a path that Allah made possible and safe for them.

The miracle came after the Children of Israel had reached what appeared to be an impossible dead end. Pharaoh and his army were behind them, while the sea stood before them. Moses declared that his Lord was with him and would guide him (26:62). Faith did not mean pretending that danger was absent. It meant refusing to conclude that Pharaoh’s power was greater than Allah’s power. The sea that represented death became the road of deliverance for those who trusted and followed.

### Rescue and Judgment Together

The verse joins two outcomes: Allah rescued the oppressed and drowned the oppressors. Divine mercy and divine justice appear in the same event. The sea did not merely remove the Children of Israel from danger; it also ended Pharaoh’s immediate pursuit. A theology that speaks only of mercy may leave the oppressed without hope of justice, while a theology that speaks only of judgment can forget the possibility of rescue. The Qur’an holds both together.

Pharaoh’s drowning was not an arbitrary punishment. It followed a long pattern of oppression, denial, persecution, and refusal of clear signs. When he finally claimed belief at the moment of destruction, it was too late for that forced confession to become a life of voluntary obedience (10:90–92). The lesson is not that repentance is difficult for Allah; it is that postponing repentance until the unseen becomes unavoidable is not the same as believing while one can still choose.

The Qur’an says that Pharaoh’s body was preserved as a sign for those who came after him (10:92). The historical oppressor who claimed supreme authority became an object lesson in human weakness. His army, weapons, wealth, and command structure could not overcome a divine decree. Power that is detached from humility becomes vulnerable to the very arrogance that produced it.

### “Before Your Very Eyes”

The phrase “before your very eyes” emphasizes that the Children of Israel witnessed both deliverance and judgment. They did not receive a vague promise that their situation would improve; they experienced the transformation of an impossible situation. Witnessing a miracle, however, did not make them permanently faithful. The later history of the sūrah shows that extraordinary signs can be followed by ingratitude if the heart does not submit.

This is a warning against treating signs as substitutes for obedience. A person may witness a remarkable event and still return to old desires, as the worship of the calf will show. Miracles can establish evidence, but they do not force love, gratitude, or moral perseverance. The human will remains capable of turning away even after seeing what should have been enough to awaken it.

### A Pattern for the Oppressed

The account has a universal moral dimension. Pharaoh represents the illusion that state violence, propaganda, and coercion can make oppression permanent. Moses represents the duty to speak truth to power and lead the vulnerable toward safety without adopting the tyrant’s methods. The Children of Israel represent a people who must learn that liberation is not complete until fear of Pharaoh is replaced by worship of Allah and justice among themselves.

Believers should not turn the story into a promise that every oppressed group will be rescued in the exact same manner or time. Allah’s wisdom and decree differ across circumstances. But the story establishes that oppression is never outside His knowledge and that the apparent permanence of tyranny is an illusion. It also commands those with ability to resist injustice, protect the vulnerable, and use freedom responsibly.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 10:90–92 on Pharaoh’s final claim, drowning, and the preserved body as a sign.
- Qur’an 20:77–79 and 26:60–68 on Moses being directed to the sea, the parting, and Pharaoh’s destruction.
- Qur’an 28:4–6 on Pharaoh’s oppression and Allah’s promise concerning the oppressed.
- Qur’an 17:103–104 on Pharaoh’s pursuit and the Children of Israel’s settlement after deliverance.
- Qur’an 14:6 on remembering the rescue as a divine favour and a test.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the fast of ʿĀshūrāʾ and Moses’ deliverance from Pharaoh.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:51

> And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights for Moses, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully.

**Commentary:**

### The Covenant at Sinai

After describing deliverance from Pharaoh and the sea, Allah recalls the forty-night appointment with Moses. The period was a preparation for receiving revelation and covenant. Sūrah al-Aʿrāf explains that Allah appointed Moses for thirty nights and completed them with ten, making forty in total (7:142). The number marks a significant period of spiritual preparation, not an indication that Allah needed time to acquire knowledge or develop a decision.

Moses’ absence created a test for the community. The people had witnessed the signs of Allah and been rescued from Pharaoh, but they were required to continue in obedience when their prophet was not physically present. This exposes a recurring weakness in religious communities: people may follow guidance while a strong leader is visible, then revert to inherited symbols or group passions when leadership is temporarily absent.

The appointment was not a withdrawal of divine care. Moses was receiving the revealed guidance that would organize their collective life. A community cannot be sustained by emotion after liberation; it needs a covenant, law, worship, and moral discipline. The forty nights therefore belong to the formation of a people who had been freed from Pharaoh but still needed to be freed from the habits of servitude and dependence upon visible power.

### The Calf as a Return to Visible Power

In Moses’ absence, many of the Children of Israel worshipped a calf. Sūrah Ṭā Hā describes the role of al-Sāmirī, who fashioned the calf and produced a deceptive appearance that led people astray (20:85–97). The Qur’an emphasizes that the calf could not speak, guide, or benefit them. Their worship was not a reasonable conclusion drawn from evidence; it was a return to a visible object that could be handled, displayed, and treated as a centre of collective identity.

The calf represented the danger of replacing the unseen Creator with a tangible symbol. The Children of Israel had just seen Pharaoh’s material power collapse, yet the psychological desire for a visible object of dependence remained. Faith in the unseen does not mean believing in an abstraction. It means worshipping the living, knowing Lord who cannot be reduced to an image. Idolatry attempts to make the divine manageable, local, and controllable; revelation calls the human being to submit to Allah without attempting to confine Him in a created form.

The Qur’an’s criticism is not limited to statues. Any created thing can function as a “calf” if people give it the devotion, obedience, hope, or fear that belongs to Allah. Wealth, political power, personality cults, ethnic identity, and institutional reputation can all become objects of ultimate allegiance. The believer asks whether he would continue to obey Allah if the visible source of social approval disappeared.

### “In His Absence”

The phrase refers to Moses’ absence from the community, not to Allah’s absence. Allah is never absent. The people were tested by the absence of the messenger who had led them, and their response revealed whether their faith was attached to Allah or merely to a human leader. This distinction is essential. Prophets are honoured and obeyed because Allah sends them, but they are not themselves objects of worship. When a prophet dies or is absent, the obligation to worship Allah continues.

The Prophet Muhammad (ﷺ) taught this principle at his own death. When some Companions were overwhelmed by grief, Abū Bakr (raḍiyallāhu ʿanhu) reminded them: “Whoever worshipped Muhammad, Muhammad has died; whoever worships Allah, Allah is alive and does not die,” then recited 3:144 (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). The continuity of faith is with Allah, not with the physical presence of any human being.

### Wrongdoing and Accountability

The verse calls the calf-worshippers wrongdoers—*ẓālimūn*. They placed worship where it did not belong and harmed their own spiritual standing. The term does not erase the distinction between those who initiated the deviation, those who followed knowingly, those who resisted, and those who later repented. Qur’anic judgment is precise, and Allah knows the responsibility of each person.

The event also reveals that a miracle does not automatically create lasting faith. The sea had been parted before them, but their hearts were still vulnerable to visible substitutes. Faith must therefore be renewed through revelation, prayer, remembrance, and obedience. The problem was not insufficient evidence but the desire for an easier god—one that would not command, judge, or require trust.

- Qur’an 7:142 on the thirty-plus-ten nights completing forty for Moses.
- Qur’an 20:83–97 on the calf, al-Sāmirī, Aaron’s warning, and Moses’ return.
- Qur’an 7:148–152 on the calf and the consequences of worshipping it.
- Qur’an 21:51–67 on Ibrahim’s rejection of visible idols and the inability of idols to benefit.
- Qur’an 3:144 and Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī on worship belonging to Allah, not to a messenger.
- For the spiritual meaning of the calf as a return to visible substitutes for Allah, see Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:51.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:52

> Even then We ˹still˺ forgave you so perhaps you would be grateful.

**Commentary:**

### Mercy After a Grave Wrong

After mentioning the worship of the calf, Allah recalls that He still forgave the Children of Israel. The phrase “even then” emphasizes the greatness of the mercy. Their offence was not a minor lapse of etiquette; it was a return to idolatry after deliverance, revelation, and unmistakable signs. Yet Allah opened a path by which the community could be cleansed and restored.

The forgiveness does not mean that the calf-worship was insignificant or that repentance could be replaced by a casual apology. The following verse recalls that Moses was given the Scripture so that they might be guided, and verse 54 describes the severe form of repentance required for this particular communal crime. Divine forgiveness is generous, but it does not abolish moral seriousness. Allah forgives the repentant while still teaching the community that rebellion has consequences.

The Qur’an repeatedly joins forgiveness with a condition of turning back. Allah says that He forgives sins for those who repent and reform (25:70), and that He accepts the repentance of those who return before death makes their regret involuntary (4:17–18). The door of mercy is broad, but it is entered through sincerity, not through presumption. A person who says he wants forgiveness while protecting the sin and refusing to repair its harm has not taken the path Adam took.

### Forgiveness Is Meant to Produce Gratitude

The purpose clause is “so perhaps you would be grateful.” Gratitude—*shukr*—is more than saying “thank you.” It is recognizing the Giver, acknowledging the value of the gift, and using it in the way the Giver intends. If Allah forgives idolatry, the appropriate response is renewed worship of Allah alone. If He restores a community after error, the community should become more humble and careful, not more confident in its ability to sin without consequence.

The Qur’an connects gratitude with increase: “If you are grateful, I will surely increase you” (14:7). This increase may be in blessing, guidance, stability, or the ability to recognize and use gifts well. Ingratitude, by contrast, turns a blessing into an argument against its recipient. The Children of Israel received forgiveness, but their later history would show that forgiveness must be remembered if it is to produce reform.

Gratitude also changes the way a person remembers his past. The repentant sinner does not deny what happened, but he does not treat the past as proof that mercy is impossible. He remembers the sin with humility, the forgiveness with hope, and the lesson with vigilance. This is healthier than either pride—“I never did wrong”—or despair—“I can never be forgiven.”

### The Difference Between Mercy and Approval

The fact that Allah forgave them must not be misunderstood as approval of the calf. Allah’s mercy toward a sinner is not a declaration that the sin was good. A parent may forgive a child while still correcting the behaviour; a court may allow rehabilitation while still naming the crime; Allah may erase guilt through repentance while still preserving the lesson and worldly consequences.

The distinction matters in religious communities. Forgiveness should not be used to conceal abuse, protect a powerful wrongdoer from accountability, or pressure victims to pretend that harm did not occur. Repentance includes ending the wrongdoing and restoring rights. Where public harm was done, public repair may be necessary. Divine mercy does not require human beings to abandon justice.

### A Sign of Hope for Every Sinner

The verse remains a source of hope. If Allah forgave a people who had fallen into idolatry after receiving extraordinary favours, no sincere repentant person should imagine that his return is futile. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that Allah stretches out His hand by night and by day to accept repentance, until the sun rises from the west (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2759). This imagery communicates the continuing opportunity to return while life remains.

The correct response to forgiveness is not to calculate how much disobedience one can safely commit. It is to become grateful, more obedient, and more protective of the heart. Forgiveness is a new responsibility. The one who has been rescued should not run back toward the danger from which he was rescued.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:17–18, 25:70, and 39:53 on repentance, forgiveness, and the limits of delayed return.
- Qur’an 7:149–153 and 20:82–97 on the calf, repentance, and Allah’s forgiveness.
- Qur’an 14:7 on gratitude and increase.
- Qur’an 2:37 on Adam’s repentance as another model of humble return.
- Qur’an 5:39 on repentance joined to reform and restoration.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 2759, on Allah’s continual acceptance of sincere repentance before the final signs of the Hour.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:53

> And ˹remember˺ when We gave Moses the Scripture—the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺ that perhaps you would be ˹rightly˺ guided.

**Commentary:**

### Revelation After Rescue and Error

Following the reminder of the calf and forgiveness, Allah recalls giving Moses the Scripture. Deliverance from Pharaoh was not the end of the mission. A people freed from oppression still need a revealed standard by which to organize worship, justice, family life, public conduct, and relations with other communities. Freedom without moral guidance can reproduce new forms of oppression, so Allah gave the Children of Israel the Torah as a source of direction.

The word *kitāb* means the Book or scripture. The word *furqān* is commonly understood as the criterion that separates truth from falsehood, right from wrong, and guidance from misguidance. The two descriptions belong together. Revelation is not only a collection of sacred narratives; it is a criterion for judgment. It teaches people how to evaluate claims, desires, customs, power, and inherited traditions rather than allowing the strongest group or loudest voice to define truth.

The Qur’an uses *furqān* for revelation in other places as well. It describes the Qur’an as that which Allah sent down as the Criterion (25:1), and says that the Torah and Gospel contained guidance and light (5:44–46). The final revelation confirms the truth of the earlier scripture and stands as a guardian over it (5:48). Muslims therefore believe that Moses received genuine divine guidance, while also believing that the Qur’an is the final and authoritative criterion for disputes about revelation.

### The Need for a Standard

Human beings possess reason and moral intuition, but these gifts are affected by desire, fear, culture, and social power. A society may call exploitation “commerce,” oppression “security,” and betrayal “strategy.” Revelation provides an independent standard that can judge both the individual and the community. It corrects the powerful and comforts the oppressed, while refusing to make either group the source of morality.

A criterion is useful only if it is consulted. The Children of Israel received the Scripture, but later verses will describe people who accepted some of it and rejected other parts according to convenience (2:85). Muslims can repeat this failure by praising the Qur’an as a book of blessings while avoiding the passages that command justice, honesty, patience, modesty, or accountability. A scripture becomes a criterion when it is allowed to judge the reader, not when the reader uses it merely to judge others.

### Guidance Requires Response

The purpose of the gift is stated as “so that perhaps you would be rightly guided.” *Laʿallakum tahtadūn* does not suggest uncertainty in Allah’s knowledge. It describes the intended result from the human perspective: the Scripture gives them the means to find the right way, but they must receive and follow it. Revelation is a light placed before the traveller; it does not remove the traveller’s responsibility to walk.

The phrase also displays divine mercy. Allah did not leave the Children of Israel to reconstruct their moral order from memory after centuries of oppression. He gave them commands, limits, stories, promises, and warnings. The Book made the route visible after they had been rescued from Pharaoh and after they had shown how easily fear and longing for visible power could lead them to the calf.

### Scripture and the Formation of Character

A revealed book guides not only by listing laws but by forming a people. It teaches gratitude for deliverance, restraint in prosperity, justice in conflict, care for the weak, and repentance after error. The Torah was a covenantal document that joined worship with public ethics. The Qur’an’s later account of the Children of Israel shows both the blessings they received from this guidance and the consequences of manipulating or neglecting it.

The same is true for the Qur’an. Its criterion must enter the heart, tongue, marketplace, home, court, and political life. Recitation is a great act of worship, but the aim of recitation is guidance. The Prophet (ﷺ) was sent to recite the revelations, purify people, teach the Book and wisdom, and teach what they did not know (2:151). Learning and transformation belong together.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 5:44–48 on the Torah, Gospel, and Qur’an as revelation, guidance, light, and criterion.
- Qur’an 25:1 on the Qur’an as *al-Furqān*.
- Qur’an 2:185 and 3:3–4 on the Qur’an and earlier revelation as guidance and criterion.
- Qur’an 2:151 on recitation, purification, teaching the Book, and wisdom.
- Qur’an 17:9 and 39:23 on the Qur’an guiding to what is most upright and affecting receptive hearts.
- For the meaning of *furqān* and its relation to the Torah and the Qur’an, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:53.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:54

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf, so turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves. That is best for you in the sight of your Creator.” Then He accepted your repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

### Repentance After the Calf

Moses tells his people plainly that they have wronged themselves by worshipping the calf. The wording does not soften idolatry into a harmless cultural expression. They had received deliverance, witnessed Allah’s signs, and been given a covenant, yet they directed worship to a created object. The wrong was first against Allah’s exclusive right to worship, but it also harmed the people themselves by returning them to dependence upon a powerless symbol.

Moses then calls them “my people.” His correction is firm, but it comes from responsibility and care rather than contempt. A prophet does not excuse the sin in order to preserve popularity, nor does he abandon the sinners when they need a way back. He names the offence, identifies its cure, and reminds them that repentance is in their own interest: “That is best for you in the sight of your Creator.”

### A Severe Communal Penance

The command to “execute yourselves” or “kill one another” is a specific ruling given to the Children of Israel for this extraordinary act of apostasy and idolatry. Classical exegetes generally understood it to mean that those who had not participated in the calf-worship were commanded to kill those who had participated, or that the guilty were required to submit to a severe penitential execution among themselves. The precise historical mechanics are not detailed in the Qur’an, and the text’s central point is the seriousness of the communal crime and the magnitude of the mercy that followed.

This command must not be detached from its prophetic and covenantal setting and turned into a general permission for individuals or groups to kill people whom they accuse of unbelief. Islamic law does not authorize vigilante violence. Judgment, due process, public authority, and the specific legal conditions of any capital ruling cannot be replaced by private anger. The verse narrates a unique command to an earlier community under its prophet; it is not a licence for modern religious violence.

The severity of the penance also reflects the historical context. The calf-worship threatened to destroy the community’s central covenant immediately after its liberation and before its revealed order had been established. Their repentance required more than verbal regret because the offence had become a public movement. Nevertheless, the Qur’an’s emphasis is not on celebrating punishment. It is on showing that even after grave rebellion, Allah opened a path of return.

### Repentance Requires Truth and Cost

Sincere repentance is often costly. A person who has harmed others may need to confess, restore property, withdraw from a corrupt institution, accept lawful consequences, or repair a reputation he damaged. Repentance that costs nothing may still be genuine, but a person should not expect Allah’s forgiveness while refusing every form of responsibility. The Children of Israel were required to confront the reality of what they had done rather than hiding behind collective excuses.

At the same time, repentance is not self-destruction for its own sake. Allah does not command people to harm themselves arbitrarily. The severe ruling belonged to a revealed legal order and was accompanied by Allah’s acceptance of their return. In the final Sharīʿah, repentance from disbelief is not carried out by private killing; the door is belief, abandonment of falsehood, sincere return, and righteous action. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that Islam erases what came before it, and that repentance erases what preceded it when it is sincere (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 121).

### The Creator and the Right to Forgive

Moses directs them to repent to their Creator—*bāriʾikum*. The title recalls Allah as the One who brings creation into proper form. The One they disobeyed is the One to whom they must return. No idol, priest, ancestor, or human intermediary can own the right to forgive. Intercession and prayer may occur only by Allah’s permission; forgiveness itself belongs to Him.

The verse ends by naming Allah *al-Tawwāb* and *al-Raḥīm*. He accepts repentance repeatedly and shows mercy to those who return. His mercy does not contradict the severity of the command; it is what makes the severe correction a path to restoration rather than annihilation. The people were not left in their sin, and they were not told that their past made obedience impossible.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:148–153 and 20:83–97 on the calf, Aaron’s warning, al-Sāmirī, and the repentance of the Children of Israel.
- Qur’an 4:17–18, 25:70, and 39:53 on sincere repentance and Allah’s forgiveness.
- Qur’an 5:32 and 17:33 on the sanctity of life and the prohibition of killing without lawful right.
- Qur’an 6:164 on individual responsibility and the fact that no person bears another’s burden.
- Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, 121, on Islam erasing what preceded it and the virtue of sincere return.
- For the classical interpretation and legal limits of the command in this verse, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:54; it must not be used to justify vigilantism.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:55

> And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes,” so a thunderbolt struck you while you were looking on.

**Commentary:**

### Demanding the Unseen on Human Terms

The Children of Israel demanded that they see Allah with their own eyes before believing Moses. Their request was not an innocent question about the nature of divine revelation. It made physical sight a condition for faith and treated the Creator as though He must appear before human senses on human terms. Revelation teaches that Allah is real and knowable through His signs, names, acts, and revealed speech, but He is not a created body subject to human inspection.

The Qur’an records a similar demand elsewhere: “We will not believe you until you bring forth for us from the earth a spring... or you bring Allah and the angels before us face to face” (17:90–92). These demands do not arise from a genuine desire to follow truth once evidence appears. They are methods of postponement—new conditions added whenever an earlier sign has been given. The problem is not absence of evidence but an insistence that evidence take a form chosen by the denier.

### The Distinction Between This World and the Hereafter

Islamic theology distinguishes between the impossibility of seeing Allah in the ordinary conditions of this worldly life and the believers’ seeing Him in the Hereafter by His permission. When Moses asked to see his Lord, Allah said, “You will never see Me, but look at the mountain; if it remains firm in its place, then you will see Me.” When Allah manifested to the mountain, it crumbled and Moses fell unconscious (7:143). This shows the limitation of created beings in the present world, not a denial that Allah may grant a vision of Him in the next.

The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that believers will see their Lord in the Hereafter just as clearly as they see the full moon, without the moon’s being confused with Him or physically touched (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī; Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The two teachings are compatible: worldly sight cannot bear the unveiled vision, while Allah can grant the people of Paradise a capacity suited to that honour. No creature comprehends Allah, but Allah grants whatever form of knowledge and vision He wills.

### The Thunderbolt as a Sign

The people were struck by a thunderbolt while they were looking on. The event is a punishment and a sign, but the Qur’an does not describe it as proof that Allah is unjust or that all requests to understand Him deserve destruction. Their demand came after clear signs and in a context of repeated rebellion. They sought to impose a material condition upon belief rather than submit to the guidance already given.

The thunderbolt also turns their demand back upon them. They asked for direct sight, and they were confronted with a manifestation of power they could not control. The sight they demanded did not make them masters of the unseen; it exposed their weakness. The Qur’an similarly describes thunderbolts as warnings to those who dispute about Allah while relying upon worldly power (13:13; 41:13).

### Faith Is Not Blindness

The verse should not be used to praise irrational belief or to prohibit sincere questions. The Qur’an invites people to reflect upon creation, consider the signs of Allah, and ask people of knowledge when they do not know (16:43). Faith is not the refusal to think. It is the willingness to allow sound evidence and revelation to govern the mind rather than demanding that Allah conform to a creature’s preferred test.

A believer may ask how revelation came, how angels exist, or how resurrection will occur, provided the question seeks understanding rather than an excuse for denial. The proper response to matters beyond human perception is to accept what Allah has disclosed and avoid inventing details about what He has withheld. The Children of Israel had been given enough guidance to obey; their demand for an impossible condition was a form of arrogance.

### The Danger of Conditional Faith

Conditional faith says, “I will obey if Allah gives me the exact experience I demand.” This turns worship into a negotiation in which the creature claims the position of judge. Genuine faith says, “I will seek evidence, and when truth becomes clear, I will submit.” The difference is humility. Many people want signs that remove the need for trust, patience, and moral choice. But if every unseen reality were made physically unavoidable, the test of willing submission would disappear.

The believer therefore asks Allah for certainty, not for a spectacle dictated by the ego. He looks at the signs in the horizons and within himself, listens to revelation, and prepares for the meeting in the Hereafter. Allah’s transcendence is not a barrier to knowledge; it is the reason knowledge of Him must be received through the forms He has chosen to disclose.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 4:153 and 7:143 on the demand to see Allah and the encounter of Moses with the mountain.
- Qur’an 17:90–93 on demanding impossible signs and seeing Allah openly.
- Qur’an 6:103 on visions not encompassing Allah, while Allah encompasses all visions.
- Qur’an 13:13 and 41:13 on thunderbolts as warnings and signs of divine power.
- Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim on the believers seeing their Lord in the Hereafter.
- For the theological distinction between worldly and eschatological vision, see Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī, and al-Nawawī’s commentary on the relevant ḥadīths in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:56

> Then We brought you back to life after your death, so that perhaps you would be grateful.

**Commentary:**

### Life After the Thunderbolt

Allah recalls that He brought the Children of Israel back to life after the thunderbolt struck them. The event was a sign of divine power and a reprieve after punishment. They had demanded a direct experience of the unseen and were overwhelmed by a manifestation they could not bear; Allah then restored them so that they could continue their earthly responsibility. The passage moves rapidly from judgment to mercy, showing that Allah’s correction is not a desire to annihilate but an opportunity to return.

The verse describes a genuine restoration from death, not merely recovery from fainting or ordinary unconsciousness, according to the understanding of the classical exegetes. Other passages connect the event with the people who asked to see Allah (7:155) and use the broader sequence of death and life as evidence that resurrection is possible. The One who restores a people after death can certainly raise all human beings on the Last Day.

### A Living Demonstration of Resurrection

The revival was a compressed demonstration of the reality that people often find difficult to imagine. The Qur’an argues elsewhere: “How can you disbelieve in Allah when you were lifeless and He gave you life, then He will cause you to die, then He will bring you to life?” (2:28). The Israelites experienced a smaller, earthly sign of this sequence. Death and restoration were no longer an abstract doctrine to them; they had passed through a version of it themselves.

A sign, however, does not remove the need for obedience. The same community that experienced deliverance from Pharaoh, the parting of the sea, the thunderbolt, and revival would still need reminders about gratitude and covenant. Miracles establish evidence, but they do not mechanically produce character. A heart that refuses to submit can witness an extraordinary event and later return to old desires. The purpose of the sign is fulfilled when it leads to recognition and reform.

### “So That Perhaps You Would Be Grateful”

The purpose clause is “so that perhaps you would be grateful.” Gratitude is the appropriate response to being restored. Allah did not owe them another earthly opportunity after their defiance, yet He gave one. The word *laʿallakum* describes the intended result from the human perspective; Allah does not doubt the future. He teaches them that mercy should awaken gratitude, not entitlement.

Gratitude includes recognizing the Giver, speaking of His favour without arrogance, and using the renewed life in obedience. A person who survives an illness, danger, or serious mistake is not merely fortunate in a random universe. He has been given time, and that time is a trust. The wise response is not to assume that survival proves approval of one’s life; it is to ask what reform Allah is inviting through the reprieve.

The Qur’an often places gratitude opposite ingratitude. “Work, O family of David, in gratitude” (34:13), and “few of My servants are grateful” (34:13). Gratitude is active work. It appears in prayer, charity, justice, restraint, and care for others. Saying that one is grateful while using renewed life to repeat the same rebellion is a contradiction.

### Mercy Does Not Cancel Accountability

The revival also clarifies the relationship between mercy and accountability. Allah’s mercy gave them another chance, but the event did not make their previous demand acceptable. Forgiveness and continued life did not erase the lesson. In the same way, a repentant person may be forgiven while still needing to repair worldly harm and change the habits that led to sin.

The verse should comfort anyone who feels that a failure has ended his possibility of return. Allah can restore what seems spiritually dead. But it should also warn against wasting a reprieve. Life after danger is a new opportunity, not an assurance that danger will never return. The believer thanks Allah by becoming more truthful, more humble, and more obedient.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 2:28, 7:155, and 40:11 on death, restoration, and Allah’s power over life.
- Qur’an 7:143 on Moses’ encounter with the mountain and the limitations of worldly vision.
- Qur’an 34:13 on gratitude as active obedience.
- Qur’an 14:7 on gratitude and increase.
- Qur’an 23:15–16 and 75:36–40 on death, resurrection, and human purpose.
- For the nature of the event and its connection to the demand in 2:55, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:55–56.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:57

> And ˹remember when˺ We shaded you with clouds and sent down to you manna and quails, ˹saying˺, “Eat from the good things We have provided for you.” The evildoers ˹certainly˺ did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves.

**Commentary:**

### Mercy in the Wilderness

After recalling the thunderbolt and revival, Allah remembers how He cared for the Children of Israel in the wilderness. The desert exposed them to heat, thirst, hunger, and the insecurity of travel. Allah shaded them with clouds and provided food without requiring the ordinary agricultural labour that a settled community would need. The blessings were not earned by the people’s power; they were signs of mercy and provision during a difficult journey.

The cloud is described as shade. The Qur’an does not require us to imagine that every detail of its form was identical to an ordinary weather system. It presents a protective provision suited to the desert. Other passages mention this period as a time in which Allah gave them shade, manna, and quails (7:160; 20:80). The historical details beyond what revelation states should not be treated as certain simply because later reports speculate about them.

### Manna and Quails

*Manna* is commonly understood as a sweet or naturally gathered provision, though classical reports describe it in more than one way. *Salwā* is generally understood as quail or a similar small bird. The central meaning is clear even where the precise biological identification is not: Allah provided wholesome food in an environment where ordinary food supplies were difficult to secure.

The command was, “Eat from the good things We have provided for you.” Divine provision is meant to be received with gratitude, not suspicion or entitlement. Food is lawful to enjoy, but its source should produce recognition of Allah and care for others. The Children of Israel were not merely being fed; they were being trained to trust their Provider day by day rather than recreate the security structures of Pharaoh.

The wilderness provision also teaches that miraculous abundance does not remove spiritual responsibility. The people had shade, food, water, and rescue, yet later complaints and rebellion showed that the human heart can become dissatisfied even while surrounded by blessings. Gratitude must be cultivated; it does not automatically follow from receiving more.

### Provision and the Test of Contentment

The phrase “good things” includes lawful, wholesome, and beneficial provision. The Qur’an repeatedly commands believers to eat what is good and lawful and to avoid excess (2:168; 7:31). Provision is not measured only by variety or luxury. Simple food received with gratitude can be better for the heart than abundance that produces arrogance, waste, or constant complaint.

The story also exposes the danger of comparing one’s provision with another person’s. The desert provision was suited to the people’s circumstances and was renewed according to Allah’s wisdom. Believers are not forbidden to seek improvement, but dissatisfaction can become a form of ingratitude when it leads to contempt for lawful blessings already present. The right response to need is lawful effort and prayer, not rejection of every gift because it is not the gift one imagined.

### Wronging Themselves

The verse concludes that the evildoers did not wrong Allah; they wronged themselves. Allah is independent of creation. Human obedience does not increase His dominion, and human disobedience does not diminish it. When people reject provision, break covenant, or misuse blessings, the loss falls upon them. They deprive themselves of gratitude, guidance, community, and the reward of obedience.

This principle protects the believer from imagining that Allah’s commands are for His benefit. Allah commands prayer, charity, fasting, and justice because these acts purify and protect human beings. He is not enriched by their worship. The Qur’an says, “Whoever strives, strives only for his own soul; Allah is free of need of the worlds” (29:6).

The verse therefore joins material provision with moral evaluation. Shade, food, and safety are gifts; the way people respond to them reveals their character. The grateful person receives, shares, and obeys. The wrongdoer consumes the gift while denying the Giver and turns provision into another reason for complaint or rebellion.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:160 and 20:80 on shade, manna, and quails in the wilderness.
- Qur’an 2:168, 7:31, and 16:114 on lawful, good provision and avoiding excess.
- Qur’an 29:6 and 35:15 on human dependence and Allah’s independence from creation.
- Qur’an 14:7 and 34:13 on gratitude as a response to divine provision.
- Qur’an 20:81 on eating from good provision without transgressing within it.
- For the interpretation of manna and salwā and the moral lesson of the provision, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:57.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:58

> And ˹remember˺ when We said, “Enter this city and eat freely from wherever you please; enter the gate with humility, saying, ‘Absolve us.’ We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers.”

**Commentary:**

### Entering the Promised City

Allah recalls the command to enter a city and enjoy its abundant provision. The city is not named in this āyah. Many classical exegetes identified it with Jerusalem or the Holy Land, drawing upon related passages such as 5:21 and 7:161, but the Qur’an’s main concern is the people’s response to the command, not the geographical debate. Where revelation leaves a detail unnamed, it is better to preserve that restraint than to present a conjecture as certainty.

The command follows years of desert travel and hardship. The people were being brought from dependence and fear toward settlement. Yet entering a city was not merely a military or economic achievement; it was a moral test. They were to receive the land with gratitude, humility, repentance, and obedience rather than with the arrogance of conquerors who imagine that possession proves superiority.

“Eat freely from wherever you please” shows the breadth of lawful provision. Allah does not make gratitude incompatible with enjoyment. Food, settlement, and security are blessings when received within His limits. The instruction to enjoy provision is placed beside the command to enter humbly, teaching that material abundance should deepen submission rather than produce entitlement.

### The Gate and the Word of Repentance

The people were told to enter the gate with humility and to say, “Absolve us”—*ḥiṭṭah*. The word is associated with asking for sins to be lifted or burdens to be removed. The entry was therefore a ritual of repentance and surrender. They were not to enter boasting of their history, power, or supposed merit, but acknowledging their need for Allah’s forgiveness.

The Qur’an’s related account says they were instructed to say “ḥiṭṭah” and to enter the gate prostrating, with the promise that their sins would be forgiven and the reward of the good-doers increased (7:161). Humility here is not self-contempt. It is truthful recognition: Allah gave the victory, Allah provided the land, and Allah alone forgives sin. The community’s relationship with the land was therefore subordinate to worship and moral responsibility.

The promise to multiply the reward of good-doers reveals Allah’s generosity. Forgiveness removes the burden of past wrong, while multiplication of reward gives the obedient person more than he has earned by strict calculation. The Qur’an says that Allah does not allow the reward of good deeds to be lost and multiplies charity many times over (4:40; 2:261).

### Land, Power, and Humility

The verse offers a corrective to every community that interprets possession as divine permission to act unjustly. A place may be promised or granted for a purpose, but the purpose remains obedience and justice. The Qur’an warns that covenants are conditional and that Allah’s promise is not a guarantee for wrongdoers (2:124). Land, political authority, and economic prosperity are tests of conduct, not certificates of moral superiority.

The command to enter humbly is relevant to personal success as well. When a person receives a job, home, degree, victory, marriage, or public honour, he is tempted to attribute it entirely to his own ability. The Qur’anic posture is to enjoy the blessing while saying, in effect, that it came by Allah’s grace and remains subject to His judgment. Humility protects achievement from becoming an idol.

### Forgiveness Before Possession

The order is striking: before the people settle into abundance, they are instructed to ask forgiveness. This teaches that no new beginning is spiritually safe without repentance. People often want a new environment to solve an old moral problem, but the heart travels with the person. The wilderness can be replaced by a city while complaint, pride, and disobedience remain.

The believer therefore enters every blessing through gratitude and repentance. He asks Allah to remove the sins that would corrupt the blessing and to help him use the blessing in a way that pleases Him. Material provision and spiritual cleansing are not separate subjects; the way a person receives the first reveals whether he values the second.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 5:21 and 7:161 on entering the Holy Land and the command of humility and repentance.
- Qur’an 2:124 on Allah’s covenant not extending to wrongdoers.
- Qur’an 4:40 and 2:261 on Allah’s multiplication of good deeds and reward.
- Qur’an 16:114 and 7:31 on enjoying good provision with gratitude and moderation.
- Qur’an 28:76–83 on wealth, arrogance, and the difference between possession and righteous use.
- For *ḥiṭṭah*, the gate, and the classical identification of the city, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:58.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:59

> But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say. So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness.

**Commentary:**

### Distorting a Command of Repentance

The previous verse described a simple command: enter with humility and ask Allah to remove your burdens. The wrongdoers changed the words they had been instructed to say. The failure was not merely a pronunciation mistake. The context presents a deliberate alteration of a revealed instruction, replacing humility and repentance with another expression or attitude. Their change reflected contempt for the command and a refusal to enter the city in the posture Allah required.

The related account in Sūrah al-Aʿrāf says that the wrongdoers changed the word to something other than what they were told, and Allah sent upon them a punishment from heaven because of their wrongdoing (7:162). Classical reports mention that some mocked the command or substituted a word with a different meaning. The details of those reports vary, but the moral point is stable: twisting a divine instruction in order to avoid its intended meaning is rebellion, even when the outward form resembles obedience.

### The Difference Between Error and Defiance

Not every mistake in recitation or misunderstanding carries the same moral weight. A person may forget, mispronounce, misunderstand, or act on incomplete knowledge and then correct himself. The verse speaks of *ẓulm* and *fisq*—deliberate wrongdoing and rebellious departure. The wrongdoers knew what they had been commanded and altered it as an act of contempt or evasion.

This distinction is essential in religious life. Scholars have always recognized honest interpretive disagreement, differences in recitation, and mistakes made despite sincere effort. What is condemned is knowingly changing revelation, concealing its meaning, or using technical language to make disobedience look like compliance. The Qur’an warns against those who write claims with their own hands and say, “This is from Allah,” for worldly gain (2:79).

The same problem appears when a community repeats religious words while removing their moral content. It may speak about justice while protecting oppression, repentance while celebrating pride, or worship while directing ultimate loyalty to wealth and power. Mere vocabulary cannot substitute for the reality signified by the words. Allah judges the intention and action behind the phrase.

### Punishment from Above

The punishment came “from the heavens.” The phrase indicates a punishment decreed and sent by Allah, not a random natural event outside His knowledge. The Qur’an does not require us to identify its exact physical form beyond what the text states. Some reports describe a plague or affliction, but such details should be treated with appropriate caution unless supported by a sound revelation.

The punishment demonstrates that disobedience has consequences even after a community has received repeated favours. Mercy should not be confused with immunity. Allah’s patience gives time for repentance, but deliberate mockery of His commands hardens the heart and corrupts communal life. The verse follows the pattern of the passage: blessing, instruction, distortion, and consequence.

### The Seriousness of Religious Mockery

The wrongdoers did not merely fail to understand a ritual. They treated a command of repentance as an opportunity for mockery. The Qur’an warns against making Allah’s revelations a subject of amusement (9:64–66). Humour does not automatically erase meaning. When someone deliberately ridicules Allah, His signs, or the sacred obligations He revealed, the act can become a form of rejection rather than harmless entertainment.

At the same time, the verse must not be used to justify uncontrolled punishment by private persons. The Qur’an assigns judgment and lawful enforcement to legitimate authority under just rules, and it forbids transgression. The moral lesson is that Allah sees and judges defiance; believers must uphold truth and lawful order without becoming agents of vengeance.

### A Warning Against Empty Formalism

This āyah challenges the temptation to comply externally while resisting inwardly. The wrongdoers entered or were commanded to enter, but they refused the humility that made the act meaningful. Religious practice can be emptied in the same way when people preserve the outer form while changing the intention, words, or ethical demands of revelation.

The cure is sincerity. The believer asks what Allah actually commanded, seeks to understand it accurately, and obeys without turning the command into a platform for ego. He does not invent a convenient substitute and then demand that the substitute receive the reward of obedience. Repentance begins when the person stops editing the truth to protect his pride.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:161–162 on the command, the changed word, and punishment for wrongdoing.
- Qur’an 2:79 on inventing claims and attributing them to Allah for worldly gain.
- Qur’an 9:64–66 on making Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger the object of mockery.
- Qur’an 5:2 and 7:56 on not allowing anger or wrongdoing to lead to transgression.
- Qur’an 17:15 and 28:59 on the establishment of proof before punishment.
- For the variant reports concerning the changed word and the punishment, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:59; the secure lesson is the condemnation of deliberate distortion.

## Sūrah al-Baqarah 2:60

> And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, “Strike the rock with your staff.” Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place. ˹We then said,˺ “Eat and drink of Allah’s provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”

**Commentary:**

### Water in the Wilderness

The Children of Israel were again tested by the conditions of the wilderness. Moses prayed for water, and Allah instructed him to strike the rock with his staff. Twelve springs emerged, corresponding to the twelve tribes, and each tribe knew its place of drinking. The miracle provided not only water but order. A large community was given access to a scarce resource without being left to constant conflict over who would drink first or who would control the supply.

The Qur’an presents the event as a real sign of Allah’s power. The staff was a means through which the miracle occurred, but the source of the water was Allah. This distinction is fundamental: created means can be used, but they do not possess independent power. Moses did not worship the staff or rely upon it apart from Allah’s command. The believer uses lawful causes while knowing that the outcome remains under Allah’s control.

Sūrah al-Aʿrāf describes the same favour: Allah revealed to Moses to strike the rock, and twelve springs flowed from it; each group knew its drinking place (7:160). The repeated number twelve connects the provision to the organization of the Children of Israel as a people descended from the twelve sons of Jacob. The miracle was communal, visible, and suited to their immediate need.

### Provision and Fair Distribution

The verse says that each tribe knew its drinking place. This suggests a principle of organized access to common resources. Water is a basic necessity, and the community must prevent rivalry, hoarding, and violence around it. The arrangement did not deny the people’s shared provision; it made the provision accessible and orderly. Justice often requires clear systems, not merely good intentions.

The lesson extends to every resource upon which people depend. Land, food, housing, medicine, education, and public funds should not be controlled by a small group while the vulnerable are left without access. Allah’s provision is not a licence for private monopolies that create avoidable suffering. The Qur’an commands that wealth not circulate only among the rich (59:7) and condemns withholding ordinary assistance (107:4–7).

The command to “eat and drink” shows that spiritual life does not require rejecting lawful material needs. Hunger and thirst are real, and Allah’s mercy addresses them. Enjoying food and water becomes worship when it is received with gratitude, obtained lawfully, shared fairly, and not turned into waste or arrogance.

### Do Not Spread Corruption

The warning follows immediately: “Do not go about spreading corruption in the land.” The connection is deliberate. A community that receives provision can still turn it into a source of injustice. Water may be hoarded, distribution may become a means of political control, and abundance may create waste. The people were therefore told that provision carries moral responsibility.

*Fasād* includes corrupting worship, breaking covenants, harming people, wasting resources, and using authority unjustly. The Qur’an says that corruption appears on land and sea because of what people’s hands have earned (30:41). A society can receive a miracle of provision and still be destroyed if its people use the gift to dominate one another. Material abundance without taqwā is not security.

The command also links individual consumption with public ethics. “Eat and drink” is followed by “do not spread corruption,” not by a permission to consume without limits. A believer asks where provision came from, whether others were deprived to obtain it, and whether his use damages the environment or future generations. Gratitude includes restraint and responsible distribution.

### Moses as a Leader of a Community

Moses’ prayer shows that a prophet brings the needs of his people to Allah. He does not treat spiritual leadership as indifference to food, water, security, or organization. At the same time, he does not answer need through corruption or favoritism. Allah provides a just arrangement for the tribes, and Moses conveys the command to use it without transgression.

The scene also illustrates the balance between miracle and human responsibility. Allah brings the water forth, but the people must go to their designated springs, drink, avoid waste, and refrain from conflict. Divine provision does not cancel practical action. It establishes the conditions for responsible action and tests whether the recipients will use the gift according to the Giver’s command.

### A Lesson for the Present

Water remains a sign of life and mercy. The Qur’an says, “We made from water every living thing” (21:30), and reminds human beings that if their water were to sink into the earth, no one could bring flowing water except Allah (67:30). These verses encourage gratitude, conservation, and humility. Access to water is a trust with personal, social, and environmental dimensions.

The account closes this group of favours with a warning. The Children of Israel received shade, food, forgiveness, revelation, and water, yet gifts alone do not create faithfulness. The proper response to provision is to worship the Provider, distribute blessings justly, and avoid corruption. Any community that forgets these obligations can turn a mercy into a cause of ruin.

### Evidence and references

- Qur’an 7:160 on the twelve springs and each tribe’s drinking place.
- Qur’an 21:30 and 67:30 on water as the basis of life and a sign of Allah’s power.
- Qur’an 30:41 and 59:7 on corruption, human responsibility, and the fair circulation of resources.
- Qur’an 2:57 and 20:80–81 on provision in the wilderness and the command not to transgress in it.
- Qur’an 7:31 and 107:4–7 on moderation, avoiding waste, and sharing assistance.
- For the miracle, the twelve tribes, and the moral warning, see Tafsīr al-Ṭabarī, Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr, and Tafsīr al-Qurṭubī on 2:60.
