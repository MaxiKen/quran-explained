# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 51–60

We are completing the catalogue of favours that opened at verse 47. The pattern by now is unmistakable, and the surah is building it deliberately: a favour is named, the response to it is recorded, and the response is almost always refusal. Deliverance from Pharaoh, then the sea — and then, within weeks, the calf (verses 51 to 52). The Torah given as a criterion, and the path back from apostasy prescribed (verses 53 to 54). The demand to see Allah and the thunderbolt, followed by resurrection from the dead (verses 55 to 56). Shade, and food falling from the sky (verse 57). A city offered on the simplest possible terms, and the terms turned into a joke (verses 58 to 59). And water brought out of a struck rock, organised so precisely that no tribe would quarrel over it (verse 60). What emerges across these ten verses is not a list of blessings but an argument: every one of these favours was received by people who had just been given the one before it, and none of them produced lasting gratitude. The surah is not cataloguing generosity for its own sake. It is demonstrating that the failure was never on the supply side.

---

## Verse 51

**"And ˹remember˺ when We appointed forty nights for Moses, then you worshipped the calf in his absence, acting wrongfully."**

In simple terms, Allah appointed forty nights for Moses to come to Him, and while Moses was away the Children of Israel took a calf and worshipped it, thereby wronging themselves.

*Wa idh wa'adna Musa arba'ina laylatan* — "and when We appointed for Moses forty nights." The Qur'an supplies the breakdown of this period elsewhere, and the two verses complete each other: "And We appointed for Moses thirty nights and completed them with ten, so the appointed time of his Lord was completed as forty nights" (7:142). So the appointment was thirty nights, extended by ten. The counting by nights rather than days is normal in Arabic reckoning, though the commentators also noted that nights are mentioned because they were the hours of intimate address between Musa and his Lord.

Forty recurs in this tradition as a period of testing and preparation. In 5:26 the same people are forbidden the land for forty years of wandering. Whether or not anything should be built on the number — and it is safer not to build much — the narrative function is clear: a sustained period of absence, long enough for a community to be left to itself.

*Thumma ittakhadhtum al-'ijla min ba'dihi* — "then you took the calf after him." The story is told more fully in Surah Ta-Ha and Surah al-A'raf. Moses went to the mountain; a figure called al-Samiri fashioned a calf from the ornaments the people had with them, and it produced a lowing sound; the people took it as a god (7:148; 20:85–88, 20:95–97). Note the material: according to 7:148, the calf was made *from their own ornaments*. They took the gold they owned, melted it, and worshipped what they had made.

Two points of accuracy matter here, and both concern how this story is told.

The first is that Harun (Aaron) did not make the calf. This is a significant divergence from the Biblical account in Exodus 32, where Aaron fashions the golden calf. The Qur'an explicitly exonerates him: Moses returns and seizes his brother's head and beard in anger, and Harun answers, "O son of my mother, indeed the people overpowered me and were about to kill me, so do not let the enemies gloat over me and do not place me among the wrongdoing people" (7:150), and again in 20:92–94. So in the Qur'anic account, Harun opposed them, was overwhelmed, and was vindicated — while the culprit is named as al-Samiri. Anyone reading this passage alongside the Bible should know that the Qur'an deliberately corrects the attribution.

The second is about the lowing sound. Reports explaining it — the most famous being that al-Samiri took dust from the traces of the hoof of the angel's mount, and that it gave the image motion — belong to the narrative material transmitted in the commentaries. They should be cited as reports rather than as established fact, and nothing in the lesson depends on them.

*Wa antum zalimun* — "while you were wrongdoers." The construction makes the wrongdoing circumstantial: they took the calf *in a state of* wronging themselves. And note what word is used. Not "disbelievers" here, but *zalimun* — those who wrong. The Qur'an's consistent framing, stated outright at 2:57, is that sin is self-harm.

The single most important thing about this verse is where the surah has placed it. It comes immediately after verse 50 — the sea parting and the army drowning while they watched. The people who saw that, within weeks, made a god out of their own jewellery while their prophet was on a mountain. The surah is making an argument about evidence, and it is the same argument it has been making since verse 6: seeing does not produce faith.

Notice also the timing within the verse. The calf was taken *in his absence*. The moment the prophet's back was turned, the thing they had just sworn to collapsed. The commentators did not miss the lesson: a faith that holds only while the strong figure is in the room is not yet a faith.

Now apply this honestly. The calf was made from their own ornaments — that is, from their own wealth, their own resources, their own hands. They did not worship a foreign god imposed on them; they manufactured one out of what they already had and then prostrated to it. That is a precise description of how idolatry actually works in any era. The modern calf is rarely a rival religion. It is your career, which you built, and which now dictates every moral decision you make. It is the money you earned and now cannot risk losing. It is the institution you helped found and now cannot criticise. It is the political identity, the nation, the party, the platform. It is, most commonly, the self. In every case the structure is identical: something you made, which you now serve.

And the second lesson is about the absence. How many people are religious only in the presence of the people who hold them to it — the parent, the spouse, the teacher, the community, the app that tracks their streak? The test of verses 51 is what happens in the forty nights when nobody who matters is watching. That is when the calf is made.

## Verse 52

**"Even then We ˹still˺ forgave you so perhaps you would be grateful."**

In simple terms, after that enormous sin, Allah still pardoned them, so that they might respond with gratitude.

*Thumma 'afawna 'ankum min ba'di dhalika* — "then We pardoned you after that." The verb *'afa* means to pardon, and its root meaning is to efface and wipe away, as the wind effaces tracks in sand. The pardon is not a suspension of the consequence; it is an erasure. And the phrase *min ba'di dhalika* — "after that" — carries the weight of the whole verse. After *that*. After the sea. After the calf. The supplied translation catches the force of it well: "Even then We still forgave you."

There is a question of ordering here that is worth naming, because it explains something about how the Qur'an narrates. This verse states the pardon, but verse 54 goes on to describe the process by which it was reached — the repentance and the severe expiation. So the surah announces the outcome before narrating the path to it. The commentators noted that this is deliberate: the pardon is stated first so that the mercy is not lost inside the severity of what follows. The Qur'an frequently orders material thematically rather than chronologically, and this is one of those places.

*La'allakum tashkurun* — "so that perhaps you would be grateful." The purpose of the pardon is named, and it is not relief. It is gratitude — which in the Qur'an's vocabulary is not a feeling but a response: thanks expressed in obedience. A pardon that produces no change has failed of its purpose. The same clause closes verse 56, so that two of the greatest favours in this passage — the pardon after the calf and the revival after the thunderbolt — are both stated to have the same intended end.

Now, what should a reader take from this?

The first thing is hope, and it should be stated without qualification. If the people who built an idol out of their own gold, weeks after watching an army drown, could be pardoned, then the door is open to anyone. This is the same door that opened for Adam at verse 37, and the surah will use the identical divine Names at verse 54 to close both episodes. There is no sin that has not been preceded by a worse one that was forgiven.

But the second thing is the warning hidden in the hope, and the surah is constructing it in front of us. This is the second time in this passage that a pardon is followed by another failure. The catalogue that follows shows the pattern repeating: the manna arrives and is complained about (verse 61); the city is offered and the terms are mocked (verse 59); water comes from a rock and corruption is still spread (verse 60). Forgiveness received without gratitude does not reform anyone; it only postpones the reckoning and, in the logic of 2:15, lengthens the rope.

There is a third and more personal application. Gratitude for forgiveness is not a matter of feeling relieved. The test of whether you have actually received a pardon is whether you have stopped doing the thing you were pardoned for. A person who repeatedly asks forgiveness for the same sin and repeatedly returns to it has not yet understood what the pardon was *for*. The purpose clause in this verse is the key: it was given so that you would be grateful, and gratitude, in this Book, is a life that has turned around.

And there is a fourth, which is the hardest and the most needed: if you have been forgiven by Allah, the appropriate response to someone else's failure is not severity. The people of this verse were pardoned the worst sin in the Book. No one reading it is in a position to withhold mercy from anybody.

## Verse 53

**"And ˹remember˺ when We gave Moses the Scripture—the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺ that perhaps you would be ˹rightly˺ guided."**

In simple terms, Allah gave Moses the scripture, which functions as the criterion distinguishing truth from falsehood, so that they might be guided by it.

*Wa idh atayna Musa al-kitaba wa al-furqana* — "and when We gave Moses the Scripture and the Criterion." There is a genuine grammatical question here about whether *al-furqan* is a second gift or a description of the first. The majority of commentators, followed by the translation you are reading, took it as an apposition: the Scripture, *which is* the Criterion — meaning the Torah itself is what distinguishes right from wrong, truth from falsehood, lawful from unlawful. A minority read it as two things given together. The first reading is the stronger one, and the difference does not affect the substance.

*Furqan* comes from the root meaning to separate and distinguish. The word is used in the Qur'an for several things: for the Torah here; for the Qur'an itself in 25:1 ("Blessed is He who sent down the Criterion upon His servant"); for revelation generally in 3:4; and for the deliverance and discernment Allah grants in 8:29 ("if you fear Allah, He will grant you a criterion"). What runs through all of them is the idea of a decisive separation — the thing that lets you tell the difference.

This is the theological heart of the verse, and it is the part most worth holding onto. The scripture is called the criterion because it is what makes moral discrimination possible. It is not merely inspiration, or wisdom literature, or a record of a people's religious experience. Its stated function is to *separate*: this is true and that is false, this is permitted and that is forbidden, this is just and that is oppressive. And crucially, the criterion is external to you. It is revealed, not generated. That is what makes it a criterion at all — a standard cannot be the same thing as the thing being measured.

*La'allakum tahtadun* — "so that perhaps you would be guided." As at verse 52, the purpose of the gift is named, and it is guidance. Notice the pairing across these two favours: pardon is given so that you would be grateful; scripture is given so that you would be guided. Between them they name the two things a community needs — to be released from what it has done, and to be shown where to go.

It is also worth saying plainly, for any reader of any background, how highly Islam regards the Torah. This is not a grudging acknowledgement. The Qur'an says: "Indeed, We sent down the Torah, in which was guidance and light" (5:44), and it describes the prophets, the rabbis and the scholars as judging by it (5:44). A Muslim is required to believe in the Torah as a genuine revelation from Allah, as verse 4 of this surah commanded. The Islamic position is not that the earlier scriptures were false, but that they were true, that they were addressed to their time and people, and that the Qur'an has been sent as the confirming criterion over them — the standard by which disputed questions are now settled.

Two applications follow.

The first is about what a scripture is *for*. The verse states the purpose, and it is guidance. A scripture that is reverenced, carried, decorated, quoted in arguments and sworn upon, but not consulted to determine what is true and what to do, is not being used for the purpose stated here. The measure of your relationship with revelation is not how much of it you have memorised but how much of it you have handed authority over.

The second is about the criterion itself. Every age produces its own candidates for the role — public opinion, the consensus of a group, personal intuition, what feels right, what a majority votes for. The Qur'an's claim is that the criterion cannot be manufactured by the person being tested. It has to come from outside you. Which means the practical question this verse puts to a modern reader is simple and uncomfortable: when you need to decide what is right, what do you actually consult first, and how much of that consultation is really you?

## Verse 54

**"And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Surely you have wronged yourselves by worshipping the calf, so turn in repentance to your Creator and execute ˹the calf-worshippers among˺ yourselves. That is best for you in the sight of your Creator.” Then He accepted your repentance. Surely He is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms, Moses told his people that they had wronged themselves by taking the calf, and commanded them to turn in repentance to their Creator and to carry out a severe act of purification among themselves, which would be better for them; and Allah then accepted their repentance.

*Ya qawmi innakum zalimtum anfusakum bi-ittikhazikum al-'ijla* — "O my people, you have wronged yourselves by your taking of the calf." Note where the wrong is located: *anfusakum*, yourselves. The sin is real and grave, and it is described primarily as self-destruction rather than as an injury done to God — a frame the Qur'an repeats at verse 57 of this passage.

*Fa-tubu ila bari'ikum* — "so turn in repentance to your Creator." The Name used here is *al-Bari'*, from a root meaning to bring into being and to proportion, and also to be free and clear of defect. The commentators pointed out the precision of the choice: they had worshipped a created object, and they are summoned back to the One who creates and who is free of every resemblance to what He makes. The Name that is invoked is the one that dismantles the sin. And note that the repentance is directed *to their Creator*, not to Moses and not through any intermediary.

*Fa-uqtulu anfusakum* — "then kill yourselves / one another." This is the most difficult clause in the batch, and it is impossible to handle responsibly by moving past it quickly.

The first question is what it means. The majority of commentators understood it as: those among you who did not worship the calf are to execute those who did — reading *anfusakum* in its well-attested Arabic sense of "one another," as in 4:29's "do not kill one another." Others understood it as each guilty person taking his own life. The transmitted reports describe a great number being killed before the punishment was lifted; those numbers — commonly given in the tens of thousands — come from reports in the commentaries and from earlier scriptural tradition, and are not stated in the Qur'an, so they must not be presented as Qur'anic data.

The second and more important question is what status this ruling has. It must be said plainly: this was a specific, time-bound divine command to a specific people through a prophet, given as the prescribed expiation for the gravest sin in the Book, committed by a community that had just witnessed the parting of the sea and whose entire covenant hung in the balance. It is not a general law, and it never has been. No school of Islamic law, in any century, has derived from this verse a permission for any person or group to kill anyone. Muslim jurists classify it as a specific ruling (*hukm khass*) addressed to that community by direct revelation, and specific revelation of that kind is not generalisable. Any attempt to use this verse to justify violence is a misuse with no basis in the tradition.

The third question is the one a modern reader will actually ask, and it deserves an honest answer rather than a defensive one. The command is severe, and the discomfort it produces is legitimate. Within the Qur'an's own frame, there are three things that make it intelligible, though not comfortable. Life and death belong to Allah alone, and death is not the ultimate harm in a book whose entire orientation is toward what follows death. The sin being expiated is *shirk* — the one sin the Qur'an consistently places beyond all others in gravity — committed in full knowledge, immediately after the deliverance. And the narrative itself ends not in slaughter but in mercy: the reports state that the punishment was lifted and the survivors were honoured, and the verse closes by announcing that He accepted their repentance. It should also be said that the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ, whose community was given the final law, never imposed anything of this kind, and that the Islamic law on apostasy is a separate and heavily disputed question that must not be collapsed into this verse.

*Dhalikum khayrun lakum 'inda bari'ikum* — "that is better for you in the sight of your Creator." The command is presented as being in their interest, and the reasoning is internal to the Qur'an's logic: expiation and martyrdom in this life are better than entering the next life in a state of shirk.

*Fa-taba 'alaykum* — "then He turned to you in acceptance." And the verse closes with *innahu huwa al-tawwabu al-rahim* — the Accepter of Repentance, the Most Merciful. Those are the exact Names with which verse 37 closed the repentance of Adam. The parallel is deliberate and it is the point of the verse: the same door that opened for the first human being opened for this community, through the same two Names.

So what should a reader take from this verse? Not a precedent — that is precisely what it is not. What it gives is a measure. It establishes, in the starkest available terms, how grave the abandonment of faith is, and how completely accessible the return is. Both are held in the same breath: the appalling severity of the expiation, and the immediate, unqualified acceptance that follows. The theology of the verse is entirely in that combination. Sin is not small, and the door is not closed.

There is one further application that is legitimate and personal. The calf they made came from their own ornaments, and the remedy was to destroy what had come between them and their Creator. Every person has some version of that: the thing they have built, accumulated or attached themselves to, which now stands where God should stand. The Qur'an's consistent instruction is not to negotiate with it but to remove it, and to do it decisively. That is a far cry from any permission to harm another person, which this verse nowhere gives and which Islamic law nowhere permits on its basis.

## Verse 55

**"And ˹remember˺ when you said, “O Moses! We will never believe you until we see Allah with our own eyes,” so a thunderbolt struck you while you were looking on."**

In simple terms, they demanded of Moses that they would not believe him until Allah showed Himself to them openly, and a thunderbolt struck them while they watched.

*Lan nu'mina laka hatta nara Allaha jahratan* — "we will never believe you until we see Allah openly." The word *jahratan* means manifestly, publicly, plainly — something seen clearly rather than inferred. So the demand was for direct, unambiguous visible manifestation.

The offense in this verse is not the desire for certainty. It is in the grammar of the demand: *hatta* — "until." This is a condition imposed on God. "We will not believe unless You do this." That is not the posture of a seeker; it is the posture of someone dictating terms. The Qur'an records this same reflex among the opponents of Muhammad ﷺ — "those who do not expect the meeting with Us say: why are not angels sent down to us, or why do we not see our Lord?" (25:21) — and the answer given there is the same one implied here: they have already been given enough to be accountable, and their demand is a form of refusal rather than a request for help.

It is worth distinguishing their demand from Moses' own request. In 7:143, Moses asked his Lord to show Himself, and Allah answered by manifesting to a mountain, which crumbled to dust, and Moses fell unconscious. Moses' request was made by a prophet who already believed, out of longing, and it was answered with a demonstration of why the request could not be fulfilled in this life — "You will not see Me." Their demand was made by people who placed it as a precondition for believing at all.

*Fa-akhadhatkum al-sa'iqatu* — "so the thunderbolt seized you." There is a variation between the accounts that is worth noting honestly. Here the instrument is the *sa'iqah*, a thunderbolt. In 7:155, describing what appears to be the same episode with the seventy men Moses chose, the word used is *rajfah*, a violent trembling or earthquake. The commentators reconciled this in two ways: either two descriptions of one event, or two distinct events. Both readings are held. Similarly, 4:153 places this demand and its thunderbolt *before* the taking of the calf, whereas this surah places the calf first. That is not a contradiction; it is the Qur'an narrating thematically rather than chronologically, arranging the material to serve the argument of each passage.

*Wa antum tanzurun* — "while you were looking on." Note the echo. The identical phrase closed verse 50, where they watched Pharaoh's army drown. Here they watch the destruction come for themselves. The rhetorical echo is unmistakable and devastating: the same people who stood safely on the shore watching their enemies perish now stand watching themselves perish.

On the underlying theology, this verse speaks directly to a question the modern world asks constantly. Can God be seen? The mainstream position of Ahl al-Sunnah, already noted at verse 46, is that the believers will see their Lord in the Hereafter, on the basis of 75:22–23 and hadiths transmitted by so many chains that they reach mass transmission — and that no one sees Him in this life. The Qur'an states the barrier plainly in the story of Moses: the mountain could not bear it, and "vision does not perceive Him" in this world (6:103). So the demand of verse 55 was not merely presumptuous; it was asking for something that the created order cannot sustain.

Now the application, which is unusually direct. The objection "I would believe if God would just show Himself" is one of the most common refrains of the modern age, and this verse is the Qur'an's answer to it. The answer has two parts, and this surah has already supplied both. First, the demand is not really about evidence: these people had watched the sea part and an army drown, and they had made an idol within weeks. Second, the posture is wrong: "until" is not a question, it is an ultimatum. The surah's case throughout is that what is being asked for has already been given in a form appropriate to creatures — a text that can be examined, a criterion that can be applied, a creation that can be read — and that the real obstacle is not the absence of a sign but the refusal to receive the ones that have arrived.

There is a subtler version of the same demand that catches religious people rather than sceptics. "I will change when Allah changes my situation." "I will commit when I get a sign." "I will give this up once I understand why He asked it." Each of those is a *hatta* — a condition placed on God — and each of them quietly postpones obedience indefinitely. The verse's answer, compressed into a single thunderbolt, is that the terms are not ours to set.

## Verse 56

**"Then We brought you back to life after your death, so that perhaps you would be grateful."**

In simple terms, after the thunderbolt killed them, Allah raised them back to life, so that they might be grateful.

*Thumma ba'athnakum min ba'di mawtikum* — "then We raised you after your death." The verb is *ba'atha*, the same verb the Qur'an uses throughout for the Resurrection itself. That is not incidental. What happened to them was a resurrection in history — a small-scale, witnessed, bodily return from death — and it is described in exactly the vocabulary used for the great rising on the Last Day.

Theologically, this is one of the Qur'an's several demonstrations that resurrection is not a metaphor. The Qur'an does not only argue for the Resurrection by reasoning from the first creation; in several places it shows Allah actually doing it in history: the revival of these people here; the slain man of Israel brought back to life by being struck with part of the sacrificed cow (2:72–73, later in this surah); the sleepers of the cave (18:19, 18:25); the man who passed by a ruined town and was made to die for a hundred years and then raised (2:259); the birds Abraham assembled and called (2:260); and the dead raised by Jesus with Allah's permission (3:49, 5:110). Each of these is a worked example of the thing the sceptic denies.

*La'allakum tashkurun* — "so that perhaps you would be grateful." The same purpose clause as verse 52. Pardon and revival are both aimed at gratitude.

Now the uncomfortable observation that this verse forces on the reader, and it is the one the surah has been building since verse 50.

These people were given precisely what the sceptic says he needs. They did not merely hear about the afterlife. They died. They were dead. And then they were alive again, and they knew it. If there is any experience that ought to settle the question permanently, it is that one. And the surah records what came of it: ingratitude continued. Complaints about the food followed within verses (2:61). Mockery of a divine command followed (2:59). Corruption continued to be spread (2:60).

That is the most important thing this verse says to a modern reader, and it should be stated plainly because it is the answer to the most common demand of our time. The problem is never evidence. A person who says "I would believe if I experienced something" is describing a condition that has been fulfilled for an entire nation and did not hold them. What produces and sustains faith is not the volume of evidence received but the posture of the one receiving it — which is exactly what verse 26 said: the same revelation guides many and leaves many astray, and the difference is in the receiver.

There is also an individual application that is gentler and more immediately useful. Being brought back from the brink is a repeated experience in ordinary human life: the illness that receded, the accident that missed, the diagnosis that was wrong, the year that should have broken you and did not. The stated purpose of that revival is the same as theirs — *la'allakum tashkurun*. The question this verse puts to anyone who has been given their life back is whether it changed anything, or whether, like them, you simply carried on as before.

And there is one more thing, which is the ground of hope in a verse that otherwise reads as an indictment. He raised them. Even after the demand, even after the thunderbolt, even after everything that was coming, He raised them. The revival was mercy addressed to people who had not deserved it — which is the only kind of mercy there is.

## Verse 57

**"And ˹remember when˺ We shaded you with clouds and sent down to you manna and quails, ˹saying˺, “Eat from the good things We have provided for you.” The evildoers ˹certainly˺ did not wrong Us, but wronged themselves."**

In simple terms, in the desert Allah shaded them with cloud and sent down food — manna and quails — and told them to eat of the good things He provided; and the verse closes by noting that those who did wrong harmed no one but themselves.

*Wa zallalna 'alaykum al-ghamama* — "and We shaded you with cloud." The verb is from the root for shade, and the form indicates a continuous covering. The setting is the wilderness after the deliverance, where there was no shelter and no shade, and the sun was a genuine danger. Allah gave them a canopy. The same favour is recorded at 7:160 in nearly identical words. It is a small thing by comparison with a parted sea, and that is exactly what makes it worth pausing over: most of what keeps human beings alive is made of small things they have stopped noticing.

*Wa anzalna 'alaykum al-manna wa al-salwa* — "and We sent down to you manna and quails." *Mann* was a sweet substance that came down for them; *salwa* is most commonly explained as quails, a bird that came to them in abundance. Both are mentioned in 20:80 as well. The commentators noted that *mann* shares its root with the word for favour and grace — the food was itself named after the quality of the giving.

Two details about this provision are theologically loaded. First, it required no labour: no ploughing, sowing, harvesting, baking or hunting. Food simply arrived, daily. Second, the transmitted accounts indicate that they were instructed to take only what each day required and not to store it, and that what was stored spoiled — an allusion to which appears in the hadith recorded in the two Sahihs from Abu Hurayyad: "Were it not for the Children of Israel, meat would not spoil." These details are transmitted rather than stated in the Qur'an, but they fit the internal logic of the passage exactly. The provision was designed as a daily exercise in trust. Take today's portion. Do not hoard. Tomorrow it comes again.

*Kulu min tayyibati ma razaqnakum* — "eat from the good things We have provided for you." The same command is given to the believers at 2:172: "O you who believe, eat from the good things We have provided for you and be grateful to Allah." The repetition across the two communities is instructive — the terms of provision are the same for everyone: it is good, it is given, and it is to be received with gratitude rather than complaint.

*Wa ma zalamuna wa lakin kanu anfusahum yazlimun* — "and they did not wrong Us, but they were wronging themselves." This is one of the most important sentences in the Qur'an for understanding what sin is, and it is repeated throughout the Book (10:44, 30:9, 45:15). Wrongdoing does not diminish Allah. It cannot. The hadith qudsi recorded by Muslim from Abu Dharr states it in the plainest terms: "O My servants, were the first of you and the last of you, the human of you and the jinn of you, to be as wicked as the most wicked heart of any single one of you, that would not decrease My dominion in anything at all."

Two applications carry this verse into the present.

The first is about the shape of provision. The manna came daily and was not to be stored, which makes it the opposite of everything modern economic life trains us toward. Our entire system is built on accumulation, forecasting, insurance and control of the future. The wilderness provision was built on sufficiency for today and trust for tomorrow. No one is being asked to abandon prudence — the Qur'an commands tying the camel and commands preparation — but the daily rhythm of the manna is a standing reminder that security does not live in the storehouse. It lives in the Provider. The person who has provision and no trust is anxious; the person who has trust and little provision is not.

The second is the last clause, and it is the therapeutic heart of the verse. Sin is self-harm. Every act of disobedience in the Qur'an is described in this direction — not as an injury done to a wounded deity who needs vindicating, but as damage the sinner does to himself. That reframing matters enormously for how a person relates to Allah after failure. The appropriate response to sin is not to hide from an offended God but to stop hurting yourself and return to the One who was never diminished by it. Shame says "you are disgusting, so hide." This verse says "you are harming yourself, so stop." The second is what produces repentance; the first is what produces despair and concealment.

## Verse 58

**"And ˹remember˺ when We said, “Enter this city and eat freely from wherever you please; enter the gate with humility, saying, ‘Absolve us.’ We will forgive your sins and multiply the reward for the good-doers.”"**

In simple terms, they were commanded to enter a city, where they could eat freely and abundantly, to enter its gate in a posture of humility, and to ask for forgiveness — in exchange for which Allah promised to forgive their sins and to increase the reward of those who do good.

*Udkhulu hadhihi al-qaryata* — "enter this city." The city is not named here, and the commentators differed over which it was. The most commonly transmitted identifications are Jerusalem and Jericho. Both are held, and the Qur'an's deliberate silence on the name tells us the identity is not the point; the event and its terms are. Related passages speak of entering "the Holy Land which Allah has assigned to you" (5:21) and of a people of great strength within it (5:22), which fits the general region rather than settling the specific city.

*Fa-kulu minha haythu shi'tum raghadan* — "and eat from it wherever you wish, in abundance." The word *raghadan* is the same one used in verse 35 for Adam in Paradise: ease, plenty, no stinting. The echo is deliberate. As at the beginning, the pattern is abundance with one condition attached.

*Wa udkhulu al-baba sujjadan* — "and enter the gate prostrating." This is prostration *to Allah*, performed as an act of gratitude and humility at the moment of entry. It should not be confused with the prostration of honour to Adam in verse 34, which was prostration to a creature and is forbidden in the final law; this is worship of the Creator, of the same kind Muslims perform in every prayer. The instruction is about posture at the threshold: you enter the gift on your face before God, not upright and entitled.

*Wa qulu hittatun* — "and say: absolve us." The Arabic word *hittah* comes from a root meaning to put down, set down, remit. The commentators explained it as an utterance asking for the laying down of a burden — "put down from us our sins." It is a single compressed word, and it is worth noticing that the thing asked for is exactly what the word means: a weight being set down.

*Naghfir lakum khatayakum* — "We will forgive you your sins." The condition is explicit: the posture and the utterance, and the forgiveness follows. The terms could hardly be simpler — enter humbly, ask, and you are forgiven.

*Wa sa-nazidu al-muhsinin* — "and We will increase the good-doers." Note the two tiers. Forgiveness is the baseline offered to all of them on these terms. Increase — more reward, more of everything good — is promised to *al-muhsinin*, those who do *ihsan*. *Ihsan* is the word the Prophet ﷺ defined in the famous narration of Jibril as worshipping Allah as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, then knowing that He sees you. It is excellence rather than minimum compliance: doing the thing well, beautifully, with the heart in it. The verse therefore holds out two levels — the floor of forgiveness, and the open ceiling of *ihsan* — and the second is available to anyone prepared to reach for it.

Now the practical reach of this verse, which is considerable and mostly about the posture of receiving.

The terms offered here were stunningly easy: walk in, bow, say one word, and be forgiven and increased. The scandal of the next verse — and the reason this one is in the surah at all — is that they were refused. Not because they were hard, but because they were simple, and simplicity offends a person who wants to negotiate.

The contemporary versions of that refusal are easy to recognise. The terms of forgiveness in Islam are unambiguous: regret, stop, resolve, and restore what is owed to others. There is no ceremony, no intermediary, no fee, no waiting period. And yet people construct elaborate reasons why it cannot be that simple — either adding requirements God did not impose, or refusing the terms because they want a version of religion that does not require bowing. Both are refusals of the gate.

The *ihsan* clause has its own application, and it is the remedy for religious mediocrity. The difference between the forgiven and the increased is the difference between doing what is required and doing it as though you could see Him. Two people perform the identical prayer; one is at the floor and one is at the ceiling, and the difference is entirely interior. That is the most encouraging and most demanding thing in the verse: the ceiling is open to anyone, at any moment, with no permission required.

## Verse 59

**"But the wrongdoers changed the words they were commanded to say. So We sent down a punishment from the heavens upon them for their rebelliousness."**

In simple terms, those among them who did wrong substituted something else for the word they had been told to say, and a punishment came down on them from the sky because of their rebellion.

*Fa-baddala alladhina zalamu qawlan ghayra alladhi qila lahum* — "then those who wronged substituted a saying other than what had been said to them." Note the precision of the subject: *alladhina zalamu*, those who did wrong — not "they" as a whole. The Qur'an attributes the act to a subset and not to the entire people, which is a consistent discipline throughout the Book and one worth imitating. No community is condemned wholesale for the acts of some of its members.

What exactly did they substitute? The transmitted reports — and they should be cited as reports rather than as established fact — describe them altering the word mockingly, saying something like "a grain" instead of "absolve us," and entering on their backsides instead of prostrating. Whether the change was in the word, in the manner, or in both, the nature of the offence is the same and it is the point of the verse: they were given a command, and they responded to it with ridicule.

That distinction matters. This is not weakness, forgetfulness or failure to understand. It is mockery. The commanded word was simple, the reward was enormous, and they turned it into a joke. In the moral vocabulary of the Qur'an, mockery of a divine command belongs to a different and more serious category than failing to keep it. Verse 34 recorded a refusal grounded in pride; verse 14 recorded mockery of the believers; here the mockery is aimed directly at the command itself.

*Fa-anzalna 'ala alladhina zalamu rijzan mina al-sama'i* — "so We sent down upon those who wronged a punishment from the sky." *Rijz* denotes a punishment with a quality of defilement about it, and the commentators commonly explained it as a plague. The transmitted reports describe a great mortality. As with the numbers in verse 54, those details come from reports and are not stated in the Qur'an; the text establishes only that a punishment came down and that it was from the heaven.

That phrase — *mina al-sama'i*, from the sky — is the detail the surah wants you to hold onto, because of what it follows. Verse 57: "We sent down to you manna and quails." Same verb, same direction, same heaven. The sky that rained food on them now rains punishment. The source is identical; what changed is what they did with what came from it. That is a compact statement of a principle the Qur'an repeats constantly: the same revelation that is mercy to one person is destruction to another, and the difference is not in the sending but in the receiving.

*Bima kanu yafsuqun* — "because of what they were doing in rebellion." The verb form indicates settled practice. And the word is *fisq* — the same root that defined the lost in verse 27: those who break the covenant, sever what should be joined, and spread corruption. The vocabulary links this act back to the opening of the surah. Mocking a command is not a momentary lapse; in the Qur'an's taxonomy it belongs with the conduct of the *fasiqun*.

Now the applications, and there are three.

The first is about mockery, and it is the one this verse is aimed at most directly. There is a vast difference between struggling with a command and making a joke of it. The casual remark about the prayer, the shared eye-roll at a ruling, the ironic post about something sacred, the "edgy" joke in a group chat — none of these is a failure of understanding, and the verse places them in a category that is not minor. It is worth being careful here about one thing: struggling, doubting, and failing are all human and are all met with mercy in this Book. Ridicule is different in kind, and this verse is the evidence.

The second is the discipline of attribution. *Alladhina zalamu* — those who wronged, not "they." The Qur'an refuses to blame a whole people for the act of some, in a passage whose entire function is to indict. If that restraint is maintained here, there is no context in which a reader is permitted to abandon it.

The third is the sky. Whatever you are receiving — provision, health, revelation, ease — is coming from the same source that sends the consequence. Gratitude is what keeps the same heaven a place of mercy rather than a place of punishment. And this is precisely where the catalogue of favours has been heading: ten blessings in a row, and the response recorded to nearly every one of them was refusal.

## Verse 60

**"And ˹remember˺ when Moses prayed for water for his people, We said, “Strike the rock with your staff.” Then twelve springs gushed out, ˹and˺ each tribe knew its drinking place. ˹We then said,˺ “Eat and drink of Allah’s provisions, and do not go about spreading corruption in the land.”"**

In simple terms, when Moses prayed for water, Allah told him to strike a rock with his staff, and twelve springs gushed out of it — one for each tribe, so that everyone knew exactly where to drink — and they were told to eat and drink of Allah's provision and not to go about corrupting the earth.

*Wa idh istasqa Musa li-qawmihi* — "and when Moses sought water for his people." The verb is the form that means to ask for water, to pray for drink. The first thing to notice is Moses' posture: he did not organise a solution or negotiate with his people; he turned to Allah on their behalf. Intercession for the practical needs of the community is presented here as the work of a leader.

*Fa-qulna idrib bi-'asaka al-hajar* — "We said: strike the rock with your staff." By any ordinary calculation the instruction is absurd. A rock is the hardest thing at hand and a staff is a stick. The command asks for the one action that could not possibly produce water, which is the point: the water came from Allah, and the means were chosen so that no one could attribute the result to the means. The staff of Moses appears throughout his story — it becomes a serpent before Pharaoh (7:107, 20:17–21, 26:32) and it is the instrument here — and in every case the instrument exists to make the source unmistakable.

*Fa-infajarat minhu ithnatata 'ashrata 'aynan* — "and there gushed from it twelve springs." The verb depicts a forceful bursting forth. The parallel passage at 7:160 records the same event. The number twelve corresponds to the twelve tribes of Israel, and that correspondence is not incidental decoration.

*Qad 'alima kullu unasin mashrabahum* — "each group knew its drinking place." Here is the detail that reveals the wisdom behind the miracle, and it deserves emphasis because it is so often skipped. Allah did not merely produce water; He produced it in a form that eliminated the possibility of conflict over it. A single spring for twelve tribes would have produced disputes, queueing, encroachment and eventually bloodshed between relatives. Twelve springs, each identified with a specific tribe, made the question "whose water is this?" structurally impossible to ask. The miracle was not only generous; it was organised to preserve peace among people who had already shown themselves capable of almost anything.

This is a pattern worth watching for throughout the Qur'an: Allah's provision is frequently arranged in a way that removes the occasion for human conflict. The same logic appears in the laws of inheritance, of water rights, of grazing, of market conduct. The provision is not only sustenance; it is social architecture.

*Kulu wa-shrabu min rizqi Allahi* — "eat and drink from the provision of Allah." The attribution is repeated at the very moment the water is flowing from a rock they have seen struck. It would be the easiest thing in the world to credit the staff, or Moses, or the rock, or the geology. The verse forecloses all of that: this is *rizq Allah*, the provision of Allah, and the means through which it arrived changes nothing about the source.

*Wa la ta'thaw fi al-ardi mufsidin* — "and do not go about in the land spreading corruption." The prohibition is attached directly to the provision. You have been given; now do not corrupt. This is the fifth occurrence of the root *f-s-d* in this surah — at verses 11, 12, 27, 30 and now here — and the repetition is the structure of the surah's argument. Provision and prohibition travel together throughout: abundance is never given without a moral condition attached, and the condition is always the same. Do not corrupt the earth that was made for you and placed under your stewardship.

Three applications close this verse, and with it the catalogue.

The first is about attribution. Provision that arrives through visible means — a salary, a client, a harvest, a market, a government — is no less *rizq Allah* than water from a struck rock. The habit of crediting the means rather than the Source is the quietest and most widespread form of ingratitude, and this verse is a direct correction of it.

The second is about the social architecture of provision. Whenever resources are shared, the question of who gets what is the question that destroys communities. The twelve springs are a model of what good provision looks like: sufficient, and arranged so that no one needs to fight for his share. Any system, family or institution in which people must compete destructively for what they need has failed this principle, whatever else it achieves.

And the third is the closing prohibition, which is the note on which the entire catalogue of ten favours ends: you have been given everything, and the one thing asked in return is that you not corrupt the earth. That is the whole terms-of-reference of human stewardship, restated at the end of ten blessings. The surah's verdict, spelled out in the verses that follow, is that this one condition was the one they could not keep.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Verses 51 to 60 complete the catalogue of ten favours, and they do it in a way that turns the catalogue into an indictment. The calf was made from their own ornaments while Moses was on the mountain, and they were pardoned (51–52). The scripture was given as the criterion by which to distinguish truth from falsehood, and the way back from apostasy was opened (53–54). They demanded to see Allah and were struck down, then raised from the dead as a worked demonstration of the Resurrection (55–56). Cloud and food came down without labour, and they were told that wrongdoing harms no one but the wrongdoer (57). A city was offered on the simplest terms imaginable — enter humbly, ask forgiveness — and the terms were turned into a joke, so that the same heaven that rained manna rained punishment (58–59). And water burst from a rock in twelve springs, one for each tribe, arranged so that no one would need to quarrel, with a single condition attached: eat, drink, and do not spread corruption (60).

Three conclusions emerge from the whole list. First, the failure was never on the supply side: no community in the Qur'an was given more, and none responded with less. Second, the problem is not evidence but posture. These people saw the sea part, tasted death and returned, ate food that fell from the sky, and still complained, mocked and corrupted — which is why the surah's central challenge, at verse 23, is a text to be examined rather than a miracle to be demanded. And third, blessing and obligation are inseparable. Every favour in this list arrives with a condition attached, and the condition is always the same one that opened the surah's address to humanity at verse 21: worship your Lord, and do not corrupt the earth He gave you.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators genuinely differ — the identity of the city in verse 58, whether *al-furqan* is a second gift or a description, and how *uqtulu anfusakum* is to be read — the disagreement has been presented rather than asserted. Four cautions have been observed: the report that the calf's sound came from dust taken from the traces of the angel's mount is cited as transmitted material rather than as established fact; the numbers of those killed in verses 54 and 59 come from reports and are not Qur'anic data; the stories of what was substituted for *hittah* are transmitted reports rather than text; and the Qur'an's ordering of the calf and the demand to see Allah has been noted as thematic rather than chronological, since 4:153 places them in a different sequence. Verse 54 has been treated at length because it is difficult, and four things have been stated explicitly: the meaning of the command, its status as a specific rather than general ruling, the fact that no school of Islamic law has ever derived a permission to kill from it, and the legitimacy of the modern reader's discomfort with it. Finally, the divergence between the Qur'anic and Biblical accounts of Aaron's role in the making of the calf has been stated plainly, since the Qur'an deliberately exonerates him.
