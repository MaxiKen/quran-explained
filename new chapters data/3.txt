1 | Alif-Lãm-Mĩm. | The surah opens with the disconnected letters Alif, Lãm, and Mĩm. These are among the huruf al-muqatta'at, the mysterious letter combinations that appear at the beginning of twenty-nine chapters of the Quran. The precise meaning of these letters is known fully only to Allah, and this is part of the wisdom behind them. Throughout Islamic scholarship, several plausible interpretations have been offered, though none is definitive. One well-supported understanding is that these letters serve as a challenge to the Arabs of the time who prided themselves on their eloquence and mastery of the Arabic language. The Quran is composed of these very same letters—alif, lãm, mĩm, and the rest of the Arabic alphabet—yet they could not produce anything like it. This points to the miraculous nature of the revelation. The presence of these letters also captures the listener's attention right at the outset. When the recitation begins with these disjointed sounds, the ear is drawn in, and the mind is alerted that something profoundly important is about to follow. Another dimension is that these letters signal the beginning of a new surah and a new thematic unit, marking a clear break from what came before. Some scholars have also noted that in the context of Surah Al-Imran, the letters may point to some of the divine names, with the alif referring to Allah, the lãm to Latif, the Mĩm to Majid or Malik. However, the most cautious and safe approach is to say that while we can reflect on possible wisdoms, the true knowledge of what these letters signify rests with Allah alone. They remind the believer that there are matters of the unseen which the human mind cannot fully grasp, and this humility before divine knowledge is a key posture of faith. The inclusion of these letters at the start of a Medinan surah that will address deep theological questions—about the nature of God, the status of Jesus, and the reality of revelation—is fitting. It immediately establishes that this Book is not from human invention but from a source beyond human comprehension.
2 | Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him —the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. | This verse is the great declaration of tawhid, the absolute oneness of Allah, and it contains the most comprehensive description of His essential attributes. The statement "There is no god worthy of worship except Him" is the core of Islamic faith. It negates all false objects of worship—whether idols, human beings, natural forces, or inner desires—and affirms that worship, devotion, and ultimate reliance belong to Allah alone. This is not merely a theoretical claim but a complete reorientation of the human heart and life. The two names that follow explain why Allah alone deserves worship. "The Ever-Living," al-Hayy, means that His life is perfect, eternal, and self-sufficient. He does not experience sleep, fatigue, decline, or death. Every other living being derives its life from Him, and its life is temporary, fragile, and dependent. Because He is the Ever-Living, He is always present, always hearing, always aware. The believer who truly grasps this never feels alone and never despairs, knowing that the Living Lord is with them. "The All-Sustaining," al-Qayyum, comes from the root meaning to stand or to maintain. It means that Allah is self-existing and that He sustains and upholds everything else. The heavens, the earth, the angels, all creatures—everything depends on Him for its existence moment by moment. If He were to withdraw His sustaining power for an instant, the entire universe would collapse into nothingness. This combination of al-Hayy and al-Qayyum appears in the greatest verse of the Quran, Ayat al-Kursi, and is known to contain the greatest name of Allah. When a believer internalises these two names, trust in Allah becomes natural. The One who is perfectly alive and perfectly sustaining can be relied upon entirely. In the context of this surah, which will challenge the Christian belief in the divinity of Jesus, this verse lays the foundation. Jesus, however honoured, was a human being who was born, ate, slept, and passed away. He was neither al-Hayy nor al-Qayyum in the essential sense. Allah alone is.
3 | He has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book in truth, confirming what came before it, as He revealed the Torah and the Gospel | Having established who Allah is, the surah now affirms the reality of revelation, addressing the Prophet Muhammad directly. "He has revealed to you the Book in truth" means that the Quran has descended from Allah, not as a myth, a human composition, or a poetic work, but as an embodiment of truth. The word "truth," al-haqq, indicates that its content is true in what it reports about the unseen, true in its teachings about right and wrong, true in its promises and warnings, and true in its claim to be from the Lord of the worlds. This revelation did not come in a vacuum. It "confirms what came before it," meaning that the Quran is not a new, disconnected message. It is the culmination and confirmation of the same essential call delivered by earlier prophets. The Torah, revealed to Moses, and the Gospel, revealed to Jesus, are specifically mentioned. The original revelations given to these great messengers came from the same source, called to the same worship of the one God, and contained the same core truths. The Quran confirms the original, uncorrupted message that was in those scriptures. This confirmation serves multiple purposes. It validates the long line of prophethood, showing that Muhammad is not an innovator but a brother to all previous messengers. It also challenges the Jews and Christians of Arabia, who possessed these scriptures, to recognise that the Quran is consistent with the pure monotheism they find in their own books, provided they strip away later additions and distortions. The mention of the Torah and the Gospel in a Medinan surah, where the Prophet was in frequent contact with Jewish and Christian communities, is strategic. It invites them to see Islam not as a strange foreign religion but as the restoration of the faith of their own prophets. The believer today takes from this verse a deep respect for all genuine divine revelation and an understanding that the truth is one continuous stream flowing from Allah through history, reaching its final and preserved form in the Quran.
4 | previously, as a guide for people, and ˹also˺ revealed the Standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺. Surely those who reject Allah’s revelations will suffer a severe torment. For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment. | The verse continues the theme of revelation by describing its purpose and what it is accompanied by. The Torah and the Gospel were revealed "previously, as a guide for people." The original function of these scriptures was to lead humanity out of darkness into light, showing the path to Allah's pleasure and a righteous life. However, the Quran does not merely serve as a guide; Allah "also revealed the Standard," the Furqan. The Furqan is that which separates, distinguishes, and divides truth from falsehood, right from wrong, light from darkness, and the lawful from the unlawful. While earlier scriptures also contained this criterion in their pure forms, the term here often refers specifically to the Quran, or to the quality of discernment that revelation gives. The Quran enables a believer to navigate the confusions of life with clarity. Every verse, every command, every story serves as a criterion by which to judge matters. The believer does not float in a sea of moral relativism but possesses a standard granted by the Creator. After this clear mercy, the verse turns to warn: "Surely those who reject Allah's revelations will suffer a severe torment." Rejection here is not mere ignorance but deliberate denial after the truth has been made clear. The severity of the punishment matches the severity of the crime—rejecting the very words of the Creator sent for human guidance. It is a denial of the greatest gift a human can receive. The verse then grounds this warning in two of Allah's names: "For Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment." The name Aziz, translated as Almighty, combines the meanings of strength, honour, and irresistible power. No one can overpower Him, escape His grasp, or challenge His decree. He is fully capable of carrying out His warnings, and no helper or intercessor can avail anyone against His will. The believer reads this not to despair but to take seriously the call of the Quran. The same Lord who guides is also the Lord who holds to account, and a sound heart holds both hope and fear in balance.
5 | Surely nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah. | After warning of punishment for those who reject revelation, this verse establishes the foundation upon which divine judgment rests: the complete and perfect knowledge of Allah. "Nothing on earth or in the heavens is hidden from Allah." The statement is absolute and all-encompassing. It covers the smallest atom, the faintest whisper, the most secret thought lurking in the depths of a heart. The earth, with its mountains, oceans, buried treasures, hidden graves, and secret conspiracies, lies fully exposed before Him. The heavens, with their galaxies, stars, angels, and whatever unseen realms exist beyond human perception, are transparent to His knowledge. There is no corner of creation where something can escape His awareness. This attribute of divine omniscience is profoundly comforting for the believer who suffers in silence, who is wronged without witnesses, who struggles with inner pains that no other human understands. The knowledge that Allah sees, hears, and knows everything assures the believer that no good deed is lost, no tear falls unnoticed, and no injustice will ultimately go unaddressed. For the wrongdoer, however, this verse is deeply unsettling. The crimes committed in darkness, the carefully hidden plots, the secret mockery, the concealed disbelief—all of it is an open book to Allah. The very thought should awaken conscience and inspire repentance. This verse connects to the preceding warning of punishment. The torment is not arbitrary; it is based on a complete and precise record of every soul's deeds and intentions. Allah's judgment is perfect because His knowledge is perfect. There is no need for witnesses, investigations, or evidence on the Day of Judgment beyond His own all-encompassing awareness. In daily life, the believer who keeps this verse alive in the heart develops the quality of ihsan—worshipping Allah as if seeing Him, knowing that even if one does not see Him, He truly sees all.
6 | He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise. | Allah's comprehensive knowledge introduced in the previous verse is now illustrated with a profoundly intimate example: His shaping of every human being in the mother's womb. "He is the One Who shapes you in the wombs of your mothers as He wills." The act of shaping, yusawwirukum, points to a deliberate, artistic, and wise formation. Every human being is not a random collection of cells but a carefully fashioned creation. The womb is a place of darkness, hidden from all eyes, where over the course of months a single cell transforms into a fully formed infant with intricate organs, unique fingerprints, and a soul breathed into it by the Creator. This shaping happens "as He wills," meaning that Allah alone decides the features, the gender, the constitution, and the capacities of each person. Parents do not design their children; they participate in a process entirely under divine control. The mention of the womb also reminds humanity of its origin. The most powerful king, the wealthiest tycoon, the most arrogant denier—each began as a drop of fluid in a mother's womb, utterly dependent and powerless. This should humble every soul and erode the foundations of arrogance. From this sign of creation, the verse returns to the declaration of tawhid: "There is no god worthy of worship except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise." The link is clear. The One who shapes you in the womb, who knows you before you know yourself, who grants you form and faculties, is the only One deserving of worship. His exclusive right to divinity is demonstrated by His exclusive power to create and fashion. The names "Almighty" and "All-Wise" tie creation to power and wisdom. His might means nothing escapes His creative decree; His wisdom means that every shape, every feature, every capacity is given according to a perfect plan. For the believer, this verse inspires awe, gratitude, and a profound sense of worth. One is not an accident of nature but a deliberate creation of a Wise and Powerful Lord.
7 | He is the One Who has revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Book, of which some verses are precise—they are the foundation of the Book—while others are elusive. Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking ˹to spread˺ doubt through their ˹false˺ interpretations—but none grasps their ˹full˺ meaning except Allah. As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, “We believe in this ˹Quran˺—it is all from our Lord.” But none will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason. | This verse provides a crucial key to understanding the Quran's structure and the correct approach to its interpretation. It states that the Book contains two categories: "precise" verses, muhkamat, and "elusive" verses, mutashabihat. The precise verses are clear in meaning, foundational, and constitute the core teachings. They include the clear commandments, the creedal truths, the moral principles, and the unambiguous narratives. These are "the foundation of the Book," the mother of the Book, to which one always returns. The elusive verses are those whose meanings are not immediately clear to everyone, or that can bear multiple interpretations, or whose ultimate reality is known only to Allah, such as the nature of His attributes, the details of the unseen realms, and the disconnected letters at the beginning of chapters. The verse identifies a spiritual disease: "Those with deviant hearts follow the elusive verses seeking to spread doubt through their false interpretations." A heart that deviates from sincerity does not go to the precise verses for guidance. Instead, it hunts through the elusive verses to find material that can be twisted to support false ideas, create confusion among the believers, or cast doubt on the faith. This was a tactic used by some Christians who tried to use Quranic verses about Jesus to justify his divinity, and by hypocrites who sought to undermine certainty. The verse then clarifies that "none grasps their full meaning except Allah." Some pause here, while others connect what follows: "As for those well-grounded in knowledge, they say, 'We believe in this Quran—it is all from our Lord.'" Both readings carry wisdom. The well-grounded scholars do not obsess over what they cannot fully comprehend. They submit their intellects to the divine source, confident that whatever Allah intended is the truth, even if their minds cannot fully grasp it. This teaches intellectual humility. The pursuit of knowledge is noble, but the limits of human understanding must be acknowledged. Only "people of reason," ulu al-albab, who use their intellects properly and recognise their boundaries, will internalise this lesson.
8 | ˹They say,˺ “Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us. Grant us Your mercy. You are indeed the Giver ˹of all bounties˺.” | This verse captures the immediate response of the people of reason, the well-grounded in knowledge, upon hearing the description of deviant hearts in the previous verse. Their reaction is not pride in their own understanding but a humble and urgent supplication to Allah. The prayer begins, "Our Lord! Do not let our hearts deviate after you have guided us." This is one of the most profound supplications in the Quran. It reveals that guidance is not a one-time event that a person owns and cannot lose. The heart is constantly turning, and it is Allah alone who keeps it firm. The Prophet Muhammad, the most guided of all human beings, frequently used to supplicate, "O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion." When asked about this, he explained that the heart is between two fingers of the Most Merciful, and He turns it as He wills. The well-grounded scholars know that knowledge alone does not protect against deviation. The deviant hearts mentioned earlier also had knowledge, but their inner corruption led them astray. So the believer turns to the source of steadfastness. The prayer continues, "Grant us Your mercy." After asking for firmness, they ask for mercy. Firmness without mercy is mere rigidity; mercy without firmness can become laxity. Together they create a balanced, upright spiritual state. Mercy here encompasses forgiveness of past sins, assistance in the present, and protection from future error. The supplication concludes with the acknowledgment: "You are indeed the Giver of all bounties." Al-Wahhab, the Giver, is the One who bestows gifts continuously and without any expectation of return. Guidance, firmness, mercy—these are not earned by human effort alone. They are pure gifts from Allah. The believer who truly understands the nature of the heart's vulnerability will never feel secure in their own piety. This verse teaches the believer to live in a constant state of supplication, never taking guidance for granted, and always seeking refuge in the One who bestows without limit.
9 | Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the ˹promised˺ Day—about which there is no doubt. Surely Allah does not break His promise. | The supplication of the well-grounded continues by anchoring their entire worldview in the reality of the Day of Judgment. "Our Lord! You will certainly gather all humanity for the promised Day—about which there is no doubt." The gathering, al-jam', is a comprehensive and inescapable assembly. Every human being who has ever lived, from Adam to the last soul, will be brought together on a single plain before their Lord. There is no exception, no escape, no evasion. The phrase "about which there is no doubt" emphasises the absolute certainty of this event. In the human experience, almost everything carries some degree of uncertainty about the future. But this Day, because it is promised by the Truthful One, is as certain as if it had already occurred. The believers mention the Day of Gathering in their supplication because it is the ultimate context that gives meaning to their request for steadfastness and mercy. They want firm hearts now so that they may stand with security on that Day. They ask for mercy now so that they may receive the full mercy then. The statement "Surely Allah does not break His promise" is the rational and spiritual ground of their confidence. Allah's promise is not like human promises, which are vulnerable to forgetfulness, inability, or change of intention. His promise is an expression of His perfect truthfulness, and His power ensures its fulfilment. By ending the supplication this way, they affirm that they are not merely hoping for the best; they are relying on the unbreakable word of the Lord of the worlds. For the believer reading this, the verse connects the inner life of the heart with the grand cosmic events of the future. The fear of deviation, the longing for mercy, and the certainty of judgment form an interconnected spiritual awareness. One cannot truly pray for steadfastness without a living consciousness of the Day when all will stand before their Lord and account for how they responded to His guidance.
10 | Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah—and they will be the fuel for the Fire. | From the sincere supplication of the believers, the surah now turns to the stark reality of those who reject faith. It addresses a deep-seated assumption that humans often hold: that worldly resources signify divine favour and can serve as a shield against misfortune. The verse shatters this assumption. "Neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah." Wealth, which humans spend their lives accumulating and upon which they pin their hopes of security, will be utterly useless on the Day of Judgment. It cannot purchase a reprieve, it cannot bribe the recording angels, it cannot buy a cool breeze against the heat of the Fire. Children, whom people see as their legacy, their strength, and their support in old age, will similarly be of no avail. A person may take pride in the number of their offspring, thinking that a large family is a sign of worldly success and a protection. But on that Day, the mother will forget her nursing child, and every soul will be occupied with its own terrifying concern. The phrase "against Allah" means that these things cannot defend a person from Allah's judgment, cannot avert His punishment, and cannot serve as a ransom. What a human needs in that moment is a sound heart, faith, and righteous deeds—none of which wealth or children can provide if one has disbelieved. The verse then delivers the chilling conclusion: "and they will be the fuel for the Fire." The word "fuel," waqud, is the same word used for the kindling that feeds a flame. It creates a terrifying image: the disbelievers themselves are the material that keeps the Fire burning. Their bodies, their wealth that they hoarded, their pride in their lineage—all of it becomes nourishment for the flames. This is not a punishment imposed from outside but a direct consequence of a life lived in rejection of the Creator. The verse serves as a profound warning, redirecting human ambition from the accumulation of worldly assets to the investment in faith and righteousness, which alone can benefit a person when every worldly prop is stripped away.
11 | Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them —they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins. And Allah is severe in punishment. | The warning of the previous verse is now reinforced through historical precedent. The surah tells the believers and warns the disbelievers that what they face is not new. "Their fate will be like that of the people of Pharaoh and those before them." The people of Pharaoh are among the most frequently mentioned communities in the Quran because their story serves as an enduring archetype of wealth, power, and technological achievement combined with arrogant rejection of the truth. Pharaoh possessed a mighty kingdom, great armies, monumental architecture, and control over the resources of Egypt. Yet when he rejected the signs brought by Moses and claimed divinity for himself, all his resources could not save him from a humiliating end. The sea swallowed him and his hosts, and his preserved body became a sign for later generations. "Those before them" refers to earlier nations such as the people of Noah, Aad, Thamud, and others who also rejected the messengers sent to them. The common thread among all of them is stated clearly: "they all rejected Our signs, so Allah seized them for their sins." The rejection of divine signs—whether those signs were clear miracles, sound arguments, or the natural proofs of Allah's existence and oneness—is the fundamental crime. The seizure, akhdh, implies a sudden, decisive, and inescapable grip. When Allah seizes, there is no escape, no rescue, no second chance in that moment. The historical ruins of these nations, some of which the Arabs passed by in their trade journeys, stood as silent witnesses to this truth. The verse concludes, "And Allah is severe in punishment." Severity here is not cruelty; it is the appropriate, just response to sustained, arrogant rebellion against the Creator who provided every blessing. The purpose of mentioning historical examples is not to gloat over destruction but to awaken the living. The believer reads these stories and takes heed, knowing that the laws of Allah do not change and that what befell past nations can befall any who repeat their behaviour.
12 | ˹O Prophet!˺ Tell the disbelievers, “Soon you will be overpowered and driven to Hell—what an evil place to rest!” | This verse contains a direct instruction to the Prophet Muhammad to deliver a clear warning to the disbelievers. It combines a prophecy of worldly defeat with a warning of otherworldly punishment. "Soon you will be overpowered" refers to an imminent military defeat at the hands of the believers. In the context of the surah's revelation in Medina, this was a powerful statement. The Muslims were a small, often embattled community, facing the much larger and wealthier Quraysh of Mecca and the hostile Jewish tribes of Medina. A prediction that the disbelievers would be overpowered would have seemed laughable to the arrogant leaders of Quraysh. Yet the prophecy was fulfilled. The Quraysh were defeated at Badr and then progressively lost their power until Mecca itself was conquered peacefully. This fulfilment served as a sign of the truth of the Quran. The second part of the warning is even more dire: "and driven to Hell—what an evil place to rest!" The word "driven," yuhsharun, evokes the image of being herded, of being gathered and pushed forward without dignity or choice. The disbelievers who lived in arrogance and exercised power over others will find themselves helpless, driven like cattle toward the Fire. The final phrase, "what an evil place to rest," carries a tone of tragic irony. A place of rest, mihad, is normally a bed or a comforting abode where one seeks peace after exertion. But Hell is the opposite—a place of ceaseless torment that provides no rest, no peace, no relief. For the believer, this verse provides confidence. The worldly dominance of those who oppose the truth is temporary. Their apparent strength is an illusion; the outcome is already decreed by the Lord of history. The verse also reminds the believer of the urgency of delivering the message. The Prophet is commanded to tell them, and by extension, the believers are entrusted with conveying the truth clearly, even as the ultimate outcome rests with Allah.
13 | Indeed, there was a sign for you in the two armies that met in battle— one fighting for the cause of Allah and the other in denial. The believers saw their enemy twice their number. But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills. Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight. | The surah now turns to a recent, tangible sign that the believers could witness with their own eyes: the Battle of Badr. "Indeed, there was a sign for you in the two armies that met in battle." The battle is presented not merely as a military event but as a divine sign, an ayah, containing lessons for those who reflect. "One fighting for the cause of Allah and the other in denial" distinguishes the two sides by their motivations, not their worldly status. The believers fought for a transcendent purpose—to establish the word of Allah, to defend the right to worship Him alone. The disbelievers fought out of tribal pride, protection of their idolatrous traditions, and economic interests. This distinction matters because the outcome is tied to the cause, not merely the numbers. "The believers saw their enemy twice their number." At Badr, the Muslims numbered just over three hundred men, poorly equipped, with only a few horses. The Quraysh army was around a thousand strong, well-armed, and confident of an easy victory. By all material calculations, the Muslims should have been annihilated. Yet they saw their enemy as twice their number because Allah reduced the enemy's strength in their eyes—a psychological support that bolstered their hearts. The verse then gives the eternal principle: "But Allah supports with His victory whoever He wills." Victory is not a function of material strength, numerical superiority, or advanced weaponry. It is a gift from Allah, granted to whomever He wills according to His wisdom. The lesson is explicit: "Surely in this is a lesson for people of insight." Insight, basar, is more than eyesight; it is the ability to see beyond the surface of events to their deeper spiritual significance. A person of insight looks at Badr and understands that divine help is real, that faith and obedience are more powerful than numbers, and that the outcome of any conflict lies in the hand of Allah. This lesson transcends time. Every believer facing overwhelming odds can draw strength from this verse, knowing that the same Lord who granted victory at Badr is capable of granting victory today.
14 | The enjoyment of ˹worldly˺ desires—women, children, treasures of gold and silver, fine horses, cattle, and fertile land—has been made appealing to people. These are the pleasures of this worldly life, but with Allah is the finest destination. | After describing the Battle of Badr and the distinction between those who fight for Allah and those who fight in denial, the surah shifts to an analysis of what motivates human beings. It exposes the nature of worldly attachments and redirects the believer's focus. "The enjoyment of worldly desires has been made appealing to people." The Arabic term zuyyina implies that these desires have been beautified, made to appear glittering and irresistible to the human soul. The verse then catalogues the primary objects of human desire. "Women" is mentioned first, as the natural inclination toward the opposite sex is one of the most powerful drives in human life. "Children" represent the desire for legacy, continuity, and the love that fills a parent's heart. "Treasures of gold and silver" encompass wealth in its accumulated form, the sense of security and power that money provides. "Fine horses" represented the height of transportation, military strength, and status display in the ancient world—the equivalent today of luxury vehicles and advanced technology. "Cattle" represented productive wealth, the livestock that provided food, clothing, and trade goods. "Fertile land" symbolised real estate, agricultural bounty, and rooted prosperity. The list covers the full spectrum of worldly enjoyments. The verse then puts all of this in perspective: "These are the pleasures of this worldly life." The word "pleasures," mata', denotes something temporary, a provision for a journey, not the destination itself. The worldly life is the place of this mata', not the place of ultimate fulfilment. The verse does not condemn these things as evil in themselves; they are provisions. But attachment to them as ultimate goals is a delusion. The contrast is drawn sharply: "but with Allah is the finest destination." Husn al-ma'ab, the beauty of the final return, is incomparably better. Paradise is not merely a place of more intense versions of these pleasures; it is a qualitatively different reality where there is no fear, no sadness, no depletion, and above all, the pleasure of Allah. The believer uses the lawful enjoyments of this world as a means to reach that destination, not as an end in themselves.
15 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall I inform you of what is better than ˹all of˺ this? Those mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever, and pure spouses, along with Allah’s pleasure.” And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants, | Having listed the beautified desires of this world, the verse now presents a divine offer. Allah commands the Prophet to ask, "Shall I inform you of what is better than all of this?" The question is an invitation, a call to the human heart that naturally yearns for what is good and beautiful. The word "better," khayr, implies a comparison that is overwhelmingly in favour of what is to come. There is no denial that the worldly items mentioned have their appeal, but something infinitely more excellent is available. The description that follows is layered. "Those mindful of Allah," the muttaqun, are those who live with a constant awareness of their Lord, who guard themselves from what displeases Him, and who orient their lives around His commands. For them, the reward is "Gardens with their Lord under which rivers flow, to stay there forever." The gardens are not distant from Allah; they are with their Lord, in His proximity, under His honour. The flowing rivers are a symbol of life, purity, and ceaseless refreshment, a stark contrast to the arid deserts of Arabia where water was the most precious resource. The promise of eternity, "to stay there forever," means there is no fear of loss, no expiration date on joy, no boredom, and no decline. Then mention is made of "pure spouses," azwaj mutahharah. These are spouses purified from everything that troubles human relationships in this world—free from physical impurities, emotional hurts, jealousy, and the imperfections that mar earthly unions. Then comes the pinnacle: "along with Allah's pleasure." Ridwan, divine pleasure, is the greatest reward of all. A believer might enjoy all the other blessings, but if they sense that Allah is displeased, there is no true joy. Conversely, to know that Allah is pleased is the ultimate bliss. The verse concludes by reminding that "Allah is All-Seeing of His servants." He sees the inner struggles, the sacrifices made to remain mindful, the temptations resisted, and the sincere longing for His pleasure. This awareness encourages the believer to strive, knowing nothing is overlooked.
16 | who pray, “Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive our sins and protect us from the torment of the Fire.” | The description of the mindful continues by giving voice to their constant supplication. The muttaqun, introduced in the previous verse as those who will receive the magnificent reward, are characterised by a specific prayer that flows from their hearts. They say, "Our Lord! We have believed, so forgive our sins and protect us from the torment of the Fire." This is a remarkable prayer in its structure and its spirit. The statement "We have believed" is not a boast or a claim of perfection. It is an affirmation of faith that serves as the basis upon which they then request forgiveness. They present their belief to Allah not as a merit that entitles them to reward, but as a reason for them to seek His mercy. They understand that faith itself is a gift from Him, and they humbly ask that He complete His favour by forgiving the sins that inevitably accompany even a believing life. The connection between faith and seeking forgiveness is crucial. True faith produces a heightened sensitivity to one's own shortcomings. The more a believer knows of Allah's majesty and their own weakness, the more they seek forgiveness. The Prophet Muhammad, whose past and future sins were forgiven, used to seek forgiveness more than seventy times a day. The prayer also shows a balanced spiritual state. The believers affirm their faith—there is no despair, no denial of the grace they have received—yet they also acknowledge their sins and fear the Fire. This stands between the two extremes of smug self-righteousness and hopeless despair. "Protect us from the torment of the Fire" expresses a healthy fear that motivates action. The mindful do not presume upon Allah's mercy while persisting in heedlessness. They actively seek protection from the Fire, and this seeking is itself a form of worship and a sign of true mindfulness. For the believer today, this verse provides a model supplication that can be recited daily, a constant reminder to tie one's faith to the humble request for forgiveness and protection, never taking either for granted.
17 | ˹It is they˺ who are patient, sincere, obedient, and charitable, and who pray for forgiveness before dawn. | The portrait of the mindful continues, now describing their observable qualities—the fruit of the faith and supplication mentioned in the previous verse. These are "the patient," al-sabirin. Patience here is a comprehensive quality. It includes patience in obeying Allah, patience in refraining from what He forbids, and patience in the face of trials and hardships. Life is filled with tests, and the mindful person does not crumble or complain excessively. They hold steady, knowing that Allah's promise is true and that ease follows hardship. "The sincere," al-sadiqin, are those whose inward reality matches their outward actions. They are truthful in their speech, honest in their dealings, and genuine in their faith. Sincerity rejects hypocrisy in all its forms. A sincere person says what they believe and does what they say, living with integrity. "The obedient," al-qanitin, are those who submit with devotion. The term carries a sense of continuous, humble obedience, a standing before Allah in worship with a quiet and devoted heart. It is not the obedience of resentment but of love and reverence. "The charitable," al-munfiqin, are those who spend from what Allah has given them for His cause. Charity is a practical demonstration that the heart is free from the grip of material attachments. The mindful do not merely love with words but give from their wealth. Finally, "and who pray for forgiveness before dawn," al-mustaghfirina bi al-ashar. The time just before dawn, the sahar, is the last part of the night when the world sleeps and the devoted soul rises to commune with its Lord. This is the time of the night prayer, tahajjud, and the pre-dawn meal before fasting. It is a moment of special divine proximity, when Allah descends to the lowest heaven, asking who seeks forgiveness that He may forgive them. The mindful do not sleep through this precious time; they seek it out, finding in its stillness a sweetness of worship that the day's busyness cannot offer. Together, these five qualities form a complete spiritual and moral character, showing that mindfulness is not an abstract state but a lived reality.
18 | Allah ˹Himself˺ is a Witness that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—and so are the angels and people of knowledge. He is the Maintainer of justice. There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him—the Almighty, All-Wise. | This majestic verse presents the most profound testimony to divine unity ever uttered. "Allah Himself is a Witness that there is no god worthy of worship except Him." This is extraordinary. When a truth is presented in a court, witnesses are called to testify. Here, Allah Himself, the Supreme Being, bears witness to His own oneness. No higher authority exists to confirm this truth. His testimony is the ultimate proof, for He is the All-Knowing, the Truthful, and He knows His own essence and attributes perfectly. His testimony is not based on inference or evidence external to Himself; it is the direct declaration of the One who is the reality behind all existence. The testimony is then joined: "and so are the angels and people of knowledge." The angels, despite their closeness to Allah, their purity, and their knowledge of the unseen, testify to His oneness. Their worship—their bowing, prostrating, glorifying, and obeying—is a continuous testimony. The "people of knowledge," ulu al-ilm, are those endowed with true understanding. Their study of creation, revelation, and the human soul leads them to the inescapable conclusion that there is no deity but Allah. This elevates the status of true scholars in Islam. Their knowledge is not merely academic; it is a witness-bearing activity that aligns them with the angels and with Allah's own testimony. "He is the Maintainer of justice," qa'iman bi al-qist. Allah upholds justice in His being, His actions, and His decrees. His oneness ensures ultimate justice, for if there were multiple gods, conflict and imbalance would ensue. The verse then repeats the declaration "There is no god worthy of worship except Him" and adds the names "the Almighty, All-Wise." The repetition reinforces the central truth, and the names ground it in His irresistible power and perfect wisdom. For the believer, this verse is a wellspring of faith. It connects one's own testimony of faith, the shahada, to the grandest cosmic testimony. When a believer says "la ilaha illa Allah," they are joining a chorus that includes Allah's own witness, the angels, and the people of knowledge across time.
19 | Certainly, Allah’s only Way is Islam. Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute ˹among themselves˺ out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them. Whoever denies Allah’s signs, then surely Allah is swift in reckoning. | With the testimony of divine oneness firmly established, the verse now declares the name and nature of the path that leads to that One. "Certainly, Allah's only Way is Islam." The word "Islam" here is not merely the name of a religion that began with Prophet Muhammad. It refers to the act of submission to Allah, a state of being in which the entire self—heart, mind, and limbs—is surrendered to the will of the Creator. This has been the core message of every prophet from Adam onward. In this sense, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus were all Muslims in that they submitted their wills to Allah. The verse asserts that this submission is the only acceptable way to Allah. Any path that refuses to submit, that reserves part of the self for ego, desire, or other deities, is not the way that leads to Allah's pleasure. "Those who were given the Scripture did not dispute among themselves out of mutual envy until knowledge came to them." This sentence diagnoses a tragic spiritual disease. The People of the Book originally received clear knowledge of the truth. They knew that Allah is one and that submission to Him is the path. But after this knowledge came to them, they did not simply accept it and unite. Instead, they fell into disputes, divisions, and sects. The driving force behind this division was baghy—mutual envy, transgression, and the desire for power and status. Knowledge that should have united them became the occasion for rivalry and fragmentation. This serves as a warning to the Muslim community as well: possessing knowledge of the truth is not enough; it must be accompanied by humility and sincerity. "Whoever denies Allah's signs, then surely Allah is swift in reckoning." Denial after knowledge is a grave matter. The swiftness of Allah's reckoning is both a warning and a comfort. It means that the consequences of actions are certain and inescapable, but also that the believers should not be impatient; the judgment will come at its appointed time and will be swift and decisive.
20 | So if they argue with you ˹O Prophet˺, say, “I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers.” And ask those who were given the Scripture and the illiterate ˹people˺, “Have you submitted yourselves ˹to Allah˺?” If they submit, they will be ˹rightly˺ guided. But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah is All-Seeing of ˹His˺ servants. | The surah now provides the Prophet with the precise response to use when those who dispute with him engage in argument. "So if they argue with you, say, 'I have submitted myself to Allah, and so have my followers.'" The command is to respond not with angry polemic but with a clear declaration of one's own position. The Prophet and those who follow him have made a decisive choice: they have surrendered their entire beings to Allah. This declaration is not merely defensive; it is an invitation wrapped in testimony. By clearly stating his stance, the Prophet presents Islam as a lived reality. The verse then instructs him to turn the question back to the disputants: "And ask those who were given the Scripture and the illiterate people, 'Have you submitted yourselves to Allah?'" The two groups addressed are the People of the Book—Jews and Christians—and the ummiyyun, the unlettered ones, referring to the Arab polytheists who had no scripture. The call is universal. No one is excluded from the invitation to submit to their Creator. The question is direct and personal, forcing a response. It is not an abstract theological debate; it is a call to make a commitment. "If they submit, they will be rightly guided." The outcome of submission is clear and immediate: guidance. There is no long process, no waiting period. The moment a person surrenders to Allah, they are on the path of guidance. The purpose of argument is not to score points but to bring people to this submission. "But if they turn away, then your duty is only to deliver the message." This is a profound relief and a clear delineation of responsibility. The Prophet is not responsible for the choices of others. He cannot force hearts to submit; only Allah can guide. His duty is to deliver the message clearly and completely. Once that is done, the matter rests with Allah, who is "All-Seeing of His servants." He sees who accepts and who rejects, and He will deal with each accordingly. For all believers, this verse provides a model for conveying the truth: clarity, conviction, humility, and the recognition that ultimate guidance is Allah's domain.
21 | Indeed, those who deny Allah’s signs, kill the prophets unjustly, and kill people who stand up for justice—give them good news of a painful punishment. | After the Prophet is instructed to deliver the message and leave the outcome to Allah, the surah now describes the ultimate fate of those who not only reject the truth but actively wage war against it. The verse addresses “those who deny Allah’s signs,” meaning they persistently reject the revelations that come with clear proofs, whether the verses of the Quran or the earlier scriptures. Their denial is not born of ignorance but of arrogance. The next phrase, “kill the prophets unjustly,” points to a historical reality and a spiritual crime of the highest order. Several Israelite prophets were murdered by their own people when they delivered the message of God, such as Zechariah and John the Baptist according to some narrations, though the Quran states they were killed unjustly. Killing a prophet is an act of defiance against the Creator who sent him. Then the verse adds, “and kill people who stand up for justice.” These are the righteous souls who enjoin what is right and command fairness, even in the face of tyranny. In the immediate Medinan context, this alluded to the Jewish tribes who had a history of such transgressions, and who were now opposing the Prophet Muhammad and his followers who called to justice. The command “give them good news of a painful punishment” uses irony to intensify the warning. Good news is normally for something joyful, but here it is a torment that awaits them. This is a severe rhetorical device that shakes the listener. For the believer, this verse establishes the sanctity of prophetic authority and the duty to speak for justice, knowing that the highest human crime is to silence the voice of truth. It also provides comfort: those who are killed for standing for justice are honoured, and their oppressors will face an inescapable reckoning. The painful punishment is not merely physical but also the spiritual agony of being forever separated from Allah’s mercy, a consequence that fits the magnitude of their crimes.
22 | They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter. And they will have no helpers. | Having identified the heinous crimes of denying signs, killing prophets, and killing those who enjoin justice, the surah now pronounces the complete bankruptcy of such people. “They are the ones whose deeds are wasted in this world and the Hereafter.” The word “wasted,” habita, carries the sense of something becoming null, void, and utterly fruitless. Any good deed they might have done—charity, maintaining family ties, acts of bravery, or even some forms of worship—is rendered worthless because the foundation of faith is missing and because their evil actions have eclipsed everything else. In this world, their deeds yield no lasting benefit; their plots eventually fail, their legacy becomes one of infamy, and whatever they built crumbles. In the Hereafter, there will be no reward, no lightening of punishment, and no credit in their account. The verse then states, “And they will have no helpers.” On the Day of Judgment, every human relationship will be severed. Those who relied on their wealth, their armies, their influential allies, or their tribal connections will find themselves utterly alone. No intercessor can help them without Allah’s permission, and no permission will be granted for those who committed such enormities without repentance. This verse serves as a powerful corrective to the human tendency to evaluate success by outward achievements. A person may appear to prosper in this life despite their corruption, but the verse declares that their deeds are wasted even in this world in terms of real, eternal value. The absence of helpers is a direct consequence of their severing of ties with Allah. Since they rejected the only true Helper and Guardian, they are left with no one when they need help the most. The believer reflects on this and understands that without sincere faith and adherence to justice, all worldly accomplishments are like ashes blown away by the wind on a stormy day.
23 | Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures? Yet when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes, some of them turn away heedlessly. | The attention now shifts to a specific group whose behaviour illustrates the earlier themes of denying signs and rejecting justice. “Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scriptures?” The address draws the listener to ponder the state of certain Jewish scholars and leaders in Medina. The phrase “a portion of the Scriptures” indicates that they had received the Torah, with its clear laws and guidance, but they did not uphold it in its entirety; they took the parts that suited them and abandoned the rest. This selective adherence is a grave spiritual flaw. The situation described is that “when they are invited to the Book of Allah to settle their disputes, some of them turn away heedlessly.” The Book of Allah here can refer to the Torah itself, to which they were invited to judge according to its rulings, or to the Quran, which the Prophet recited as the final arbiter. Historical reports mention an incident where the Prophet was asked to judge between two Jewish groups regarding a case of adultery. They wanted a lighter punishment than the stoning prescribed in the Torah. When the Prophet asked them to bring the Torah and read it, the truth became clear, but some still turned away, not wanting the full law to be applied. Their turning away was not due to doubt about the authenticity of the Book, but because following it would force them to submit to a ruling against their desires. “Heedlessly” describes the state of their hearts—they are not considering the consequences, they are ignoring the truth as if it does not matter. This verse warns all people of scripture and knowledge never to approach Allah’s book selectively. True faith requires complete submission, even when the ruling is against one’s own interests.
24 | This is because they say, “The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.” They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying. | The verse now diagnoses the root cause of the heedless turning away mentioned previously. “This is because they say, ‘The Fire will not touch us except for a few days.’” Some among the Jews held a belief that even if they were punished for their sins, their time in Hell would be limited to a short period—often cited as forty days, corresponding to the period they had worshipped the golden calf, or just a few days, after which they would be admitted to Paradise because they were the chosen people. This claim had no basis in any authentic revelation from God; it was a comforting fabrication. The Quran exposes this as a delusion: “They have been deceived in their faith by their wishful lying.” The phrase “wishful lying” translates the Arabic ma kanu yaftarun, which implies they were forging falsehoods and then believing them because they aligned with their desires. This is a profound spiritual illness. A person can convince themselves of a merciful outcome without any warrant from God, and that false hope makes them lax in obedience and bold in transgression. They then turn away from the truth because it disturbs their comfortable illusion. The verse warns every believer against wishful thinking in religion. Salvation is not a birthright; it requires sincere faith and righteous deeds. The idea that a particular identity, lineage, or affiliation guarantees immunity from punishment is a satanic trap. True faith does not rest on self-invented assurances but on the clear promises and threats of the Quran, balanced with hope in Allah’s mercy and fear of His justice. The verse dismantles the complacency of those who treat religion as a cultural inheritance rather than a living commitment of the heart.
25 | But how ˹horrible˺ will it be when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt—when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged! | After exposing their false hopes, the verse shatters all illusions by directing attention to the terrifying reality that awaits. “But how horrible will it be when We gather them together on the Day about which there is no doubt.” The interrogative “how” carries a tone of dreadful amazement, inviting the listener to imagine the shock and horror of those who lived on wishful thinking when they finally face the undeniable truth. The Day of Gathering is certain; there is no doubt about its coming. All humanity will be assembled, including those who denied it or trivialised its consequences. The scene is one of absolute helplessness. Then the principle of perfect justice is stated: “when every soul will be paid in full for what it has done.” The word “paid,” tuwaffa, means that every deed, small or large, open or hidden, will be recompensed exactly. Nothing will be overlooked. The liar who claimed a few days of punishment will see the true record of their deeds and the punishment they had denied. The phrase “and none will be wronged” assures that there will be no injustice whatsoever. Those who expected a light punishment will receive what they earned; no more, but certainly no less. The verse is a call to wake up from the slumber of false security. It urges the believer to prepare for that Day with deeds that can stand its scrutiny, rather than relying on baseless traditions or cultural assumptions. For those who wronged others, it is a warning that every right will be restored to its owner, and no one will escape accountability. The terrible nature of that Day for the disbelievers is matched only by the justice and mercy it represents for the believers who lived in sincere submission.
26 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O Allah! Lord over all authorities! You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please; You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please—all good is in Your Hands. Surely You ˹alone˺ are Most Capable of everything.” | In response to the challenges faced by the believers from the powerful and arrogant, Allah instructs the Prophet to turn to Him with this profound supplication. “O Allah! Lord over all authorities!” The address, “Malik al-Mulk,” means Owner of all sovereignty and kingdom. Every dominion, every throne, every power on earth or in the heavens belongs to Allah alone. No ruler truly owns their authority; they are merely temporary trustees. Then the supplication continues: “You give authority to whoever You please and remove it from who You please.” This is a statement of absolute divine control over political fortunes. The rise and fall of nations, empires, and leaders are not the results of mere human effort or historical accident; they are decreed by Allah’s will. The believers, who were a small and oppressed community, are reminded that the current dominance of the Quraysh or the Persians and Romans is not permanent. Allah can strip it away and grant it to the weak. This verse is said to have been revealed as a promise that the Muslims would eventually inherit the empires of Persia and Rome, a promise that materialised within a generation. “You honour whoever You please and disgrace who You please.” Honour, ‘izzah, comes from Allah, not from wealth, lineage, or worldly power. A person may be despised by society but be honoured in the sight of Allah. Conversely, the haughty tyrant may be disgraced in ways no one could foresee. “All good is in Your Hands” teaches that all goodness—including authority and honour—comes solely from Him; evil is not attributed to Him. The supplication concludes: “Surely You alone are Most Capable of everything.” This affirms His omnipotence as the ground for all that was said. For the believer, this prayer is a source of comfort and a means of seeking the best of both worlds. It also instils humility in leaders and hope in the oppressed.
27 | You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night. You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living. And You provide for whoever You will without limit. | The supplication continues, praising Allah through the signs of His power in the universe. “You cause the night to pass into the day and the day into the night.” This is the daily miracle that humans often overlook. The seamless merging of light and darkness, the gradual increase and decrease of the night and day through the seasons, is a sign of a perfectly regulated creation under a sovereign will. The One who manages this cosmic rhythm is the One who controls the rise and fall of kingdoms mentioned in the previous verse. Then the verse moves to another sign: “You bring forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living.” This expresses a law that pervades existence. A living plant springs from a seemingly dead seed. A chick emerges from an egg. A believer can be born from disbelieving parents, and a disbelieving child from believing parents. Civilizations rise from decay and then decay again. Resurrection from the grave is the ultimate manifestation of this principle. This assures the heart that Allah can bring life to a dead situation—whether it be a barren land, a hopeless circumstance, or a community facing extinction. “And You provide for whoever You will without limit.” “Without limit,” bighayri hisab, means without measure, without reckoning, and without expecting anything in return. Allah’s provision is vast and can flow to whom He wills, regardless of human calculations. A poor person can become rich overnight, and a rich person can lose everything. The believer, reflecting on these three signs, learns to trust that the same Lord who alternates night and day, brings out the living from the dead, and provides without measure, can easily grant victory, honour, and sustenance to His sincere servants, just as He can strip them from the arrogant.
28 | Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers—and whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah—unless it is a precaution against their tyranny. And Allah warns you about Himself. And to Allah is the final return. | After establishing that all authority and honour belong to Allah, the surah gives a crucial directive that shapes the social and political identity of the Muslim community. “Believers should not take disbelievers as guardians instead of the believers.” The word “guardians,” awliya’, refers to intimate allies, protectors, and confidants to whom one entrusts one’s secrets and upon whom one relies for support in a way that compromises faith or community loyalty. This does not forbid fair dealing, kindness, or just coexistence, as clarified elsewhere; it prohibits a relationship of dependency that blurs the boundaries of faith. In Medina, some Muslims were inclined to maintain close alliances with certain Jewish tribes or with the hypocrites, even when such alliances threatened the unity and security of the believers. The verse warns that “whoever does so will have nothing to hope for from Allah”—he cuts himself off from divine assistance and guidance. However, there is a crucial exception: “unless it is a precaution against their tyranny.” This allows for outward conformity or friendly speech when a believer fears persecution or harm, provided the heart remains firm in faith. This is the basis of the concession known as taqiyyah in dire situations, as practiced by the believer in Pharaoh’s court. The verse then adds, “And Allah warns you about Himself.” This is a profound statement meaning that Allah cautions you regarding His own punishment and His right to hold you accountable. It is a reminder not to take His commands lightly. “And to Allah is the final return” anchors the entire matter in the Hereafter, where hidden intentions will be judged. For the believer today, this verse calls for a careful examination of where one places one’s ultimate trust and loyalty, ensuring that faith takes precedence over worldly alliances.
29 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah. For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And Allah is Most Capable of everything.” | This verse deepens the instruction on alliances by reminding everyone that outward compliance or concealment does not escape divine scrutiny. The Prophet is told to say, “Whether you conceal what is in your hearts or reveal it, it is known to Allah.” The heart is the true locus of loyalty and faith. A person may outwardly dissociate from disbelievers while harbouring love and reliance on them in their heart, or they may show friendliness under coercion while remaining true in their heart. Allah knows the reality in both cases. The verse establishes the principle that sincerity is measured by the heart’s condition, and no act of concealment can hide one’s true state from the Creator. “For He knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.” His knowledge is not limited to human hearts; it encompasses every atom in the entire cosmos. The movement of the galaxies, the thoughts of an ant, the secret conspiracies of kings—all are an open book to Him. This vast knowledge is then linked to His power: “And Allah is Most Capable of everything.” He not only knows the hidden but has the power to reward or punish accordingly. For the believer living under pressure, this verse brings immense comfort. If one is forced to hide their faith, Allah knows the faith that is in their heart and will reward it. For the hypocrite or the weak-hearted, it is a sharp warning. There is no such thing as a private life hidden from Allah. The believer who internalises this verse lives with a constant sense of being seen, which purifies intentions and encourages consistency between the inner and outer self. It also fosters a deep trust that no act of loyalty to Allah, however secret, is ever lost.
30 | ˹Watch for˺ the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done. And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off. And Allah warns you about Himself. And Allah is Ever Gracious to ˹His˺ servants. | The reminder of Allah’s knowledge leads naturally to a depiction of the Day when that knowledge will manifest in judgment. “Watch for the Day when every soul will be presented with whatever good it has done.” On that Day, a person will come face to face with their own deeds. Every act of goodness, no matter how small or secret, will be brought forward and made visible. For the righteous, this is a moment of profound joy. But the verse immediately adds a contrasting image: “And it will wish that its misdeeds were far off.” The soul will see its sins laid out, and the shame and regret will be so overwhelming that it will wish for an impossible distance between itself and those deeds. The wish that the misdeeds were far off does not mean they will be removed; it expresses the agony of facing them. The verse then repeats the stern caution: “And Allah warns you about Himself.” This warning is repeated because the human heart is prone to forgetfulness and needs to be jolted into awareness. But the verse does not end on a note of despair. It concludes, “And Allah is Ever Gracious to His servants.” This is a beautiful balance. The warning is real, but it comes from a Lord who is Ra’uf, deeply kind and compassionate. His warning is itself an act of grace, intended to steer the servants away from destruction. He does not wish to punish; He wishes to forgive. The believer holds these two truths together: a healthy fear that prevents sin, and a lively hope in Allah’s grace that encourages repentance and good deeds. This verse is a microcosm of the Quranic approach to the soul, combining warning and glad tidings in perfect measure.
31 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you and forgive your sins. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” | This verse is known as the verse of love and is one of the most profound tests of faith. It was reportedly revealed when a group of people claimed to love Allah, or when the Jews and Christians claimed they were the beloved children of God. The Prophet is instructed to say, “If you sincerely love Allah, then follow me.” The love of Allah is the highest aspiration of the human soul, but it is not a mere sentimental claim. The proof of this love is obedience to the Messenger. Following the Prophet means adopting his way, his Sunnah, in belief, worship, character, and daily conduct. It is not possible to love Allah while ignoring or rejecting the one He sent as a guide. This principle shatters all claims of a direct, unmediated spiritual relationship with God that bypasses the revealed path. The result of this following is extraordinary: “Allah will love you and forgive your sins.” Here, the sequence is crucial. Human love for Allah, expressed through following the Prophet, attracts Allah’s love. And when Allah loves a servant, as stated in a sacred hadith, He becomes the hearing with which they hear, the seeing with which they see, the hand with which they strike, and the foot with which they walk. The servant’s supplications are answered, and they are granted protection and honour. On top of that love, sins are forgiven. The verse ends by mentioning two divine names: “All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” This assures that no matter how many sins one has committed, the path of sincere love and following the Messenger is wide open and leads to complete forgiveness. For the believer, this verse shifts the focus from merely professing faith to actively modelling one’s life after the Prophet. Love is not just a feeling; it is a force that manifests in action and sacrifice.
32 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Obey Allah and His Messenger.” If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers. | The call to follow the Prophet is now formulated as a direct command: “Say, ‘Obey Allah and His Messenger.’” Obedience to Allah and obedience to the Messenger are joined as a single requirement. One cannot claim to obey Allah while disobeying the Messenger, because the Messenger is the conveyer of Allah’s commands. In fact, as other verses state, whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed Allah. This coupling is foundational to Islamic law and spirituality; the Quran and the authentic Sunnah together form the complete source of guidance. The verse then addresses the consequence of refusal: “If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers.” Turning away after this clear and gracious invitation is not a minor offence; it is kufr, disbelief. The statement that Allah does not like the disbelievers indicates that they are deprived of His love, His mercy, and His good pleasure. It is a state of spiritual ruin. The verse was revealed in a context where the Prophet was constantly inviting the People of the Book and the polytheists to submit to the one God and to accept his prophethood. Despite his compassion and the clarity of the message, many turned away. This verse consoles the Prophet and the believers: those who refuse do so at their own peril, and they should not be surprised if they fail to receive divine love and guidance. At the same time, it is a stark warning to any who might think that a general belief in God is sufficient without adhering to the specific message brought by His final Messenger. True love and obedience are demonstrated through concrete action, and the refusal to do so places one outside the circle of Allah’s pleasure.
33 | Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ’Imrân above all people ˹of their time˺. | The surah now begins a long passage that addresses the status of the great prophetic families, setting the stage for the stories of Mary, Zechariah, John, and Jesus. “Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ‘Imrân above all people of their time.” The word “chose,” istifa, means to select, purify, and prefer. Adam was chosen as the first human and the first prophet, honoured by the prostration of the angels and taught the names of all things. Noah was chosen as the second father of humanity, the patient warner who persevered for centuries. The family of Abraham includes his sons Ishmael and Isaac, and from them descended a long line of prophets, including Jacob, Joseph, Moses, David, Solomon, and finally Muhammad, peace be upon them all. The family of ‘Imrân is understood to be the household that includes Moses and Aaron, and also, as later verses show, Mary and Jesus, since ‘Imrân is the name of Mary’s father. By mentioning these chosen families, Allah refutes the claims of both the Jews and the Christians who argued about who was more favoured. The truth is that Allah chose certain individuals and families for prophethood and divine mission based on His wisdom, not on the basis of racial or tribal superiority. This selection was for specific times and purposes. The verse also honours the family of the Prophet Muhammad by extension, as he is from the family of Abraham through Ishmael. For the believer, the verse instils respect for the entire chain of prophethood and an understanding that the honour of being chosen is tied to fulfilling the responsibilities of conveying the message, not to worldly privilege. It also prepares the heart for the upcoming account of how Allah chose Mary and Jesus for a special role.
34 | They are descendants of one another. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing. | The verse reinforces the unity and continuity of the chosen line by stating, “They are descendants of one another.” This means that the families mentioned are not isolated; they form a single spiritual and genealogical tree. Adam is the father of all, Noah is a descendant of Adam and the ancestor of all post-flood humanity. The family of Abraham branches into the Israelite line and the Ishmaelite line, and the family of ‘Imrân is a branch within the Israelites. Thus prophethood flowed through one blessed lineage, though it was not automatically inherited; each prophet was individually chosen. The phrase “descendants of one another” can also be understood to mean that they are a single community in terms of their faith and mission, sharing the same core message of monotheism, even though they lived in different times. The verse ends with “And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” He hears the prayers of His chosen ones and knows their hearts and the sincerity of their devotion. He also hears the claims and arguments of those who dispute about them, and He knows the truth of their spiritual lineage. This divine attribute assures that His selection is based on perfect knowledge, not on appearance or human bias. He hears the supplication of the wife of ‘Imrân, the prayer of Zechariah, and the devotion of Mary, as will be detailed in the subsequent verses. The connection between All-Hearing and All-Knowing here is a subtle preparation for the narratives that follow, where prayers are answered and hidden piety is rewarded. For the believer, this verse deepens the sense that the message of Islam is not new but is the culmination of a long history of divinely guided souls, all connected by their submission to the One God.
35 | ˹Remember˺ when the wife of ’Imrân said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service, so accept it from me. You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” | The surah now zooms in on a specific moment in that blessed lineage: the vow of the mother of Mary. “Remember when the wife of ‘Imrân said…” Her name, not mentioned in the Quran but known in some traditions as Hannah, is less important than her act of devotion. She was a righteous woman, married to ‘Imrân, a leader among the Israelites, and she longed for a child. When she sensed the life within her womb, she turned to her Lord in a moment of pure surrender and said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service.” The Arabic word muharraran means liberated or freed—she vowed that this child would be dedicated solely to the service of the sacred temple in Jerusalem, free from all worldly obligations and devoted to worship. This was a practice known for boys, who would serve the temple, but she made the vow even before knowing the gender of the child. Her prayer, “so accept it from me,” reflects her humility and her awareness that Allah is not in need of her offering; she is the one seeking His acceptance and pleasure. She then invokes Allah’s attributes: “You alone are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” She trusts that He hears her whispered prayer and knows her sincere intention. This short sentence captures the essence of true devotion: an unconditional offering of the most precious thing one has—in this case, an unborn child—to the service of the Creator. The believer learns from her example to dedicate one’s family, talents, and life itself to Allah, and to constantly seek His acceptance, never presuming that one’s good deeds are automatically accepted. The stage is now set for the birth of the one who would become the mother of Jesus.
36 | When she delivered, she said, “My Lord! I have given birth to a girl,”—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered—“and the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.” | The moment of delivery brought a surprise, but not a disappointment in the ordinary sense. “When she delivered, she said, ‘My Lord! I have given birth to a girl.’” Her words carry a tone of presenting the matter to Allah, as if to say, “You know what I had intended, and You have given me this.” The parenthetical phrase, “and Allah fully knew what she had delivered,” interrupts to affirm that Allah was perfectly aware of the great value and destiny of this female child, even if the mother could not fully comprehend it at the time. Her next words, “and the male is not like the female,” can be read as her acknowledging that a boy, who could serve the temple without certain physical limitations, was not the same as a girl who would face natural constraints. However, Allah’s plan was greater: this girl would herself be a miraculous sign. She then declared, “I have named her Mary,” which in their language meant the devoted worshipper or the maidservant of the Lord, thus sealing her dedication through her very name. Then comes a profound supplication: “and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.” This mother’s protective prayer was extraordinarily powerful. According to an authentic hadith recorded by Al-Bukhari, every child born is touched by Satan at birth and lets out a cry from that touch, except Mary and her son Jesus. Because of this specific prayer, both Mary and her offspring were shielded from Satan’s touch. This shows the immense power of a parent’s sincere supplication and also establishes the special purity of Mary and Jesus, not in the sense of divine nature, but in their being divinely protected from the first contact with the devil that other humans experience. The believer learns to seek protection for their children through such prayers, trusting in Allah’s response.
37 | So her Lord accepted her graciously and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing—entrusting her to the care of Zachariah. Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions. He exclaimed, “O Mary! Where did this come from?” She replied, “It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.” | The answer to the mother’s sincere dedication came swiftly and beautifully. “So her Lord accepted her graciously.” The acceptance, taqabbala, was with husn, a beautiful acceptance, meaning Allah was pleased with the offering and made it grow into something greater than expected. “And blessed her with a pleasant upbringing” means He caused Mary to grow in stature, character, and spirituality in the best manner. Part of this divine care was “entrusting her to the care of Zachariah.” There was a dispute among the priests over who would be her guardian, and they cast lots, which fell to Zachariah, the prophet and her relative, ensuring she was raised under prophetic eyes. The story then takes a miraculous turn: “Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions.” The sanctuary, al-mihrab, was her private prayer chamber. He would find out-of-season fruits—summer fruits in winter, winter fruits in summer—or food that had no apparent earthly source. Amazed, he asked, “O Mary! Where did this come from?” Her response is a model of faith and recognition of Allah’s power: “It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.” She did not attribute the provision to any natural cause; she directly referred it to Allah’s will. This miracle served a dual purpose: it honoured Mary and confirmed her chosen status, and it also became the catalyst for Zachariah’s own prayer for a child. Seeing Allah provide miraculously for the young girl, the elderly Zachariah’s heart was moved to hope for a son despite his old age and his wife’s barrenness. The verse teaches that when Allah wills a provision, He sends it from directions one cannot imagine, and that those who dedicate themselves to His service will find their needs met in unexpected ways.
38 | Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord, saying, “My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring. You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers.” | The sight of Mary’s miraculous provision stirred something deep in Zachariah’s soul. “Then and there,” hunalika, meaning at that very place and in that very moment of witnessing Allah’s generosity, he turned to his Lord. His prayer is a beautiful example of prophetic etiquette. He begins, “My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring.” The phrase “from You” or “by Your grace,” min ladunka, indicates a gift that is special, unmediated by normal worldly causes, and coming directly from Allah’s divine treasury. He did not demand a child as a right; he asked for a pure gift from divine grace. He specified “righteous offspring,” dhurriyyatan tayyibah, because a child is not a blessing in itself if they are not righteous. A child who goes astray can be a source of grief, but a righteous child is a continuous joy and a source of ongoing reward. Zachariah’s prayer, though it arose from a personal longing, was also connected to the broader mission of prophethood, as he wanted someone to inherit the spiritual legacy and guide the people. He then appeals to Allah’s attribute: “You are certainly the Hearer of all prayers.” This acknowledgment is an act of faith. Despite his old age, despite his wife’s barrenness, he prays and declares that Allah hears prayers. He does not impose conditions or express doubt. For the believer, this verse demonstrates the proper response to seeing signs of Allah’s power: to turn one’s own hopes to Him and ask for what seems impossible. It also teaches the importance of asking for righteous children above all, and the confidence that Allah hears every sincere call, no matter how distant the fulfilment appears.
39 | So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary, “Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John who will confirm the Word of Allah and will be a great leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.” | The response to Zachariah’s prayer was immediate and majestic. “So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary.” The call came in the midst of his worship, in the very place where he had seen Mary’s provisions. The angels, as a group, announced the glad tidings, indicating the greatness of the news. The message is direct: “Allah gives you good news of the birth of John.” The name John, Yahya in Arabic, was itself divinely chosen, as indicated in another chapter. The name carries the meaning of life and spiritual vitality. John is described with four powerful attributes. First, “who will confirm the Word of Allah.” The “Word of Allah” here is a title for Jesus, who is referred to as a word from Allah because he was created by the divine command “Be” without a father. John would be the one to testify to Jesus, to believe in him, and to prepare the people for his coming. Second, “a great leader,” sayyid. John would be a master, a chief among his people in knowledge, piety, and nobility. Third, “chaste,” hasur, which means he would be one who restrains his desires, living in purity and focused entirely on worship and his mission. Some interpretations indicate he did not marry, but the essence is his mastery over worldly passions. Fourth, “a prophet among the righteous.” This is the highest rank, combining prophethood with perfect righteousness. This description of John serves as a reassurance to Zachariah and also as a prelude to the even more remarkable birth of Jesus, whom John would serve and confirm. For the believer, this verse shows that when Allah grants a blessing, He grants it completely, with honour, purity, and a noble purpose.
40 | Zachariah exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” He replied, “So will it be. Allah does what He wills.” | Upon hearing the angelic announcement, Zachariah’s immediate response was not one of disbelief but of wondering inquiry. He exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” The question of “how,” anna, is a request for understanding, not a challenge to divine power. Zachariah was fully aware of the natural laws that rendered childbearing impossible in his situation; he himself had reached extreme old age, and his wife had been barren throughout her life. In human terms, two insurmountable obstacles stood in the way. Yet he did not say, “This cannot happen.” He simply sought to comprehend the manner in which Allah would bring it about, which is a natural human reaction to a supernatural promise. The divine reply came, though the text does not specify whether it was through the angel or directly: “So will it be.” The Arabic “kadhalika” is a powerful affirmation that what has been promised will definitely occur, exactly as announced. The addition, “Allah does what He wills,” is the ultimate explanation. There is no need to explain the biological mechanism; the will of Allah suffices as the cause. When He wills a thing, He merely says “Be,” and it is. This short statement contains the entire theology of divine omnipotence. It closes the door on the idea that God is bound by the laws He has established in nature. He is the Creator of those laws and can suspend them or override them according to His wisdom. For the believer, this exchange is profoundly hopeful. No matter how impossible a situation appears—be it a childless couple, a financial deadlock, or a heart hardened by sin—the statement “Allah does what He wills” is a firm anchor. It reminds one that the power of Allah is not limited by human calculations, and that sincere prayer, accompanied by patient submission, can open doors that logic deems permanently shut.
41 | Zachariah said, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” He said, “Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures. Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.” | After receiving the glad tidings of a son, Zachariah’s heart naturally sought reassurance and a tangible marker of the promise’s fulfilment. He asked, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” This request was not born of doubt, for he had just been told “So will it be” and had accepted it. Rather, it was a request for a sign that would allow him to experience the joy of certainty and to know when the conception had occurred, so that he could thank Allah accordingly. Prophets sometimes asked for signs to strengthen their hearts, as Abraham asked to be shown how Allah gives life to the dead. Allah responded by giving him a very specific sign: “Your sign is that you will not be able to speak to people for three days except through gestures.” This was a temporary, miraculous condition. For three full days and nights, Zachariah would lose the power of speech, not because of any physical ailment, but as a divine sign. He would be able to communicate only through gestures, isharah. This inability to speak would be a personal confirmation that his prayer had been answered and that his wife had conceived. But the sign came with a command: “Remember your Lord often and glorify Him morning and evening.” The muteness was not a punishment but a gift of seclusion for intensified worship. While unable to speak to people, his tongue was to remain busy with the remembrance of Allah. This teaches that even when a believer is granted a great blessing or is awaiting its fulfilment, the primary occupation should be dhikr. Zachariah’s three days of silence became a period of deep spiritual retreat, culminating in the birth of John. The mention of “morning and evening” sets a pattern for the believer: bookending the day with the glorification of Allah. For the Muslim, this resonates with the prescribed prayers at dawn, afternoon, and after sunset, as well as the voluntary remembrances of the morning and evening adhkar. Zachariah’s experience shows that when a divine promise is given, the proper posture is not anxious watching but peaceful, worshipful waiting.
42 | And ˹remember˺ when the angels said, “O Mary! Surely Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women of the world. | After the interlude about Zachariah and the promise of John, the surah returns to the story of Mary with a dramatic announcement. The angels now address Mary directly. “O Mary! Surely Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women of the world.” Three verbs are used, each carrying a distinct dimension of her honour. “Selected you,” istafaki, means that Allah chose her from among all the women of her time for a specific divine purpose. This selection was not random; it was based on His foreknowledge of her piety and suitability for the monumental role of being the mother of Jesus. “Purified you,” tahharaki, means that Allah cleansed her spiritually and physically. Some scholars interpret this as protection from major sins, from the touch of Satan, and from the impurities of menstruation and postnatal bleeding that would have prevented continuous service in the sanctuary. She was set apart in purity. “Chosen you over all women of the world,” faddalaki ‘ala nisa’i al-‘alamin, is a statement of her unique status. According to many scholars, this means she was the most virtuous woman of her time, if not of all time. An authentic hadith states that the best women of Paradise are Khadijah, Fatimah, Maryam, and Asiyah, the wife of Pharaoh. Thus Mary’s rank is unparalleled. This address from the angels was a direct communication, a form of inspiration or perhaps a visible angelic visitation, confirming that she was no ordinary servant of Allah. The timing of this announcement is understood to be before the annunciation of Jesus, preparing her heart for the even greater news to come. For the believer, this verse honours Mary in a way that no other scripture does, affirming her purity and chosen status without deifying her. She remains a human being, a servant of Allah, but one elevated to the highest rank among women due to her devotion and the role she was to play in the divine plan.
43 | O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down.” | After honouring Mary with the declaration of her chosen status, the angels give her a direct command that outlines the path of those who are chosen. “O Mary! Be devout to your Lord.” The word “be devout,” uqnuti, comes from qunut, which means continuous, humble obedience coupled with a prolonged standing in prayer. It is an instruction to dedicate herself entirely and constantly to the worship of Allah, with a heart full of reverence and love. The chosen one must be a devoted worshipper, not someone who rests on the laurels of being selected. The command then becomes more specific: “prostrate yourself in prayer,” usjudi, meaning to place the forehead on the ground in the ultimate act of submission. Prostration is the physical posture that symbolises the soul’s complete surrender to its Creator. The angels call her to this act, even though she was already a devout worshipper, to emphasize that the blessing of being chosen requires an intensification of worship, not complacency. The final part of the command is “and bow along with those who bow down.” The instruction to bow with others, irka’i ma’a al-raki’in, has a profound communal dimension. Mary is not called to an isolated spirituality; she is told to join the congregation of worshippers. This indicates that even the most spiritually elevated souls are to connect with the community of believers and participate in collective worship. In the context of Mary’s life, this likely refers to her praying behind Zachariah and the other devoted worshippers in the temple. The verse beautifully combines inner devotion, physical acts of worship, and communal participation. For the believer, it is a template of complete servitude. The one who is honoured by Allah must respond with more devotion, expressed in private prostration and public prayer. It also subtly refutes any notion that Mary was above the need to worship Allah; she herself was commanded to submit.
44 | This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺. | After narrating these details, the surah pauses to address the Prophet Muhammad and establish the divine origin of the information. “This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you.” The phrase “news of the unseen,” anba’ al-ghayb, underscores that the Prophet Muhammad had no natural way of knowing these intimate details of a story that took place centuries before his birth in a land far from Arabia. He was unlettered, not a scholar of Jewish or Christian scriptures. The revelation of these accurate accounts served as proof of his prophethood. The specific mention that “You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued about it” provides an irrefutable test of authenticity. The historical record, as preserved in the Quran and confirmed by some Judeo-Christian traditions, tells that when Mary was presented to the temple, the priests disputed over who would take charge of her, each desiring the honour of raising this dedicated child. They decided to cast lots by throwing their pens or arrows into the river, and only Zachariah’s pen floated or remained, signalling that he was divinely chosen. The Prophet could not have known this obscure detail, which even many scholars among the People of the Book might not have been aware of. The mention of their arguing also highlights human weakness even among the religious elite, while simultaneously showing Allah’s wisdom in resolving the dispute through the casting of lots. For the believer, this verse strengthens faith in the Quran as the unaltered word of Allah. It also teaches that the knowledge of the unseen belongs to Allah alone, and He shares what He wills with His chosen messengers. The verse builds anticipation for the even more remarkable news about Jesus that is about to be revealed.
45 | ˹Remember˺ when the angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him, his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary; honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺.” | The narrative now reaches its climax with the annunciation to Mary. The angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him.” The glad tidings, yubashshiruki, indicate that what is to come is a source of joy, though it would also bring trials. The child is described as “a Word from Him,” kalimatin minhu. Jesus is called a Word because he came into existence by the direct command of Allah, “Be,” without a father, making him a manifestation of Allah’s creative word. He is not the Word of God in the sense of a divine entity; rather, he is a created being brought about by that word. The angels then give his name and titles: “his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary.” The title Messiah, al-Masih, means the anointed one, one purified and blessed. Jesus is uniquely associated with his mother, “son of Mary,” emphasizing his human birth and refuting those who would claim he is the son of God. The angels continue: “honoured in this world and the Hereafter.” In this world, he was given prophethood, miracles, and a message that spread far and wide. In the Hereafter, he will have an exalted station and will intercede, by Allah’s permission, and his followers who truly believed in him and then in Muhammad will be honoured. “And he will be one of those nearest to Allah,” min al-muqarrabin, is the highest rank of spiritual proximity. This is the station of the angels nearest to the Throne and the greatest prophets. The entire description is a balanced, honourable portrayal that corrects the extremes: the Jews who rejected him as a false prophet, and the Christians who elevated him to divinity. Jesus is a noble servant, a prophet, and a sign, but a servant nonetheless. The believer accepts this complete description with reverence and love for Jesus as one of the mightiest messengers of Allah.
46 | And he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy and adulthood and will be one of the righteous.” | The angels continue the description of Jesus by mentioning two remarkable aspects of his life. “He will speak to people in his infancy and adulthood.” The mention of speaking in the cradle, fi al-mahdi, is a specific miracle that would occur shortly after his birth. When Mary would return to her people with the infant Jesus, they would accuse her of unchastity. At that moment, Jesus, a newborn baby, would miraculously speak, declaring himself to be the servant of Allah, thus exonerating his mother from any blame. This cradle speech is detailed in Surah Maryam. By this sign, his prophethood was demonstrated from the very first days of his life. The second part, “and adulthood,” kahlan, refers to his speaking as a mature man. Jesus would receive revelation as an adult, preach the Gospel, and debate with the scholars of the Children of Israel. The term kahl includes the idea of reaching full maturity and strength, between thirty and forty years of age. Some scholars note that this promise of speaking in maturity also implies that he would come back to earth, because he was raised to heaven before reaching old age. He will descend as a just ruler, speaking to people in adulthood before his death. The angels further declare, “and he will be one of the righteous,” min al-salihin. Righteousness here encompasses everything: his character, his actions, his complete alignment with Allah’s will. It also refutes any claim that he was a sinner or in any way morally compromised. This verse provides a complete portrait of Jesus: a prophet who spoke with divine authority from cradle to maturity, living a life of perfect righteousness. For the believer, it affirms that every prophet sent by Allah is a model of righteousness, and the Christian claim that Jesus had to die for the sins of humanity because humans are inherently sinful finds no support in this pure depiction of a righteous servant of God.
47 | Mary wondered, “My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?” An angel replied, “So will it be. Allah creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is! | Upon hearing the impossible news, Mary responded with a question that came from a pure heart. “My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?” The question is similar to Zachariah’s, but the circumstance is even more miraculous. Mary was not old and barren; she was a young, chaste virgin, not married and not touched by any man, lawfully or unlawfully. Her question was not a challenge to Allah’s power but an inquiry into the manner, and also a declaration of her innocence, should anyone later question her. The angel’s reply is direct and majestic: “So will it be.” This is the divine decree, final and certain. The angel then provides the universal principle behind this miracle: “Allah creates what He wills.” The creation of Jesus without a father is not beyond Allah, who created Adam without a father or a mother, and Eve from a male without a female. If He wills something, no natural law stands in His way, because He is the author of those laws. Then the verse delivers the ultimate theological explanation: “When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!” This is the kun fa yakun formula. Allah does not need physical means, time, or gradual processes. His command alone brings a thing into existence from nothing. The creation of Jesus in the womb of Mary was as instantaneous as the divine will. The phrase “Be!” is not a spoken sound but an expression of His irresistible will. For the believer, this verse anchors the heart in the absolute omnipotence of Allah. No matter how impossible a situation seems—whether it be a childless couple, a dead-end in life, or the resurrection of the dead—the command “Be” is sufficient. This also serves as a refutation of those who used the miraculous birth of Jesus as proof of his divinity. The miracle is not a sign of Jesus’s divinity but a sign of Allah’s creative power.
48 | “And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel,” | The angelic announcement continues with a further description of the blessings and abilities Allah would grant to Jesus. “And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom.” The term “writing,” al-kitab, refers to the skill of written language, the ability to read and write. In the context of his mission, it also indicates that Jesus would be given a scripture, the Injil or Gospel, and would be learned in the divine books. “Wisdom,” al-hikmah, is a comprehensive term that includes deep understanding of religion, sound judgment, the ability to act and speak appropriately, and the knowledge of the inner realities of things. A prophet must combine scriptural knowledge with practical wisdom to guide people effectively. Then the verse specifies, “the Torah and the Gospel.” Allah would teach Jesus the Torah, the scripture revealed to Moses, confirming his deep knowledge of the Law. He would also be taught the Gospel, the new scripture that would be revealed to him, which would confirm the Torah while bringing certain easements and clarifications. This indicates that Jesus was not an unlettered man, nor was he unaware of the previous revelation. He was thoroughly grounded in the divine law that preceded him and was then entrusted with the new revelation. This detail is important in the dialogue with the People of the Book; it shows that Jesus did not come to abolish the Torah but to confirm and fulfil it, albeit with some modifications permitted by Allah. For the believer, this verse reinforces the high status of Jesus as a prophet of knowledge. It also highlights the value of learning and wisdom in Islam. Allah teaching a prophet directly is the highest form of education. While ordinary believers do not receive direct revelation, they are urged to seek knowledge and wisdom through study and reflection, following the legacy of the prophets.
49 | and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will. I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life— by Allah’s Will. And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses. Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe.’ | The angelic message now outlines the mission and the supporting miracles of Jesus. He will be made “a messenger to the Children of Israel,” meaning his primary prophetic mission was to the Israelites, not to all humanity directly, though his message, like all prophetic messages, called to universal truths. He would come “with a sign from your Lord,” the word ayah signifying a miracle that confirms his truthfulness. The first miracle mentioned is the creation of a bird from clay. He would shape a bird out of clay, breathe into it, and it would become a living, flying bird, “by Allah’s Will.” This miracle is a direct demonstration of Allah’s creative power working through him. The repetition of “by Allah’s Will” is critical; it emphasizes that Jesus does not possess any independent divine power. He is utterly dependent on Allah’s permission. This parallels the creation of Adam from clay and the breathing of the spirit into him, establishing Jesus as a new Adam. Then he would “heal the blind and the leper.” The blind, akmah, refers to those blind from birth, and the leper, abras, suffered from a disfiguring skin disease that was considered incurable at the time. These healings were acts of mercy and signs of divine power. “And raise the dead to life—by Allah’s Will.” This is the ultimate miracle, showing Allah’s power over life and death, prefiguring the general resurrection. The final miracle is less spectacular but equally miraculous: he would “prophesize what you eat and store in your houses,” meaning he would inform them of what they had consumed in secret and what they had saved away, exposing hidden realities. This gave him insight into their daily lives, proving his connection to divine knowledge. He concludes, “Surely in this is a sign for you if you truly believe.” The signs are clear, but only those with sincere faith benefit.
50 | “And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you. I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of Allah and obey me.” | Jesus continues his address to the Children of Israel by clarifying his relationship to the existing scripture. “And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me.” He does not come to abolish the Law of Moses but to affirm its divine origin and to bear witness to its truth. This confirmation aligns him with the continuous chain of prophets and distinguishes him from a false prophet who would contradict previous revelation. The Gospel, in its original form, did not nullify the Torah; it reinforced its moral and monotheistic core. Then he announces a specific legislative change: “and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you.” Due to the transgressions of the Israelites, certain things were made unlawful for them as a punishment, as mentioned in Surah Al-An’am (6:146) and other verses. Jesus came with divine permission to lift some of these prohibitions, making the law less burdensome as a mercy from Allah. This does not mean he had the authority to independently legislate; he was acting by Allah’s command. The statement “I have come to you with a sign from your Lord” reminds them that his authority is derived from Allah, and the sign—the miracles already described—should be sufficient proof. Then he issues the core command: “so be mindful of Allah and obey me.” Taqwa, mindfulness of Allah, is the foundation: an inner consciousness that governs all actions. Obedience to the messenger is the practical manifestation of that consciousness. By linking the two, Jesus sets the path of salvation: fear Allah and follow the prophet He has sent. This verse is a direct rebuttal to the Christian doctrine that Jesus came to fulfil the law in a way that makes obedience irrelevant. On the contrary, he emphasised obedience. For the Muslim, this verse illustrates that all prophets called to the same core message: worship Allah alone, fear Him, and follow the messenger of your time. The specific legal details may change, but the call to taqwa and obedience is eternal.
51 | “Surely Allah is my Lord and your Lord. So worship Him ˹alone˺. This is the Straight Path.’” | Jesus concludes his opening proclamation with the very essence of his message, which is the essence of Islam. “Surely Allah is my Lord and your Lord.” The word “my Lord” is crucial. Jesus declares his own servitude to Allah, placing himself alongside his followers as a fellow worshipper, not an object of worship. He does not say “my Father and your Father” in the exclusive sense. He says Rabbi, my Lord, the One who created me, sustains me, and to whom I will return. By saying “your Lord,” he calls them to the same relationship of servitude and love. This statement demolishes any claim of his divinity. If he were God, he would not say “my Lord,” because God has no Lord. The logical conclusion follows: “So worship Him alone.” The command to worship Allah, u’buduhu, is the natural outcome of recognising Him as Lord. Worship is the exclusive right of the Creator. Then Jesus declares, “This is the Straight Path,” hadha siratun mustaqim. The Straight Path is the path of monotheism, the path that leads directly to Allah’s pleasure and Paradise. By stating this, Jesus aligns his teaching with that of every prophet from Adam to Muhammad. There is no deviation, no mystery of a triune god, no need for a divine saviour to die for sins. The path is clear and direct: worship the One God, live righteously, and follow His guidance. For the believer, this verse is a treasure because it presents the testimony of Jesus himself, in the Quran, as a Muslim in the true sense—one who submits to Allah. It creates a powerful bond of love and respect for Jesus, while firmly maintaining the absolute oneness of Allah. It also reminds the believer that the Straight Path is not a complex theological labyrinth but a simple, profound truth that any sincere heart can grasp: Allah alone is Lord, so worship Him alone.
52 | When Jesus sensed disbelief from his people, he asked, “Who will stand up with me for Allah?” The disciples replied, “We will stand up for Allah. We believe in Allah, so bear witness that we have submitted.” | After presenting the core message, the surah shifts to the response of the people and the emergence of a faithful core. “When Jesus sensed disbelief from his people,” meaning he perceived their rejection, their stubbornness, and their plots against him. The word “sensed,” ahassa, indicates a deep, intuitive awareness born of his prophetic insight. The disbelief of the Children of Israel, particularly their leaders, became evident through their arguments, their refusal to accept his signs, and their eventual conspiracy to kill him. In this moment of growing hostility, Jesus turned to the wider community and asked, “Who will stand up with me for Allah?” He sought ansar, helpers or supporters, who would aid him in his mission to call people to Allah. This was a call for commitment, a recruitment of those who would sacrifice and struggle for the truth. The response came from the disciples, the hawariyyun, the pure and sincere followers who were chosen by Allah. They declared, “We will stand up for Allah.” They pledged their support to the cause of Allah, not to a personal ambition of Jesus. Their next words clarify their motivation: “We believe in Allah, so bear witness that we have submitted.” They affirmed their faith in Allah and declared their submission, Islam. The disciples are thus presented as model believers. Their faith was not in Jesus as a deity but in Allah, and they submitted to His will as taught by Jesus. They also asked Jesus to “bear witness” to their declaration, making him a witness for them on the Day of Judgment. For the believer, this verse highlights the importance of having a faithful community that supports the prophets and their mission. It also elevates the disciples as exemplars of immediate, unwavering response to the call of truth, providing a parallel to the companions of Prophet Muhammad who stood by him in times of hardship.
53 | ˹They prayed to Allah,˺ “Our Lord! We believe in Your revelations and follow the messenger, so count us among those who bear witness.” | The declaration of the disciples is immediately followed by a heartfelt supplication, which reveals the inner state of true believers. They pray, “Our Lord! We believe in Your revelations.” They address Allah directly, showing that their faith is rooted in a personal relationship with the Creator, not merely in allegiance to a human teacher. Their belief is in the “revelations,” which includes the signs Jesus brought, the miracles they witnessed, and the Gospel being revealed through him. Then they say, “and follow the messenger.” They make a specific commitment to follow Jesus, the messenger sent to them. This coupling of belief in Allah’s revelations and following the messenger is the hallmark of true faith. One without the other is incomplete. They then make a request: “so count us among those who bear witness.” The phrase “those who bear witness,” al-shahidin, has a rich meaning. It refers to those who testify to the truth of monotheism, who witness the signs of Allah with open hearts, and who become witnesses for the truth before humanity. The disciples wanted to be written in the divine record as part of this noble company. Their prayer also echoes the prayer of Mary’s mother and connects to the broader theme of the surah: being among the witnesses to truth. For the believer, this supplication is a model. After declaring faith and committing to follow the Prophet Muhammad, one should constantly ask Allah to be counted among the truthful witnesses, not among the hypocrites or those who turn back. The verse also shows that the disciples were not passive followers; they actively sought a high spiritual station. Their prayer was answered, and they are honoured in the Quran as true believers. This encourages the Muslim to supplicate not only for forgiveness but also for a place among the righteous and the witnesses on the Day of Judgment.
54 | And the disbelievers made a plan ˹against Jesus˺, but Allah also planned —and Allah is the best of planners. | The narrative now turns to the dark conspiracy that formed against Jesus. “And the disbelievers made a plan against Jesus.” The disbelievers, the leaders of the Jews who rejected his prophethood, plotted to have him arrested and killed. They schemed to put an end to his mission, which they saw as a threat to their authority and their interpretation of the religion. Their “plan,” makaru, carries the connotation of a secret, deceitful scheme. However, the verse immediately counters with “but Allah also planned,” wa makara Allah. This is one of the profound expressions of the Quran where the same verb is used for human action and divine action, but with vastly different meanings. Human makr is deception and evil plotting. Divine makr is not deception in a negative sense but a perfect, hidden counter-plan that nullifies the schemes of the evildoers and delivers the righteous from their harm. It is a response of justice, executed with absolute wisdom and precision. The verse then declares, “and Allah is the best of planners.” No plot against His prophets can ever succeed in its ultimate objective. The disbelievers planned to kill Jesus, but Allah planned to save him, raise him up, and preserve his honour. Their plan led them to their own humiliation, while Jesus was elevated. This verse is a source of immense comfort to believers facing conspiracies. The most powerful adversaries cannot outmaneuver Allah. Their plans, no matter how carefully crafted, are like a spider’s web against a storm when confronted by the divine will. The verse also teaches that the conflict between truth and falsehood is not decided by worldly power; it is decided by Allah’s hidden direction of events. The subsequent verses will reveal how Allah’s plan unfolded.
55 | ˹Remember˺ when Allah said, “O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself. I will deliver you from those who disbelieve, and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment. Then to Me you will ˹all˺ return, and I will settle all your disputes.” | The divine plan is now disclosed in a direct address from Allah to Jesus. “O Jesus! I will take you and raise you up to Myself.” The word “take you,” mutawaffika, comes from tawaffa, which can mean to take in sleep or to take in death, and here, according to the majority interpretation, it means that Allah caused Jesus to fall into a state of sleep and then raised him bodily to the heavens, without experiencing death. This is supported by the consensus that Jesus is alive in the heavens and will return before the end of time. “Raise you up to Myself” indicates an honourable ascension, lifting him away from the reach of his enemies. “I will deliver you from those who disbelieve” means He will save him from their plot to kill him and will purify him of their false accusations. The next part is a major promise: “and elevate your followers above the disbelievers until the Day of Judgment.” The followers of Jesus, meaning those who adhered to his true message of monotheism, which ultimately was fulfilled and perfected in Islam, will be given supremacy over those who disbelieved. This includes the early Christian believers who were persecuted but eventually triumphed, and the Muslim community, which is the true continuation of Jesus’s message. This supremacy is in terms of proof, guidance, and ultimately the truth. The verse concludes with the ultimate accountability: “Then to Me you will all return, and I will settle all your disputes.” All the conflicting claims about Jesus—whether he was God, son of God, a false prophet, or a true prophet—will be judged by Allah on the Day of Resurrection. This statement brings the entire narrative into the eschatological framework, reminding everyone that the final word belongs to Allah.
56 | “As for those who disbelieve, I will subject them to a severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter, and they will have no helpers.” | Allah’s address continues with the specific judgment for the rejectors. “As for those who disbelieve,” referring to those who rejected Jesus after clear signs came to them, and who persisted in their denial, “I will subject them to a severe punishment in this life and the Hereafter.” The punishment in this life includes the destruction of Jerusalem by the Romans in 70 CE, a catastrophic event that decimated the Jewish community there, and the various forms of humiliation and suffering that have befallen those who rejected the prophets throughout history. It also includes the spiritual torment of being lost and the worldly consequences of transgression. The punishment in the Hereafter is the eternal fire, which is far more severe. The coupling of punishment in both lives underscores that the rejection of a prophet is not a light matter; its consequences ripple through time and into eternity. The final phrase, “and they will have no helpers,” echoes an earlier warning in the surah. No intercessor, no ally, no power will be able to shield them from Allah’s judgment. Their wealth, their numbers, their ancestors will not avail them. For the believer, this verse reinforces the seriousness of faith and the dire consequence of rejecting the truth after it has been made clear. It also provides a sobering perspective on the historical and eschatological fate of those who oppose the prophets. While it is a warning, it is also a call to gratitude for those who believe, because they have been spared this dual punishment through Allah’s mercy. The verse also serves as part of the divine comfort to Jesus: his enemies will not go unpunished.
57 | “And as for those who believe and do good, they will be rewarded in full. And Allah does not like the wrongdoers.” | The judgment is now given for the believers, forming the perfect balance of divine justice. “And as for those who believe and do good,” the combination of faith and righteous deeds is the invariable Quranic condition for salvation. Faith in the heart must manifest in actions that align with Allah’s commands. This applies to the followers of Jesus who believed in his true message, and by extension, to the followers of all prophets, including the final prophet Muhammad. The reward described is “they will be rewarded in full,” yuwaffihim ujurahum. This means their reward will be complete, without any reduction, without any loss, and without any injustice. Every prayer, every act of charity, every moment of patience will be accounted for and recompensed generously. The phrase “in full” also implies that the reward will be exactly what was promised, and that Allah’s generosity may add to it manifold. The verse then adds the important qualifier: “And Allah does not like the wrongdoers.” This statement serves as a boundary. The believers who are rewarded are not the wrongdoers. Wrongdoers, al-zalimin, are those who transgress, who associate partners with Allah, who oppress others, and who refuse to repent. Even if they have some faith, persistent wrongdoing without repentance can jeopardise their full reward. The verse subtly distinguishes between true believers who combine faith with good deeds and those who may claim belief but persist in injustice. For the believer, this verse is a promise of hope and a call to integrity. The reward is full and generous, but it requires genuine faith expressed in righteous conduct. It also reminds that Allah’s love and full reward are reserved for those who stay away from wrongdoing, reinforcing the connection between salvation and moral behaviour.
58 | We recite ˹all˺ this to you ˹O Prophet˺ as one of the signs and ˹as˺ a wise reminder. | The narrative segment concludes with a direct address to the Prophet Muhammad, linking the stories back to the present revelation. “We recite all this to you as one of the signs.” The word “recite,” natluhu, indicates that the Quran is a recitation, an oral transmission of divine origin. The stories of Zachariah, Mary, John, and Jesus are “signs,” ayat, meaning they serve as proofs of the Quran’s truth, as moral lessons, and as evidence of Allah’s power and wisdom. The singular “as one of the signs” suggests that this entire account is a single, coherent sign among many others in the Quran. The second part, “and a wise reminder,” dhikr hakim, adds another dimension. “Reminder,” dhikr, indicates that the story is not new information in the sense of a novelty; it is a reminder of timeless truths about God, prophethood, and human nature that are embedded in the human soul and confirmed through revelation. “Wise,” hakim, describes the reminder itself as being full of wisdom, flawless in its presentation, and containing profound lessons. The wisdom lies in how the story refutes false doctrines, honours the prophets, guides the believers, and warns the disbelievers. For the believer, this verse validates the entire preceding narrative as a source of guidance. It is not just history; it is a sign and a reminder meant to transform the heart. The verse also comforts the Prophet, affirming that what he is reciting is from Allah and that its wisdom will be apparent to those who reflect. Every believer who recites or hears these stories should approach them with the awareness that they are reciting a sign and receiving a wise reminder.
59 | Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust, then said to him, “Be!” And he was! | With the narrative background now complete, the surah delivers the decisive argument that settles the dispute over the nature of Jesus. “Indeed, the example of Jesus in the sight of Allah is like that of Adam.” The verse uses the word “example,” mathal, which means a case that serves as a comparison to clarify a truth. Jesus’s miraculous birth without a father is not unique in a way that proves his divinity. Adam’s creation is the greater miracle: Adam had neither a father nor a mother. If Jesus being born without a father makes him the son of God, then Adam would have an even greater claim, which no one asserts. “He created him from dust, then said to him, ‘Be!’ And he was!” The creation of Adam from dust, formed by Allah’s hands, and given life by the divine command “Be,” is the ultimate demonstration of Allah’s creative power. Jesus’s creation in the womb of Mary by the same command “Be” is simply a variation of the same divine act. This verse was reportedly revealed when a Christian delegation from Najran visited the Prophet in Medina and debated about the nature of Jesus. They argued that Jesus’s fatherless birth proved his divine sonship. The Quran refutes this logic by presenting the case of Adam, which was even more astonishing. The verse elegantly closes the door on all attempts to deify Jesus, while still honouring his miraculous birth as a sign of Allah’s power. For the believer, it is a call to use reason in matters of faith, comparing claims logically and submitting to the clear argument provided by Allah. It also reinforces the foundational Islamic belief that Jesus is a servant and prophet of Allah, created by His command, just like Adam, and not a partner in divinity.
60 | This is the truth from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt. | The discussion about Jesus concludes with a firm declaration and a directive to the Prophet and, through him, to all believers. “This is the truth from your Lord.” The word “truth,” al-haqq, carries the weight of absolute, undeniable reality. What has been said about Jesus—his nature as a Word from Allah, his miraculous birth, his prophethood, his ascension, and the comparison with Adam—is not a theory or an opinion. It is the truth that has come directly from the Lord of the worlds. The phrase “from your Lord” adds a personal and authoritative dimension. The source of this truth is the One who created Jesus, who knows his reality perfectly, and who has no need to fabricate or compromise. Therefore, the believer should accept it with complete certainty. The command follows: “so do not be one of those who doubt.” Here, the address can be understood as directed primarily to the Prophet Muhammad, to reassure him and strengthen his resolve in the face of the arguments of the Christians and others. Since the Prophet was free from doubt, the command serves as a means of reinforcing his steadfastness and, by extension, is a warning to all believers not to entertain doubts about the clear truths revealed in the Quran. Doubt, in this context, is the hesitation that arises from listening to the false claims and twisted arguments of those who dispute the nature of Jesus. The believer is instructed to hold firmly to what Allah has declared as the truth, without wavering. This verse closes the section on Jesus with a strong call to certainty, drawing a line between the believers who accept the divine truth and those who remain in doubt and confusion. It teaches that true faith requires intellectual and spiritual resolution, not a wavering mind that is tossed by every argument.
61 | Now, whoever disputes with you ˹O Prophet˺ concerning Jesus after full knowledge has come to you, say, “Come! Let us gather our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves—then let us sincerely invoke Allah’s curse upon the liars.” | After presenting the decisive argument comparing Jesus to Adam, the surah now arms the Prophet with an ultimate challenge to those who persist in argumentation despite the clear truth. The verse addresses a scenario where disputation continues: “Now, whoever disputes with you concerning Jesus after full knowledge has come to you.” The “full knowledge” refers to the conclusive revelation about Jesus’s true nature—his miraculous birth, his prophethood, his servitude to Allah, and the rejection of his divinity or sonship. This knowledge leaves no room for doubt or continued obstinate argument, yet some will still dispute. The instruction given is not to engage in endless debate but to propose a dramatic and decisive confrontation: “Come! Let us gather our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves—then let us sincerely invoke Allah’s curse upon the liars.” This event is known as the Mubahala, the mutual invocation of curse. The Prophet was to bring his closest family, and the Christian disputants were to bring theirs, and they would all earnestly pray that Allah’s curse and punishment fall upon the party that was lying about Jesus. The inclusion of “our children and your children, our women and your women, ourselves and yourselves” intensifies the solemnity. A person risks everything—their own life and the lives of their dearest loved ones—in this invocation. It shows absolute certainty in one’s position. The historical context is that a Christian delegation from Najran came to Medina and debated with the Prophet about Jesus. When they refused to accept the truth, this verse was revealed. The Prophet invited them to the Mubahala. They hesitated, consulted among themselves, and ultimately declined, fearing the consequences. They knew the Prophet was truthful, and they chose a treaty instead. For the believer, this verse demonstrates the supreme confidence that truth brings. The Prophet was willing to stake everything on the truth of his message. It also teaches that when argumentation reaches a deadlock, one can appeal to divine judgment in a profound way. However, this specific method was a prophetic practice and is not a general ritual for everyday disputes.
62 | Certainly, this is the true narrative, and there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah. And indeed, Allah ˹alone˺ is the Almighty, All-Wise. | After the powerful challenge of the Mubahala, the surah reaffirms the absolute veracity of what has been revealed. “Certainly, this is the true narrative.” The word “this” refers to the entire account of Jesus, Mary, John the Baptist, and the surrounding events. It is not a myth, a legend, or a borrowed tale; it is al-qasas al-haqq, the story that corresponds perfectly with reality. The Qur’an is correcting the distortions that crept into the Christian and Jewish traditions and is providing the definitive version of events as known by the All-Knowing. The declaration then returns to the central tenet of faith: “and there is no god worthy of worship except Allah.” This statement reinforces that the entire narrative’s purpose is to establish tawhid. The story of Jesus, so often used to justify shirk, is actually the greatest proof of Allah’s oneness, because it demonstrates His unique creative power. The verse ends with two powerful names: “And indeed, Allah alone is the Almighty, All-Wise.” The name Almighty, al-Aziz, reassures that despite the opposition of those who dispute, Allah’s plan is invincible. The truth will prevail, and no force can overcome it. The name All-Wise, al-Hakim, indicates that every detail of the story—the virgin birth, the miracles, the raising of Jesus—was decreed with perfect wisdom, whether humans grasp it or not. For the believer, this verse is a source of certainty. One can rest knowing that the Qur’anic narrative is true and that all competing narratives are false to the extent they contradict it. It also connects the story back to the fundamental purpose of revelation: to call people to worship the One true God. The verse subtly reminds the People of the Book that the scriptures they possess, if examined honestly, would lead them to this same conclusion.
63 | If they turn away, then surely Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of the corruptors. | The verse addresses the possible negative response to the preceding challenge and the clear truth. “If they turn away,” meaning if after all these arguments, the clear narrative, and the invitation to the Mubahala, they still reject the message and refuse to accept the truth about Jesus, then the matter is handed over to Allah. The phrase “then surely Allah has perfect knowledge of the corruptors” carries both a warning and a comfort. “The corruptors,” al-mufsidun, are those who cause corruption on earth by spreading false beliefs, misleading others, and rejecting divine guidance. Their turning away is not a sign of the truth’s weakness; it is a sign of their own inner corruption. Allah’s perfect knowledge of them implies that He is fully aware of their motives, their obstinacy, and the harm they cause. No corrupt act escapes His attention. Consequently, He will deal with them accordingly, in this life or the next, or both. For the Prophet and the believers, this verse provides relief from the burden of feeling responsible for others’ rejection. Once the message has been delivered with clarity and the invitation has been made, the role of the messenger is complete. The outcome is in Allah’s hands. He knows who is sincere and who is corrupt, and His judgment is flawless. This also serves as a warning to the corruptors themselves: you may turn away now, but you cannot hide from Allah’s knowledge. Your corruption is recorded. For the believer today, this verse teaches the balance between active dawah and tawakkul. One should present the truth with wisdom and sincerity, but not despair or become frustrated when people reject it, because the ultimate judgment lies with Allah, who knows exactly who is corrupt and who is merely mistaken.
64 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Let us come to common terms: that we will worship none but Allah, associate none with Him, nor take one another as lords instead of Allah.” But if they turn away, then say, “Bear witness that we have submitted ˹to Allah alone˺.” | The surah now opens a direct and gracious invitation to the People of the Book, calling them to a shared foundation. The Prophet is instructed to say, “O People of the Book! Let us come to common terms.” The phrase “common terms,” kalimatin sawa’in baynana wa baynakum, is a beautiful expression of seeking unity on a just and equal basis. The invitation is not a demand for immediate conversion to all the specifics of Islamic law, but a call to unite upon the core principle that all true prophets taught. The common terms are then specified: “that we will worship none but Allah, associate none with Him, nor take one another as lords instead of Allah.” This threefold agreement encapsulates pure monotheism. First, exclusive worship of Allah alone. Second, the rejection of shirk, associating partners with Him, whether they be idols, saints, angels, or prophets. Third, the rejection of the deification of human beings, “nor take one another as lords.” This was a direct critique of the way some Jews and Christians elevated their rabbis, priests, and saints to the status of lawgivers or objects of worship, and it also struck at the Christian worship of Jesus. The verse then anticipates the reaction: “But if they turn away, then say, ‘Bear witness that we have submitted to Allah alone.’” If they refuse even this basic common ground, the believers are to declare their own clear identity. They are Muslims, submitted to Allah alone, and they bear witness to this truth. The rejection of others does not change their stance. This verse is immensely significant for interfaith dialogue. It shows that Islam calls to what is already present in the original teachings of the Torah and Gospel. It prioritizes agreement on the core before discussing differences. The believer learns to extend this invitation with wisdom and clarity, and if it is rejected, to maintain a firm but dignified witness to the truth.
65 | O People of the Book! Why do you argue about Abraham, while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him? Do you not understand? | The invitation to common ground leads naturally to a discussion about Abraham, the father of monotheism whom both the Jews and Christians claimed as their own. The verse asks with a tone of reproach, “O People of the Book! Why do you argue about Abraham?” The Jews claimed Abraham was a Jew, and the Christians claimed he was a Christian, each trying to claim him for their own faith community. The Qur’an now demolishes this dispute with a simple historical fact: “while the Torah and the Gospel were not revealed until long after him?” Abraham lived centuries before Moses, to whom the Torah was given, and even longer before Jesus, who received the Gospel. The concepts of Judaism and Christianity as organized religions with their specific laws and scriptures did not exist in Abraham’s time. How could he have been a Jew or a Christian? The rhetorical question “Do you not understand?” challenges their intellect and their honesty. The argument is clear and irrefutable. Abraham was a man who submitted to the one God, and his faith predated all later divisions. This verse teaches the importance of historical truth in theological debates. Claims must be backed by reality. For the believer, it underscores that Islam is not a new religion but a return to the original faith of Abraham, stripped of later additions and distortions. The argument also prepares the way for the next verse, which clarifies exactly what Abraham was.
66 | Here you are! You disputed about what you have ˹little˺ knowledge of, but why do you now argue about what you have no knowledge of? Allah knows and you do not know. | The reproach intensifies. “Here you are! You disputed about what you have little knowledge of.” This refers to matters about which they had some scriptural information but misunderstood and distorted, like the laws of the Torah or certain stories. They argued among themselves and with others based on this incomplete knowledge. The verse then points out the greater absurdity: “but why do you now argue about what you have no knowledge of?” This refers to the faith and status of Abraham, a subject on which their scriptures did not give them clear information, yet they argued vehemently. Their disputes were based on conjecture, tribal pride, and religious rivalry rather than revealed truth. The statement “Allah knows and you do not know” closes the matter. Allah alone possesses complete knowledge of the unseen and of the true state of Abraham. Human beings are limited and should not speak with certainty about matters where they lack knowledge. This verse is a powerful lesson in intellectual humility. The believer is taught not to argue about things of which they have no sure knowledge, especially in religion. Many sectarian conflicts and theological divisions arise from people insisting on claims that are not firmly grounded in revelation. The cure is to acknowledge the limits of one’s knowledge and to refer matters to Allah. It also warns against the arrogance that leads people to contest the truth brought by a prophet on the basis of their own flawed traditions.
67 | Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian; he submitted in all uprightness and was not a polytheist. | Having refuted the claims of the Jews and Christians about Abraham, the surah now provides the definitive description of the patriarch. “Abraham was neither a Jew nor a Christian.” This is a categorical denial of the labels that the later religious communities tried to impose on him. Judaism, as a formal religious system, emerged long after Abraham with the revelation of the Torah. Christianity came into existence even later. To call Abraham a Jew or a Christian is an anachronism and a misrepresentation. The verse then provides the positive identification: “he submitted in all uprightness,” kana hanifan musliman. The term hanif refers to one who turns away from all false beliefs and inclines solely toward the truth. Musliman means that he was one who submitted entirely to the will of Allah. This is the core of his identity. He was not defined by a later tribal or denominational label but by his pure, undivided devotion to the one God. The phrase “and was not a polytheist,” wa ma kana min al-mushrikin, emphasizes that his faith was absolutely free from any form of associating partners with Allah. This is crucial because the Arabian polytheists also claimed descent from Abraham and claimed to follow his religion, yet they worshipped idols. The verse cleanses Abraham’s legacy of all such associations. For the believer, this verse defines the true identity of a follower of Abraham. It is not a name or a lineage but a state of pure monotheism and submission. The verse invites both Jews and Christians to abandon their partisan claims and to emulate Abraham in being hanif and muslim. It also reminds Muslims that their claim to Abraham is valid only if they truly uphold the pure monotheism he embodied.
68 | Indeed, those who have the best claim to Abraham are his followers, this Prophet, and the believers. And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe. | The surah now establishes who truly belongs to the legacy of Abraham, after clarifying what Abraham was not. “Indeed, those who have the best claim to Abraham” are not those who simply boast of lineage or label themselves with his name, but those who are closest to him in faith and practice. The verse identifies three groups. First, “his followers,” meaning those in his own time who accepted his message and walked his path of monotheism. Second, “this Prophet,” meaning Muhammad. The Prophet Muhammad is specifically singled out because his message is the purest and most complete restoration of the Abrahamic faith. Third, “and the believers,” meaning the Muslim community that follows the Prophet Muhammad. Their faith in the one God and their submission to His will make them the true heirs of Abraham’s spiritual legacy. The criterion is not bloodline but belief. An authentic hadith explains that when Allah wanted to show Abraham his descendants in Paradise, Abraham saw a community that included people he did not recognize. He was told they are from your progeny, but they came after you, indicating that the Muslim ummah, though chronologically distant, is spiritually his direct heir. The verse ends with a comforting promise: “And Allah is the Guardian of those who believe.” Al-Wali, the Guardian, is the protector, ally, and helper. The believers who follow Abraham’s path are under Allah’s direct care. For the believer, this verse is an invitation to earn the honour of being counted among the true followers of Abraham by embodying his faith, not just claiming his name. It also severs the notion of religious exclusivity based on race or tribe.
69 | Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you ˹believers˺. They mislead none but themselves, yet they fail to perceive it. | After clarifying the truth about Abraham and the true path of faith, the surah warns the believers of a hidden danger. “Some of the People of the Book wish to mislead you believers.” This refers to a faction among the Jews and Christians who, driven by envy and resentment, actively schemed to create doubts and confusion among the Muslims. They would ask seemingly innocent questions, twist scripture, or sow discord, hoping to turn the believers away from their religion. The verse exposes their intention. However, it immediately neutralizes the fear: “They mislead none but themselves, yet they fail to perceive it.” The plot to mislead others ultimately rebounds on the plotters. By engaging in deception and working against the truth, they increase their own misguidance and incur a greater burden of sin upon themselves. They think they are harming the believers, but they are actually deepening their own spiritual ruin. The phrase “yet they fail to perceive it” highlights their delusion. They are so blinded by their malice that they cannot see the true damage they are causing to their own souls. For the believer, this verse is a comforting shield. It does not deny that some people will try to mislead, but it assures that the sincere believer who holds firm to the truth will not be harmed by such schemes in the ultimate sense. The enemy’s efforts are wasted. It also teaches that plotting against the truth is a self-destructive act. The believer, therefore, responds not with fear or anxiety, but with vigilance and trust in Allah’s protection. The verse also emphasizes the importance of being aware of malicious intentions while maintaining spiritual confidence.
70 | O People of the Book! Why do you reject the signs of Allah while you bear witness ˹to their truth˺? | The surah now turns directly to address the People of the Book again with a penetrating question. “O People of the Book! Why do you reject the signs of Allah while you bear witness to their truth?” The “signs of Allah” here include the verses of the Quran, the prophecies in their own scriptures about the coming of a final prophet, and the clear proofs of Prophet Muhammad’s truthfulness. The verse asserts that they are not merely ignorant; they actually recognize and bear witness to the truth of these signs. Their own books foretold the coming of a prophet from the line of Ishmael, with specific descriptions that matched Muhammad. Many among the Jewish scholars and Christian monks recognized him as the promised one. The question “why do you reject” exposes the irrationality and injustice of their position. Rejection in the face of knowledge and witness is not an innocent error; it is a willful suppression of the truth. The motive is often worldly: fear of losing status, leadership, or material gain. The verse forces a moment of introspection. It is not a rhetorical accusation but a call to conscience. For the believer, this verse underscores a universal principle: the greatest tragedy is not ignorance but the rejection of truth after it has been recognized. It also serves as a warning to all believers not to become like those who know the truth yet fail to act upon it. The verse encourages Muslims to be sincere in seeking and following the truth wherever it appears, and to avoid the arrogance that leads to denial even when the signs are clear.
71 | O People of the Book! Why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide the truth knowingly? | The rebuke continues with an even more specific charge. “O People of the Book! Why do you mix the truth with falsehood and hide the truth knowingly?” The first part, “mix the truth with falsehood,” labis al-haqqa bi al-batil, describes a deliberate tactic of some scholars among them. They would take the genuine revelations in the Torah and Gospel and blend them with fabricated interpretations, oral traditions, and outright distortions. By doing this, they made it difficult for ordinary people to discern what was from God and what was human invention. The truth became obscured by layers of falsehood. The second part, “and hide the truth knowingly,” addresses the concealment of clear prophecies about the coming of Muhammad and other truths that they were obligated to disclose. They knew the truth but chose to suppress it for personal or communal advantage. The adverb “knowingly” emphasizes that this was not an honest mistake but a conscious decision. This verse exposes a grave spiritual crime: the manipulation of divine scripture. It warns the People of the Book, and by extension all believers, about the severe consequences of distorting and concealing religious knowledge. Scholars and teachers bear a heavy responsibility to transmit knowledge faithfully. The verse also serves as a lesson for Muslims to be truthful in their own speech, to avoid mixing personal opinions or cultural biases with the clear teachings of Islam, and to never hide beneficial knowledge out of fear or favour. The clarity and purity of the message must be preserved at all costs.
72 | A group among the People of the Book said ˹to one another˺, “Believe in what has been revealed to the believers in the morning and reject it in the evening, so they may abandon their faith.” | The surah now exposes a specific cunning plot hatched by a faction of the People of the Book. “A group among the People of the Book said to one another…” This indicates a secret conspiracy. Their plan was to outwardly profess belief in the Quran in the morning, and then publicly reject it in the evening. The idea behind this was psychological warfare. They would say, “We are learned people of scripture; we looked into the Quran and initially thought it was true, but upon deeper inspection we found it to be false.” The goal was “so they may abandon their faith.” By creating a public display of acceptance followed by dramatic rejection, they hoped to plant seeds of doubt in the hearts of the believers. The new and some weak-hearted Muslims might think, “If these scholars who know the previous scriptures found a flaw, maybe our faith is mistaken.” This tactic reveals a deep understanding of human psychology by the plotters. However, their scheme was exposed by Allah, rendering it ineffective against those with true faith. For the believer, this verse is a timeless warning about the methods used by those who seek to undermine faith. It teaches that attacks are not always frontal; they can come through feigned friendship, false testimonials, and the manipulation of credibility. The defense against such tactics is a solid grounding in knowledge and a strong connection to the community of believers. The verse also illustrates the lengths to which some opponents of truth will go, driven by jealousy and hatred. It reminds the believers to be cautious and to seek refuge in Allah from the plots of the schemers.
73 | And only believe those who follow your religion.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Surely, ˹the only˺ true guidance is Allah’s guidance.” ˹They also said,˺ “Do not believe that someone will receive ˹revealed˺ knowledge similar to yours or argue against you before your Lord.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Indeed, all bounty is in the Hands of Allah—He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.” | The exposure of their secret talks continues. They told one another, “And only believe those who follow your religion.” This means they should not genuinely believe in the Prophet Muhammad or his message; their feigned belief in the morning and rejection in the evening was only to be performed before those who already shared their own faith or perhaps to convince their own followers. The instruction was to keep their true allegiance to their own community. The Prophet is then commanded to respond with the truth: “Say, ‘Surely, the only true guidance is Allah’s guidance.’” This negates their claim that guidance is confined to their group. True guidance is what Allah gives, and it is now with the believers. The plotters further said, “Do not believe that someone will receive revealed knowledge similar to yours or argue against you before your Lord.” They were jealous that prophethood had been given to an Arab, an Ishmaelite, rather than remaining among the Israelites. They feared that this new revelation would be used as an argument against them on the Day of Judgment. The Prophet is told to respond: “Indeed, all bounty is in the Hands of Allah—He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing.” Prophethood and revelation are Allah’s bounty; He chooses whom He wills. No one can monopolize His grace. This response breaks the back of their envy and affirms the divine freedom to bestow favour. For the believer, this verse instills confidence that Allah’s guidance is above all human claims of exclusivity and that His bounty is vast and wise.
74 | He chooses whoever He wills to receive His mercy. And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty. | The previous verse’s refutation of the plotters’ jealousy finds its complete statement here. “He chooses whoever He wills to receive His mercy.” The mercy referred to is primarily prophethood and the revelation of scripture, but it extends to all forms of divine selection, including guidance, knowledge, and righteousness. The choice is entirely Allah’s prerogative. Human envy, objections, and scheming cannot alter this reality. No nation can claim a permanent monopoly on divine favour. The fact that the final prophet emerged from the descendants of Ishmael rather than Isaac is a demonstration of this truth. The verse then magnifies the attribute of Allah: “And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.” The Arabic word for bounty here is fadl, which encompasses grace, favour, and generosity. “Infinite” translates al-‘azim, the magnificent, the tremendous. Allah’s bounty is not limited; it cannot be hoarded by one group or exhausted by His giving. The fact that He gave to one does not mean He cannot give to another. This truth is a comfort to all believers who feel that others may have more worldly blessings, knowledge, or status. The true bounty is with Allah, and He gives it to whom He wills according to His wisdom. The verse also teaches the Muslim to never be envious of the spiritual or worldly gifts given to others, because Allah’s treasures are limitless. Instead, one should ask Allah from His bounty. It is a verse of hope and a reminder that Allah’s generosity is not constrained by human expectations.
75 | There are some among the People of the Book who, if entrusted with a stack of gold, will readily return it. Yet there are others who, if entrusted with a single coin, will not repay it unless you constantly demand it. This is because they say, “We are not accountable for ˹exploiting˺ the Gentiles.” And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly. | The surah moves from exposing their theological schemes to exposing their ethical inconsistencies. It acknowledges that the People of the Book are not a monolithic group. “There are some among the People of the Book who, if entrusted with a stack of gold, will readily return it.” A “stack of gold,” qintar, refers to a massive amount of wealth. These trustworthy individuals are scrupulous in fulfilling their trusts, regardless of the sum. However, “yet there are others who, if entrusted with a single coin, will not repay it unless you constantly demand it.” The single coin, dinar, represents a small amount. Such people are dishonest and will default on even minor debts unless pursued relentlessly. The verse then traces this moral failing to a false theological justification: “This is because they say, ‘We are not accountable for exploiting the Gentiles.’” Some among them believed that the prohibitions against theft and fraud applied only within their own community, but that taking the property of non-Jews or non-People of the Book was permissible. This was a corruption of their religion, an invention of a self-serving loophole. The verse concludes, “And so they attribute lies to Allah knowingly.” By claiming divine sanction for their dishonesty, they commit a double crime: theft and blasphemy. For the believer, this verse is a powerful lesson that religion must govern all dealings with all people, not just co-religionists. Ethical integrity is universal. It also warns against the temptation to twist scripture to justify one’s worldly desires. The trustworthy are praised, and the treacherous are condemned, irrespective of their religious label.
76 | Absolutely! Those who honour their trusts and shun evil—surely Allah loves those who are mindful ˹of Him˺. | The contrast drawn in the previous verse between the trustworthy and the treacherous is now resolved with a clear moral principle. “Absolutely! Those who honour their trusts and shun evil…” The word “absolutely,” bala, forcefully negates the false claim of the dishonest group that they have no accountability toward outsiders. It asserts the true standard. “Those who honour their trusts” refers to fulfilling all trusts, whether financial, contractual, or spiritual, with everyone, regardless of their faith. “And shun evil,” wa yattaqi, means they are mindful of Allah, avoiding what He has forbidden. The combination of fulfilling trusts and having taqwa is the essence of a sound moral character. The verse then gives the ultimate reward: “surely Allah loves those who are mindful of Him.” Allah’s love is the highest aspiration. It is not reserved for those who merely perform rituals, but is intimately tied to ethical conduct in social and financial dealings. Honouring a trust is an act of worship that attracts divine love. This verse corrects the moral relativism of those who think their actions can be justified by a corrupt theology. True piety is demonstrated in honesty and integrity. For the believer, it is a reminder that every transaction, every promise, and every debt is a test of faith. Being trustworthy is not just a social virtue; it is a direct command from Allah and a path to His love. The verse also links taqwa with amana, teaching that mindfulness of Allah naturally expresses itself as trustworthiness.
77 | Indeed, those who trade Allah’s covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain will have no share in the Hereafter. Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them on the Day of Judgment. And they will suffer a painful punishment. | Continuing the theme of trust and honesty, the surah now delivers a severe warning against breaking covenants and oaths. “Indeed, those who trade Allah’s covenant and their oaths for a fleeting gain…” The “covenant” refers to the primordial covenant of faith as well as any solemn pledge made in Allah’s name. The “oaths” include sworn agreements and promises. Trading them means breaking them in exchange for some worldly benefit—money, status, or convenience. The gain is “fleeting,” temporary and worthless in the grand scheme. The punishment for this betrayal is described in terrifying terms. “They will have no share in the Hereafter.” This is a complete deprivation of any good in the eternal life. Then the verse intensifies: “Allah will neither speak to them, nor look at them, nor purify them on the Day of Judgment.” These three negations represent the ultimate rejection. Allah’s speaking to someone implies mercy and honour; His not speaking to them signifies anger and dismissal. His looking at them would indicate care and compassion; His not looking means they are beneath His regard. His purifying them would mean forgiveness; His refusal to purify means they are left with their sins. This is a picture of absolute divine contempt and abandonment. The verse concludes, “And they will suffer a painful punishment,” adding physical torment to spiritual rejection. This severe warning underscores the sanctity of oaths and covenants in Islam. Breaking a promise, especially one made in Allah’s name, is not a small matter. It is a form of contempt for the divine. Believers must take their words and contracts seriously, knowing that worldly gain from deceit is a ruinous trade.
78 | There are some among them who distort the Book with their tongues to make you think this ˹distortion˺ is from the Book—but it is not what the Book says. They say, “It is from Allah”—but it is not from Allah. And ˹so˺ they attribute lies to Allah knowingly. | The exposure of the dishonest continues with the most serious form of treachery: scriptural distortion. “There are some among them who distort the Book with their tongues.” This refers to a practice where certain Jewish and Christian scholars would deliberately twist the words of the scripture during recitation, altering the pronunciation, intonation, or even inserting words to change the meaning, all while pretending to read the authentic text. The goal was “to make you think this distortion is from the Book.” They deceived their audiences into believing that what they were reciting was the genuine revelation. But the Quran categorically states: “but it is not what the Book says.” The Arabic is “wa ma huwa min al-kitab”—it is not part of the Book at all. Furthermore, “They say, ‘It is from Allah’—but it is not from Allah.” They falsely attribute their fabrications to God. The verse ends with the now-familiar refrain: “And so they attribute lies to Allah knowingly.” This is the gravest sin: forging speech and claiming it as divine. The verse exposes the corruption that had occurred in the transmission and interpretation of previous scriptures. It also serves as a severe warning to all who handle religious texts and teachings. To alter the word of God, add to it, or subtract from it while claiming divine authority is to invite destruction upon oneself. For the Muslim, this reinforces the divine protection of the Quran, which Allah promised to preserve, unlike the earlier scriptures that were left to the care of their scholars. It also teaches the importance of preserving the purity of Islamic teachings and speaking truthfully about Allah, never attributing to Him what He did not say.
79 | It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to say to people, “Worship me instead of Allah.” Rather, he would say, “Be devoted to the worship of your Lord ˹alone˺”—in accordance with what these prophets read in the Scripture and what they taught. | After exposing the distortions of corrupt scholars, the surah now clarifies the true character of the prophets, answering the false claim that any prophet could have commanded people to worship him. “It is not appropriate for someone who Allah has blessed with the Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood to say to people, ‘Worship me instead of Allah.’” The word “appropriate,” ma kana, indicates an impossibility; such a thing is unthinkable for a true prophet. The gifts mentioned—Scripture, wisdom, and prophethood—are the very things that deepen a person’s knowledge of Allah and humility before Him. A true prophet would never abuse these gifts by calling people to his own worship. Instead, “Rather, he would say, ‘Be devoted to the worship of your Lord alone.’” The term “devoted,” rabbaniyyin, refers to those who are deeply learned and dedicated to the Lord. The prophet would call people to become devoted worshippers of Allah. This is “in accordance with what these prophets read in the Scripture and what they taught.” The scripture they taught from contained the command to worship Allah alone, and their teaching reinforced that. This verse refutes the Christian claim that Jesus asked people to worship him, or that he is divine. Jesus was given the scripture and prophethood; it is inconceivable that he would contradict his own scripture by demanding worship. For the believer, this verse establishes a clear criterion: any leader, no matter how pious or knowledgeable, who calls people to venerate him beyond what is due, is going against the prophetic model. True scholars and prophets direct all worship to Allah alone.
80 | And he would never ask you to take angels and prophets as lords. Would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted? | The verse continues the same theme, expanding the scope of what a true prophet would never command. “And he would never ask you to take angels and prophets as lords.” This addresses the various forms of excessive veneration that had crept into religious communities. Some nations elevated angels to the status of divine beings, praying to them or claiming they were daughters of God. The Christians elevated Jesus, a prophet, to the status of Lord and God. Even among the Jews, some turned Ezra into a figure of semi-divine reverence, and among Muslims later, some would excessively venerate saints. A true prophet would never sanction such practices. The rhetorical question follows: “Would he ask you to disbelieve after you have submitted?” The word “submitted,” muslimun, indicates that the people have already entered into Islam, the state of surrender to Allah alone. For a prophet to then command them to worship him or others would be an act of leading them back to disbelief, kufr. This is logically and theologically impossible. The verse is a powerful argument against the deification of prophets. It points out the inconsistency of believing in a prophet and then worshipping him or elevating others to divine status. The submission to Allah is the very heart of the message. The verse thus draws a clear line between legitimate love and respect for prophets and angels and the worship that is due to Allah alone. For the believer, it serves as a warning against the subtle forms of shirk that can arise from excessive devotion to righteous figures, and it reinforces the pure monotheism that is the core of Islam.
81 | ˹Remember˺ when Allah made a covenant with the prophets, ˹saying,˺ “Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom, if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him.” He added, “Do you affirm this covenant and accept this commitment?” They said, “Yes, we do.” Allah said, “Then bear witness, and I too am a Witness.” | After clarifying that no prophet would ever command worship of himself or of angels, the surah now unveils the solemn covenant that binds all prophets together and prepares for the coming of the final messenger. “Remember when Allah made a covenant with the prophets.” This is a unique and weighty scene from the unseen realm. Allah gathered the souls of all the prophets—from Adam to Jesus—and took from them a binding covenant, the mithaq. The content of the covenant is: “Now that I have given you the Book and wisdom, if there comes to you a messenger confirming what you have, you must believe in him and support him.” The phrase “the Book and wisdom” refers to the scripture and the deep understanding that each prophet received. The condition is clear: if a messenger appears during their lifetime, whose message confirms the truth they already possess, they are obligated to believe in him and actively support him. This covenant is understood to be specifically about Prophet Muhammad, as he is the messenger who would confirm all previous scriptures and come after all of them. Every prophet took this pledge, binding themselves and their true followers to recognize and follow the final prophet when he appeared. Allah then asks, “Do you affirm this covenant and accept this commitment?” The question, though the answer is known, serves to emphasize the gravity of the contract. The prophets replied with absolute submission, “Yes, we do.” They accepted the heavy responsibility. Then Allah declared, “Then bear witness, and I too am a Witness.” This makes the covenant a witnessed, unbreakable agreement. Allah’s own witness is the greatest possible guarantee. This verse has profound implications. It shows that Islam is not a competitor to previous religions but their intended culmination. The prophets themselves prepared their nations for the coming of Muhammad. It also explains why Moses and Jesus, in their authentic teachings, pointed toward the final prophet. For the believer, this verse reinforces the unity of the prophetic mission and the obligation upon all people, including those who follow earlier prophets, to accept Muhammad when his message reaches them.
82 | Whoever turns back after this, they will be the rebellious. | Having established the weighty covenant taken from all the prophets, the surah now pronounces the verdict on anyone who turns away from this binding agreement. “Whoever turns back after this, they will be the rebellious.” The act of turning back, tawalla, here refers to rejecting the covenant by refusing to believe in and support the messenger who confirms the previous scriptures—specifically Prophet Muhammad. The “this” refers to the covenant itself, which is an affirmation of the greatest importance, witnessed by Allah Himself. To turn back after such a solemn pledge is not a minor slip; it is outright rebellion. The word “rebellious,” al-fasiqun, describes those who step outside the bounds of obedience, who knowingly defy the divine command. In Quranic terminology, fisq is a severe state of moral and spiritual deviation. This verse applies directly to the People of the Book who recognized the truth of the Prophet Muhammad but rejected him out of pride, envy, or fear of losing their status. They had the description of the coming prophet in their scriptures, which was part of the covenant their prophets had passed down. Their rejection was a direct violation of that covenant. However, the warning extends to all who, after receiving clear knowledge of Islam, turn away. The verse serves as a sobering reminder that the covenant is not merely an ancient story; it is a living obligation. The believer who accepts Islam is fulfilling this ancient covenant; the one who rejects it is joining the ranks of the rebellious. The verse also teaches the importance of honouring commitments made to Allah. True faith requires steadfastness, and breaking a covenant after affirming it is a path to spiritual ruin.
83 | Do they desire a way other than Allah’s—knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly, and to Him they will ˹all˺ be returned? | After declaring the rebellious nature of those who turn back from the covenant, the surah poses a rhetorical question that appeals to both reason and the observable reality of the universe. “Do they desire a way other than Allah’s?” The word “desire,” yabghun, suggests a deliberate seeking after something else, an active preference for a different path. The “way of Allah,” din Allah, is Islam, the religion of submission to the one true God. The question is incredulous: knowing what they know, how can they seek an alternative? The verse then provides the compelling evidence: “knowing that all those in the heavens and the earth submit to His Will, willingly or unwillingly.” Everything in the cosmos—the angels in the heavens, the sun and moon, the mountains and trees, the animals on earth—all follow the divine law that governs existence. They are all in a state of Islam, submission. The angels submit willingly in perfect obedience. Even those humans who reject faith submit unwillingly in terms of their physical existence; their bodies are governed by Allah’s biological laws, and they cannot escape His decree of life and death. The entire universe is a vast display of submission to the Creator. In this context, the one who rejects the religion of submission is a glaring anomaly, a rebel against the very order to which everything else conforms. The verse concludes, “and to Him they will all be returned,” reminding that all will face their Lord for judgment. The universal submission of creation will be mirrored in the Day of Judgment, when every knee will bow before Allah. For the believer, this verse inspires awe and a sense of belonging to the cosmic order. By embracing Islam, one aligns with the sun, the stars, the angels, and all of creation in the worship of the one God. It also serves as a powerful argument against those who seek other paths, pointing them to the overwhelming testimony of the universe itself.
84 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “We believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants; and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord—we make no distinction between any of them, and to Him we ˹fully˺ submit.” | In response to the rejection of the People of the Book and their sectarian divisions, the Prophet is instructed to make a clear and comprehensive declaration of faith that defines the Muslim identity in relation to all previous prophets. “Say, ‘We believe in Allah.’” The declaration begins with the core: belief in Allah, the one true God. Then it expands to the revelations: “and what has been revealed to us,” meaning the Quran, the final scripture given to the Muslim ummah. Then it reaches back through history: “and what was revealed to Abraham, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and his descendants.” The mention of Abraham and his sons Ishmael and Isaac, and Jacob and the tribes, encompasses the patriarchs of both the Arab and Israelite lines. Then the major prophets are named: “and what was given to Moses, Jesus, and other prophets from their Lord.” Moses, given the Torah, and Jesus, given the Gospel, are specifically honoured. The phrase “and other prophets” embraces all the messengers not named, ensuring no prophet is excluded from the Muslim’s belief. The crucial clause follows: “we make no distinction between any of them.” This does not mean they are all equal in rank, but that in terms of believing in them as true prophets, the Muslim does not discriminate. The Jews rejected Jesus and Muhammad; the Christians rejected Muhammad; the Muslims accept all. Then the declaration culminates: “and to Him we fully submit,” wa nahnu lahu muslimun. This is the final seal: we are those who submit to Allah alone, following the path of all His prophets. This verse is a magnificent summary of the Islamic creed regarding prophethood. It establishes the universal, inclusive nature of Islam, which honours the entire chain of divine guidance. For the believer, it fosters love for all prophets and a sense of connection to a vast, blessed history. It also serves as a powerful invitation to the People of the Book to join this comprehensive faith that respects Moses and Jesus while embracing the final message.
85 | Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them, and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers. | After the beautiful declaration of faith in all prophets and submission to Allah, the surah delivers a definitive statement that clarifies the exclusive nature of salvation after the coming of the final revelation. “Whoever seeks a way other than Islam, it will never be accepted from them.” The word “Islam” here is understood in its specific, completed sense as the religion brought by Prophet Muhammad. While previous prophets taught Islam in its generic form of submission to Allah, and their followers were Muslims in that sense, after the advent of the final prophet, the only acceptable path to Allah is the specific shariah of Islam. The verse states that any other religion—whether it be Judaism, Christianity, or any other belief system—will not be accepted by Allah if it is chosen after the truth of Islam has been made clear. The reason is not arbitrary; it is because these other paths contain distortions or incomplete laws, while Islam is the preserved, complete, and final guidance. The verse then spells out the consequence: “and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers.” The losers, al-khasirun, are those who suffer the ultimate loss—the loss of Paradise, the loss of Allah’s pleasure, and the loss of their own souls. This is the most tragic form of bankruptcy. This verse does not contradict the fact that those who sincerely followed Moses or Jesus before the coming of Muhammad will be rewarded; it applies to those who, after receiving the message of Islam, reject it. The verse underscores the urgency of the call to Islam and the importance of not delaying one’s acceptance. For the believer, it is a source of certainty and gratitude, knowing that they are on the only path that leads to eternal success. It also motivates the sharing of Islam with others, because the stakes are eternal.
86 | How will Allah guide a people who chose to disbelieve after they had believed, acknowledged the Messenger to be true, and received clear proofs? For Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people. | The surah now addresses a perplexing and tragic case: those who had faith, recognized the truth, and then turned away. “How will Allah guide a people who chose to disbelieve after they had believed?” The question “how,” kayfa, expresses astonishment and implies impossibility. These are not people who were ignorant; they had already experienced the light of faith. Yet they “chose to disbelieve”—their rejection was a deliberate choice, not a mistake. The verse then lists the aggravating factors: “acknowledged the Messenger to be true, and received clear proofs.” They had personally recognized the truthfulness of the Prophet Muhammad, perhaps through his character, his miracles, or the Quran’s message. They had been given “clear proofs,” bayyinat, which left no room for doubt. Such people were well-known in Medina; some were from the Jewish community who knew the signs of the awaited prophet as they knew their own children, yet refused to follow him. When a person reaches such a level of clarity and still rejects, their hearts become sealed by their own choice. The verse explains, “For Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.” This does not mean Allah is unwilling; it means that by their persistent wrongdoing and rejection, they have made themselves unworthy of His guiding grace. Allah’s guidance is a gift, but it is withdrawn from those who consistently and arrogantly refuse it. The verse teaches a profound lesson about the nature of faith and apostasy. Faith is not a one-time event; it must be guarded. Those who turn away after clear knowledge are in a perilous state, and their return becomes increasingly difficult. For the believer, it is a warning to treat faith as the most precious possession and to constantly pray for steadfastness, never assuming that one is immune from deviation.
87 | Their reward is that they will be condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity. | Having described the tragic state of those who disbelieve after clear proofs, the surah now announces their punishment in terms of a universal condemnation. “Their reward is that they will be condemned by Allah, the angels, and all of humanity.” The word “reward,” jaza’, is used ironically; what awaits them is not a reward but a recompense befitting their crime. The condemnation, la’nah, means to be cursed, to be expelled from Allah’s mercy, and to be subject to disgrace. The source of this curse is staggering in its comprehensiveness. First, “by Allah,” the Creator Himself curses them. There is no greater calamity than to be cursed by the One who holds all power and mercy. Second, “the angels,” the pure and noble beings who inhabit the heavens, curse them, adding a cosmic dimension to their disgrace. Third, “all of humanity,” al-nas ajma’in, means that on the Day of Judgment, the entirety of mankind will bear witness against them and call for their punishment. Some scholars explain that this applies to all people, both believers and disbelievers, each in their own way; believers will curse them for their wrongdoing, and even disbelievers will curse their leaders who misled them. This verse reveals the social and cosmic isolation that awaits those who reject faith after it has been made clear. In this life, they may have enjoyed prestige and followers, but in the Hereafter, they stand condemned by the entire creation. For the believer, this verse is a stark warning about the consequences of apostasy. It also provides comfort, knowing that those who oppose the truth and harm the believers will not escape divine justice. The unanimity of creation’s condemnation underlines the severity of their crime.
88 | They will be in Hell forever. Their punishment will not be lightened, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺. | The curse described in the previous verse is now given its concrete, terrifying form. “They will be in Hell forever.” The phrase “forever,” khalidina fiha, indicates an eternal, unending stay for those who die in a state of disbelief after having clear proof. This is the punishment for the most severe form of kufr—rejecting the truth after recognizing it. The verse then denies any hope of relief: “Their punishment will not be lightened.” The torment will not decrease in intensity over time, nor will there be any breaks or moments of respite. In Hell, there is no gradual acclimatization to suffering; it remains fresh and severe. The second denial follows: “nor will they be delayed from it.” There will be no reprieve, no postponement, no temporary suspension of punishment. The suffering is continuous and unrelenting. This verse paints a terrifying picture that is meant to shake the heart of anyone who might be considering turning back from faith. It stands in sharp contrast to the descriptions of Paradise, where joy is eternal, ever-fresh, and uninterrupted. The eternal nature of both destinations is one of the most powerful motivators in the Quran. The verse also refutes any idea that Hell is only temporary for such people, as some have speculated. The text is explicit: khalidina, forever. For the believer, this verse serves as a shield against complacency and a powerful deterrent from the path of apostasy. It also highlights the immense value of the gift of faith, which saves one from such an unspeakable fate. The verse is a call to gratitude for guidance and a reminder of the seriousness of turning away.
89 | As for those who repent afterwards and mend their ways, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | Immediately after the terrifying description of eternal punishment, the surah opens the door of mercy wide, demonstrating the perfect balance of divine justice and forgiveness. “As for those who repent afterwards and mend their ways…” The word “repent,” tabu, means to turn back to Allah with sincere regret for one’s disbelief and sins. “Afterwards” refers to after their apostasy and their rejection despite clear proofs. Even for such a severe crime, the door is not closed. “Mend their ways,” aslahu, means they rectify their conduct, restore what they have corrupted, and demonstrate the genuineness of their repentance through righteous actions. True repentance is not just a verbal statement; it is a transformation of behaviour. The verse then declares, “then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The emphasis with “surely,” inna, provides absolute assurance. The names “All-Forgiving,” al-Ghafur, and “Most Merciful,” al-Rahim, are the perfect attributes to follow such a heavy warning. Al-Ghafur indicates that Allah covers and forgives sins completely, no matter how great. Al-Rahim indicates that His mercy is an integral part of His nature and is extended to His servants continuously. This verse prevents despair. No matter how far a person has strayed, no matter how openly they have rejected the truth, if they turn back to Allah before death, they will find Him ready to forgive. The condition is sincere repentance and amendment. This combination of severe warning and open mercy is characteristic of the Quran’s method of nurturing the soul, balancing fear and hope. For the believer, this verse is a lifeline. It also teaches that one should never lose hope for anyone, including those who have apostatized, for Allah’s mercy can reach them if they choose to return. It also encourages constant self-assessment and renewal of repentance.
90 | Indeed, those who disbelieve after having believed then increase in disbelief, their repentance will never be accepted. It is they who are astray. | The surah now qualifies the open door of mercy mentioned in the previous verse by describing a category for whom repentance is no longer available. “Indeed, those who disbelieve after having believed then increase in disbelief…” This describes a pattern of persistent, deepening rejection. They did not simply fall into disbelief once; they continued to add to their disbelief, whether by opposing the truth more aggressively, leading others away, or committing further crimes against the faith. Their hearts become progressively hardened and their rejection becomes their identity. The verse then states, “their repentance will never be accepted.” This does not mean that Allah refuses to forgive someone who turns to Him sincerely from any state. Rather, it describes the outcome of their own spiritual trajectory. When a person continuously increases in disbelief, they reach a point where they will never repent. Their hearts are so sealed, and their arrogance so entrenched, that genuine repentance becomes psychologically and spiritually impossible. Allah’s statement that it will never be accepted is a declaration of the reality of their condition: they will not produce sincere repentance, so no acceptance will occur. This is akin to the verse about Pharaoh, whose repentance at the moment of drowning was not accepted because it was not sincere faith but desperation. “It is they who are astray.” They are completely lost, far removed from the path of guidance. This verse serves as a powerful warning against the gradual nature of spiritual decline. Disbelief is not always a sudden fall; it can be a slow process of increasing sin and doubt. The believer must be vigilant against the small steps that lead to a hardened heart. It also teaches that while Allah’s mercy is vast, one must not presume upon it by persisting in sin.
91 | Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world, it would never be accepted from them. It is they who will suffer a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers. | The warning against dying in disbelief reaches its climax with a vivid image of the utter futility of worldly wealth on the Day of Judgment. “Indeed, if each of those who disbelieve then die as disbelievers were to offer a ransom of enough gold to fill the whole world…” The word “ransom,” fidya, refers to anything offered to secure release from punishment. The hypothetical ransom here is staggering: enough gold to fill the entire earth. Gold was the most precious and enduring form of wealth in human experience. If any person could possess such a quantity—impossible as it is—and offer it to escape the Fire, “it would never be accepted from them.” The rejection is absolute. No amount of material wealth can substitute for faith and righteous deeds. The verse then states the consequence: “It is they who will suffer a painful punishment, and they will have no helpers.” The painful punishment is the inevitable outcome, and the absence of helpers, nasirun, means no friend, family, ally, or intercessor can save them. This verse totally demolishes the materialistic worldview that equates wealth with security. On that Day, the currency of the Hereafter is not gold but iman. For the believer, this verse encourages prioritizing faith and good deeds over the accumulation of wealth, reminding them that what truly matters cannot be bought. It also serves as a stark warning to those who reject faith, thinking they can somehow escape through their resources or connections. The verse emphasizes the urgency of embracing faith before death, after which all doors close permanently.
92 | You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish. And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah. | After the heavy warnings about the fate of the disbelievers, the surah turns to the believers with a positive command that defines the standard of true piety. “You will never achieve righteousness until you donate some of what you cherish.” The word “righteousness,” al-birr, is a comprehensive term encompassing all goodness, piety, and moral excellence. It is the highest goal of a believer. The verse states that this lofty station is unattainable unless one gives from what they truly love. This is not about giving surplus or unwanted items; it is about sacrificing something dear to the heart—be it wealth, a favourite possession, or anything one is attached to. The essence of charity that pleases Allah is giving what is valuable to oneself, motivated by love for Allah above love for the material thing. This verse was reportedly revealed when some companions asked about the best charity, or specifically when Abu Talhah, a companion, gave away his most beloved garden, Biruha, in charity after hearing this verse. He had a beautiful orchard that he loved, and when he learned that righteousness required giving what one cherishes, he immediately donated it for the sake of Allah. The verse ends with assurance: “And whatever you give is certainly well known to Allah.” No act of charity, however small, is lost or forgotten. Allah knows the intention behind it, the value of what was given, and the sacrifice it entailed. He will reward it abundantly. For the believer, this verse sets the bar for generosity. It challenges one to examine their giving and ask: am I giving what I truly love, or just what I can easily spare? It encourages a deep, sacrificial generosity that purifies the soul from the love of material possessions and draws one closer to the station of true righteousness.
93 | All food was lawful for the children of Israel, except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true.” | The surah now addresses a specific argument made by the Jewish community in Medina. They criticized some aspects of Islamic dietary laws, claiming they were deviations from the original faith of Abraham. The verse sets the record straight. “All food was lawful for the children of Israel, except what Israel made unlawful for himself before the Torah was revealed.” “Israel” here refers to Prophet Jacob, who was also known as Israel. According to some traditions, Jacob suffered from a painful ailment—some say sciatica—and he vowed that if Allah healed him, he would abstain from the foods he loved most, such as camel meat and milk. This was a personal vow, not a divine legislation. Before the revelation of the Torah, all wholesome foods were lawful for his descendants. The dietary restrictions that the Jews later followed came as part of the Torah, often as a punishment for their transgressions, as mentioned in other Quranic verses. Thus, the Jewish dietary laws were not the original Abrahamic practice but a later development. The Prophet is then instructed to challenge them: “Say, ‘Bring the Torah and read it, if your claims are true.’” This is a bold demand. The Torah they possessed contained the dietary laws, but it also contained the evidence that these laws were given to Moses and were not part of the faith of Abraham or Jacob. By appealing to their own scripture, the verse exposes their inconsistency. For the believer, this verse teaches the importance of grounding religious claims in authentic scripture. It also shows that religious laws can change according to divine wisdom, and the final law of Islam is the perfected guidance. The verse also highlights the Quran’s deep knowledge of previous scriptures.
94 | Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah, they will be the ˹true˺ wrongdoers. | After the challenge to bring the Torah and read it, the surah pronounces judgment on those who, even after the truth is made clear, persist in falsehood. “Then whoever still fabricates lies about Allah…” The word “fabricates,” iftara, means to invent, to forge, to falsely attribute something to Allah. This includes persisting in false claims about dietary laws, about the nature of prophethood, or about any aspect of religion without evidence. After being invited to verify the truth from their own scripture, if they continue to spread falsehood, they are guilty of a most heinous crime. The verse declares, “they will be the true wrongdoers.” The word “wrongdoers,” al-zalimun, indicates those who commit the ultimate injustice: lying against the Creator. To attribute to Allah something He did not say is to violate the most sacred boundary. Such a person is not merely mistaken; they are a wrongdoer of the highest degree. The verse establishes a clear principle: once the truth has been presented and the evidence has been offered, continued opposition is not a matter of honest disagreement but of willful fabrication and injustice. This serves as a warning to all who handle religious knowledge—scholars, preachers, and ordinary believers—to be scrupulous in attributing only what is genuinely from Allah. For the believer, it instills a deep caution about speaking in the name of religion without knowledge. It also provides reassurance that those who spread lies about Allah will not escape His judgment, and the believer should not be swayed by their claims, no matter how authoritative they seem.
95 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah has declared the truth. So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.” | In response to all the false claims and fabrications, the Prophet is instructed to make a simple, powerful declaration that redirects the focus to the original, pure faith. “Say, ‘Allah has declared the truth.’” This is a statement of ultimate authority. What Allah says is the truth, and the truth is what He has revealed. There is no higher standard. This dismisses all human fabrications and disagreements by appealing to the divine verdict. The command then follows: “So follow the Way of Abraham, the upright—who was not a polytheist.” The “Way of Abraham,” millata Ibrahim, is the primordial religion of pure monotheism and submission to Allah. The description “upright,” hanifan, reinforces that Abraham was one who turned away completely from false gods and false beliefs, inclining solely toward the truth. The specific mention that he “was not a polytheist,” wa ma kana min al-mushrikin, is crucial. Both the Arab polytheists and the People of the Book claimed Abraham, yet the polytheists associated idols with Allah, and some among the People of the Book had fallen into shirk by deifying prophets and saints. Abraham was absolutely free from all such associations. Thus, to follow his way, one must abandon all forms of shirk and embrace pure tawhid, which is exactly what Islam represents. This verse is a call to return to the original, unadulterated faith of the patriarch, stripped of all later innovations. For the believer, it reinforces the identity of Islam as the direct continuation of Abraham’s faith. It also clarifies that true adherence to Abraham is not about lineage or labels but about embodying his pure monotheism.
96 | Surely the first House ˹of worship˺ established for humanity is the one at Bakkah—a blessed sanctuary and a guide for ˹all˺ people. | The discussion of Abraham’s faith naturally leads to the mention of the first house of worship built for that faith. “Surely the first House of worship established for humanity is the one at Bakkah.” Bakkah is another name for Makkah, and some say it refers specifically to the area of the Kaaba. The verse declares that the Kaaba is the very first sanctuary built on earth for the worship of the one true God. According to authentic traditions, the Kaaba was first built by the angels or by Adam himself, and after it was lost or destroyed over time, Prophet Abraham and his son Ishmael rebuilt its foundations, as mentioned in Surah Al-Baqarah. This makes the Kaaba the most ancient center of monotheism, predating the Temple of Solomon and all other places of worship. The verse describes it as “a blessed sanctuary,” mubarakan, meaning it is filled with divine blessings—spiritual, material, and historical. “And a guide for all people,” hudan lil-‘alamin, means that the House itself is a source of guidance. The pilgrimage to it, the prayers directed toward it, and the rituals performed there all guide humanity to the worship of Allah and the remembrance of Abraham’s legacy. This verse establishes the Kaaba’s superiority and its independence from the claims of Jews and Christians who argued that Jerusalem was the exclusive holy city. The Kaaba was there before Jerusalem, and Abraham, the father of both lines, was the one who raised its foundations. For the believer, this verse deepens the reverence for the Kaaba as the spiritual center of the Muslim world, a symbol of unity and a direct link to the most ancient tradition of monotheism.
97 | In it are clear signs and the standing-place of Abraham. Whoever enters it should be safe. Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people. And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of ˹any of His˺ creation. | The description of the sacred House continues with a list of its distinguishing features. “In it are clear signs.” These signs, ayatun bayyinat, include the physical and spiritual evidences of its sanctity: the Black Stone, the well of Zamzam, the fact that it is a place of safety, and the historical miracles associated with it. One of the most significant signs is “the standing-place of Abraham,” Maqam Ibrahim. This is the stone upon which Abraham stood while building the walls of the Kaaba with Ishmael. It still exists today near the Kaaba, bearing the imprint of his feet, a tangible link to the father of prophets. The verse then declares: “Whoever enters it should be safe.” The sanctuary of Makkah is a haram, a sacred precinct where violence is forbidden. This safety is both a divine command and a historical reality; since ancient times, even the pagan Arabs respected its sanctity, and Islam reinforced it. Then the verse establishes the great obligation: “Pilgrimage to this House is an obligation by Allah upon whoever is able among the people.” The Hajj is a duty that every adult, sane, free Muslim who is physically and financially capable must perform at least once in their lifetime. The obligation is directly from Allah, and it is a pillar of Islam. The verse concludes with a stern warning: “And whoever disbelieves, then surely Allah is not in need of His creation.” The one who denies the obligation of Hajj, despite being able, enters into disbelief because they have rejected a fundamental command of Allah. Allah is not in need of anyone’s worship; disobedience harms only the disobedient. For the believer, this verse emphasizes the immense importance of Hajj and the sacredness of Makkah, serving as a lifelong aspiration and a call to fulfil this duty with reverence.
98 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! Why do you deny the revelations of Allah, when Allah is a Witness to what you do?” | After establishing the centrality of the Kaaba and the religion of Abraham, the surah returns to the direct address to the People of the Book, who were opposing the Prophet and denying the truth. The command is: “Say, ‘O People of the Book! Why do you deny the revelations of Allah?’” The “revelations of Allah” here refer to the Quranic verses, the clear signs of the Prophet’s truthfulness, and the evidence in their own scriptures that confirmed him. The question “why” is an appeal to conscience and reason. What justification do they have for denying what they know to be true? The question implies that there is no valid reason, only stubbornness and worldly motives. Then the verse adds a profound reminder: “when Allah is a Witness to what you do?” Allah is fully aware of their denial, their secret schemes, their concealed acknowledgment of the truth, and their motives. The fact that Allah is witnessing their actions should terrify them and prompt them to reconsider. Nothing is hidden from Him. This verse is both a warning to the People of the Book and a consolation for the Prophet, reminding him that Allah sees the truth of the matter. For the believer, it reinforces the constant awareness of Allah’s witness over all actions and words. It also demonstrates the Quranic approach of addressing opponents with respectful yet penetrating questions that invite self-reflection.
99 | Say, “O People of the Book! Why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah—striving to make it ˹appear˺ crooked, while you are witnesses ˹to its truth˺? And Allah is never unaware of what you do.” | The rebuke continues with a more specific accusation. Not only do they deny the truth themselves, but they actively work to prevent others from accepting it. “Why do you turn the believers away from the Way of Allah?” This refers to their efforts to create doubts among the Muslims, to spread false narratives about the Prophet, and to discourage those who were considering embracing Islam. Their methods included twisting scripture, casting aspersions, and psychological manipulation. The phrase “striving to make it appear crooked” reveals their intent. The “Way of Allah” is inherently straight, but they desire to present it as crooked, filled with contradictions and errors, to repel people. This is a grave sin: not only being astray oneself but actively leading others astray. The verse adds, “while you are witnesses to its truth?” They possess the knowledge and the scriptural evidence that confirm the truth of the Prophet and his message. Their own books contain the signs, yet they hide them and lead others away. This makes their crime a double one: denial and misguidance. The verse concludes with the repeated warning: “And Allah is never unaware of what you do.” This emphasis on Allah’s awareness is meant to shake their hearts and warn them of the impending divine accountability. For the believer, this verse is a call to be steadfast in the face of such schemes and to trust that Allah is fully aware of the plots of the enemies of truth. It also serves as a warning never to be a cause of others turning away from the straight path.
100 | O believers! If you were to yield to a group of those who were given the Scripture, they would turn you back from belief to disbelief. | Having addressed the People of the Book, the surah now turns to the Muslim community with a direct warning born from the previous revelations. “O believers! If you were to yield to a group of those who were given the Scripture…” The word “yield,” tuti‘u, means to obey, to follow, to pay heed to. It implies a willing compliance. The warning is not about forced conversion but about the danger of listening to and being influenced by a hostile faction among the People of the Book. The “group,” ta’ifah, is a specific segment, not all of them. These are the ones who actively oppose the truth and wish to mislead the believers. The outcome of yielding to them is devastating: “they would turn you back from belief to disbelief.” They would not merely cause doubt or sin; they could potentially cause a person to abandon their faith entirely. This could happen through persistent argumentation, clever distortions, or social pressure. The verse is not promoting isolation or hatred; rather, it is a realistic assessment of the spiritual danger posed by those who are actively hostile and have a deep understanding of scripture that they twist to serve their ends. For the believers in Medina, this was a very real threat, as some Muslims were inclined to trust and confide in certain Jewish tribes, risking their faith. The warning serves as a protective measure. For the believer today, it teaches the importance of spiritual discernment. While Islam encourages knowledge and dialogue, it also warns against naively adopting the company or the ideas of those who are determined to undermine faith. The defense is a strong grounding in Islamic knowledge and a vigilant heart.
101 | How can you disbelieve when Allah’s revelations are recited to you and His Messenger is in your midst? Whoever holds firmly to Allah is surely guided to the Straight Path. | After warning the believers against yielding to the hostile faction of the People of the Book, the surah now turns to the believers’ own foundations, asking a question that is meant to fortify their hearts against any doubt. “How can you disbelieve when Allah’s revelations are recited to you and His Messenger is in your midst?” The question “how can you” is one of surprise and encouragement, not accusation. It is as if to say: given the immense gifts you have, how could disbelief ever find a way into your hearts? The phrase “Allah’s revelations are recited to you” refers to the Quran, which was being revealed fresh and recited constantly among them. The believers were hearing the words of their Lord directly, with their meanings explained by the Prophet. This constant exposure to revelation was a powerful anchor; the living connection to the divine word made slipping into disbelief almost unthinkable. Then an even greater gift is mentioned: “and His Messenger is in your midst.” The physical presence of Prophet Muhammad among the believers was the greatest blessing they could have. They could see his character, ask him questions, witness his miracles, and learn directly from his example. The presence of the Messenger was a visible, tangible proof of the truth. In a hadith, the Prophet once said, “I have left among you two things; you will never go astray as long as you hold fast to them: the Book of Allah and my Sunnah.” This verse combines both sources of guidance: the recited revelation and the living example. Given these two, how could anyone fall into disbelief? The verse then gives the key to protection: “Whoever holds firmly to Allah is surely guided to the Straight Path.” Holding firmly, ya‘tasim, means to cling tightly, to seek protection, to never let go. The object of holding is “Allah,” which implies holding onto His religion, His book, and His Messenger’s teachings. The promise is absolute: such a person is guided to the Straight Path, the path that leads to Allah’s pleasure and Paradise. For the believer in every age, this verse is a powerful reminder that the resources for steadfastness are always available. The Quran is recited among us, and the authentic Sunnah preserves the Messenger’s guidance. The question “how can you disbelieve” echoes whenever a believer wavers, reminding them to look at what they already possess. The path to steadfastness is to cling to Allah with all one’s heart, making the Quran and Sunnah the core of life.
102 | O believers! Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves, and do not die except in ˹a state of full˺ submission ˹to Him˺. | Building on the call to hold firmly to Allah, this verse commands a profound level of God-consciousness. “O believers! Be mindful of Allah in the way He deserves.” The phrase “in the way He deserves,” haqqa tuqatihi, denotes the truest, most complete form of taqwa. It means to obey Him without disobedience, to remember Him without forgetfulness, and to thank Him without ingratitude. The early scholars, including Ibn Mas‘ud, explained that this means to be obeyed and not disobeyed, remembered and not forgotten, thanked and not denied. Some scholars said this verse was later considered to be qualified by the verse “So be mindful of Allah as much as you are able” in Surah Al-Taghabun, not to abrogate it but to acknowledge human weakness and to encourage striving to the utmost. The command thus sets the highest ideal, while recognizing that perfection is unattainable, the believer’s aim should be to strive for it. The second command is equally momentous: “and do not die except in a state of full submission to Him.” The word “submission” translates the Arabic muslimun, meaning to die as a Muslim, one who submits to Allah. This does not mean one should wish for death, but rather one should live in such a constant state of Islam that whenever death comes, it finds them in that state. The believer cannot guarantee the moment of death, but they can control their present state of faith and obedience. This verse instills a sense of urgency and consistency. A person should not delay repentance or obedience, because death is unpredictable. The Prophet Muhammad used to frequently supplicate, “O Turner of hearts, keep my heart firm upon Your religion,” showing that even he was concerned about dying in a state of submission. For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivator to maintain taqwa and Islam at all times. It is a call to live with the end in mind, ensuring that one’s final moment is a moment of surrender to the Lord. The combination of the two commands—perfect mindfulness and dying in submission—frames the entire spiritual journey: a life of striving, ending in a state of grace.
103 | And hold firmly to the rope of Allah and do not be divided. Remember Allah’s favour upon you when you were enemies, then He united your hearts, so you—by His grace—became brothers. And you were at the brink of a fiery pit and He saved you from it. This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so that you may be ˹rightly˺ guided. | The surah now moves from individual piety to communal unity, addressing a critical need in the Medinan community. “And hold firmly to the rope of Allah and do not be divided.” The “rope of Allah,” habl Allah, is a powerful metaphor. It signifies the Quran, as stated in a hadith: “The Quran is the rope of Allah.” It is the link between the heavens and the earth, between Allah and His servants. Holding onto it together means uniting around the revealed guidance. The command “do not be divided” prohibits factionalism, sectarianism, and internal strife. The believers are to be one united body, their strength rooted in their shared adherence to the Quran. To encourage this, the verse reminds them of a very recent history: “Remember Allah’s favour upon you when you were enemies, then He united your hearts.” Before Islam, the tribes of Aws and Khazraj in Medina were locked in bitter, long-standing wars. Hatred and vengeance were the norm. Then Islam came, and Allah transformed their hearts. They became brothers, loving one another, sacrificing for one another. This was a miraculous change that no worldly power could have achieved. The verse emphasizes, “by His grace,” underscoring that unity is a divine gift, not a human accomplishment. The reminder deepens: “And you were at the brink of a fiery pit and He saved you from it.” The “fiery pit” represents the Hellfire that awaited them due to their disbelief and warfare, but it also symbolizes the chaos and destruction of their pre-Islamic society. Allah saved them from both through Islam. The verse concludes, “This is how Allah makes His revelations clear to you, so that you may be rightly guided.” The clarity of these lessons is a mercy meant to keep them on the path of unity and faith. For the believer today, this verse is a timeless antidote to division. It calls for unity around the Quran and Sunnah, not around personalities or sects. It reminds Muslims that the bond of faith is stronger than tribal or national loyalties, and that holding firmly together is essential for survival and guidance.
104 | Let there be a group among you who call ˹others˺ to goodness, encourage what is good, and forbid what is evil—it is they who will be successful. | After the command to hold firmly to the rope of Allah and avoid division, the surah provides a practical mechanism to sustain the community’s spiritual and moral health. “Let there be a group among you who call others to goodness.” The phrase “from you,” minkum, indicates that this group is drawn from the entire community, not an external entity. They are to “call to goodness,” al-khayr, which encompasses all that is beneficial and righteous, particularly the teachings of Islam. The verse then specifies two complementary duties: “encourage what is good and forbid what is evil.” This is the famous obligation of al-amr bi al-ma‘ruf wa al-nahy ‘an al-munkar. Ma‘ruf is everything recognised as good by the Shariah and sound intellect—prayer, charity, honesty, justice. Munkar is everything recognised as evil—idolatry, lying, oppression, corruption. The verse singles out a group, not necessarily every individual, because this duty requires knowledge, wisdom, and the capacity to carry it out effectively. However, the Prophet also said, “Whoever among you sees an evil, let him change it with his hand; if he cannot, then with his tongue; and if he cannot, then with his heart, and that is the weakest of faith.” So while a dedicated group may take on the public role, every believer has a level of responsibility according to their ability. The verse promises, “it is they who will be successful.” True success, al-muflihun, is not measured by wealth or power but by fulfilling this duty and earning Allah’s pleasure. This verse establishes that a Muslim community cannot remain passive in the face of wrongdoing. If evil becomes widespread and no one speaks out, the whole community is in danger of divine punishment. For the believer, it is a call to be part of this effort, either by directly participating in da‘wah and moral guidance, or at least by supporting those who do. The verse also teaches that the path to success is through collective moral responsibility, not just individual piety.
105 | And do not be like those who split ˹into sects˺ and differed after clear proofs had come to them. It is they who will suffer a tremendous punishment. | Building on the command to unity and the institution of calling to goodness, the surah now warns against the opposite behaviour: division after knowledge. “And do not be like those who split into sects and differed after clear proofs had come to them.” The immediate reference is to the People of the Book, the Jews and Christians, who received clear scriptures and unambiguous guidance, yet they fragmented into warring sects. Some became polytheists, others denied prophets, and they fought over theological and legal issues out of envy and worldly ambition. The “clear proofs,” al-bayyinat, leave no excuse; division after such clarity is a sign of spiritual corruption. The command “do not be like them” is a direct warning to the Muslim community not to repeat the same mistakes. The Prophet Muhammad prophesied that his Ummah would divide into many sects, but only one would be saved: the one that follows what he and his companions were upon. This verse serves as a guard against that fragmentation. The consequence is severe: “It is they who will suffer a tremendous punishment.” The punishment is described as “tremendous,” ‘azim, because the crime is tremendous. Dividing the religion corrupts the message, misleads the masses, and weakens the community’s witness to the world. It is an act of rebellion against the clear commands of Allah. The verse does not mean that all scholarly disagreements are forbidden; differences of opinion within the framework of the Quran and Sunnah, approached with sincerity and respect, are part of the permissible diversity. The condemned division is that which stems from desires, leads to enmity, and creates separate sects with conflicting core beliefs. For the believer, this verse instills a love for unity and a fear of causing disunity. It encourages focusing on the vast common ground and leaving judgment on scholarly differences to the scholars, while maintaining brotherhood and avoiding sectarian partisanship.
106 | On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy. To the gloomy-faced it will be said, “Did you disbelieve after having believed? So taste the punishment for your disbelief.” | The surah now shifts the scene to the Day of Judgment to illustrate the ultimate consequence of division and unity, faith and disbelief. “On that Day some faces will be bright while others gloomy.” The “Day” is the Day of Resurrection. Faces are the mirrors of the soul’s state. The “bright” faces, tabyaḍḍu, are those that shine with joy, honour, and light because of their faith and good deeds. The “gloomy” faces, taswaddu, are those darkened with humiliation, grief, and despair. Blackening of the face is the visible sign of punishment and disgrace. The verse then focuses on the gloomy-faced and the terrifying address they will receive: “To the gloomy-faced it will be said, ‘Did you disbelieve after having believed? So taste the punishment for your disbelief.’” This question is not a request for information but a scathing rebuke. They were once believers or were given the opportunity to believe, yet they turned away. Their apostasy or rejection after the truth came to them is the cause of their ruin. The phrase “taste the punishment” makes the suffering immediate and sensory. This verse connects directly to the earlier discussion about those who turn back from faith after clear proofs, and to the warning against division. Those who split into sects and abandoned true faith will face this humiliation. For the believer, the image of the bright faces is a source of hope and motivation to remain steadfast. The image of the gloomy faces is a stark warning against the consequences of allowing doubt, desires, or societal pressures to lead one away from the path. It reinforces the importance of dying in a state of full submission, as commanded earlier. The verse also serves as a reminder that on that Day, all will be laid bare, and inner reality will become visible on the face.
107 | As for the bright-faced, they will be in Allah’s mercy, where they will remain forever. | In contrast to the gloomy-faced, the fate of the bright-faced is announced with comforting assurance. “As for the bright-faced, they will be in Allah’s mercy, where they will remain forever.” The brightness of their faces is not the cause of their salvation but the reflection of their salvation. The “mercy” here, rahmah, is comprehensive. It includes Paradise itself, which is the greatest mercy, as well as the specific pleasure of Allah, the vision of Him, and the countless blessings that constitute the reward. To be “in Allah’s mercy” means to be enveloped completely by divine goodness and protection, with no fear of ever leaving that state. The phrase “where they will remain forever,” hum fiha khalidun, provides the ultimate security: eternity in bliss. This verse is a beautiful promise that creates a longing in the believer’s heart. It connects to the earlier description of the believers as the successful and those who hold firmly to Allah’s rope. Their unity and devotion lead to this everlasting mercy. The contrast between the temporary nature of worldly divisions and the eternal nature of the Hereafter’s reward underlines the importance of prioritizing faith over fleeting disputes. For the believer, this verse is a source of hope that outweighs the fear generated by the previous warning. It reminds us that Allah’s mercy is vast and that those who strive to remain on the path, despite their imperfections, will find their place in that eternal mercy. It also encourages a focus on the qualities that lead to a bright face—sincere faith, good deeds, and unity with the believers—and a detachment from the causes of a gloomy face.
108 | These are Allah’s revelations We recite to you ˹O Prophet˺ in truth. And Allah desires no injustice to ˹His˺ creation. | After the powerful eschatological imagery, the surah pauses to reaffirm the nature and purpose of the revelation. “These are Allah’s revelations We recite to you in truth.” The word “these” encompasses all that has been mentioned: the stories, the commands, the warnings, and the promises. They are “revelations,” ayat, from Allah, not human inventions. “Recite to you” underscores the oral transmission of the Quran to the Prophet, and through him to humanity. The phrase “in truth,” bil-haqq, has a double meaning. First, the revelations are conveyed with absolute truthfulness; their content is real. Second, they are conveyed for a just and wise purpose, not in vain. The verse then asserts a fundamental principle: “And Allah desires no injustice to His creation.” The word “injustice,” zulm, is anything that violates rights or causes undue harm. Allah categorically denies any intention of injustice. This means that the punishments described earlier for the disbelievers and the gloomy-faced are not arbitrary or unfair; they are the just recompense for the choices made. Similarly, the mercy for the bright-faced is pure grace, exceeding what they may “deserve” by deeds alone. The verse assures humanity that the divine system is built on perfect justice. No soul will be wronged. This principle is comforting for the oppressed and a warning for the oppressor. For the believer, this verse strengthens trust in Allah’s wisdom. Even when one sees suffering or cannot understand certain divine decrees, the knowledge that Allah desires no injustice provides peace. It also reinforces that the Quran is not a harsh or arbitrary text; it is the truth from a Lord who is perfectly just and merciful.
109 | To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. And to Allah ˹all˺ matters will be returned ˹for judgment˺. | The reaffirmation of divine justice leads naturally to a declaration of Allah’s absolute ownership and the final return. “To Allah alone belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.” This statement encompasses every being, every object, every atom in the universe. Ownership, mulk, is complete and undisputed. There is no co-owner, no partner. This includes the believers, the disbelievers, the angels, the jinn, the prophets, the idols falsely worshipped—all are His property, under His command. This reality has profound implications. It means that no one can truly possess anything in this world; we are merely temporary trustees. It also means that Allah’s will is sovereign; what He decrees for His creation is based on His wisdom and ownership. The second half of the verse, “And to Allah all matters will be returned for judgment,” explains the ultimate destination of everything. All affairs, all deeds, all disputes, all destinies will be brought back to Allah for His final decision. The Day of Judgment is the culmination of this ownership, when Allah will judge among His creation with perfect justice, as established in the previous verse. The combination of absolute ownership and the final return creates a complete worldview: we are living in a universe owned by Allah, and we are journeying back to our Owner for accountability. For the believer, this verse inspires humility. Since all belongs to Allah, there is no room for arrogance over what one possesses. It also inspires hope, because the Owner is also the Most Merciful, and His judgment will not wrong anyone. The verse also reinforces the concept of Tawakkul—reliance on Allah—because the One who owns everything is fully capable of providing and protecting.
110 | You are the best community ever raised for humanity—you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah. Had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them. Some of them are faithful, but most are rebellious. | After establishing the universal truths of ownership and return, the surah now addresses the Muslim community with an extraordinary declaration of their status and responsibility. “You are the best community ever raised for humanity.” The word “you” refers to the companions of the Prophet and, by extension, the entire Muslim ummah that follows their path. “Best community,” khayra ummah, is a divine compliment and a heavy mantle of honour. The reason for this distinction is immediately given: “you encourage good, forbid evil, and believe in Allah.” These three qualities were explained earlier in verse 104, but here they are the very definition of the community’s excellence. The order is significant: the outward actions of encouraging good and forbidding evil are mentioned first, and then the inward foundation of true belief in Allah. This shows that faith must manifest in active moral engagement with society. The verse then states, “Had the People of the Book believed, it would have been better for them.” This refers to their believing in the Prophet Muhammad and the Quran. Their rejection means they have lost this honour. The verse then provides a balanced assessment: “Some of them are faithful, but most are rebellious.” This acknowledges that not all are the same; there are those among the People of the Book who are sincere believers, like Abdullah ibn Salam and others, who accepted Islam. But the majority of them are “rebellious,” fasiqun, those who wilfully disobey and stray from the truth. This balance prevents unjust generalisation and maintains the Quranic principle of individual accountability. For the believer, this verse is both a source of pride and a weighty responsibility. The ummah is best only as long as it upholds the duty of calling to good and forbidding evil with sincere faith. If the ummah abandons this duty, it loses its distinction. The verse motivates every Muslim to contribute to this collective mission, ensuring the community remains worthy of this divine praise.
111 | They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance. But if they meet you in battle, they will flee and they will have no helpers. | Having described the rebellious majority among the People of the Book, the surah now comforts the believers by exposing the limits of the enemy’s power. “They can never inflict harm on you, except a little annoyance.” The word “harm,” darar, means serious damage or defeat. The verse promises that the hostile elements among the People of the Book, and by extension all enemies of truth, cannot truly harm the believers in a fundamental way. The “little annoyance,” adha, refers to verbal abuse, slander, plots, and minor skirmishes—things that may cause temporary discomfort but cannot destroy the faith or the community. This was a precise description of what the Muslims faced in Medina: hurtful words, conspiracies, and occasional small-scale attacks, but never a decisive blow. The verse then makes a prophecy: “But if they meet you in battle, they will flee and they will have no helpers.” History proved this true. When the Jewish tribes broke their treaties and faced the Muslims in direct confrontation, they were defeated and many were expelled. Their fear was real; their boasts were hollow. The statement “they will have no helpers” strips them of any illusion of support. For the believer, this verse is a timeless assurance. No matter how powerful the enemies of Islam appear, they cannot inflict lasting harm on the true faith. The trials may come, but they refine rather than destroy. The believer is taught to endure the “little annoyance” with patience and to trust that the outcome is in Allah’s hands. It also serves as a deterrent against fearing the enemy too much, because their real strength is illusory.
112 | They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go, unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people. They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery for rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering ˹His˺ prophets unjustly. This is ˹a fair reward˺ for their disobedience and violations. | The description of the rebellious faction’s fate continues with a declaration of their perpetual humiliation. “They will be stricken with disgrace wherever they go.” The word “disgrace,” al-dhillah, is a deep-seated humiliation that clings to them. Historically, this has been seen in the repeated subjugation and diaspora of those Jewish tribes that rejected the prophets and opposed the truth, unless they found protection. The exception is noted: “unless they are protected by a covenant with Allah or a treaty with the people.” A “covenant with Allah” refers to a dhimmi pact under Islamic rule, granting them security and rights. A “treaty with the people” refers to any protection extended by a powerful group. Outside of such protections, they live under the shadow of disgrace. The verse then explains the root cause: “They have invited the displeasure of Allah and have been branded with misery.” Their own actions drew down divine wrath, and the branding with misery, al-maskanah, is a mark of spiritual and material poverty. The specific crimes are listed: “rejecting Allah’s revelations and murdering His prophets unjustly.” This refers to the historical pattern of the Israelites in killing prophets like Zechariah and John, and attempting to kill Jesus, and their current rejection of the Quran. The punishment is summed up: “This is a fair reward for their disobedience and violations.” There is no injustice; it is a fitting recompense. For the believer, this verse is not a blanket condemnation of all Jews or People of the Book for all time; it is a description of a specific spiritual trajectory for those who persist in these crimes. It serves as a warning against rejecting revelation and transgressing sacred limits. The lesson is that sin, especially when collective and habitual, leads to tangible humiliation, unless one seeks the protection of Allah through repentance and faith.
113 | Yet they are not all alike: there are some among the People of the Book who are upright, who recite Allah’s revelations throughout the night, prostrating ˹in prayer˺. | In order to maintain justice and prevent overgeneralisation, the Quran immediately balances the previous severe description. “Yet they are not all alike.” This is a crucial corrective. Among the People of the Book are individuals who are completely different from the rebellious faction. “There are some among the People of the Book who are upright.” The word “upright,” qa’imah, denotes a community that is steadfast, just, and committed to the truth. They are described with a beautiful image of devotion: “who recite Allah’s revelations throughout the night, prostrating in prayer.” Their worship is intense, humble, and deeply spiritual. They spend the hours of darkness reciting the scriptures that they have, which would include the unchanged remnants of the Torah and Gospel, and for those who later embraced Islam, the Quran. Their prostration, sujud, is the ultimate physical expression of submission. This description matches those among the People of the Book who, like the Negus of Abyssinia, the monk Bahira, or Abdullah ibn Salam, recognized the truth of Islam and submitted to it. Some commentators note that this verse was revealed about a group of Jews who spent the night in prayer and then accepted Islam in the morning. The verse highlights that sincere devotion can be found anywhere, and Allah acknowledges it. For the believer, this teaches an important principle: individuals are judged by their own faith and actions, not by the collective guilt of their community. It encourages a fair and respectful view of those among other faiths who show genuine piety, while also calling them to complete guidance by embracing the final Prophet.
114 | They believe in Allah and the Last Day, encourage good and forbid evil, and race with one another in doing good. They are ˹truly˺ among the righteous. | The description of the upright from the People of the Book continues with their doctrinal and ethical qualities. “They believe in Allah and the Last Day.” True faith in Allah and the Hereafter is the foundation. This separates them from the polytheists and the materialists. They believe in the one God and in the accountability of the final Day. “Encourage good and forbid evil” repeats the quality that is the hallmark of the best community. These righteous individuals, even before embracing Islam fully, were known to promote virtue and oppose vice. “And race with one another in doing good,” yusari‘una fi al-khayrat, paints a picture of enthusiastic competition in righteousness. They do not merely perform good deeds; they hasten toward them and strive to excel. This is a sign of a vibrant, living faith that is not complacent but dynamic. The verse then gives a definitive testimony: “They are truly among the righteous.” The word “righteous,” al-salihin, is a seal of divine approval. Their place is secured among the good-doers. This verse, along with the previous, was instrumental in the conversion of several notable figures. It acknowledges that the door is open; anyone who embodies these qualities is on the path, and the natural culmination is to accept Islam when it is presented. For the believer, this sets a standard for personal conduct. It also demonstrates the Quran’s fairness in acknowledging virtue wherever it exists, while still affirming that complete guidance is found in following Muhammad. It encourages Muslims to embody these same qualities—faith, urging good, racing to do good—to be counted among the righteous.
115 | They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done. And Allah has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of those mindful ˹of Him˺. | The surah now promises that the good deeds of these upright individuals will not be lost. “They will never be denied the reward for any good they have done.” The phrase “never be denied,” lan tukfaru, literally means their reward will not be covered up or disbelieved. Allah will fully acknowledge and recompense every act of goodness. This includes the deeds they performed before accepting Islam, if they eventually embrace it. The Prophet Muhammad said, “If a person embraces Islam sincerely, Allah will reward him for every good deed he did before.” This provides immense hope for those who come from other backgrounds; their previous charity, truthfulness, and worship, done for the sake of God as they knew Him, will find its reward. The verse then reminds, “And Allah has perfect knowledge of those mindful of Him.” The word “mindful,” al-muttaqin, refers to those who are conscious of Allah and guard themselves from sin. Allah’s knowledge of them is complete; He knows their struggles, their intentions, and their hidden piety. This assures that no good deed goes unnoticed, even if the world ignores it. For the believer, this verse encourages a generous view of the potential for salvation among sincere seekers from other traditions, while maintaining the necessity of following the final prophet to complete one’s faith. It also inspires Muslims to persevere in good deeds, trusting that every effort is registered with Allah. The verse creates a bridge of mercy and justice, showing that Allah’s fairness extends beyond the borders of the Muslim community to any heart that truly seeks Him.
116 | Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah. It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever. | After the hopeful verses about the righteous from the People of the Book, the surah returns to the fate of those who persist in disbelief, drawing a sharp contrast. “Indeed, neither the wealth nor children of the disbelievers will be of any benefit to them against Allah.” This echoes verse 10, reinforcing the message that worldly assets cannot protect a person from divine judgment. Wealth, which people hoard, and children, who are seen as pride and strength, will be utterly useless when standing before Allah. The phrase “against Allah” means they cannot avert His punishment, buy His favour, or serve as a ransom. The verse then declares, “It is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever.” The word “residents,” ashab, means companions or inhabitants, indicating a lasting, intimate association with the Fire. The eternal nature of their punishment, khalidun, is explicitly stated. This is the fate of those who die in a state of disbelief, having rejected the truth after it reached them. The contrast with the previous verse is stark: the upright receive reward for their good; the disbelievers find all their worldly possessions worthless. For the believer, this serves as a sobering reminder not to envy the wealth and children of those who reject faith. Their apparent prosperity is a temporary illusion that will end in eternal loss. It also highlights the immense value of faith, which is the only currency that matters in the Hereafter.
117 | The good they do in this worldly life is like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind, destroying it ˹completely˺. Allah never wronged them, but they wronged themselves. | The futility of the disbelievers’ situation is now illustrated with a powerful agricultural metaphor. “The good they do in this worldly life is like the harvest of an evil people struck by a bitter wind, destroying it completely.” The “good” refers to any charitable acts, moral deeds, or virtues they might perform. While they do not have true faith, they may do things that appear good, like feeding the poor, being honest, or helping others. However, because these deeds are not founded on faith in Allah and are done for other motives—recognition, cultural norms, or mere humanism—they lack spiritual life. The metaphor describes a “harvest,” zira‘, which represents their accumulated works. The “evil people” are those who wronged themselves through disbelief, referring to the disbelievers. A “bitter wind,” rih fiha sirr, is a frigid, scorching wind that burns and destroys crops. When it strikes the harvest, it leaves nothing behind. Similarly, on the Day of Judgment, the good deeds of the disbelievers will be turned to dust, providing no benefit. The verse clarifies, “Allah never wronged them, but they wronged themselves.” The destruction of their deeds is not due to any injustice from Allah; it is the natural consequence of their own rejection of faith. They wronged themselves by failing to build their deeds on the proper foundation. For the believer, this verse underscores the crucial role of intention and faith. Good deeds are only valuable when done for Allah’s sake. It also serves as a warning not to rely on one’s own moral efforts without checking the state of one’s heart. The metaphor beautifully illustrates the emptiness of a life without the anchoring of iman.
118 | O believers! Do not associate closely with others who would not miss a chance to harm you. Their only desire is to see you suffer. Their prejudice has become evident from what they say—and what their hearts hide is far worse. We have made Our revelations clear to you, if only you understood. | After the general discussion of disbelievers, the surah gives a specific, practical warning regarding certain groups in Medina who were friendly on the outside but harboured deep hatred. “O believers! Do not associate closely with others who would not miss a chance to harm you.” The word “closely,” bitanah, refers to intimate, trusted friends and confidants whom you bring into your inner circle. The warning is against taking such people from those who are not of your faith and who harbor animosity. “Their only desire is to see you suffer,” reveals their true wish: to see the believers in difficulty, hardship, and disunity. The next phrase, “Their prejudice has become evident from what they say,” means that despite their outward pleasantries, slips of the tongue, sarcastic remarks, and subtle insults reveal the hatred in their hearts. “And what their hearts hide is far worse,” indicates that what they conceal is even more venomous than what they show. This verse was revealed regarding some hypocrites and certain Jews in Medina who pretended friendship but plotted against the Muslims. The verse concludes, “We have made Our revelations clear to you, if only you understood.” The warnings and guidance in the Quran are clear enough to protect the believers, provided they reflect on them. For the believer, this verse teaches the importance of being wise in choosing close companions and confidants. The inner circle should be from the people of faith and righteousness. It does not command cutting off all relations with non-Muslims, as trade, neighbourhood, and general courtesy are encouraged elsewhere, but it warns against entrusting one’s deepest matters to those who have proven their enmity. Discernment is key.
119 | Here you are! You love them but they do not love you, and you believe in all Scriptures. When they meet you they say, “We believe.” But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹May you˺ die of your rage!” Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart. | The psychological profile of the hostile group is further exposed. “Here you are! You love them but they do not love you.” The believers, in their goodwill and desire for unity, were inclined to trust and befriend these people. They wished well for them, hoping they would embrace faith. But the feeling was not mutual; their hearts were filled with resentment. “And you believe in all Scriptures,” means the Muslims believe in the Torah, the Gospel, and all the prophets, so they have an inclusive respect that should naturally draw the People of the Book. Yet the hostile faction responds with hypocrisy: “When they meet you they say, ‘We believe.’” They put on a facade of faith and agreement when in the company of the believers. “But when alone, they bite their fingertips in rage.” This is a vivid picture of suppressed fury and frustration. Biting the fingertips is an expression of extreme anger and helplessness against the believers’ success. The Prophet is then instructed: “Say, ‘May you die of your rage!’” This is not a curse but a declaration of their certain fate: they will remain in their rage until it consumes them, while the believers continue to prosper. The verse concludes, “Surely Allah knows best what is hidden in the heart.” The hypocrites’ inner state, no matter how well concealed, is an open book to Allah. For the believer, this verse provides comfort by exposing the true nature of the enemy. It also teaches that outward declarations are not always sincere; one should judge by actions and consistent patterns. The verse encourages the believers to remain steadfast and not be overly distressed by the hidden hatred of others, because Allah is fully aware and will deal with it.
120 | When you ˹believers˺ are touched with good, they grieve; but when you are afflicted with evil, they rejoice. ˹Yet,˺ if you are patient and mindful ˹of Allah˺, their schemes will not harm you in the least. Surely Allah is Fully Aware of what they do. | The exposure of the hypocrites’ true feelings reaches its climax. “When you believers are touched with good, they grieve; but when you are afflicted with evil, they rejoice.” This is the ultimate sign of enmity: the opposite of what a true friend feels. Any success of the Muslims—a military victory, an economic boom, a new convert—causes them sorrow. Any setback—a defeat, a drought, an internal dispute—brings them joy. This was especially evident during the Battle of Uhud, when the hypocrites and the hostile Jews were happy about the Muslims’ losses. Their emotions are completely inverted from those of a believer. Yet the verse delivers a powerful assurance: “Yet, if you are patient and mindful of Allah, their schemes will not harm you in the least.” Patience, sabr, and taqwa, mindfulness of Allah, are the shields. As long as the believers remain steadfast and God-conscious, the plots of the enemy will cause no real harm. They may cause some pain and temporary setbacks, but the core of the community and its ultimate success are protected. The phrase “in the least,” shay’an, negates even the smallest harm. The verse concludes, “Surely Allah is Fully Aware of what they do.” This is a recurring theme: the enemies’ actions are under divine surveillance, and they will be held accountable. For the believer, this verse is a formula for resilience. When facing the Schadenfreude of enemies—their joy at one’s misfortune—the response is to double down on patience and taqwa. The verse frees the believer from the emotional drain of trying to win over those who will never love them, and redirects energy to what truly protects and strengthens.
121 | ˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you left your home in the early morning to position the believers in the battlefield. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing. | The surah now transitions into a detailed reflection on the Battle of Uhud, drawing crucial lessons from the events that transpired in the month of Shawwal, 3 AH. The verse opens with a direct address to the Prophet, drawing the listener into a specific, vivid memory: “Remember, O Prophet, when you left your home in the early morning to position the believers in the battlefield.” The phrase “left your home” (ghadawta min ahlika) specifically refers to the Prophet departing from his house in Medina, leaving his family behind, to go out to Uhud. This immediately personalizes the event; the Messenger was not a distant commander but a man who left the comfort of his home for a dangerous mission. The “early morning” (bukratan) emphasizes the urgency and the sacrifice. The Prophet had consulted with his companions—his personal preference was to remain in Medina and defend from within, but the majority, particularly the younger companions eager for battle, insisted on going out to meet the Quraysh. Once the decision was made, the Prophet put on his armor and did not take it off, teaching that once consultation is concluded and a decision is made, one should proceed with trust in Allah. “To position the believers in the battlefield” (tubawwi’u al-mu’minina maqa‘ida lil-qital) highlights his active leadership. He himself was arranging the archers and the infantry, strategically placing them on the slopes of Mount Uhud. He assigned fifty archers to a specific hill under the command of Abdullah ibn Jubayr, with strict orders not to leave their post under any circumstance. This attention to detail demonstrates that reliance on Allah (tawakkul) does not negate careful planning. The verse ends with two divine names: “And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” As the Prophet and the believers marched out, Allah heard their supplications, their discussions, and the whispers of the hypocrites who turned back under Abdullah ibn Ubayy. He knew the sincerity of the believers, the hesitation of the faint-hearted, and the ultimate wisdom behind the events about to unfold. For the believer today, this verse provides a powerful model of leadership and decision-making. It highlights the balance between shura (consultation), meticulous planning, and ultimate reliance on Allah. The fact that the Muslims would face a severe test at Uhud does not negate the value of the process; rather, it shows that worldly outcomes are sometimes tests, and the process itself—obedience, consultation, and effort—is part of the worship.
122 | ˹Remember˺ when two groups among you ˹believers˺ were about to cower, then Allah reassured them. So in Allah let the believers put their trust. | The narrative zooms into a critical moment of psychological vulnerability before the battle. “Remember when two groups among you believers were about to cower.” The two groups referred to were the Banu Salamah and the Banu Harithah, two clans of the Ansar. When Abdullah ibn Ubayy, the head of the hypocrites, withdrew his three hundred men from the Muslim army, citing the Prophet’s refusal of his opinion, the smaller Muslim force of seven hundred was suddenly faced with the intimidating prospect of fighting a much larger Quraysh army without even the numbers they had counted on. The sight of a major faction deserting on the eve of battle shook the resolve of these two clans. They began to falter, their hearts sinking, wondering how they could possibly stand against the enemy now. The verb “were about to cower” (hammat ta tashkalu) indicates an internal intention to fail or to weaken, not an actual retreat. It was a crisis of the heart that was averted by divine intervention. The verse continues, “then Allah reassured them” (fa Allahu waliyyuhuma). The term “reassured” translates the concept of Allah being their Wali—their Protector, Guardian, and Ally. He fortified their hearts, removed their fear, and kept them steadfast alongside the Prophet. This divine intervention shows that steadfastness in battle is not merely a function of personal bravery; it is a gift from Allah to those who turn to Him. The incident teaches that even among the sincere believers, moments of weakness can arise, but Allah’s support can stabilize them if they do not give in to the fear. The verse then draws the universal lesson: “So in Allah let the believers put their trust.” The Arabic construction “wa ‘ala Allahi falyatawakkali al-mu’minun” uses a command form that emphasizes the exclusive reliance a believer should have. Trust in Allah is the antidote to fear and the source of strength. When human props are removed—when allies desert, when numbers dwindle—the believer learns to depend solely on Allah. This verse beautifully transitions from a specific historical event to an eternal principle. For the contemporary reader, it is a reminder that in moments of betrayal, isolation, or overwhelming odds, the heart must anchor itself not in the visible means but in the invisible, unwavering support of Allah. The incident of the two clans also serves to erase any shame; if Allah forgave and steadied them, then any believer who falters and then recovers should not despair.
123 | Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were ˹vastly˺ outnumbered. So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful. | To strengthen the believers’ hearts shaken by the defection at Uhud, the Quran now reminds them of a recent, undeniable miracle: the Battle of Badr. “Indeed, Allah made you victorious at Badr when you were vastly outnumbered.” Badr took place in the second year of the Hijrah, just a year before Uhud. The Muslims numbered around three hundred and thirteen men, mostly poorly equipped, facing a well-armed Quraysh force of nearly a thousand. By all material calculations, the Muslims should have been wiped out. Yet Allah granted them a decisive, astonishing victory. The key word is “made you victorious” (nasarakum); the victory is attributed entirely to Allah. The believers were merely the means, but the outcome was divinely ordained. The verse connects this historical fact to a present condition: “So be mindful of Allah, perhaps you will be grateful.” The word “mindful” (ittaqu) links the past victory to the current need for taqwa. The victory at Badr was not a blank cheque; it was a sign that obedience and God-consciousness lead to divine support. If the believers wish for similar success, they must maintain their taqwa. The phrase “perhaps you will be grateful” (la‘allakum tashkurun) indicates that reflection on past divine favours is a pathway to gratitude. Gratitude, in turn, preserves and increases blessings. This verse implicitly addresses the question of why the Muslims faced hardships at Uhud. The answer is not that Allah abandoned them, but that they needed to examine their own state of obedience, as the following verses will reveal. The mention of Badr also serves to counter the psychological blow of the enemy’s numbers. At Badr they were outnumbered even more severely, yet they won. So at Uhud, with a better ratio, they should not have doubted. For the believer today, this verse teaches the importance of reflecting on one’s past “Badrs”—the moments in life when, against all odds, Allah’s help came. Those memories are a reservoir of faith that can be drawn upon in current trials. It also emphasizes that victory is tied to taqwa, not to mere numbers or weapons. The conditional “perhaps you will be grateful” underscores that gratitude is the expected human response to divine favor, and ingratitude can lead to its withdrawal.
124 | ˹Remember, O Prophet,˺ when you said to the believers, “Is it not enough that your Lord will send down a reinforcement of three thousand angels for your aid?” | The surah continues the flashback to Badr, revealing a moment of divine reassurance given through the Prophet. “Remember, O Prophet, when you said to the believers, ‘Is it not enough that your Lord will send down a reinforcement of three thousand angels for your aid?’” This refers to the anxious hours before the Battle of Badr when the believers saw the massive Quraysh army and felt their own small numbers. The Prophet, in his role as the compassionate leader and conveyor of glad tidings, consoled them with the promise of angelic help. The question format, “Is it not enough?” (a lan yakfiyakum), is a powerful rhetorical device meant to jolt the heart into recognizing the magnitude of divine support. Three thousand angels descending from the heavens would, in any rational calculation, more than compensate for any numerical disadvantage. The angels were to be a reinforcement (mumidd), meaning they would supplement the believers’ strength and fight alongside them. According to authentic hadiths, the angels did indeed fight at Badr, visible with marks on their turbans, and the believers witnessed enemies falling while no human hand seemed to strike them. This verse serves multiple purposes in the context of Uhud. It reminds the believers that Allah’s help had come in the most miraculous form just a year earlier. Why then should they despair now? It also subtly hints that the promise of angelic support at Uhud was conditional on their steadfastness, as the next verse will make explicit. The mention of “three thousand” is specific; some narrations mention that first one thousand, then three thousand, then five thousand angels were promised depending on the believers’ conduct. For the believer today, this verse reinforces the reality of the unseen and the existence of divine forces that can intervene in human affairs. The question “Is it not enough?” echoes in the heart whenever a believer faces a situation where they feel outnumbered or overwhelmed. It serves as a powerful reminder that Allah’s support is not measured by human numbers, but by His infinite power. The prophet’s words model the role of a leader in boosting the morale of the community through reminders of Allah’s promises.
125 | Most certainly, if you ˹believers˺ are firm and mindful ˹of Allah˺ and the enemy launches a sudden attack on you, Allah will reinforce you with five thousand angels designated ˹for battle˺. | The promise of angelic support is now clarified with the specific conditions that govern it. “Most certainly, if you believers are firm and mindful of Allah…” The word “firm” (tasbiru) means to exercise patience, steadfastness, and endurance in the heat of battle. The word “mindful” (tattaqu) refers to taqwa—obedience and avoidance of what Allah forbids. Together, these two qualities are the spiritual prerequisites for receiving divine aid. They involve both an active inner steadfastness and an outer compliance with Allah’s commands. The phrase “and the enemy launches a sudden attack on you” (wa ya’tukum min fawrihim hadha) refers to the scenario where the Quraysh immediately attack with full force. If, under those conditions, the believers maintain patience and taqwa, the promise is: “Allah will reinforce you with five thousand angels designated for battle.” The term “designated” (musawwimin) means marked, either with distinct signs, or trained and prepared. Some recitations suggest a variant meaning “sent down” or “mounted.” The increase from three thousand to five thousand angels indicates that the level of divine support is proportional to the level of steadfastness and need. At Badr, the believers were steadfast, and the angels came. The conditionality is crucial because it sets up the contrast with Uhud, where the archers abandoned their post and the believers’ ranks lost their patience. Because they did not fully meet the condition, the promised angels did not descend at Uhud. This verse is a masterclass in the theology of divine assistance. Allah’s help is not automatic; it is tied to human effort and spiritual commitment. The promise is generous, but it requires the believers to hold up their end. The mention of five thousand angels also highlights that Allah’s resources are limitless; whether three thousand or five thousand, the numbers are trivial for the Lord of the heavens. For the contemporary believer, this verse applies to any struggle in the path of Allah, whether it be a personal battle against sin, a communal effort for justice, or the intellectual struggle to defend truth. The formula remains the same: sabr and taqwa attract divine aid. It also serves as a diagnostic tool: when defeat or difficulty occurs, one should examine the state of patience and mindfulness within the community, rather than simply blaming external factors.
126 | Allah ordained this ˹reinforcement˺ only as good news for you and reassurance for your hearts. And victory comes only from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise. | After describing the promise of the angels, the surah clarifies the ultimate purpose and source of victory. “Allah ordained this reinforcement only as good news for you and reassurance for your hearts.” The word “only” (wa ma ja‘alahu Allahu illa bushra) is vital. The angelic reinforcements were not strictly necessary for victory, because Allah could have granted victory with a single command. Their purpose was to serve as glad tidings (bushra) that would lift the morale of the believers, and to give reassurance (tatma’innu bihi qulubukum) to their anxious hearts. The human heart is fragile and needs tangible signs of hope; the promise of angels was one such sign. This reveals Allah’s mercy and understanding of human psychology. He provides not only the actual help but also the emotional and psychological support that the believers need to endure the terrifying prospect of battle. The verse then delivers the core principle that must never be forgotten: “And victory comes only from Allah—the Almighty, All-Wise.” The Arabic structure (wa ma al-nasru illa min ‘indi Allah) is one of restriction—victory is exclusively from Allah. It is not from numbers, from weapons, from angels, or from strategy. The angels are merely instruments; the true source of victory is Allah alone. This demolishes any tendency to attribute success to the visible means and forget the Giver of success. The two divine names at the end—“the Almighty, All-Wise”—perfectly complement this message. Al-Aziz, the Almighty, means that Allah’s power is irresistible; no force can defeat what He wills to be victorious. Al-Hakim, the All-Wise, means that He grants victory and defeat according to a perfect wisdom, not arbitrarily. Sometimes victory is given, and sometimes it is withheld, both for reasons that serve a greater good, as the coming verses will explain. For the believer today, this verse is an anchor in a world that constantly emphasizes material factors. It teaches that while means are employed, the heart remains attached to the Lord of the means. The reassurance that the “good news” was the primary purpose of the angels also suggests that much of the Quran’s guidance is designed to provide emotional and spiritual comfort—a function that believers should actively seek in the words of Allah. The exclusive attribution of victory to Allah also fosters humility after success and prevents despair after failure, because both are in the hands of the Wise.
127 | to destroy a group of the disbelievers and humble the rest, causing them to withdraw in disappointment. | The verse continues by unfolding a further dimension of the divine wisdom behind the reinforcement. “To destroy a group of the disbelievers and humble the rest, causing them to withdraw in disappointment.” The Arabic phrase “liyaqta‘a tarafan min al-ladhina kafaru” literally means “to cut off a limb of those who disbelieve.” This is a vivid metaphor: the destruction of a portion of the enemy is like amputating a diseased limb, weakening the whole body. At Badr, this was exactly what happened. The leading chiefs of Quraysh, including Abu Jahl, Utbah, and Shaybah, were killed—seventy of the most prominent disbelievers. This was a devastating blow from which the Quraysh leadership never fully recovered. The second purpose, “or humble them” (yakhzahum), refers to the survivors who would be disgraced and humiliated, their pride shattered. The phrase “causing them to withdraw in disappointment” (yanqalibu kha’ibin) paints a picture of their forced retreat, having failed in their objective to destroy the Muslims. They returned to Mecca laden with shame. This verse reveals a fundamental strategic principle in the divine plan: the conflict with disbelief is not always about total annihilation, but often about weakening the enemy’s strength and breaking their morale. The loss of leadership at Badr humbled the Quraysh in a way that prepared the ground for the eventual conquest of Mecca. Some interpreters note that this verse also connects to the events of Uhud. While Uhud was a painful setback for the Muslims, it did not result in their destruction. The Quraysh ultimately withdrew, and the promise of Badr’s long-term effects held true. For the believer, this verse offers insight into the nature of divine victory. It may not always be immediate and total; it can be a process that involves reducing the power of falsehood step by step. It also teaches that humiliation and disappointment are part of the punishment for disbelief in this world, a foretaste of the greater humiliation in the Hereafter. The believer draws patience from this, knowing that the apparent triumph of falsehood is temporary, and its eventual decline is decreed by the Almighty.
128 | You ˹O Prophet˺ have no say in the matter. It is up to Allah to turn to them in mercy or punish them, for indeed they are wrongdoers. | After detailing the purposes of the divine plan, the verse directly addresses the Prophet with a crucial clarification of his role. “You, O Prophet, have no say in the matter.” This was revealed in a specific context. At Uhud, when the Prophet was wounded and blood was streaming down his face, some companions heard him utter words that were interpreted as a supplication against the disbelievers. There are also narrations that the Prophet, in his pain, said, “How can a people succeed who have wounded their Prophet?” In response, this verse descended, affirming that even the Prophet does not possess independent authority over judgment. The “matter” (al-amr) refers to the ultimate decision regarding the fate of the disbelievers—whether they will be guided, forgiven, or punished. The verse states: “It is up to Allah to turn to them in mercy or punish them.” The phrase “turn to them in mercy” (yatuba ‘alayhim) implies that the door of repentance and forgiveness is open to these very disbelievers, even those who wounded the Prophet. Allah can soften their hearts and bring them into Islam, as happened with many leaders of Quraysh, including Khalid ibn al-Walid and Amr ibn al-‘As, who later became great Muslim commanders. Alternatively, Allah may “punish them” (yu‘adhdhibahum) for their persistent wrongdoing. The concluding phrase, “for indeed they are wrongdoers” (fa innahum zalimun), provides the justification. Their state of zulm—wrongdoing, injustice, and transgression—makes them deserving of punishment. Yet, the decision rests with Allah, who may choose to forgive if He wills. This verse teaches profound lessons about prophetic authority and divine mercy. Even the Prophet, the most beloved of creation to Allah, cannot dictate who receives mercy or punishment. This reinforces the absolute sovereignty of Allah and also highlights the vastness of His mercy—the very people who opposed and wounded His Messenger were still offered the possibility of repentance. For the believer today, this verse instills a spirit of forgiveness and non-retaliation. No matter how severe the personal injury, one should not close the door on the possibility of the wrongdoer’s eventual repentance. It also provides comfort: the judgment of one’s enemies is in Allah’s hands, not in the hands of human emotion. The believer is called to leave the ultimate accounting to the All-Wise.
129 | To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | The absolute sovereignty that was asserted over the specific case of the disbelievers’ fate is now expanded to encompass all creation. “To Allah alone belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.” This statement of comprehensive ownership is the foundation of the previous declaration. Because Allah owns everything and everyone, He has the absolute right to forgive or punish. No one can question His decision; no one can claim any right over another except what He grants. The verse then states: “He forgives whoever He wills, and punishes whoever He wills.” This is a direct expression of divine prerogative. However, this will is not arbitrary; it is always in accordance with His wisdom, justice, and mercy. As the Quran explains elsewhere, forgiveness is for those who repent and believe, and punishment is for those who persist in disbelief and wrongdoing. The verse then concludes with two names that beautifully balance the statement: “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” Notice that the names of mercy close the verse, even though it speaks of punishment. This indicates that mercy predominates. Al-Ghafur (All-Forgiving) covers sins completely; al-Rahim (Most Merciful) ensures that mercy is an enduring attribute. Some scholars note that this verse is among the most hope-inspiring in the Quran, because it places the power to forgive entirely in the hands of a Lord described by infinite forgiveness and mercy. For the believer, this verse is a source of immense hope for oneself and for others. It prevents a judgmental attitude, because the ultimate judgment is Allah’s alone. It also encourages the believer to continually seek Allah’s forgiveness, knowing that His mercy is vast and His ownership means He can forgive whatever He wills. The verse also responds to any lingering despair from the setback at Uhud. Even in the midst of discussing war and defeat, the Quran redirects the heart to the boundless mercy of the Lord. The believer finds solace in knowing that the same Lord who allowed the trial of Uhud is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, who can heal wounds, forgive the mistakes made, and restore what was lost.
130 | O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over. And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper. | After the discourse on battle, the surah shifts to a major social and economic command that is central to the construction of a just community. “O believers! Do not consume interest, multiplying it many times over.” The word “interest” is riba, often translated as usury. Its linguistic root means to increase, but it specifically refers to the exploitative increase on loans that was widespread in pre-Islamic Arabia and remains relevant today. The phrase “multiplying it many times over” (ad‘afan muda‘afah) describes the characteristic of pre-Islamic riba, where a debtor who could not pay on time would have the debt doubled, then redoubled, creating an endless spiral of debt bondage. This form of lending exploited the poor and enriched the rich without any productive economic activity. Islam categorically prohibited all forms of riba, whether the excessive compound interest of the past or the modern institutionalized interest, because the underlying principle is the same: a guaranteed return on capital without risk, which is unjust. The prohibition of riba is one of the pillars of Islamic economic ethics, standing alongside the command to give zakat, pay charity, and uphold justice in transactions. The verse then links this prohibition to taqwa: “And be mindful of Allah, so you may prosper.” Mindfulness of Allah is the internal motivation to obey the prohibition. True prosperity (falah) is not merely material gain—in fact, the verse rejects the notion that riba brings prosperity. True prosperity lies in spiritual success, divine blessings, and sustainable economic justice. This verse is placed strategically after the battle narratives because a society that fights for justice must also embody justice in its internal economic dealings. A community that exploits its own members through riba cannot expect the divine support in battle. For the believer today, this verse is a stark reminder of the severity of engaging in interest. It challenges the dominant global economic system and calls Muslims to seek Shariah-compliant alternatives in all financial dealings. The phrase “so you may prosper” indicates that the wisdom behind the prohibition is not to restrict but to guide towards a truly successful economic and spiritual life. The believer is urged to reflect on whether their financial transactions align with the mindfulness of Allah.
131 | Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers. | The prohibition of riba is immediately followed by a severe warning that elevates the matter from an economic transaction to a spiritual life-or-death issue. “Guard yourselves against the Fire prepared for the disbelievers.” The word “guard yourselves” (ittaqu) is the same root as taqwa, meaning to take protective measures. The Fire—Hell—is described as “prepared for the disbelievers.” This description serves two purposes. First, it indicates that Hell is the ultimate destination for those who reject faith, and the deeds associated with disbelief, such as consuming riba in defiance of Allah’s command, lead one towards that destination. Second, it carries an implicit warning: if a believer engages in the practices of the disbelievers—in this case, the exploitative financial practices of the pre-Islamic Arabs—they risk sharing the fate of the disbelievers. The use of the word “prepared” (u‘iddat) indicates that the Fire is a ready, established reality, not a metaphor or a temporary state. The warning is stark, leaving no room for equivocation. In the context of the surah, which deals with the external enemies of the Muslims and the internal weakness exposed at Uhud, this verse serves as a reminder that the greater jihad includes fighting against the internal desires that lead to forbidden earnings. A society that tolerates riba is spiritually weakened, just as a soldier who disobeys orders is a liability. Some scholars note that the transition from battle to riba shows the interconnection of all aspects of Islam. Obedience on the battlefield, on the economic front, and in personal worship are all part of a single fabric of submission. For the believer, the verse instills a healthy fear that serves as a deterrent. The thought of the Fire prepared for disbelievers should be enough to make one recoil from any act that might lead towards it. It also reminds that the criteria for salvation are not merely labels of identity (“I am a Muslim”) but sincere adherence to Allah’s commands in all areas of life, including the financial.
132 | Obey Allah and the Messenger, so you may be shown mercy. | Following the warning of the Fire, the verse immediately offers the path to protection and mercy. “Obey Allah and the Messenger, so you may be shown mercy.” Obedience to Allah and His Messenger is the comprehensive command that encompasses all the previous instructions—from steadfastness in battle to the prohibition of riba. The pairing of Allah and the Messenger signifies that the Prophet’s commands are divinely sanctioned, and that true obedience to Allah is manifested through following the Messenger. The purpose clause “so you may be shown mercy” (la‘allakum turhamun) is profoundly hopeful. It transforms the stern warning into an invitation. Mercy (rahmah) is the ultimate goal of every believer. The verse teaches that mercy is not automatically bestowed; it is attained through obedience. At the same time, even the act of obeying is itself a mercy from Allah, because He guided the believers to the path. The sequence is beautiful: the prohibition of riba is a mercy, obedience is a mercy, and the ultimate outcome is mercy. This reflects the hadith Qudsi where Allah says, “My mercy prevails over My wrath.” For the believers who had just suffered the ordeal of Uhud, where some disobeyed the Prophet’s command and caused the defeat, this verse served as both a gentle rebuke and a call to renewal. Disobedience had brought pain; obedience would bring the healing balm of divine mercy. For the believer today, the verse establishes obedience as the lifeline to mercy. It is a simple, clear formula that cuts through confusion. Whenever one faces a dilemma, the question is not “What is most convenient?” but “What is the command of Allah and His Messenger?” The promise of mercy is attached to that choice. The verse also encourages repentance, because the call to obey is ongoing; no matter what past mistakes one has made, the path back to mercy remains open through renewed obedience.
133 | And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth, prepared for those mindful ˹of Allah˺. | From the call to obedience, the verse escalates the motivation by describing the glorious reward that awaits. “And hasten towards forgiveness from your Lord and a Paradise…” The verb “hasten” (sari‘u) implies urgency, speed, and racing. Life is short, and the opportunity to earn forgiveness and Paradise must be seized without delay. The first object of hastening is “forgiveness” (maghfirah), which is the purification of the soul and the removal of the burden of sins. This forgiveness is “from your Lord,” adding a personal, intimate dimension to the divine pardon. The second object is “a Paradise as vast as the heavens and the earth.” This breathtaking description expands the imagination. The heavens and the earth, in all their cosmic vastness, are the width of just one level of Paradise. How immense must the entire Paradise be? The vastness symbolises that there is no competition, no crowding, and that the mercy and reward of Allah are not limited. The verse then states that this Paradise is “prepared for those mindful of Allah” (al-muttaqin). The word “prepared” (u‘iddat) mirrors the earlier verse where the Fire was “prepared” for the disbelievers. The balance is perfect: the Fire is ready for the rejecters; Paradise is ready for the God-conscious. This verse was particularly poignant after Uhud. The shuhada (martyrs) were promised Paradise, and the living were urged to hasten to it. The pace of life should be a race towards forgiveness. For the believer, this verse creates a sense of positive urgency. It is not a life of passive waiting, but of active striving. The description of Paradise’s vastness is a comfort to those who may feel that their sins are too many; Allah’s forgiveness and His Paradise are vast enough to encompass everyone who sincerely strives. The word “hasten” also implies that the path is open now, and one should not delay repentance or good deeds, because the opportunity can vanish at any moment. The verse links the themes of forgiveness, divine mercy, and the immense reward, weaving them into a powerful motivator for the God-conscious life.
134 | ˹They are˺ those who donate in prosperity and adversity, control their anger, and pardon others. And Allah loves the good-doers. | The surah now provides a detailed description of the muttaqin, the God-conscious for whom Paradise has been prepared. This is one of the most comprehensive character sketches in the Quran. The first quality is “those who donate in prosperity and adversity.” Spending in charity (yunfiqun) is a hallmark of true piety. What distinguishes them is that their giving is constant across all circumstances. “In prosperity” (al-sarra’) refers to times of ease, health, and wealth, when giving is relatively easy. “In adversity” (al-darra’) refers to times of hardship, poverty, and personal difficulty, when giving is a true sacrifice. This comprehensive giving demonstrates that their charity is not dependent on mood or convenience but is a deeply ingrained habit of generosity. The second quality is “control their anger” (al-kazimin al-ghayz). The term “kazm” originally means to tie up or restrain, like tying up a water-skin to prevent water from spilling. They do not deny that they feel anger—they are human—but they suppress it, swallow it, and do not let it explode into harmful words or actions. This is a mark of tremendous self-discipline. The third quality, which surpasses mere restraint, is “and pardon others” (al-‘afina ‘ani al-nas). After controlling their anger, they move to the higher stage of actually forgiving those who wronged them. “Others” (al-nas) is general, indicating forgiveness towards all people, not just those close to them. This three-step progression—giving, restraining, forgiving—covers the relationship with wealth, with one’s own emotions, and with other people. The verse concludes with a divine seal of approval: “And Allah loves the good-doers” (al-muhsinin). Those who embody these qualities reach the station of ihsan, excellence, and are beloved by Allah. This verse was reportedly revealed in connection with a specific incident involving Umar ibn al-Khattab, who controlled his anger when provoked, but it applies universally. For the believer, this verse provides a practical program for spiritual growth. It is a mirror to examine oneself: Am I generous in all times? Do I swallow my anger or vent it? Do I forgive or hold grudges? The verse promises Allah’s love, which is the highest reward, to those who strive for these qualities.
135 | ˹They are˺ those who, upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness and do not knowingly persist in sin—and who forgives sins except Allah? | The portrait of the muttaqin continues, now addressing the inevitable human reality of sin. “Those who, upon committing an evil deed or wronging themselves, remember Allah and seek forgiveness…” The verse acknowledges that even the God-conscious slip. They may commit an “evil deed” (fahishah), a major sin, or “wrong themselves” (zalamu anfusahum) with a lesser sin. The crucial difference between them and the heedless is their immediate response: they remember Allah. This remembrance (dhakaru Allah) is not a mere thought; it is a vivid recollection of Allah’s majesty, His commands, His warnings, and His mercy. This remembrance shatters the veil of heedlessness that allowed the sin in the first place. It leads instantly to the second action: “seek forgiveness” (istaghfaru li-dhunubihim). They do not delay, rationalize, or compound the sin with pride. They turn directly to their Lord with repentance. The verse then asks a rhetorical question that is one of the most beautiful in the Quran: “and who forgives sins except Allah?” (wa man yaghfiru al-dhunuba illa Allah). The question emphasizes that there is no other source of forgiveness. No priest, no saint, no ritual can absolve sin; forgiveness is exclusively from Allah. This directs the heart solely to Him, fostering a direct, unmediated relationship. The verse adds the condition: “and do not knowingly persist in sin” (wa lam yusirru ‘ala ma fa‘alu wa hum ya‘lamun). Repentance must be coupled with a resolve to abandon the sin. Persistence (israr) is when one continues the sin while knowing it is wrong, making light of it. The sincere person, upon realizing the sin, stops and seeks forgiveness. This verse provides the complete formula for spiritual recovery. It gives hope to every sinner that the door of forgiveness is open, provided they turn back. For the believer, it is a lifeline. After the defeat at Uhud, where mistakes were made, this verse assured the companions that their sins could be forgiven if they turned to Allah. It also counters the despair that can follow sin, and the arrogance that dismisses sin. The believer is trained to live in a cycle of remembrance, repentance, and renewal, always aware that the sole Forgiver is their Lord.
136 | Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever. How excellent is the reward for those who work ˹righteousness˺! | The qualities of the muttaqin lead to a magnificent reward. “Their reward is forgiveness from their Lord and Gardens under which rivers flow, staying there forever.” Notice that forgiveness (maghfirah) is listed as the primary reward, even before the Gardens. This is because the removal of sin and the assurance of divine pardon is the foundation of all eternal joy. Without forgiveness, even Paradise would be shadowed by the fear of past sins. The phrase “from their Lord” (min rabbihim) personalizes the forgiveness, making it a direct gift from the Most Merciful. The Gardens (jannat) are described with the classic Quranic imagery of flowing rivers, symbolizing purity, life, and ceaseless abundance. “Staying there forever” (khalidina fiha) underscores the eternal nature of the bliss; there is no death, no eviction, no diminishing of joy. The verse then marvels: “How excellent is the reward for those who work righteousness!” The word “workers” (al-‘amilin) emphasizes that this reward is not for idle hope but for those who actively strive. The exclamation draws the reader’s attention to the incredible value of the deal: a few years of effort in this world for an eternity of bliss. This verse serves as a culmination of the character description that began with hastening to forgiveness. It answers the question: What do those who possess generosity, patience, forgiveness, and constant repentance receive? They receive the ultimate prize. For the believer, this verse reinforces the link between specific character traits and the reward. It makes the abstract promise of Paradise tangible and motivates the practical application of the qualities listed in the previous verses. It also highlights that forgiveness is the greatest need of the human soul, and when it is granted, everything else is a bonus. The verse inspires a hopeful, forward-looking attitude, encouraging the believer to continue working righteousness, no matter the setbacks, because the final reward is well worth the struggle.
137 | Similar situations came to pass before you, so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers. | The surah now pivots from describing the reward for the righteous to consoling the believers who were wounded and shaken by the defeat at Uhud. “Similar situations came to pass before you.” The term “situations” (sunan) refers to established divine laws and patterns in history. The message is clear: what happened at Uhud—the pain, the loss, the seeming setback—is not unique. It has happened to previous nations and believing communities. Trials are a sunnah, a consistent pattern. The believers are not singled out for suffering; it is part of the human experience and part of the path of faith. The verse then invites a response: “so travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers.” Travel (siru) is encouraged as a means of learning. The “land” (al-ard) is full of archaeological and historical remnants of past civilizations—the ruins of Thamud, the wells of Aad, the palaces of Pharaoh. By seeing these ruins, one can reflect on the ultimate outcome of the battle between truth and falsehood. The deniers may seem powerful temporarily, but their end is always destruction and disgrace. The command to travel and observe serves a dual purpose: it consoles the believers by showing that their current pain is not the final chapter, and it warns them by showing the fate of those who reject Allah’s signs. It also aligns with the Quranic methodology of using empirical observation to build faith. For the believer today, this verse encourages the study of history as a spiritual exercise. History is not merely dates and events; it is a repository of divine patterns. Travel, whether physical or through reading, becomes an act of worship when done with the intention of drawing lessons. The verse also provides perspective. The defeat at Uhud, while painful, was a single battle in a long history; the larger picture is that truth prevails. This perspective helps the believer cope with personal and communal setbacks by zooming out and seeing them as part of a larger, divinely governed pattern.
138 | This is an insight to humanity—a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing. | The verse directly comments on the preceding discourse and the historical patterns mentioned. “This is an insight to humanity.” The word “this” (hadha) refers to the Quranic revelation itself, including the stories, the commands, and the laws. It is a “clear insight” (bayan), a clarification that removes confusion and provides a transparent understanding of reality. It explains why things happen, what the outcomes will be, and how one should live. The term “humanity” (al-nas) indicates that the Quran’s message is universal, not confined to any tribe or era. Then the verse specifies a subgroup: “a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing.” While the Quran is an insight for all, it becomes a “guide” (huda) and a “lesson” (maw‘izah) specifically for the muttaqin. They are the ones who benefit from it, because their hearts are receptive. The guide shows the way, and the lesson touches the heart and prompts action. The combination of bayan, huda, and maw‘izah covers the intellectual, directive, and emotional dimensions of the Quran’s impact. For the believers still reeling from Uhud, this verse reassured them that the revelation they were receiving was precisely the tool they needed to make sense of their experience. It was not just a random event; it was woven into a fabric of divine guidance that clarifies, directs, and softens the heart. For the contemporary believer, this verse affirms that the Quran is the ultimate source of clarity in a confusing world. It encourages the believer to approach the Quran not merely as a text to be recited for reward, but as a living insight, guide, and admonition. The verse also subtly calls the believer to rise to the level of the God-fearing, so that the Quran may fulfill its function in their life fully.
139 | Do not falter or grieve, for you will have the upper hand, if you are ˹true˺ believers. | The surah now delivers a direct, uplifting command to the Muslim community in the aftermath of Uhud. “Do not falter” (la tahinu) means do not become weak, cowardly, or slack in your resolve. The physical and emotional wounds of the battle could lead to demoralization and fear of future confrontations. The verse categorically forbids this state of weakness. “Or grieve” (la tahzanu) addresses the deep sadness over the loss of seventy martyrs, including the beloved uncle of the Prophet, Hamza. Grieving is a natural human emotion, but the command is not to let grief immobilize or consume you. The believer is allowed to feel sorrow, but it must not turn into despair that weakens one’s commitment to the cause. The reason for this command follows: “for you will have the upper hand” (wa antum al-a‘lawn). This is a powerful promise. Despite the military setback, the ultimate victory belongs to the believers. The phrase “al-a‘lawn” means the highest, the most exalted, the ones who will prevail. The promise is conditional, as indicated by the next phrase: “if you are true believers.” The condition of true faith (mu’minin) is the key. The upper hand is not guaranteed by numbers or weapons, but by genuine, active faith. If the believers maintain their iman, learning from their mistakes, they will inevitably rise again. History proved this promise; within a few years, the Quraysh were defeated, Mecca was conquered, and Islam spread. For the believer facing any setback—personal, professional, or communal—this verse is a balm. It acknowledges the pain but redirects the focus to the future. The believer is trained not to wallow in defeat but to rise, strengthened by faith. The phrase “if you are true believers” serves as a self-check: the condition for victory is not a label of Islam but the reality of iman in the heart and actions.
140 | If you have suffered injuries ˹at Uḥud˺, they suffered similarly ˹at Badr˺. We alternate these days ˹of victory and defeat˺ among people so that Allah may reveal the ˹true˺ believers, choose martyrs from among you—and Allah does not like the wrongdoers— | The consolation continues with a logical comparison. “If you have suffered injuries at Uhud, they suffered similarly at Badr.” The verse acknowledges the wound the Muslims received—seventy dead, and the Prophet himself injured. But it immediately balances the equation: a year earlier, at Badr, the disbelievers suffered a similar or even greater blow, with seventy of their chiefs killed and seventy taken captive. War is not one-sided; both sides suffer. This reminder prevents the believers from seeing themselves as perpetual victims. The verse then unveils a profound divine wisdom: “We alternate these days of victory and defeat among people.” The “days” (al-ayyam) refer to the changing fortunes of war and life. Allah deliberately causes these shifts, giving victory to one group at one time and to another at another time. This alternation is not random; it has specific purposes. The first purpose: “so that Allah may reveal the true believers.” Testing through hardship distinguishes the sincere from the hypocrite. At Uhud, the hypocrites deserted, while the sincere remained and fought. The second purpose: “and choose martyrs from among you.” Shahada, martyrdom, is a high honor. Through these battles, Allah selects certain believers to attain this rank, granting them eternal life and nearness to Him. The deaths of the martyrs are not a waste; they are a divine selection. The verse closes with the parenthetical statement: “and Allah does not like the wrongdoers.” This refers to the hypocrites who abandoned the believers, and the disbelievers who transgressed. Their actions are known, and Allah’s dislike of them is a warning. For the believer, this verse provides a framework for understanding loss. No suffering is meaningless; it serves to purify the community, expose the truth, and elevate the ranks of the faithful. The alternation of days also teaches humility—victory is not a permanent possession, but a trust from Allah that He gives and takes according to His wisdom. The believer learns to accept both good and bad days with patience and faith, knowing that both are part of a divine plan.
141 | and distinguish the ˹true˺ believers and destroy the disbelievers. | This verse continues the divine purposes behind the alternation of victory and defeat mentioned in the previous verse. The alternation of days serves to “distinguish the true believers” (wa liyumahhisa Allahu alladhina amanu). The Arabic word yumahhis comes from mahs, which means to purify or to test in order to separate the pure from the impure, much like gold is refined in fire to remove its dross. At the Battle of Uhud, the believers underwent a severe trial that exposed the true nature of hearts. The hypocrites, led by Abdullah ibn Ubayy, withdrew from the army before the battle even began, revealing their lack of genuine faith. During the battle itself, when the tide turned against the Muslims, many who had been outwardly committed fled in panic, while a small core remained steadfast around the Prophet. This process of sifting and distinguishing was an act of divine mercy for the community in the long run, because it made clear who could be relied upon in future struggles. It also had a profound effect on the sincere believers themselves: the ordeal burned away any remaining attachments to this world and deepened their reliance on Allah. The second purpose is “and destroy the disbelievers” (wa yamhaqa al-kafirin). The verb yamhaq means to obliterate gradually, to wipe out, or to diminish until nothing remains. This was fulfilled in multiple stages. The disbelievers, despite their apparent victory at Uhud, did not achieve their ultimate goal of wiping out Islam. Instead, their moral and military strength was progressively eroded in the years that followed, culminating in the conquest of Mecca and the removal of idolatry from the Arabian Peninsula. The verse thus reassures the believers that the temporary success of the enemy is not a sign of divine approval, but part of a process that will eventually lead to their ruin. For the believer today, this verse provides a lens through which to view tests and hardships. Trials serve to purify the community, to separate the sincere from the insincere, and to set the stage for the eventual defeat of falsehood. It teaches that when believers face difficulties, they should not despair but reflect on what is being purified in their own hearts. The destruction of the disbelievers mentioned here is not only physical; it can also be intellectual and moral. The arguments of disbelief are gradually eroded, and the systems built on injustice crumble over time. This dual purpose—purification of the believers and destruction of the disbelievers—is a recurring sunnah of Allah in the struggle between truth and falsehood. The believer is therefore encouraged to remain patient and to trust that the trial is a refining fire, and that the final outcome is with the people of taqwa.
142 | Do you think you will enter Paradise without Allah proving which of you ˹truly˺ struggled ˹for His cause˺ and patiently endured? | After describing the divine purposes behind trials, the surah now addresses a misconception that some believers might have harbored: that mere verbal profession of faith is sufficient for entry into Paradise. “Do you think you will enter Paradise without Allah proving which of you truly struggled for His cause and patiently endured?” The question “do you think” (am hasibtum) is a rhetorical challenge to any sense of complacency or entitlement. The Companions had endured much—migration, poverty, persecution—and after the victory at Badr, some might have assumed that their place in Paradise was secured without further trials. The defeat at Uhud shattered that assumption and reminded them that Paradise is earned through genuine struggle and patience. The phrase “truly struggled” translates the word jahadu, which denotes striving with utmost effort, particularly in the face of an enemy. But the term is broader than physical fighting; it encompasses the inner struggle against one’s own desires, the struggle to remain obedient, and the struggle to uphold the truth in all circumstances. The verse specifies that this struggle must be coupled with “patiently endured” (sabaru). Sabr is the quality that sustains the struggler through hardship, keeping them from despair or disobedience. The combination of jihad and sabr is essential: one cannot struggle effectively without patience, and patience without action is insufficient. Allah’s knowledge of who possesses these qualities is eternal, but He manifests that knowledge in the realm of human experience through tests, so that on the Day of Judgment, the reward will be based on evident, witnessed deeds. This is part of divine justice. For the believers at Uhud, the verse was a call to re-evaluate themselves. Had they truly struggled and been patient? The archers who left their post lacked patience; those who fled lacked both struggle and patience. The verse serves as a timeless reminder that the path to Paradise is not a walk of comfort but a road paved with tests. The believer should never assume that they are safe; they must continually prove their faith through actions and steadfastness. It also provides comfort, because it frames hardship as the very test that leads to Paradise. The struggle is not a sign of Allah’s abandonment but the means by which one qualifies for His eternal reward.
143 | You certainly wished ˹for the opportunity˺ for martyrdom before encountering it, now you have seen it with your own eyes. | The surah now reminds the believers of a wish they had expressed before the battle, holding up a mirror to their own previous desires. “You certainly wished for the opportunity for martyrdom before encountering it.” Before the Battle of Badr, many companions who had not participated in that great victory expressed a fervent longing to be present at a battle where they could attain martyrdom (shahadah). They prayed for the chance to lay down their lives in the cause of Allah, to prove their devotion, and to attain the high rank of a martyr. This wish was sincere, born from a genuine love for the Hereafter and a desire to be counted among the most honoured of the believers. The verse then brings the reality into sharp focus: “now you have seen it with your own eyes.” At Uhud, the opportunity they had wished for was laid before them. They saw the flashing swords, the falling arrows, the faces of death. The experience was not a romantic ideal but a terrifying, painful reality. The verse does not condemn them for wishing for martyrdom; rather, it points out the gap between the idealised wish and the harsh reality, and it implies that the wish carries a heavy responsibility. When the moment of truth arrived, some of them wavered and fled. The lesson is profound: it is easy to express lofty aspirations from a distance, but the true test is when the aspiration materialises and demands everything from you. The believers are being taught to be careful what they wish for, and to prepare themselves spiritually so that when the test comes, they can live up to their words. This verse also serves as a psychological analysis of the human condition. Many people aspire to great acts of sacrifice, but when the opportunity presents itself, they hesitate. The cure is to train oneself in smaller acts of daily sacrifice, so that the greater sacrifice becomes possible. For the believer today, the verse prompts a sincere self-examination. One may speak of wanting to serve the deen, to give for the sake of Allah, or to be among the steadfast, but the actions in daily life reveal the truth. The companions were not condemned for their momentary failure; the surah’s purpose is to instruct and uplift, not to humiliate. Thus, the verse is a compassionate reminder that the path of true devotion requires a realism about one’s own weaknesses and a reliance on Allah’s help to fulfill one’s noble intentions.
144 | Muḥammad is no more than a messenger; other messengers have gone before him. If he were to die or to be killed, would you regress into disbelief? Those who do so will not harm Allah whatsoever. And Allah will reward those who are grateful. | The Battle of Uhud brought a moment of extreme crisis when a rumour spread that the Prophet Muhammad had been killed. Many of the believers were thrown into despair and confusion, and some lost their will to fight. This verse was revealed to address that very moment and to establish a fundamental principle of Islamic faith. “Muhammad is no more than a messenger; other messengers have gone before him.” This simple statement reorients the focus of faith. The Prophet is a human being, chosen by Allah to deliver the message. He is not divine, nor is his physical existence the source of the religion’s permanence. Just as previous messengers—Moses, Jesus, and others—completed their missions and passed away, so too would Muhammad eventually leave this world. The faith does not stand on the mortal life of one man; it stands on the eternal God who sent him. The verse then poses a sharp question: “If he were to die or to be killed, would you regress into disbelief?” The word “regress” (inqalabtum) implies turning on one’s heels, retreating from the path. The question exposes the weakness of a faith that is attached to a person rather than to the Lord of that person. True faith in Allah and His Messenger means that the death of the Messenger, however painful, does not shake the believer’s commitment to the message. The verse continues: “Those who do so will not harm Allah whatsoever.” Apostasy and turning back after the Prophet’s death do not diminish Allah’s majesty or His plan in the slightest. The harm falls entirely on the apostates themselves. The verse concludes with a promise: “And Allah will reward those who are grateful.” The grateful ones are those who remain steadfast, recognizing that the gift of guidance is from Allah and that the Prophet’s death does not nullify that gift. The incident of Uhud, where the rumour of the Prophet’s death caused such turmoil, became the occasion for this crucial clarification. Later, when the Prophet did pass away in 11 AH, Umar ibn al-Khattab, in his shock, initially refused to believe it, but Abu Bakr al-Siddiq recited this very verse, bringing the community back to its senses. Abu Bakr’s words, “Whoever worshipped Muhammad, let him know that Muhammad is dead; but whoever worshipped Allah, let him know that Allah is Alive and never dies,” echo the spirit of this verse. For the believer today, this verse is an anchor in times of personal loss or communal crisis, reminding that faith is in the eternal, not in the mortal. It also teaches gratitude for the guidance that remains with us after the Messenger’s departure, preserved in the Quran and Sunnah.
145 | No soul can ever die without Allah’s Will at the destined time. Those who desire worldly gain, We will let them have it, and those who desire heavenly reward, We will grant it to them. And We will reward those who are grateful. | Building on the lesson that the Prophet’s death does not mean the end of faith, this verse addresses the ultimate reality of death itself and the importance of one’s intention. “No soul can ever die without Allah’s Will at the destined time.” This is one of the most comforting and empowering truths in the Quran. Death is not a random accident, nor does it come at the hands of an enemy’s sword unless Allah has decreed it. The “destined time” (ajalin musamman) is a fixed, appointed term that cannot be advanced or delayed by a single moment. On the battlefield of Uhud, some believers might have thought that if they had not gone out to fight, they would have lived. This verse corrects that notion: the place and moment of death are determined by Allah alone. A person will not die before their appointed time, even if they throw themselves into danger, and they will not live a moment beyond it, even if they hide in fortified towers. This understanding frees the believer from the paralyzing fear of death and from the illusion that cowardice can extend life. The verse then contrasts two categories of people based on their ultimate desire: “Those who desire worldly gain, We will let them have it, and those who desire heavenly reward, We will grant it to them.” At Uhud, some fighters were motivated by the prospect of spoils of war, while others fought solely for the Hereafter. Allah grants both their respective aims: the worldly-minded get their fleeting goods, and the Hereafter-minded receive the eternal reward. However, the worldly-minded may not receive all they desire, and their share is limited to what Allah wills, while the Hereafter-minded receive a generous, multiplied reward. The verse ends with “And We will reward those who are grateful.” Gratefulness is the proper response to the clarity of this teaching. The grateful person uses this knowledge to prioritize the Hereafter and to face death with courage, knowing that their appointed time is in Allah’s hands. For the believer today, this verse is a powerful tool against anxiety about death and loss. It encourages a life lived with the right intention, aiming for heavenly reward, and accepting that every soul’s term is fixed. In practical terms, it means not being paralyzed by fear of danger when standing for truth, and not envying the worldly gains of those who seek only this life.
146 | ˹Imagine˺ how many devotees fought along with their prophets and never faltered despite whatever ˹losses˺ they suffered in the cause of Allah, nor did they weaken or give in! Allah loves those who persevere. | After addressing the weakness shown at Uhud, the surah now inspires the believers by drawing lessons from the history of previous prophets and their followers. “Imagine how many devotees fought along with their prophets…” The term “devotees” (ribbiyyun) refers to learned, pious, and steadfast men who dedicate themselves to their Lord. The word can also mean large numbers or groups. The verse invites the listener to reflect on the numerous battles and struggles that the followers of past prophets faced. These were not easy victories; they involved immense suffering, casualties, and hardship. Yet the mark of these true followers is that they “never faltered” (ma wahanu) despite the losses they suffered. “Faltering” here means losing resolve or becoming weak-hearted. They did not lose their morale even when they were outnumbered, wounded, or when their prophets were killed or injured. The verse further says, “nor did they weaken or give in.” “Weaken” (da‘ufu) refers to a decline in physical or moral strength, and “give in” (istakkanu) means to surrender or yield to the enemy out of despair. The triple negation—no faltering, no weakening, no giving in—paints a picture of absolute steadfastness that becomes a model for the Muslim community. The verse then states the divine love that is the reward for this quality: “Allah loves those who persevere” (al-sabirin). The perseverance mentioned here is the comprehensive patience that endures hardship without complaint and continues striving without retreat. The love of Allah is the highest accolade. For the believers at Uhud, who had seen some among them flee and others momentarily lose heart, this verse was a call to aspire to the station of those noble predecessors. It reminded them that they were part of a long chain of believers who faced similar or worse trials. The lesson is not to feel unique in suffering, but to rise to the standard set by the devoted servants of the past. For the contemporary believer, this verse provides a powerful motivation to remain steadfast in the face of personal and communal trials. When one feels like giving up, the image of those ribbiyyun who stood firm through the ages serves as a source of strength. The love of Allah for the patient becomes the ultimate goal that makes every sacrifice worthwhile.
147 | And all they said was, “Our Lord! Forgive our sins and excesses, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the disbelieving people.” | The surah continues its portrayal of the steadfast devotees by revealing the words of their supplication during the heat of battle. “And all they said was, ‘Our Lord! Forgive our sins and excesses, make our steps firm, and grant us victory over the disbelieving people.’” This is a profound insight into the psychology of the true believer in moments of extreme trial. The first thing they ask for is not victory, but forgiveness. They say, “Forgive our sins and excesses” (ighfir lana dhunubana wa israfana fi amrina). They are acutely aware that their own shortcomings might be a cause of their trials, and they turn to Allah to purify them. The mention of “excesses” (israf) refers to going beyond the bounds in any matter, even in matters of religion or personal conduct. The sincere heart attributes any difficulty first to its own flaws. This is a mark of humility and spiritual maturity. Their next request is “make our steps firm” (thabbit aqdamana). This is a prayer for steadfastness, for the ability to stand their ground and not flee. The “steps” (aqdam) being made firm is a metaphor for unwavering resolve, especially in the physical context of battle where feet are literally planted. Finally, after seeking forgiveness and steadfastness, they ask for the outward success: “grant us victory over the disbelieving people.” The sequence is crucial: inward purification, then personal steadfastness, then collective victory. This teaches that true victory is a fruit of spiritual readiness, not merely of strategic planning. The fact that this supplication is placed in the context of the Battle of Uhud is highly instructive. The Muslims at Uhud had, to some extent, faltered and disobeyed. The verse implicitly shows them the supplication they should have made, and the supplication they should make in the future. It also demonstrates that the pious predecessors were not superhuman beings devoid of fear or sin; they acknowledged their sins, feared their own weakness, and begged Allah for help. For the believer today, this supplication is a treasure. It can be recited in any struggle—whether against external enemies, inner desires, or life’s challenges. It inculcates the habit of turning to Allah first for forgiveness, then for firmness, and then for the desired outcome. It teaches that victory is ultimately in Allah’s hands, and that the believer’s primary concern should be their own relationship with their Lord.
148 | So Allah gave them the reward of this world and the excellent reward of the Hereafter. For Allah loves the good-doers. | This verse describes the magnificent outcome that the steadfast devotees received as a result of their patience and supplication. “So Allah gave them the reward of this world and the excellent reward of the Hereafter.” The “reward of this world” (thawab al-dunya) includes tangible outcomes such as victory, honour, security, and lawful spoils. In the cases of many prophets and their followers, Allah granted them triumph over their enemies, established them in the land, and blessed them with dignity and influence. The reward of this world is not to be despised; it is a legitimate divine blessing that can serve as a foretaste of the greater good to come. The “excellent reward of the Hereafter” (husna thawab al-akhirah) refers to the eternal bliss of Paradise, the pleasure of Allah, and the safety from the Fire. This reward is “excellent” (husna) because it is perfect, untainted, and everlasting. The verse explicitly mentions that Allah gave them both, not just one. This refutes the idea that those who are devoted to Allah must necessarily suffer in this world and only receive their reward in the Hereafter. While tests and trials are part of the path, the ultimate divine plan often includes worldly success and honour for the righteous, alongside the supreme reward of the Hereafter. The verse concludes by stating the reason: “For Allah loves the good-doers.” The good-doers (al-muhsinin) are those who reach the station of ihsan—excellence in worship and conduct, acting as if they see Allah. This love from Allah is the greatest reward of all, and it is the cause of both the worldly and otherworldly success. For the believers at Uhud, this verse was a powerful motivation. It showed them what was attainable if they emulated the patience and devotion of past believers. The loss at Uhud was not the final word; if they reformed and became muhsinin, they too could attain both worldly honour and Hereafter bliss. For the contemporary believer, this verse instills hope and ambition. It encourages striving for excellence in all aspects of life, knowing that Allah’s love is the ultimate prize. It also balances the often one-sided emphasis on the Hereafter by acknowledging that Allah can grant good in this life as well, as a gift and a sign of His pleasure.
149 | O believers! If you yield to the disbelievers, they will drag you back to disbelief—and you will become losers. | After the inspiring examples of past believers, the surah returns to a direct warning for the Muslim community, drawing a lesson from the recent experience. “O believers! If you yield to the disbelievers, they will drag you back to disbelief—and you will become losers.” The word “yield” (tuti‘u) means to obey, to follow, to comply with. The warning is against taking the disbelievers as guides, confidants, or authorities in matters of faith and life. The context of Uhud is crucial: the hypocrites and the enemy had tried to sow doubt and create division among the Muslims. Some believers might have been tempted to listen to the disbelievers’ arguments, to question the wisdom of the Prophet’s commands, or to adopt the enemy’s worldview to avoid conflict. The verse warns that yielding, even in small matters, is a slippery slope that can lead to apostasy. The phrase “drag you back to disbelief” (yaruddukum ‘ala a‘qabikum kafirin) evokes the image of turning on one’s heels and retreating from faith entirely. The ultimate result of such yielding is catastrophic loss: “you will become losers” (tanqalibu khasirin). The loss is absolute—loss of faith, loss of divine support, loss of this world’s true good, and loss of the Hereafter. This verse complements the earlier command not to take disbelievers as intimate friends or protectors. It specifies the danger: they will not be satisfied with mere friendship; their goal is to revert the believers to disbelief. The believer is thus called to be vigilant and uncompromising in matters of principle. This does not mean being rude or unjust, but it does mean not compromising core beliefs and practices in order to please or accommodate those who oppose Islam. The verse also serves as a psychological defense. By knowing the enemy’s ultimate intention, the believer is less likely to be deceived by superficial kindness or clever arguments. For the contemporary believer, this verse is a reminder to maintain a clear distinction between the values of Islam and the values of systems that contradict it. Whether in the workplace, in education, or in social settings, the pressure to conform to non-Islamic norms can be intense. The verse warns that gradual yielding can lead to a complete erosion of faith. The only safe path is to hold firmly to the truth and to seek strength through the community of believers and through constant connection to Allah.
150 | But no! Allah is your Guardian, and He is the best Helper. | After the strong warning against yielding to the disbelievers, the verse immediately provides the positive alternative, turning the believers’ attention to their true source of support. “But no! Allah is your Guardian, and He is the best Helper.” The opening “But no!” (bal) is an emphatic negation that overturns any assumption that the believers are without support or that they need the disbelievers’ favour. It redirects the heart away from fear of the enemy and towards trust in the Almighty. The term “Guardian” (mawla) is rich in meaning. It denotes a protector, an ally, a master, and a friend who is truly loyal and beneficial. Unlike human guardians who may fail or betray, Allah’s guardianship is perfect and unwavering. He protects His believing servants from spiritual and physical harms, guides them through difficulties, and ultimately grants them success. The believers who had just suffered a defeat at Uhud might have felt abandoned or vulnerable. This verse reassures them that their Mawla is Allah, and no defeat changes that reality. The verse then emphasizes, “and He is the best Helper” (wa ni‘ma al-nasir). Al-Nasir is the One who grants victory and assistance. He is not just a helper; He is the best possible helper, because His help is all-powerful, all-wise, and always in the best interest of the servant. When the believers were small in number at Badr, He helped them and they won. When they were larger at Uhud and suffered, it was not because His help was unavailable, but because they had lessons to learn. His help is always near for those who are patient and God-conscious. The shift from warning to reassurance is characteristic of the Quranic method. The believers are told not to fear the disbelievers’ power to harm them spiritually or materially, because Allah’s guardianship is far superior. This verse also implicitly instructs the believers not to seek guardianship and help from the wrong sources, as earlier verses had prohibited. With Allah as their Mawla and Nasir, they need no other ultimate reliance. For the believer today, this verse is a powerful source of comfort and confidence. In times of isolation, persecution, or apparent weakness, the believer recalls that Allah is their Guardian and the best Helper. This does not negate the need for practical measures, but it anchors the heart so that no situation seems hopeless. The verse encourages a constant turning to Allah for support, knowing that no enemy can overpower the one whom Allah guards.
151 | We will cast horror into the hearts of the disbelievers for associating ˹false gods˺ with Allah—a practice He has never authorized. The Fire will be their home—what an evil place for the wrongdoers to stay! | The surah now declares a specific form of divine aid that will be granted against the enemy, linking their spiritual crime to their psychological punishment. “We will cast horror into the hearts of the disbelievers.” The word “horror” (ru‘b) is an intense, overwhelming fear that destabilizes the mind and paralyzes action. This was a miraculous phenomenon witnessed at several battles, where the disbelievers, despite their superior numbers and equipment, were seized by unexplained terror and fled. At Badr, the Muslims were few and poorly armed, yet the Quraysh were filled with dread. At Uhud, although the Muslims suffered losses, the Quraysh army, under Abu Sufyan, did not press their advantage to annihilate the remaining Muslims in Medina; instead, they withdrew. Many reports indicate that Allah cast such fear into their hearts that they decided to return to Mecca, fearing a counterattack. The verse then explains the cause of this divine action: “for associating false gods with Allah—a practice He has never authorized.” Their crime is shirk, the association of partners with Allah in worship, which is the ultimate injustice. Because they committed this atrocity without any divine sanction, Allah punished them with internal terror. The verse then shifts to the ultimate punishment: “The Fire will be their home—what an evil place for the wrongdoers to stay!” The casting of horror in this world is a mere foretaste of the eternal terror and suffering that awaits them. The phrase “what an evil place for the wrongdoers to stay” (bi’sa mathwa al-zalimin) emphasizes the wretchedness of their final abode. For the believers, this verse served as a powerful reassurance. The enemy that seemed so formidable was, in reality, spiritually and psychologically vulnerable. Their false gods could not protect them from the fear that Allah casts directly into their hearts. This knowledge emboldened the believers and diminished their own fear. For the contemporary believer, this verse teaches that the power of falsehood is inherently fragile. Those who oppose the truth may appear strong, but their hearts are often filled with insecurity and terror, even if they do not show it. The believer, armed with faith, has a serenity that the disbeliever cannot possess. The verse also reinforces the gravity of shirk and its dire consequences in both worlds.
152 | Indeed, Allah fulfilled His promise to you when you ˹initially˺ swept them away by His Will, then your courage weakened and you disputed about the command and disobeyed, after Allah had brought victory within your reach. Some of you were after worldly gain while others desired a heavenly reward. He denied you victory over them as a test, yet He has pardoned you. And Allah is Gracious to the believers. | This verse is the heart of the Uhud narrative, providing a detailed analysis of why the battle turned from initial victory to painful loss. “Indeed, Allah fulfilled His promise to you when you initially swept them away by His Will.” The battle began exactly as promised. The Muslims, despite being smaller in number, were overwhelming the Quraysh. The enemy was fleeing, and the Muslims were collecting spoils. Allah’s promise of victory was true and was being fulfilled. The verse then lists the human failures that reversed the situation: “then your courage weakened and you disputed about the command and disobeyed.” The weakening of courage (fashiltum) refers to the loss of resolve when some fighters saw the spoils and lost focus. The dispute (tanaza‘tum) was among the archers whom the Prophet had stationed on the hill with explicit orders not to leave their post under any circumstances. When they saw the spoils, most of them argued that they should join the collection, despite their leader Abdullah ibn Jubayr’s reminders. The disobedience (‘asaytum) was their actual abandonment of the post. This happened “after Allah had brought victory within your reach,” meaning the triumph was virtually in their hands. Then the verse diagnoses the underlying motives: “Some of you were after worldly gain while others desired a heavenly reward.” The division of intentions weakened the unity of action. Those who desired the spoils left their post; those who sought the Hereafter stayed or fought. The result: “He denied you victory over them as a test.” The withdrawal of the initial victory was a trial, to teach the believers the consequences of disobedience and divided intentions. Yet the verse does not end with condemnation: “yet He has pardoned you. And Allah is Gracious to the believers.” Despite the grave error, Allah’s mercy enveloped them. The defeat was not a total annihilation; it was a disciplinary lesson, and forgiveness was granted. This verse is a masterclass in divine education. It shows that success depends on obedience and unity of purpose. It also reveals the immense grace of Allah, who turns even failures into opportunities for growth and then pardons His servants. For the believer, it is a timeless lesson in self-accountability. When setbacks occur, one must look at one’s own actions and intentions, correct them, and then seek Allah’s forgiveness, trusting in His graciousness.
153 | ˹Remember˺ when you were running far away ˹in panic˺—not looking at anyone—while the Messenger was calling to you from behind! So Allah rewarded your disobedience with distress upon distress. Now, do not grieve over the victory you were denied or the injury you suffered. And Allah is All-Aware of what you do. | The surah paints a vivid and painful picture of the chaos that ensued when the tide turned. “Remember when you were running far away in panic—not looking at anyone—while the Messenger was calling to you from behind!” The verb “running far away” (tus‘idun) suggests climbing and fleeing upward, scattered across the mountain slopes. The phrase “not looking at anyone” (la talwuna ‘ala ahad) conveys the single-minded panic that erased all concern for comrades or the Prophet. Meanwhile, the Prophet Muhammad, with a small band of steadfast companions, was calling out, “To me, O servants of Allah! I am the Messenger of Allah!” He was trying to rally them, but the fleeing was so widespread that his voice was ignored or unheard by many. This moment exposed the depth of the human panic instinct when faith wavers. The verse then states: “So Allah rewarded your disobedience with distress upon distress.” The “distress” (ghamm) refers to the grief, fear, and confusion that overwhelmed them. The first distress was the defeat and the rumour of the Prophet’s death; the second distress was the realization of their own failure and the loss of the battle. This accumulation of distress was a direct consequence of their earlier disobedience. The verse then offers a balm: “Now, do not grieve over the victory you were denied or the injury you suffered.” After the lesson is delivered, Allah consoles them. The lost victory and the physical and emotional injuries should not be a source of prolonged despair. The divine pedagogy is complete; what remains is to learn and move forward. “And Allah is All-Aware of what you do” reminds them that their inner state, their repentance, and their future efforts are all fully known to Allah. For the believer, this verse is a stark reminder of the consequences of disobedience to the Messenger. It also shows the incredible mercy of Allah, who after describing their humiliating flight, tells them not to grieve. The past cannot be changed, but the future can be rebuilt. The image of the Prophet calling to his fleeing companions is one of the most touching in the Quran, demonstrating his unwavering commitment to his ummah. It also serves as a call to every believer to heed the call of the Messenger in their own lives, not to flee from the responsibilities of faith when the battle—whether literal or metaphorical—becomes intense.
154 | Then after distress, He sent down serenity in the form of drowsiness overcoming some of you, while others were disturbed by evil thoughts about Allah—the thoughts of ˹pre-Islamic˺ ignorance. They ask, “Do we have a say in the matter?” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “All matters are destined by Allah.” They conceal in their hearts what they do not reveal to you. They say ˹to themselves˺, “If we had any say in the matter, none of us would have come to die here.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Even if you were to remain in your homes, those among you who were destined to be killed would have met the same fate.” Through this, Allah tests what is within you and purifies what is in your hearts. And Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart. | The verse describes the contrasting psychological states of two groups after the battle. “Then after distress, He sent down serenity in the form of drowsiness overcoming some of you.” The “some” were the sincere believers. After the extreme terror and exhaustion of the battle, Allah granted them a miraculous calmness manifesting as a light sleep or drowsiness (nu‘as). This was a sign of divine security (sakinah). In a state of fear, sleep normally flees, but the sincere were given this gift as a reassurance. In contrast, “others were disturbed by evil thoughts about Allah—the thoughts of pre-Islamic ignorance.” These “others” were the hypocrites and those with weak faith. Their minds were filled with doubts about Allah’s promise, questioning whether the Prophet was truly a messenger, whether Allah had abandoned them, and whether the whole endeavor was a mistake. Their thought pattern was that of the pre-Islamic era (al-jahiliyyah), which lacked firm trust in divine decree. They asked, “Do we have a say in the matter?” This was a rhetorical question expressing their feeling that they had been dragged into a disastrous situation without any real control or consultation. The Prophet is instructed to answer: “All matters are destined by Allah.” Victory and defeat, life and death, all are under Allah’s complete control. Then the verse reveals their hidden whispers: “They conceal in their hearts what they do not reveal to you. They say to themselves, ‘If we had any say in the matter, none of us would have come to die here.’” These were the hypocrites who believed that if they had been in charge, they would have avoided the battle altogether and thus prevented the deaths. This reveals a total lack of faith in qadr. The Prophet is commanded to respond: “Even if you were to remain in your homes, those among you who were destined to be killed would have met the same fate.” This is the exact argument made earlier about death’s appointed time. The purpose of all this is explained: “Through this, Allah tests what is within you and purifies what is in your hearts.” The trial at Uhud separated the sincere, who received serenity, from the hypocrites, who were exposed. And the sincere were also purified of their own inner impurities. The verse ends, “And Allah knows best what is hidden in the heart.” For the believer, this verse is a profound study of the effects of faith and doubt under pressure. It teaches that true iman brings divine peace, even in the midst of catastrophe, while weak faith and hypocrisy breed confusion and blame. The believer learns to recognize the peace of faith as a sign, and to reject the doubts of jahiliyyah that question divine decree when things go wrong.
155 | Indeed, those ˹believers˺ who fled on the day when the two armies met were made to slip by Satan because of their misdeeds. But Allah has pardoned them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing. | The verse addresses the painful reality of those believers who genuinely had faith but were overcome by panic and fled from the battlefield. “Indeed, those believers who fled on the day when the two armies met were made to slip by Satan because of their misdeeds.” The phrase “were made to slip” (istazallahum) indicates that Satan caused them to stumble and fall into the error of fleeing. However, the verse does not absolve them of responsibility; Satan was able to influence them “because of their misdeeds” (bima kasabu). Their prior sins or errors—such as disobeying the Prophet’s command, arguing over the spoils, or having mixed intentions—created a vulnerability that Satan exploited. This teaches that sins weaken the spiritual immune system, making a person susceptible to greater failures. The flight of these believers was not an act of outright disbelief or hypocrisy; it was a moment of weakness that they deeply regretted. The verse then offers the balm of divine mercy: “But Allah has pardoned them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.” The word “pardoned” (‘afa) is a complete and gracious forgiveness, wiping away the sin and its consequences. The combination of the divine names “All-Forgiving” (Ghafur) and “Most Forbearing” (Halim) is significant. Ghafur indicates that Allah covers and erases the sin, while Halim indicates that He does not hasten punishment but gives respite and is gentle in His correction. The flight of these believers could have merited severe punishment, but Allah’s forbearance and forgiveness encompassed them. This verse was a source of immense relief for those companions, like Uthman ibn Affan and others, who had fled in the confusion and later felt overwhelming guilt. It taught the community that while sins have consequences, the door of repentance is always open, and Allah’s mercy is vast enough to cover even the grave error of fleeing from battle. For the believer today, this verse is a powerful reminder that no matter how great the fall, if it is followed by sincere regret and amendment, Allah’s forgiveness is near. It also warns against the incremental danger of small misdeeds, which can pave the way for larger failures. The verse balances justice with mercy, showing that Satan’s role does not negate human accountability, but Allah’s forgiveness can erase the stain of even major sins.
156 | O believers! Do not be like the unfaithful who say about their brothers who travel throughout the land or engage in battle, “If they had stayed with us, they would not have died or been killed.” Allah makes such thinking a cause of agony in their hearts. It is Allah who gives life and causes death. And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do. | The surah now addresses a specific form of erroneous thinking that was spreading among some of the weaker believers and the hypocrites after Uhud. “O believers! Do not be like the unfaithful who say about their brothers who travel throughout the land or engage in battle, ‘If they had stayed with us, they would not have died or been killed.’” The “unfaithful” (kafaru) refers to the hypocrites or outright disbelievers. When news of casualties came, whether from a trading journey or from the battlefield, they would claim that the deaths could have been avoided if the deceased had only stayed home. This “if only” (law) thinking is a rejection of divine decree and a path to endless, useless regret. The verse reveals the divine punishment for this attitude: “Allah makes such thinking a cause of agony in their hearts.” The constant dwelling on hypothetical alternatives that contradict Allah’s decree creates a permanent state of grief, anxiety, and despair. The heart that does not submit to qadr is left to torment itself with scenarios that can never be. The verse then provides the correct creedal anchor: “It is Allah who gives life and causes death.” Life and death are exclusively in Allah’s hands. The time, place, and manner of death are fixed by His decree. Whether a person travels, fights, or stays home, they will die at their appointed time. This belief liberates the heart from the tyranny of “what if.” The verse concludes, “And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do.” He observes the reactions, the words, and the hidden thoughts of both the faithful and the unfaithful. For the believers, this verse is a call to sound theology and mental health. The “if only” mentality is a spiritual disease that the Prophet explicitly forbade: “If something befalls you, do not say, ‘If only I had done such and such,’ but say, ‘Qaddar Allahu wa ma sha’a fa‘al (Allah decreed and what He wills He does),’ for ‘if only’ opens the door to Satan’s work.” The believer accepts what has happened and moves forward, trusting that Allah’s decree is always ultimately good, even when it involves loss. The verse also warns against the company of those who spread this toxic mindset.
157 | Should you be martyred or die in the cause of Allah, then His forgiveness and mercy are far better than whatever ˹wealth˺ those ˹who stay behind˺ accumulate. | The verse continues the consolation for the losses suffered at Uhud, redirecting the believers’ attention from the pain of death to the immeasurable gain that comes with it for the sincere. “Should you be martyred or die in the cause of Allah, then His forgiveness and mercy are far better than whatever wealth those who stay behind accumulate.” The phrase “in the cause of Allah” (fi sabil Allah) is the crucial qualifier. When a believer’s life ends while they are striving in the path of Allah—whether by martyrdom in battle or by a natural death during a journey for His sake—the outcome is not loss but supreme profit. The verse lists two things that await such a person: “His forgiveness and mercy.” Forgiveness wipes away all sins, and mercy encompasses all forms of divine pleasure, honour, and bliss. This forgiveness and mercy are described as “far better” (khayr) than all the worldly possessions that the people who stayed behind are busy accumulating. The contrast is deliberate. Those who refrained from battle and clung to their lives and wealth may enjoy temporary material gains, but they miss out on the eternal riches. The verse is not condemning lawful wealth, but it is putting it in its proper, inferior place relative to the rewards of the Hereafter. This verse was a direct comfort to the families of the seventy martyrs of Uhud, including Hamza ibn Abd al-Muttalib, the beloved uncle of the Prophet whose body was mutilated. It assured them that their loved ones had not lost anything, but had gained something that no amount of gold or land could match. The verse also serves as a motivation for future sacrifice. It removes the fear of death by framing it as a transaction where one trades the temporary, mixed blessings of this world for the pure, eternal blessings of Allah’s forgiveness and mercy. For the believer today, this verse is a powerful antidote to the materialism that makes one cling to life at the expense of duty. It reframes death in the path of duty—whether it be in defending the oppressed, speaking truth to power, or any other form of striving for Allah’s cause—as a gateway to the greatest imaginable good. The believer learns to view the sacrifices demanded by faith not as losses but as investments with an infinite return.
158 | Whether you die or are martyred—all of you will be gathered before Allah. | This concise verse delivers a profound and all-encompassing reminder about the ultimate destiny of every human being. “Whether you die or are martyred—all of you will be gathered before Allah.” It addresses the reality that death is inevitable for everyone, regardless of the manner in which it occurs. The distinction between “die” (muttum) and “are martyred” (qutiltum) covers the two primary ways a believer might depart this life: a natural death or a violent death in the path of Allah. Both lead to the same final destination: the gathering before Allah on the Day of Judgment. The word “gathered” (tuhsharun) refers to the great assembly (hashr) where every soul from the first to the last will be brought forth to stand before their Lord for accountability. This reminder serves several purposes in the context of Uhud. First, it neutralises the fear of death that had caused some to flee. Since everyone will eventually die and be gathered before Allah, the only thing that matters is the state in which one arrives at that gathering. The person who dies in bed and the person who dies on the battlefield will both stand in the same court; the difference will be in the deeds they bring. Therefore, the one who dies as a martyr has secured a better standing. Second, it consoles those who lost loved ones. The deceased are not lost forever; they have merely preceded the living to the gathering place. The separation is temporary. Third, it warns those who cling to life at all costs, thinking they can escape death. No escape is possible; the only choice is how to invest the time before the inevitable gathering. For the believer, this verse is a constant reminder of the transience of this world and the certainty of the Hereafter. It instills a mindset of preparation. Every morning and evening, the believer is aware that they are moving closer to the day when they will be gathered before Allah. This awareness shapes priorities, decisions, and conduct. The verse also simplifies complex fears. Rather than worrying about the particular cause of death, the believer focuses on the state of their soul when death arrives. The gathering is the great equaliser; all will be there, and all will be judged justly. The believer therefore lives with the gathering in mind, striving to be among those who are gathered with honour and mercy.
159 | It is out of Allah’s mercy that you ˹O Prophet˺ have been lenient with them. Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you. So pardon them, ask Allah’s forgiveness for them, and consult with them in ˹conducting˺ matters. Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah. Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him. | This verse turns from the soldiers to the leader, addressing the Prophet Muhammad directly and, through him, all leaders and guides. It highlights a crucial quality that preserved the Muslim community after the disaster at Uhud. “It is out of Allah’s mercy that you, O Prophet, have been lenient with them.” The word “lenient” (linta) means gentle, soft, and approachable. The Prophet, despite the enormous pain he suffered—wounded, having lost his uncle and many companions, and having been disobeyed—did not react with harshness or fury. This leniency was not merely a personal trait; it was a direct gift of Allah’s mercy. It was divinely placed in his heart to save the community. The verse then explains the consequence of the opposite behaviour: “Had you been cruel or hard-hearted, they would have certainly abandoned you.” If the Prophet had been harsh (fazz) and hard-hearted (ghaliz al-qalb), the companions, already filled with guilt and grief, would have been crushed. They would have scattered, leaving the mission in ruins. This reveals a timeless leadership principle: harshness repels, gentleness attracts. The Prophet’s gentleness after their grave error was what allowed the community to heal and rebound. The verse then issues a series of commands that form a comprehensive guide for leadership: “So pardon them, ask Allah’s forgiveness for them, and consult with them in conducting matters.” Pardoning (‘fu) addresses the personal right of the leader to be angry; he should forgive. Seeking Allah’s forgiveness (istaghfir) addresses their spiritual need; the leader intercedes for them. Consultation (shawirhum) addresses their collective dignity and the practical need for diverse input. This command to consult was given even after the consultation before Uhud had led to a result that some might have considered a mistake, teaching that consultation remains a duty regardless of outcomes. The next step is “Once you make a decision, put your trust in Allah.” After consultation, a firm decision is made, and then reliance (tawakkul) is placed on Allah, not on the decision itself. The verse ends with “Surely Allah loves those who trust in Him.” This is the reward for the leader who embodies these qualities: divine love. For the believer, this verse is a comprehensive manual for any position of responsibility, whether in a family, an organisation, or a community. It balances mercy, forgiveness, spiritual care, inclusiveness, decisiveness, and ultimate reliance on Allah. It teaches that strength is in gentleness, and success is from Allah, not from mere human planning.
160 | If Allah helps you, none can defeat you. But if He denies you help, then who else can help you? So in Allah let the believers put their trust. | The surah closes this section of the Uhud narrative with the ultimate declaration of reliance on Allah, drawing the logical conclusion from all that has been said about victory and defeat. “If Allah helps you, none can defeat you.” This is the absolute statement of divine omnipotence in the context of human struggle. The Arabic “yansurkum” (helps you) covers all forms of divine aid—angels, psychological serenity, strategic advantage, or the casting of fear into the enemy. When this help is present, no human force, no matter how vast, can overcome the one whom Allah aids. The converse is equally absolute: “But if He denies you help, then who else can help you?” The word “yakhdhulkum” (denies you help) implies abandonment and withdrawal of support. If Allah leaves a people to their own devices, there is no power in the universe that can assist them. No army, no wealth, no technology, no alliance can substitute for the absence of divine help. This rhetorical question—“who else can help you?”—has an implied answer of absolute negation: no one. This truth is the cornerstone of Islamic theology regarding all human affairs, not just warfare. It ties together the lessons of Badr and Uhud. At Badr, Allah’s help came, and the outnumbered Muslims triumphed. At Uhud, when the believers’ own actions caused a withdrawal of some aspects of that help, they tasted defeat. Yet even in that defeat, Allah’s ultimate help did not abandon them; the enemy withdrew. The verse then issues the natural command: “So in Allah let the believers put their trust.” Tawakkul, or trust, is the practical application of this knowledge. It means to rely completely on Allah after doing everything within one’s power, with the heart at peace knowing that the outcome is in the best of hands. This verse is a powerful spiritual anchor. For the believer today, in any struggle—whether it be personal, professional, or communal—the principle remains unchanged. Success comes only from Allah, and no human effort can succeed without His will. The believer does their utmost, then rests their heart on Allah, knowing that if He aids, no obstacle is insurmountable, and if He withholds, no amount of striving will force success. This cultivates a profound humility, a reduction of anxiety, and a fearless determination. The verse is a declaration of independence from all worldly powers and a declaration of total dependence on the Lord of all powers.
161 | It is not appropriate for a prophet to illegally withhold spoils of war. And whoever does so, it will be held against them on the Day of Judgment. Then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged. | After the profound lessons on leadership and trust in Allah, the surah addresses a specific rumor that had circulated among some of the fighters at Uhud. The verse begins with a strong denial: “It is not appropriate for a prophet to illegally withhold spoils of war.” The word “illegally withhold” translates the Arabic yaghulla, which refers to ghulul, the act of secretly taking from the war booty before its lawful distribution. This was a serious accusation that some hypocrites or weak-hearted individuals had whispered about the Prophet, suggesting that he might have taken something for himself from the spoils. The verse categorically rejects this notion, declaring that such an act is inconceivable for a prophet. Prophets are the most trustworthy of human beings, and their integrity is absolute. They are the ones entrusted with conveying Allah’s message, and they would never betray the trust placed in them by Allah or by the community regarding material possessions. The verse then broadens the warning: “And whoever does so, it will be held against them on the Day of Judgment.” The phrase “held against them” (ya’ti bima ghalla) literally means he will bring what he stole on his back on the Day of Resurrection, publicly exposed in utter humiliation. It is a vivid image of the disgrace that awaits the one who betrays public trust. The verse continues, “Then every soul will be paid in full for what it has done, and none will be wronged.” This establishes the universal principle of divine justice. Every soul will receive exactly what it earned—whether good or evil—and there will be no injustice whatsoever. For the believers, this verse served to clear the Prophet’s name from the slander and to warn against the corruption of misappropriating public wealth. For the contemporary believer, it is a powerful reminder of the importance of financial honesty, especially for those in positions of trust. Ghulul is not limited to war booty; it applies to embezzlement, bribery, misuse of public funds, and any form of stealing from the collective property of the community. The verse instills a deep sense of accountability, linking the integrity of leadership to the very foundation of trust between a community and its guide. It also assures that no hidden crime will go unpunished; the Day of Judgment will expose all secrets.
162 | Are those who seek Allah’s pleasure like those who deserve Allah’s wrath? Hell is their home. What an evil destination! | After discussing the embezzlement of spoils, the surah contrasts two starkly different types of people, distinguished by their ultimate aims. “Are those who seek Allah’s pleasure like those who deserve Allah’s wrath?” This rhetorical question has an obvious answer: they are not alike. The first group are those who in all their actions, including their participation in jihad and their handling of spoils, seek only the pleasure of Allah (ridwan). They are sincere, honest, and mindful. The second group are those who have earned Allah’s wrath (sakhat). These are the ones who cheat, steal from the spoils, disobey the Prophet, and act with hypocrisy or greed. The phrase “deserve Allah’s wrath” indicates a state of divine displeasure that comes as a direct consequence of their actions. The verse then describes the fate of the second group: “Hell is their home. What an evil destination!” The word “home” (ma’wa) implies a permanent abode of rest, but here it is used with bitter irony. Hell is the worst possible place to settle. This verse teaches the fundamental principle that intentions determine the value and outcome of deeds. Two people may perform outwardly similar actions—both may be present in battle, both may contribute to the community—but their inner motives radically differentiate their destinies. The one who seeks Allah’s pleasure attains His approval and Paradise; the one who acts for worldly gain or with corrupt intentions earns His wrath and Hell. The verse also serves as a reminder that the hypocrites, who had slandered the Prophet and undermined the war effort, were not in the same category as the sincere believers, even if they shared the same physical space. For the believer today, this verse is a constant call to purify the heart and to examine one’s intentions before every action. Am I doing this to please Allah, or for recognition, money, or ego? The answer to that question determines which of the two groups one belongs to. The verse also provides comfort to the sincere: they may suffer in this world alongside the corrupt, but their destination is utterly different.
163 | They ˹each˺ have varying degrees in the sight of Allah. And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do. | The surah adds an important nuance: even within the two broad categories, there are ranks and degrees. “They each have varying degrees in the sight of Allah.” This means that among those who seek Allah’s pleasure, there are levels of closeness to Him based on the depth of their sincerity, the extent of their deeds, and their steadfastness. The person who gave a little and the person who gave everything are not equal. Similarly, among those who incur Allah’s wrath, there are degrees of punishment corresponding to the severity of their crimes. The hypocrite who actively sows discord is not the same as the one who merely harbors doubt. This verse establishes that divine judgment is precise and graded, not a blunt binary. Every act, every intention, every nuance is factored into the final assessment. The verse then reminds, “And Allah is All-Seeing of what they do.” His sight encompasses everything—the outward actions and the hidden motives. Nothing escapes His observation. This serves as both a comfort and a warning. For the sincere believer, it means that no small act of goodness is lost; it contributes to one’s degree in the sight of Allah. For the wrongdoer, it means that no hidden sin escapes the record. In the context of Uhud, this verse acknowledged that the companions themselves varied in their responses—some fled, some stayed, some fought heroically—and each would be rewarded or held accountable according to their state. It prevented blanket judgments and fostered a nuanced, fair assessment of people. For the believer today, this verse encourages a constant striving for higher degrees through increased devotion, knowledge, and service. It also instills humility, because one never knows one’s own degree, and it prohibits self-righteous comparison with others. The focus is shifted from judging others to perfecting one’s own record, knowing that the All-Seeing is watching.
164 | Indeed, Allah has done the believers a ˹great˺ favour by raising a messenger from among them—reciting to them His revelations, purifying them, and teaching them the Book and wisdom. For indeed they had previously been clearly astray. | After the heavy discussion of battle, loss, and internal shortcomings, the surah now lifts the spirits of the believers by reminding them of the greatest blessing they have received: the sending of the Prophet Muhammad. “Indeed, Allah has done the believers a great favour by raising a messenger from among them.” The word “favour” (manna) implies a tremendous, undeserved gift. The favor is that the Messenger is “from among them” (min anfusihim), meaning he is one of them, a human being from their own lineage and community. They knew his character, his honesty, and his trustworthiness long before he received revelation. This made his message more accessible and his example more relatable. The verse then outlines the four core functions of the Messenger. First, “reciting to them His revelations,” which refers to the Quran. The recitation itself is an act of worship and a means of constant guidance. Second, “purifying them” (yuzakkihim), which means cleansing their souls from the filth of idolatry, moral corruption, and spiritual diseases. This tazkiyah is the inner transformation that accompanies true faith. Third, “teaching them the Book,” meaning the Quran, its laws, and its meanings. Fourth, “teaching them wisdom” (al-hikmah), which includes the Sunnah, the deep understanding of religion, and the ability to act rightly in all situations. These four tasks encompass the complete program of spiritual and intellectual development. The verse concludes with a reminder of their past state: “For indeed they had previously been clearly astray.” Before the Prophet, the Arabs were steeped in the ignorance of jahiliyyah, worshipping idols, burying their daughters alive, and living in tribal warfare. The contrast highlights the magnitude of the change that the Messenger brought. For the believers at Uhud, this reminder was a call to appreciate the Prophet and to listen to him, even when his commands were difficult, like staying at the archers’ post. For the believer today, this verse is a source of immense gratitude. It frames the entire religion as a divine favour, not a burden. It also clarifies the role of the Prophet as more than a messenger of laws; he is a purifier and a teacher of wisdom, whose Sunnah remains the path to tazkiyah and hikmah. The verse inspires the believer to engage with the Quran and the Sunnah on all four levels: recitation, purification, learning, and wisdom.

Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “It is because of your disobedience.” Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything. | The surah now confronts the bewilderment and complaint of some believers after the defeat. “Why is it when you suffered casualties at Uhud—although you had made your enemy suffer twice as much at Badr—you protested, ‘How could this be?’” At Uhud, the Muslims lost around seventy men. The verse reminds the believers that they are still in a position of overall advantage, yet they were questioning why the defeat happened as if it were incomprehensible or unjust. The question “How could this be?” (anna hadha) reflects a failure to understand the cause of the setback. The verse provides the answer: “Say, O Prophet, ‘It is because of your disobedience.’” The defeat was not random or a sign of Allah’s abandonment; it was a direct consequence of the specific act of disobedience—the archers leaving their post against the Prophet’s clear command. The phrase “from yourselves” (min ‘indi anfusikum) is implied, meaning the cause originated in your own choices. The verse then adds, “Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.” This statement balances the attribution of cause to human disobedience with the affirmation of Allah’s absolute power. It means that Allah, in His capability, could have prevented the defeat even despite their disobedience, but He chose to let the natural consequences of their actions unfold as a lesson. His power is not diminished by their failure; rather, He uses events to teach and discipline. For the believers, this verse was a necessary wake-up call. It was painful but honest, cutting through any tendency to blame external factors or divine decree without acknowledging personal responsibility. For the contemporary believer, it is a lesson in self-accountability. When facing difficulties in one’s personal life, community, or society, the first question should be: What have we done to cause or contribute to this? The verse does not deny that trials can also be tests from Allah without a direct sin, but it establishes the principle that many afflictions are the fruits of our own hands. This awareness leads to repentance and correction, rather than despair or blame-shifting.

The true believers were those who remained steadfast, who did not flee, who accepted the outcome with faith, and who learned from their mistakes. The hypocrites were exposed by their words and their withdrawal. This distinction was necessary for the health of the community moving forward. It also served to honour the true believers and to make their faith evident. For the individual believer, trials are the crucible in which the genuineness of faith is proven. It is easy to claim faith in times of ease; hardship reveals the reality. The verse is incomplete here and continues in the next verse, but even this half-verse provides a profound insight: suffering in the path of faith is not meaningless. It is a tool in the hands of the All-Wise to purify the ranks, to expose hidden realities, and to solidify the community upon a foundation of tested, sincere hearts. For the believer today, this provides a framework for understanding personal trials. Instead of asking “Why me?” in a spirit of complaint, one asks, “What is Allah seeking to distinguish or purify in me through this?” The answer often leads to growth and a deeper, more authentic faith.

For the believer today, this verse is a stark warning against the disease of hypocrisy. It teaches that true faith is not about what one says in public, but what one believes and feels in the heart, especially under pressure. The test of a person’s sincerity often comes when they are called to sacrifice their comfort, safety, or wealth. The excuse given by the hypocrites is a model of the rationalizations people make to avoid difficult duties. The believer is urged to be brutally honest with themselves and to align their hearts with their tongues, seeking refuge in Allah from the hidden currents of nifaq that can seep into the soul.
168 | Those who sat at home, saying about their brothers, “Had they listened to us, they would not have been killed.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Try not to die if what you say is true!” | The surah continues to expose the attitude of the hypocrites and those who stayed behind from battle. “Those who sat at home, saying about their brothers, ‘Had they listened to us, they would not have been killed.’” These were the ones who not only refrained from participating in the battle but also, after the tragic deaths, used the occasion to justify their own cowardice and to cast aspersions on the wisdom of those who went out. Their words imply that the martyrs died needlessly because they did not follow their advice to stay home. This is a profoundly disrespectful and faithless attitude, as it trivializes the sacrifice of the martyrs and questions the judgment of the Prophet and the believers. In response, Allah instructs the Prophet with a powerful challenge: “Say, O Prophet, ‘Try not to die if what you say is true!’” This is a razor-sharp reply. They claimed that staying home would have prevented death. The challenge is: if you truly have the power to avoid death by your own choices, then try to prevent your own inevitable death when it comes. The argument echoes the earlier verses that established that death is by Allah’s decree and cannot be escaped by hiding. The challenge exposes the absurdity of their claim. They cannot prevent their own death, so how could the martyrs have prevented theirs by staying home? Their reasoning was not only factually wrong but also morally bankrupt, as it belittled the honor of martyrdom. This verse teaches that a defeatist and critical attitude after a tragedy is a sign of weak faith. The believers are to honor the fallen and learn from the events, not use them as a pretext for arrogant “I told you so” remarks. For the believer today, the verse is a reminder to avoid the hindsight bias that claims, “If only we had done X, the tragedy would not have happened,” when the matter was decreed by Allah. It also warns against those who discourage others from doing good or fulfilling duties by overemphasizing risks, and then smugly say they were right when hardship occurs. The focus should be on supporting the community and trusting Allah’s decree, not on preserving one’s own safety at all costs while demeaning the sacrifice of others.
169 | Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead. In fact, they are alive with their Lord, well provided for— | After the profound lessons on loss and sacrifice, the surah delivers one of the most powerful and hope-filled verses in the entire Quran, regarding the martyrs of Uhud and all martyrs. “Never think of those martyred in the cause of Allah as dead.” The command “never think” (la tahsabanna) is emphatic, forbidding even the mental conception that martyrs are dead in the usual sense. The word “dead” (amwat) refers to the lifeless state that ends all sensation, activity, and pleasure. The verse completely overturns this understanding for the martyrs. “In fact, they are alive” (ahya’) is the divine correction. Their life is real, not metaphorical. It is a life of a different, higher dimension, but it is genuine existence, not a state of sleep or mere memory. The verse adds, “with their Lord” (‘inda rabbihim), which is the greatest honour and pleasure. To be with the Lord implies proximity, nearness, and special care. Then, “well provided for” (yurzaqun) means they are receiving sustenance. This provision is not the food of this world but the delights of the barzakh, the intermediate realm. According to authentic hadiths, the souls of martyrs are in the bodies of green birds that roam freely in Paradise, eating from its fruits and resting in lamps suspended from the Throne of the Most Merciful. This verse was a direct comfort to the families of the seventy martyrs of Uhud, assuring them that their loved ones were not lost in the dirt of the battlefield but were alive in a state of unimaginable bliss. It transforms the perception of death in the path of Allah from a tragedy into a promotion. For the believer, this verse has sustained generations of those who sacrifice their lives for truth and justice. It provides an unshakeable certainty that the martyr does not lose life but gains a superior, eternal life. It also reframes the fear of death, making it a fear of the unknown transition rather than annihilation. The believer is called to honour the martyrs, to envy their station in a healthy way, and to strive for a life that, whether ending in martyrdom or not, is worthy of being near the Lord.
170 | rejoicing in Allah’s bounties and being delighted for those yet to join them. There will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve. | The verse continues to describe the blissful state of the martyrs. “Rejoicing in Allah’s bounties” (farihina bima atahum Allah min fadlihi). Their joy is active and continuous. They are delighted with the gifts that Allah has bestowed upon them from His grace. These bounties are the tangible rewards of Paradise that they have already begun to experience. The word “fadl” indicates that these rewards are not earned by their deeds alone but are an overflow of Allah’s generosity. This joy is not solitary; the martyrs are also “being delighted for those yet to join them” (yastabshiruna bi al-ladhina lam yalhaqu bihim min khalfihim). They eagerly anticipate the arrival of their fellow believers who are still in the world, particularly those who will be martyred after them. The martyr community in Paradise is like a joyful, welcoming party, looking forward to the arrival of their brothers and sisters. This is a beautiful depiction of the bonds of faith extending beyond death. The verse then delivers the ultimate assurance: “There will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve.” Fear (khawf) pertains to future harms; grief (huzn) pertains to past losses. The martyrs are completely safe from anything unpleasant in the future and are completely free from any sadness about the past. Their state is one of pure, untainted bliss. This verse, along with the previous one, creates a powerful motivational framework for the believer. The fear of death is replaced by a longing for the state described. The separation from loved ones is not permanent, as the martyrs are joyfully awaiting reunion. The temporary suffering of the battlefield is exchanged for eternal joy. For the families left behind, this knowledge eases the pain of loss, knowing that their loved ones are not only alive but rejoicing and waiting for them. The verse redefines the entire concept of loss: in Allah’s economy, what seems to be a loss is a monumental gain.
171 | They are joyful for receiving Allah’s grace and bounty, and that Allah does not deny the reward of the believers. | The surah further emphasizes the source and nature of the martyrs’ joy. “They are joyful for receiving Allah’s grace and bounty.” This repeats and reinforces the theme of rejoicing, but with a focus on the Giver. The martyrs’ joy is not merely in the gifts but in the fact that these gifts are “from Allah” and are an expression of His “grace” (ni‘mah) and “bounty” (fadl). The grace is the general blessings; the bounty is the extra, unmerited favor. This distinction highlights that the martyrs receive both what was promised and much more. Their joy is also rooted in something beyond their personal state: “and that Allah does not deny the reward of the believers.” The martyrs are happy not only for themselves but for the broader principle that Allah is unfailingly faithful in rewarding all believers. They see the fulfillment of Allah’s promise, and this increases their happiness. The fact that Allah does not let the reward of any believer go to waste is a universal truth that the martyrs witness firsthand, and they rejoice in the justice and generosity of their Lord. This verse provides comfort to all believers, not just martyrs, because it affirms that every good deed, every moment of patience, every act of sacrifice is preserved and will be fully rewarded. The martyrs are the foremost witnesses to this truth. For the contemporary believer, this verse encourages a trust in Allah’s promises that transcends immediate circumstances. When one struggles in the path of Allah and sees little worldly result, the conviction that Allah never denies the reward sustains the heart. The joy of the martyrs in witnessing this truth becomes an aspirational state: one day, the believer too will experience the joy of seeing the complete and perfect reward for a life of faith, and the relief of knowing that no suffering was in vain.
172 | ˹As for˺ those who responded to the call of Allah and His Messenger after their injury, those of them who did good and were mindful ˹of Allah˺ will have a great reward. | After the Battle of Uhud, a new challenge emerged that tested the believers’ resolve. The Quraysh, having withdrawn, began to regret not finishing off the Muslims. Abu Sufyan announced a plan to return and wipe out the remaining Muslims. The Prophet, informed of this, called for volunteers to pursue the Quraysh army and demonstrate that the Muslims were still strong. This expedition became known as Hamra al-Asad. “As for those who responded to the call of Allah and His Messenger after their injury” refers to the companions who, despite being wounded, exhausted, and grieving their losses at Uhud just a day earlier, answered the Prophet’s call to march out and confront the threat. Their physical and emotional state was one of severe injury (qarh), but their spirit was unbroken. The verse highlights two qualities of this group: “those of them who did good” (ahsanu) and “were mindful of Allah” (ittaqaw). Doing good refers to their actions of responding, marching, and supporting the Prophet. Mindfulness refers to their inner state of God-consciousness, which drove them to obey despite the hardship. The promise for them is “a great reward” (ajr ‘azim). This reward is specifically for those who combined outward excellence with inward taqwa. This verse illustrates the resilience that Allah loves. The believers had just experienced a devastating setback, but they did not allow the injury to become an excuse for inaction. They trusted the Prophet, they feared Allah, and they rallied. Their action was a powerful psychological victory that contributed to the Quraysh’s eventual retreat without a second battle. For the believer today, this verse is a timeless lesson in perseverance. The call of Allah and His Messenger often comes at the most difficult moments—when one is tired, hurt, or disappointed. The true test of faith is whether one responds or gives in to the desire for comfort. The “great reward” is not for those who never fall, but for those who rise after falling and continue to do good with taqwa. The verse also teaches that collective recovery from a crisis depends on the willingness of individuals to rise above their personal pain and respond to the call of duty.
173 | Those who were warned, “Your enemies have mobilized their forces against you, so fear them,” the warning only made them grow stronger in faith and they replied, “Allah ˹alone˺ is sufficient ˹as an aid˺ for us and ˹He˺ is the best Protector.” | The surah continues the story of the Hamra al-Asad expedition, providing a profound psychological portrait of true faith. “Those who were warned, ‘Your enemies have mobilized their forces against you, so fear them’…” As the Muslims prepared to pursue the Quraysh, certain people—identified as some from the disbelieving tribes or perhaps hypocrites—tried to intimidate them. They said that the Quraysh had gathered a huge army and were coming back to destroy them, so the Muslims should be afraid and not engage. This was a psychological warfare tactic designed to break their morale. However, the effect on the sincere believers was the opposite: “the warning only made them grow stronger in faith” (zadahum imanan). Instead of weakening their resolve, the intimidation increased their reliance on Allah and deepened their trust. This is a hallmark of genuine iman: threats and obstacles, when faced with a heart attached to Allah, become fuel for greater faith rather than causes for retreat. Their verbal response was a magnificent declaration of tawakkul: “Allah alone is sufficient as an aid for us and He is the best Protector.” This statement, “Hasbuna Allahu wa ni‘ma al-wakil,” is one of the most powerful supplications in Islam. It acknowledges that Allah is enough; no other aid is needed. And He is the best Wakil—the best Disposer of affairs, the best Guardian, the best One to rely upon. Prophet Ibrahim said these words when thrown into the fire, and the Prophet Muhammad and his companions said them here. The result of this attitude is described in the next verse. For the believer, this verse is a masterclass in handling fear and intimidation. The natural human reaction to threats is anxiety, but faith transforms that anxiety into an opportunity for spiritual growth. The declaration “Hasbuna Allahu wa ni‘ma al-wakil” becomes a fortress for the heart. It is not a passive resignation but an active, confident entrustment of the matter to Allah, accompanied by taking the necessary worldly steps—in this case, marching out despite the threats. This verse teaches that the believer’s strength does not come from ignoring danger but from placing it under Allah’s sufficiency.
174 | So they returned with Allah’s favours and grace, suffering no harm. For they sought to please Allah. And surely Allah is ˹the˺ Lord of infinite bounty. | This verse reveals the successful outcome of the believers’ courageous response. “So they returned with Allah’s favours and grace, suffering no harm.” The “favours” (ni‘mah) and “grace” (fadl) here include both spiritual and material blessings. They returned safely without any battle, the Quraysh having been intimidated by the Muslims’ show of strength and determination. Additionally, they conducted profitable trade along the way, gaining material benefit. The fact that they suffered “no harm” (lam yamsashum su’) was a direct fulfillment of their trust in Allah. The verse then gives the reason for this blessed outcome: “For they sought to please Allah” (ittaba‘u ridwan Allah). Their sole motivation was the pleasure of Allah. They did not march out for booty, fame, or revenge, but purely to obey Allah and His Messenger. This sincerity attracted Allah’s comprehensive protection and provision. The verse concludes, “And surely Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty.” His bounty is “great” (‘azim), meaning it is vast, magnificent, and not limited to a specific form. This verse beautifully ties together the themes of sincerity, trust, and divine reward. When believers prioritize Allah’s pleasure over their fears and worldly calculations, Allah not only protects them but bestows upon them unimagined blessings. The Hamra al-Asad expedition became a turning point; it demonstrated that the setback at Uhud had not broken the spirit of the Muslim community. For the believer today, this verse is a formula for success in any daunting situation. The sequence is: hear the call, face the intimidation, increase in faith, declare reliance on Allah, act with sincerity to please Him, and witness the blessings unfold. The “infinite bounty” of Allah is always accessible; the key is to seek His pleasure above all else.
175 | That ˹warning˺ was only ˹from˺ Satan, trying to prompt you to fear his followers. So do not fear them; fear Me if you are ˹true˺ believers. | The surah now reveals the true source of the intimidation attempt described earlier. “That warning was only from Satan, trying to prompt you to fear his followers.” The “warning” (takhwif) that the enemies had mobilized and would destroy them was not a neutral piece of news; it was a direct tool of Satan. Satan’s goal was to “prompt you to fear his followers” (yukhawwifu awliya’ahu). He wanted the believers to be afraid of his allies—the disbelievers and their armies. By instilling this fear, Satan aims to paralyze the believers, to make them retreat from the path of Allah, and to weaken their resolve. The verse then issues a powerful, liberating command: “So do not fear them; fear Me if you are true believers.” This verse establishes the exclusive nature of the fear that a believer should have. Fear of people, fear of worldly powers, fear of consequences—all of these are to be subordinated and ultimately dissolved in the fear of Allah alone. This is not to say that a believer never experiences the natural human emotion of fear when facing danger; the Prophet and his companions certainly felt it. But they did not let that fear control their decisions. The fear of Allah—which includes fear of His displeasure, fear of failing to obey Him, and fear of His punishment—must be greater. When the fear of Allah is dominant, the fear of anything else becomes manageable and ultimately insignificant. The phrase “if you are true believers” makes this the litmus test of genuine faith. The believer proves their faith by prioritizing divine fear over worldly fear. This verse is a profound psychological and spiritual liberation. It frees the believer from being enslaved by the threats of any human being or circumstance. In the modern world, the “followers of Satan” who induce fear may take many forms: oppressive regimes, economic pressures, social ostracism, or the fear of losing one’s job or status for standing by the truth. The command remains the same: do not fear them. Instead, channel that fear into a reverential awe of Allah, which empowers the soul and clarifies priorities. The verse reshapes the emotional landscape of the believer, making courage a natural byproduct of sincere faith.
176 | ˹O Prophet!˺ Do not grieve for those who race to disbelieve—surely they will not harm Allah in the least. It is Allah’s Will to disallow them a share in the Hereafter, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment. | After addressing the believers, the surah now turns to console the Prophet personally regarding those who persistently reject faith. “O Prophet! Do not grieve for those who race to disbelieve.” The Prophet was deeply pained by the rejection of his people, desiring their guidance with all his heart. The command “do not grieve” (la yahzunka) is a direct balm for his sorrow. The phrase “race to disbelieve” (yusari‘una fi al-kufr) paints a picture of people who are not merely passive disbelievers but actively rushing, competing, and vying in their rejection. This includes the hypocrites who worked to undermine Islam and the pagans who fought against it. The verse assures that their actions are futile against Allah: “surely they will not harm Allah in the least.” Their disbelief does not diminish Allah’s majesty, sovereignty, or plan. The harm is entirely upon themselves. The verse then reveals a chilling aspect of divine justice: “It is Allah’s Will to disallow them a share in the Hereafter.” Because of their own choices, Allah has decreed that they shall have no portion in the rewards of the next life. The word “share” (hazz) indicates that they have forfeited any claim to mercy or Paradise. And the consequence is “a tremendous punishment.” For the Prophet and the believers, this verse provides a perspective that alleviates the emotional burden of witnessing rejection. The grief is natural, but it should not consume the caller. The ultimate judgment is with Allah, and He is fully in control. The verse also contains a hidden warning for the believers: do not be among those who race to disbelief through sins and hypocrisy. The assurance that Allah is not harmed should not lead to complacency about the spread of evil; rather, it should lead to a calm, determined commitment to one’s own faith and to da‘wah, leaving the results to Allah.
177 | Those who trade belief for disbelief will never harm Allah in the least, and they will suffer a painful punishment. | The surah repeats and reinforces the message of the previous verse, emphasizing the terrible transaction that the disbelievers have made. “Those who trade belief for disbelief” (ishtarawu al-kufra bi al-iman) uses the metaphor of a trade or purchase. Every human being makes a transaction with their soul and their free will. The disbelievers have chosen to buy disbelief, paying the price of their faith. This is the worst possible deal, because they exchanged the eternal treasure of iman for the worthless counterfeit of kufr. The phrase “will never harm Allah in the least” is repeated for emphasis. The believers might sometimes wonder why Allah allows disbelief to persist and even flourish. The answer is that it does not harm Him at all. He is the Self-Sufficient, the Almighty. The harm is entirely self-inflicted by the disbelievers. The verse then reminds of the outcome: “and they will suffer a painful punishment.” The loss in this trade is not merely the absence of reward; it is the active infliction of severe, ongoing torment. This verse teaches the believer to view faith as the most valuable asset. It is not something to be traded for any worldly gain—money, status, relationships, or safety. Those who abandon their faith under pressure or temptation are making a catastrophic transaction. The verse also serves as a comfort to those who feel outnumbered or intimidated by the apparent success of disbelievers. Their worldly gains are the fleeting profit of a bad deal that will soon end in bankruptcy, while the believers hold the true capital that will yield eternal returns. The repetitiveness in these verses about the inability of the disbelievers to harm Allah underscores a fundamental truth of Islamic theology: human actions, whether good or evil, do not affect Allah. They affect only the doers. This liberates the believer from the illusion that the cause of Allah is dependent on any individual’s acceptance; the cause is Allah’s, and He will accomplish it with or without any particular person.
178 | Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them. They are only given more time to increase in sin, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment. | The surah now addresses a common misconception among the disbelievers and a potential source of confusion for the believers. “Those who disbelieve should not think that living longer is good for them.” When a disbeliever prospers, enjoys a long life filled with pleasures, and seems to escape punishment, both they and some believers might interpret this as a sign of divine favor or as evidence that their path is correct. The verse categorically refutes this. The long life granted to them is not a blessing but an istidraj—a gradual drawing into greater destruction. The verse explains: “They are only given more time to increase in sin.” Every extra day, month, or year of their life in a state of disbelief is an opportunity they use to accumulate more sins, more rejection, more oppression. The respite is not mercy; it is a cause of greater liability. Their record of deeds becomes heavier with evil. The outcome is inevitable: “and they will suffer a humiliating punishment.” The adjective “humiliating” (muhin) is key. The punishment is not only physically painful but deeply disgraceful, a fitting recompense for a life lived in arrogance and rejection of the truth. The worldly honor they enjoyed will be replaced by eternal shame. This verse aligns with the prophetic warning: “When you see Allah giving a servant worldly things that he desires while he is persisting in sin, that is istidraj.” It is a profound warning against envying the apparent prosperity of the wicked. For the believer, it clarifies that success is not measured by worldly longevity or comfort, but by the state of one’s faith and the quality of one’s deeds at the time of death. A short life of faith is infinitely better than a long life of disbelief. The verse also teaches that sometimes divine justice operates by withholding immediate punishment, not out of neglect, but to allow the wrongdoer to seal their own fate. This should inspire the believer to never delay repentance, because a long life is only beneficial if it is used to increase in good deeds.
179 | Allah would not leave the believers in the condition you were in, until He distinguished the good from the evil ˹among you˺. Nor would Allah ˹directly˺ reveal to you the unseen, but He chooses whoever He wills as a messenger. So believe in Allah and His messengers. It was Allah’s wisdom to test them through trials like Uhud so that the pure could be separated from the impure. This separation is a necessary process for the health and clarity of the community. The verse then addresses a related demand: “Nor would Allah directly reveal to you the unseen.” Some people wanted Allah to show them the secrets of the hearts so they could immediately know who was sincere and who was a hypocrite. The verse states that Allah does not grant direct access to the unseen (al-ghayb) to the general public. Instead, “He chooses whoever He wills as a messenger.” The knowledge of the unseen, including the secrets of hearts, is given only to the chosen prophets, and even then, only what Allah wills. The verse concludes with a promise: “And if you are faithful and mindful of Allah, you will receive a great reward.” The combination of faith and taqwa is the key to the great reward, not the possession of hidden knowledge. This verse provides a profound lesson about the nature of the Muslim community. It teaches that periods of confusion and mixing are temporary, and that tests will inevitably expose the true nature of people. The believer should not be impatient or demand miraculous knowledge; instead, they should focus on their own faith and taqwa. The role of the Prophet is to convey the message, and the role of the believer is to follow it, leaving the unseen to Allah. The verse also implies that the community should not be naive; the existence of hypocrites is a reality, and only through tests and time will they be known, usually by their actions and reactions to hardship.
180 | And do not let those who ˹greedily˺ withhold Allah’s bounties think it is good for them—in fact, it is bad for them! To “withhold” (yabkhalun) means to refuse to spend in the ways that Allah has commanded—zakat, charity, support for the community, and other righteous expenditures. The miserly person believes that by hoarding wealth, they are securing their future and doing something beneficial for themselves. The verse shatters this illusion: “in fact, it is bad for them!” (bal huwa sharrun lahum). What they see as a benefit is actually a source of immense harm. The verse then paints a terrifying picture of the punishment: “They will be leashed by their necks on the Day of Judgment with whatever wealth they used to withhold.” The wealth they loved and refused to spend will literally become a collar, a chain, a heavy burden tied around their necks. In a famous hadith, the Prophet described a miser whose wealth becomes a bald, poisonous snake that wraps around his neck, biting him, and he will cry out in agony. This is the concrete manifestation of the spiritual reality that hoarded wealth becomes a torment rather than a comfort. For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivator to overcome the innate greed of the soul. It redefines wealth as a test and a trust, not a possession. The imagery of the collar is designed to trigger a visceral aversion to stinginess. The verse also addresses the specific context of the Uhud aftermath, where the community needed financial support for widows, orphans, and the war effort. To withhold at such a time was particularly heinous. The believer is taught to view spending in the path of Allah as the best investment, and hoarding as a guaranteed loss.
181 | Indeed, Allah has heard those ˹among the Jews˺ who said, “Allah is poor; we are rich!” We have certainly recorded their slurs and their killing of prophets unjustly. Then We will say, “Taste the torment of burning! | The surah now exposes a particularly shocking statement made by some among the Jewish community in Medina. "Indeed, Allah has heard those among the Jews who said, 'Allah is poor; we are rich!'" This blasphemous utterance was made when the verse was revealed encouraging believers to give a good loan to Allah by spending in charity. Some among the Jews mockingly said that Allah must be poor and in need of a loan, while they were rich and self-sufficient. This statement reflects an extreme arrogance and a fundamental misunderstanding of the relationship between the Creator and His creation. The verse responds with the powerful affirmation that "Allah has heard" their words. He is not oblivious to their mockery; He has perfectly recorded it. The phrase "We have certainly recorded their slurs" indicates that every blasphemous word is written down and preserved for judgment. The term "slurs" (qawlahum) refers to their disrespectful and derogatory speech about Allah. The verse then adds another crime to the record: "and their killing of prophets unjustly." This links the contemporary Jewish rejection to the historical pattern of their forefathers, who not only rejected but murdered the prophets sent to them. The combination of blasphemy against Allah and murder of His messengers represents the height of spiritual criminality. The verse then delivers the verdict: "Then We will say, 'Taste the torment of burning!'" On the Day of Judgment, they will be told to taste the very punishment they mocked and denied. The word "taste" (dhuqu) implies a direct, sensory experience of the fire they ridiculed. The "torment of burning" (al-hariq) specifically indicates the painful sensation of being consumed by fire. For the believer, this verse is a grave warning against any form of disrespect toward Allah, whether in speech, thought, or attitude. It teaches that no word is ever lost, and that apparent divine silence in this world should not be mistaken for neglect. The recording of words is a recurring Quranic theme that instills a careful vigilance over the tongue. The verse also warns against the arrogance that leads people to mock divine commands, especially those related to financial sacrifice, and it connects miserliness, blasphemy, and historical rebellion as a single thread of disbelief.
182 | This is ˹the reward˺ for what your hands have done. And Allah is never unjust to ˹His˺ creation. | The verse continues the address to the blasphemers, explaining the cause of their punishment with perfect justice. "This is the reward for what your hands have done." The phrase "your hands have done" (qaddamat aydikum) is a common Quranic expression that attributes actions to the person, emphasizing personal responsibility. The punishment they face is not arbitrary; it is the direct and necessary consequence of their own deeds—their blasphemy, their mockery, their rejection, and the murder of prophets. The hands are specifically mentioned because they are the instruments of most human actions, whether writing blasphemous texts, shedding innocent blood, or withholding charity. The verse then delivers one of the most fundamental principles of Islamic theology: "And Allah is never unjust to His creation." The word "never unjust" (laysa bi zallam) uses an intensive form, meaning that Allah is absolutely not, in any way, a wrongdoer to any of His servants. Injustice (zulm) is inconceivable for Allah because He is the All-Perfect and Self-Sufficient. He has no need that would motivate Him to wrong anyone, and His knowledge and wisdom are complete, so His judgments are perfectly calibrated. This verse closes any door to the idea that divine punishment is cruel or unfair. Every person receives exactly what they have earned. The combination of these two statements—punishment is for one's own deeds, and Allah is never unjust—forms the bedrock of Islamic eschatology. It provides comfort to the believer who trusts that their own efforts will be rewarded fairly, and it serves as a warning that no one can blame Allah for their own misdeeds. In the context of the Uhud aftermath, this also serves to reinforce that the losses suffered were not due to divine injustice but were consequences of human actions or tests from divine wisdom. For the contemporary believer, this verse is a powerful reminder of personal accountability and the certainty of divine justice. It eliminates any grounds for complaining against fate or blaming external circumstances, directing the individual to focus on their own deeds and their ultimate consequences.

Then Hell will be their home—what an evil place to rest! | The verse now explains the true nature of the disbelievers' prosperity that the previous verse warned against. "It is only a brief enjoyment." The word "brief" (qalil) emphasizes the shortness and insignificance of their worldly pleasure. Compared to eternity, even a lifetime of a hundred years of luxury is a mere blink of an eye. The word "enjoyment" (mata') refers to a temporary provision for a journey, not a permanent home. The disbelievers are consuming their share of enjoyment in this world, and they will have nothing left for the Hereafter except what they have earned of sin. The verse then delivers the stark reality that follows this brief enjoyment: "Then Hell will be their home—what an evil place to rest!" The word "home" (ma'wa) is used ironically. A home is a place of comfort, safety, and rest. But for them, their true and permanent abode will be Hell. The phrase "what an evil place to rest" (bi'sa al-mihad) intensifies the horror. A mihad is a resting place or a bed; Hell is the worst possible bed one could lie upon. The contrast between the soft beds of this world and the fiery bed of Hell is devastating. This verse completely reframes the believer's perception of the disbelievers' prosperity. It is like a traveler who enjoys a few moments of shade under a tree on the way to a permanent prison. The enjoyment is not to be envied; it is to be pitied, for it is followed by unending torment. For the believer, this verse is a powerful reality check. Whenever one feels a pang of envy at the luxurious lives of those who are heedless of Allah, this verse should come to mind. The brief enjoyment is not worth the eternal consequence. The believer chooses a life of temporary struggle and discipline in exchange for eternal bliss, while the disbeliever chooses temporary pleasure for eternal suffering. The verse also serves as a motivation for da'wah; the believer wishes to warn others about the terrible destination that follows the brief enjoyment, so that they may be saved.
198 | But those who are mindful of their Lord will be in Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever—as an accommodation from Allah. And what is with Allah is best for the virtuous. | The surah now presents the beautiful contrast to the brief enjoyment and evil home of the disbelievers. "But those who are mindful of their Lord will be in Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever." The phrase "mindful of their Lord" (ittaqaw rabbahum) defines the successful group. They are those who had taqwa of their Lord, not merely a generic fear or a selective obedience. The reward is the Gardens of Paradise with flowing rivers, a symbol of perpetual life, purity, and abundance that contrasts starkly with the arid desert of Arabia and the barrenness of a life without faith. The phrase "to stay there forever" (khalidina fiha) emphasizes the eternal nature of this bliss. There is no death, no eviction, no expiry. The verse then adds a beautiful nuance: "as an accommodation from Allah" (nuzulan min 'indi Allah). The word "accommodation" (nuzul) refers to the hospitality offered to a guest—a prepared welcome, a generous reception. Paradise is not just a reward; it is a personal hospitality from Allah Himself. The phrase "from Allah" ('inda Allah) indicates the source and the location; it is something in His presence, under His honor. The verse concludes: "And what is with Allah is best for the virtuous." The "virtuous" (al-abrar) are the deeply righteous. What is with Allah—His reward, His nearness, His pleasure—is far better than anything this world can offer. This statement seals the comparison: the brief enjoyment of the disbelievers is nothing compared to the eternal hospitality of Allah for the righteous. For the believer, this verse is a love letter from the Divine. It paints a picture of the ultimate homecoming, where one is welcomed as an honored guest by the Lord of the worlds. The word "nuzul" evokes warmth, generosity, and special care. The believer aspires to be among the abrar, so that they may receive this accommodation. The verse also reinforces the supremacy of the Hereafter over the world, making the sacrifices of this life feel light and worthwhile.

"Indeed, there are some among the People of the Book who truly believe in Allah." This verse acknowledges that not all of them are the same. Among the Jews and Christians, there are sincere individuals whose hearts are open to the truth. Their belief is described comprehensively: "and what has been revealed to you believers and what was revealed to them." They believe in the Quran revealed to Muhammad and also in the authentic scriptures revealed to their own prophets. This is the mark of the true believer from the People of the Book: they do not reject the new revelation out of prejudice but embrace it as a continuation of the truth they already love. The verse then describes their attitude: "They humble themselves before Allah." The word "humble" (khashi'in) conveys a state of deep reverence, awe, and submission. Their faith is not a matter of dry acknowledgment; it is a profound spiritual experience that manifests in humility. Furthermore, they are those who are "never trading Allah's revelations for a fleeting gain." Unlike the corrupt scholars described earlier who sold the truth for money or status, these sincere believers do not compromise their faith for any worldly benefit. They hold the truth as priceless. The verse promises: "Their reward is with their Lord." It is a reward that is secure, honorable, and from the Lord directly. The verse concludes, "Surely Allah is swift in reckoning." This can mean that Allah is swift in holding the corrupt accountable, or that He is swift in rewarding the sincere, or both. It assures that the just outcome will not be delayed. According to authentic narrations, this verse was revealed about figures like the Negus of Abyssinia and others who embraced Islam from the People of the Book. For the believer, this verse is a call to fairness and an encouragement for da'wah. It teaches that there are always sincere souls among other communities who are ready to receive the truth. It also provides the criteria for recognizing such people: they believe in all revelations, they are humble, and they do not sell the truth. The verse ends the discussion of the People of the Book on a note of hope, showing that the door of mercy is open to all who sincerely turn to Allah.
200 | O believers! Patiently endure, persevere, stand on guard, and be mindful of Allah, so you may be successful. | The surah concludes with a powerful, comprehensive command that summarizes the path to success after all the lessons of Uhud and the preceding discussions. "O believers! Patiently endure, persevere, stand on guard, and be mindful of Allah, so you may be successful." The verse contains four distinct yet interconnected commands. First, "patiently endure" (isbiru). This is the general command to have sabr—to hold oneself back from despair, complaint, and disobedience when facing trials. It is the foundational quality required for every believer. Second, "persevere" (sabiru). This is a more intense form of the same root, meaning to compete in patience, to excel in it, to have more patience than the enemy has. It implies a proactive, vigorous patience that outlasts and overcomes the opposition. Third, "stand on guard" (rabitu). This term comes from ribat, which originally referred to the stationing of troops at the frontiers to guard against enemy attack. It has a military connotation but extends to any form of vigilant preparedness and steadfast guarding of the community's borders—whether physical, ideological, or spiritual. The believers are to be constantly vigilant, never letting their guard down against the enemies of faith, both external and internal, including Satan. Fourth, "be mindful of Allah" (ittaqu Allah). This is the overarching command of taqwa, which encompasses all the others. It is the consciousness of Allah that motivates patience, perseverance, and vigilance. The purpose of all these commands is stated at the end: "so you may be successful" (la'allakum tuflihun). True success (falah) is the ultimate goal—success in this life and the supreme success in the Hereafter. This single verse is a complete program for the individual believer and the community. It requires inner steadfastness, competitive endurance, collective vigilance, and constant God-consciousness. The combination of these four qualities is what leads to victory and salvation. The verse brilliantly captures the lessons of the entire surah: the need for patience in adversity, the importance of outlasting the enemy, the necessity of guarding against threats and internal division, and the centrality of taqwa as the governing principle of all action. For the believer, this final verse serves as a daily motto and a lifelong strategy. It is a call to rise to the highest levels of spiritual discipline, knowing that the promised falah is worth every effort. It closes the surah on a note of empowerment, direction, and hope.
