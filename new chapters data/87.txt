1 | Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High, | The sūrah opens with a sublime command that sets the tone for the entire revelation: “Glorify the Name of your Lord, the Most High.” The Arabic verb is sabbihi, an imperative from the root sabbaha, which means to declare the transcendence, perfection, and absolute glory of Allah. It is an act of worship that involves the tongue and the heart, acknowledging that the Lord is free from all defects, imperfections, and anything that does not befit His majesty. The command is to glorify the “Name” (ism) of the Lord, which includes glorifying the Lord Himself through His beautiful names and attributes, and also implies that the very mention of His name should be treated with the utmost reverence and elevated above any association with falsehood. The title “the Most High” translates al-A‘lā, the superlative form of ‘alī, meaning the One who is absolutely exalted in His essence, His attributes, and His authority. He is above all creation, above all limitations, above all that the human mind can conceive. This attribute provides the immediate reason for glorification: because He is the Most High, He deserves that all creation declare His transcendence. The verse stands as a direct response to the plots and arrogant claims of the disbelievers mentioned at the end of the previous sūrah. While they devise their evil plans, the Prophet and the believers are to rise above that fray by focusing on the glorification of the One who is truly above all. In a famous ḥadīth, when this verse was revealed, the Prophet (ﷺ) said, “Put it in your prostration,” meaning to recite it while bowing or prostrating. Thus, the words “Subḥāna Rabbiya al-A‘lā” became the foundational utterance of the prostration in the Muslim prayer. This instantly links the verse to the daily practice of every believer. The prostration is the physical manifestation of glorifying the Most High: the body is lowered to the ground, the highest part of the body, the forehead, is placed on the earth, symbolizing the ultimate humility before the One who is above all. The command to glorify is also a remedy for the heart burdened by the schemes of others; by turning one’s focus upward to the Most High, the believer finds relief, perspective, and strength. The verse opens a sūrah that will expand on the acts of the Most High in creation, legislation, and guidance, all of which are manifestations of His perfection. The listener is immediately drawn into a state of worship, reminded that the first and foremost duty of the creature is to glorify the Creator, the Most High.
2 | Who created and ˹perfectly˺ fashioned ˹all˺, | The glorification of the Most High is immediately justified by a description of His creative act in verse two: “Who created and perfectly fashioned all.” The Arabic uses two verbs: khalaqa, meaning He created, He brought into existence from nothing, originating the substance and form of every creature. Then fasawwā, meaning He proportioned, He fashioned perfectly, He made symmetrical and balanced. The sequence is deliberate. Creation is not a chaotic flinging of matter into being; it is followed by a meticulous shaping that brings each creature to its perfect form and function. The human being, the animals, the celestial bodies, the tiny seed, the vast mountains—all have been created and then perfectly fashioned. The word “all” is implied in the comprehensive nature of the verbs; there is no creation that escapes this perfect fashioning. The verse refutes any notion of randomness or imperfection in the divine act of creation. Everything is made with precise measure, and the perfection of the fashioning points to the wisdom of the Fashioner. In the context of the human being, this verse recalls the earlier descriptions in Sūrah ‘Abasa and Al-Infiṭār, where Allah created, fashioned, and perfected the design of the human form. The perfect balance of the skeleton, the intricate design of the eye, the complex network of the nervous system, the harmonious proportions of the face and limbs—all are manifestations of taswiyah. This perfect fashioning is a sign that the Creator is al-A‘lā, the Most High, because only an intelligence of the highest order can produce such flawless and beautiful design. The verse also implies a moral dimension. The One who fashioned the creation so perfectly did not do so in vain; He fashioned it for a purpose, and that purpose involves acknowledging and glorifying Him. The human being, fashioned in the best of forms, has the capacity and the duty to recognize his Fashioner. When the Prophet (ﷺ) recited these verses, he was inviting the disbelievers to look at their own bodies and the world around them, to see the undeniable perfection that could only come from the Most High. This reflection should lead to humility and to the glorification commanded in the first verse. The verse seamlessly connects the transcendence of Allah with the immanence of His creative touch in every detail of existence.
3 | and Who ordained precisely and inspired accordingly, | The description of the divine creative process continues and reaches its logical completion in verse three: “and Who ordained precisely and inspired accordingly.” The Arabic uses two more verbs that form a pair. The first is qaddara, meaning He measured, determined, decreed, and ordained with precise measure. After creating and fashioning, Allah assigned to every creature its specific nature, its life span, its provision, its function, its path. The word taqdīr refers to the divine determination that sets the boundaries and possibilities for every being. The second verb is hadā, meaning He guided, He inspired, He directed. After ordaining the measure, Allah gave every creature the guidance it needs to fulfill its ordained purpose. This guidance includes the instinctual knowledge given to animals—the bee knowing how to build its hive and produce honey, the bird knowing how to build its nest, the baby fish swimming to its feeding ground. It also includes the intellectual and spiritual guidance given to human beings, and supremely, the revelation given to the prophets. The verse presents a complete picture: creation, perfection, ordainment, and guidance. Nothing is left without direction. The human being, who has been given free will and intellect, receives guidance through the inner compass of the fitrah, the natural disposition, and through the external compass of the revealed scriptures. The pairing of qaddara and hadā is profoundly comforting. It means that the same Lord who ordained your life has also provided the guidance you need to navigate it. The difficulties you face are within a divine measure, and the inspiration to overcome them or to be patient through them is also provided. The verse thus refutes the despair that comes from feeling lost or purposeless. Everything has an ordained purpose and a path to fulfill it. The disbelievers who deny the resurrection and the judgment are going against the guidance that is inherent in their own creation. Their own intellect and conscience, if unclouded by arrogance, point them to the truth. The verse is also a powerful argument against the worship of idols, for idols cannot create, fashion, ordain, or guide. All these attributes belong exclusively to the Most High. The believer, reciting this, reinforces his trust in the divine decree and seeks the divinely inspired guidance in every step of life. The following verses will illustrate this ordination and guidance in the specific example of the earth’s vegetation, a sign that is visible to all.
4 | and Who brings forth ˹green˺ pasture, | The general principle of creation, ordainment, and guidance is now illustrated with a vivid, observable example from the natural world in verse four: “and Who brings forth green pasture.” The Arabic wa alladhī akhraja al-mar‘ā refers to the act of causing the fresh, green vegetation to sprout from the earth. The word mar‘ā specifically denotes pasture, the lush grass and herbage upon which cattle and animals graze. It is the vibrant, life-giving cover that transforms a barren, dead land into a verdant, thriving landscape. This image is one of the most powerful and recurrent signs in the Qur’ān, used to illustrate the power of the Creator and the reality of the resurrection. The verb akhraja means He brought out, He extracted. The green pasture was not present; it was hidden in the soil, latent in the seeds and the nutrients of the earth. The Most High brings it out through the mechanism of rain and sunlight, which He also ordains. The verse invites the listener to look at a field of green grass and see the hand of the Creator. The green colour, which has a soothing effect on the human eye, is not a random accident; it is the result of the precise ordainment of chlorophyll and photosynthesis, the intricate process by which plants convert sunlight into energy. This process sustains all animal and human life. The mar‘ā is the base of the food chain. By mentioning it, the Qur’ān reminds the human being of his utter dependence on the divine provision. The most powerful king and the wealthiest merchant cannot create a single blade of green grass. The verse also resonates with the Arabs of Mecca, who were pastoralists and traders, intimately familiar with the cycle of the seasons. They knew the desperation of a dry year when the pasture would wither and the flocks would suffer. The return of the green pasture was a matter of life and death, a direct mercy from the Most High. The choice of this example ties the lofty theological truths of the earlier verses to the daily bread of the listener. It is a sign that is both majestic in its complexity and humble in its simplicity. The believer is encouraged to see every field of green as a manifestation of the divine name al-A‘lā, and to praise Him accordingly. The scene is now set for the next verse, which completes the cycle and drives home the lesson of transience and resurrection.
5 | then reduces it to withered chaff. | The life cycle of the green pasture is brought to its inevitable conclusion in verse five: “then reduces it to withered chaff.” The Arabic fa-ja‘alahu ghuthā’an aḥwā uses a powerful image. After bringing forth the lush green pasture, the Most High causes it to dry up, to turn brown and brittle, to become a lifeless, withered residue. The word ghuthā’ refers to the dry, broken stalks and husks that are carried away by the flood or the wind, the worthless debris of what was once living and green. The word aḥwā describes something that has turned dark, blackened, and decayed after being green. This is the complete transformation from life to death, from vibrant colour to dark decay. The verse is a profound meditation on the transience of the world. The green pasture that was so beautiful and so full of life is reduced to something that is trampled, scattered, and forgotten. This is the fate of all worldly things: wealth, youth, power, beauty, all follow the same trajectory from spring to winter, from life to withering. The verse serves as a direct warning to those who are deceived by the glitter of the dunyā, as the later verses will explicitly state. It also contains a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who reduces the green pasture to withered chaff is perfectly capable of reversing that process. Just as He brings the dead earth to life, He will bring the dead bones to life. The cycle of vegetation is a miniature, yearly version of the great cycle of life, death, and resurrection that encompasses all humanity. The word ghuthā’ also carries a sense of being swept away, useless. The Meccans who prided themselves on their lineage and wealth are reminded that they too will wither and their worldly acquisitions will become like scattered chaff. The only thing that remains is what is done for the Most High. The verse connects back to the previous surahs that described the sky splitting and the earth ejecting its contents. The withering of the pasture is the small-scale sign; the great destruction of the cosmos is the large-scale reality. For the believer, this verse instills a deep sense of zuhd, detachment from the temporary glitter of the world. It teaches that the wise person invests in what never withers, the deeds done for the sake of the Lord. The first section of the sūrah concludes with this powerful image, and the next section turns to the preservation of the divine word and the ease of the path to salvation.
6 | We will have you recite ˹the Quran, O Prophet,˺ so you will not forget ˹any of it˺, | After the descriptions of the divine creative power, the sūrah turns to a direct address to the Prophet (ﷺ), providing him with a profound personal reassurance in verse six: “We will have you recite the Quran, O Prophet, so you will not forget any of it.” The Arabic sa-nuqri’uka fa-lā tansā uses the majestic plural “We” to convey the grandeur of the divine action. The verb nuqri’uka means “We will cause you to recite,” implying a continuous, divinely facilitated process of revelation and memorization. The Prophet is promised that the Qur’ān will be so firmly established in his heart and on his tongue that he will not forget it. This verse was revealed in the context of the early stages of revelation, when the Prophet (ﷺ) would often hasten to repeat the words of Jibrīl as they were revealed, fearing that he might forget them. A well-known ḥadīth in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī narrates that the Prophet used to move his tongue rapidly with the revelation, anxious to memorize it. Allah then revealed this verse and others telling him not to hasten, for its collection and recitation were guaranteed by the Almighty. The promise “you will not forget” is a divine gift, a miraculous preservation of the entire Qur’ān in the heart of an unlettered man. This was a direct answer to the Meccans who taunted the Prophet, saying that he might forget the old stories he was reciting. The Most High assures him that his memory is under divine protection. The phrase “any of it” is implied by the absolute negation lā tansā, which covers every verse, every word, every nuance. This verse also carries a broader lesson for all believers: the memorization of the Qur’ān is a blessing that comes from Allah. While human effort is required, the ultimate reliance should be on the One who facilitates the recitation. The believer who strives to learn the Qur’ān by heart is participating in a promise that Allah gave to His Prophet. The verse also highlights the intimate relationship between the Prophet and his Lord, a relationship of direct instruction and care. The One who created the green pasture and reduces it to withered chaff is the same One who personally tutors His Messenger. This should fill the heart of the Prophet and the believers with awe and gratitude. The promise of not forgetting is followed by an exception in the next verse, which further refines the understanding of divine will and knowledge.
7 | unless Allah wills otherwise. He surely knows what is open and what is hidden. | The absolute promise of memory is qualified by a profound statement in verse seven: “unless Allah wills otherwise. He surely knows what is open and what is hidden.” The Arabic illā mā shā’a Allāh introduces an exception that places everything under the authority of the divine will. The Prophet will not forget the Qur’ān except for whatever Allah may will him to forget. This does not contradict the promise; rather, it affirms that Allah’s will is supreme over all things, including the Prophet’s memory. If at any time a verse were to be abrogated, removed from the recitation, or if the Prophet were to temporarily forget something as a test or as a sign of human frailty, it would be by the deliberate will of Allah. The exception serves to teach the fundamental Islamic creed that even the blessings given to the prophets are under the control of the Bestower. The second part of the verse, “He surely knows what is open and what is hidden,” provides the basis for the exception. The Arabic innahu ya‘lamu al-jahra wa mā yakhfā indicates that Allah’s knowledge encompasses everything, the public and the secret, the visible and the concealed. The divine wisdom that determines what remains and what is forgotten is based on perfect, all-encompassing knowledge. The Prophet is comforted that whatever Allah wills regarding the revelation is based on knowledge that is beyond human perception. This verse also carries a subtle refutation of the disbelievers who whispered secret plots against the Prophet. Allah knows what they openly mock and what they hide in their hearts. For the believers, the verse is a powerful reminder of the limits of human control. We may strive to memorize, to learn, to retain, but we must always attach that striving to the will of Allah. The phrase “what is open and what is hidden” covers the entire spectrum of existence. The hidden sins of the oppressors, the hidden anxieties of the Prophet, the hidden wisdom behind every divine decree—all are known to the Most High. The verse instills a profound sense of trust and submission. The believer can rely on the fact that the All-Knowing never wills anything without a deep and perfect reason. The verse also connects back to the earlier theme of the vigilant angels recording everything. Nothing is hidden from the knowledge of Allah, and His will governs all. This is the foundation of peace for the heart that entrusts its affairs to the Lord of the worlds. The next verse will reveal the practical facilitation that flows from this divine care.
8 | We will facilitate for you the Way of Ease. | The divine care for the Prophet continues with a comprehensive promise in verse eight: “We will facilitate for you the Way of Ease.” The Arabic wa nuyassiruka li-l-yusrā is a statement of profound mercy. The verb nuyassiru means “We will make easy,” “We will facilitate,” “We will guide gently towards.” The word yusrā is the superlative form of yusr, meaning ease, and it refers to the path that is inherently easy, the way of life that aligns with human nature and leads to eternal bliss. This “Way of Ease” is Islam itself, the religion of the fitrah, the path that removes burdens, lifts heavy yokes, and brings the soul into harmony with its Creator. The verse promises that the Prophet will be guided and enabled to follow this path, to convey it, and to live it with a facility that comes from divine assistance. It is a reassurance against the harshness and difficulty of his mission. The persecution, the mockery, the plots—all are external difficulties, but the inner path, the spiritual journey, will be made easy for him. This promise was fulfilled in the Prophet’s life. Despite the monumental challenges, his heart was at ease, his character was gracious, and the religion he established was characterized by the removal of undue hardship. The Islamic law is built on the principle of ease, as the Qur’ān says elsewhere, “Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship.” The “Way of Ease” is also a description of the path to Paradise, which is made easy for the one whom Allah guides. The believer who struggles to obey will find that Allah gradually makes the acts of worship beloved and light upon the heart. The verse is a response to the earlier mention of the withered chaff. The worldly life, with its glitter, leads to difficulty and ultimate decay, but the path of faith leads to ease that lasts into eternity. This promise is not just for the Prophet; it extends to every follower who sincerely seeks the Way. The believer who feels that the burdens of life are crushing can find comfort in this verse: the Most High is actively facilitating the path of ease for those who turn to Him. The word yusrā also carries a connotation of the outcome, the ultimate ease of Paradise. The entire journey of faith, though it may contain moments of struggle, is heading towards the ultimate ease. The following verses will command the Prophet to continue reminding people, based on this facilitated way. The divine facilitation empowers the mission.
9 | So ˹always˺ remind ˹with the Quran˺—˹even˺ if the reminder is beneficial ˹only to some˺. | Having been assured of the preservation of the Qur’ān and the facilitation of the way, the Prophet receives a command in verse nine: “So always remind with the Quran—even if the reminder is beneficial only to some.” The Arabic fa-dhakkir in nafa‘ati al-dhikrā begins with the imperative dhakkir, “remind,” from the root dhikr, which means to remind, to admonish, to cause to remember. The Qur’ān is the ultimate reminder, and the Prophet’s primary role is to deliver it. The conditional clause “if the reminder is beneficial” has been interpreted in two complementary ways. One is that the Prophet should remind regardless, because the reminder is inherently beneficial; the condition expresses that its benefit is certain, as in “remind, for indeed the reminder benefits.” The second interpretation is that the Prophet should persist in reminding even if only some benefit, because the benefit to even a single soul is of immense value. Both meanings converge on the command of unwavering da‘wah. The Prophet is not to be discouraged by the persistent denial of the majority. His task is to deliver the reminder, and the outcome is in Allah’s hands. The verse is a powerful antidote to the frustration that callers to truth often feel when their message is rejected. It says: the benefit is present, whether you see it or not, and your duty is to continue reminding. The word dhikrā carries a sense of a gentle, heartfelt admonition, not a harsh lecture. The reminder is meant to touch the heart, to awaken the innate knowledge of the Creator. The verse also implies that the reminder itself is a mercy, a light that has intrinsic value. The disbelievers may reject it, but that does not diminish its truth or its benefit to those who receive it. This command is directly linked to the previous verse’s promise of facilitation. The Prophet is being equipped with ease so that he can tirelessly carry out this reminder. The believer today is the inheritor of this command; sharing the message of Islam is an act of dhikr, a reminding of the world of its Lord. The verse sets the stage for the division of humanity into those who will benefit and those who will shun the reminder, which the following verses describe. The tone is one of calm, persistent optimism rooted in divine authority, not in the fluctuating responses of the audience.
10 | Those in awe ˹of Allah˺ will be mindful ˹of it˺. | Verse ten identifies the group who will benefit from the reminder: “Those in awe of Allah will be mindful of it.” The Arabic sa-yadhdhakkaru man yakhshā uses the future particle sa- to indicate certainty. The verb yadhdhakkaru means to be mindful, to take heed, to actively remember and internalize the reminder. The characteristic of the one who does this is yakhshā, which translates as “he who fears” or “he who is in awe.” The word khashyah is a profound form of fear, a reverential awe mixed with love and deep knowledge of Allah’s greatness. It is the fear of a lover who dreads displeasing the beloved. This person, whose heart is filled with awe of the unseen Lord, will naturally respond to the reminder. The reminder is like rain; it benefits only the fertile soil. The heart that is soft with khashyah is the fertile ground where the seeds of the Qur’ān sprout. The verse explains why some people are transformed by the Qur’ān while others remain unmoved. The difference is not in the eloquence of the reciter or even in the intellectual capacity of the listener, but in the inner state of the heart. The one who has awe is already oriented towards the truth; the reminder simply activates what is already latent. This verse also connects to the earlier command to glorify the Most High. The one who is in awe of the Most High will be mindful of His words. The verse also provides a diagnostic for the believer: am I finding benefit in the Qur’ān? If not, I should examine the level of khashyah in my heart. Khashyah is cultivated by reflecting on the signs of creation, the transience of the world, and the certainty of the meeting with the Lord. The Meccan disbelievers who mocked the Prophet lacked khashyah; their hearts were hardened by their arrogance and their love of the world. They were not in awe of the Most High, so the reminder fell on deaf ears. The verse is a promise that the sincere efforts of the Prophet will not be in vain; there will always be those whose awe causes them to respond. For the believers, it is a call to nurture that awe through constant remembrance, prayer, and reflection. The next verse presents the stark contrast, the fate of the one who deliberately shuns the reminder.
11 | But it will be shunned by the most wretched, | The fate of the opposite group is sealed in verse eleven: “But it will be shunned by the most wretched.” The Arabic wa yatajannabuhā al-ashqā describes a deliberate, active avoidance. The verb yatajannabu means he keeps far away from it, he avoids it, he shuns it. This is not a passive neglect; it is a conscious turning away, an aversion rooted in dislike and arrogance. The one who does this is al-ashqā, the superlative form of shaqī, meaning the most wretched, the one who is farthest from goodness and nearest to misery. This person is the extreme opposite of the one with khashyah. His heart is so encased in pride and love of the world that the mere mention of the Qur’ān causes him to recoil. The definite article “al-ashqā” does not imply that there is only one such person, but that this is the archetype of the confirmed, unrepentant disbeliever. Some early exegetes mentioned that this verse referred specifically to certain obstinate Meccan leaders like al-Walīd ibn al-Mughīrah or Abū Jahl, but the meaning is universal. The one who shuns the reminder is the most wretched because he is choosing eternal misery over eternal bliss. The verse presents a terrifying truth: the worst state of wretchedness is not poverty, sickness, or oppression, but the spiritual state of turning away from the words of the Most High. Such a person may be wealthy, powerful, and respected in the world, but in the divine scale, he is al-ashqā. The description of his fate in the next verses will make clear the outcome of his shunning. The verse also serves as a warning to the believers to never develop an aversion to the remembrance of Allah. A person might not be among the “most wretched” initially, but if one continually ignores the reminders, the heart can harden until it reaches that state. The believer should be vigilant, always running towards the dhikr, not away from it. The stark contrast between the one who is mindful and the one who shuns is the pivot of the sūrah, and the eternal consequences of these two paths are laid out in the following verses. The reminder of the Qur’ān is the dividing line of humanity, separating the awe-filled from the wretched, the successful from the lost.
12 | who will burn in the greatest Fire, | The eternal consequence of the wretched one’s shunning is revealed in verse twelve: “who will burn in the greatest Fire.” The Arabic alladhī yaṣlā al-nāra al-kubrā uses the relative pronoun “who” to directly link the person to his punishment. The verb yaṣlā means he will enter, be exposed to, and burn in the Fire. The Fire is described as al-nār al-kubrā, the greatest Fire, the supreme Fire. This designation indicates that the Hellfire prepared for the most wretched, the obstinate disbelievers who actively shunned the reminder, is of a magnitude and intensity beyond the ordinary comprehension of lesser punishments. Some scholars have commented that the “greatest Fire” refers to the highest and most severe level of Hell, the deepest pit reserved for the worst of the disbelievers, while the “lesser Fire” mentioned elsewhere might refer to the punishments of the grave or the lower levels of Hell. The point is that the punishment fits the crime. They were given the greatest reminder, the Qur’ān, and they responded with the greatest aversion. Therefore, they face the greatest Fire. The word kubrā also carries a sense of overwhelming magnitude. The Fire is so vast and so terrible that it dwarfs any fire known in this world. The makers of the ditch who burned the believers in a pit of fuel will be placed in a fire that makes their ditch seem like a spark. The verse is devoid of any softening language; it is a blunt, terrifying statement designed to pierce the heart of the listener. The Prophet is instructed to convey this warning as part of the reminder. For the believer, the mention of the greatest Fire serves as a powerful deterrent against the sins that lead to it. It also reinforces the gratitude for the guidance that saved them from it. The believer should never feel secure from the plot of Allah; rather, one should live between hope and fear, praying to be protected from the greatest Fire. The verse connects directly to the earlier description of the withered chaff. Just as the green pasture is reduced to worthless debris, the life of the ashqā, which seemed flourishing in the world, will be reduced to fuel for the greatest Fire. The image is complete and devastating.
13 | where they will not ˹be able to˺ live or die. | The torment of the greatest Fire is further described with a chilling paradox in verse thirteen: “where they will not be able to live or die.” The Arabic thumma lā yamūtu fīhā wa lā yaḥyā uses a negation of both death and true life. The one who enters the greatest Fire will suffer a state of perpetual agony where neither of the two possible releases is available. Death, which in this world is an escape from severe pain, will not come. The damned will wish for annihilation, crying out “O Mālik, let your Lord put an end to us,” but the wish will not be granted. True life, which is characterized by peace, joy, and well-being, will also be absent. They will exist in a suspended state of continuous, undying torment. This description is one of the most terrifying depictions of Hell in the Qur’ān. It uses a simple, stark juxtaposition to convey an infinite suffering. In this world, life and death are the two poles of existence. Every pain eventually ends, either by recovery or by death. In the greatest Fire, both of these exits are sealed shut. The agony is constant, and it never ceases, and it never lessens. The verse refutes the idea that Hell is a place of annihilation or a temporary purgatory for the disbelievers. The fire does not consume them to the point of non-existence; they remain alive to taste the punishment. The use of thumma, which usually indicates a sequence, here adds a sense of “moreover,” piling one horror upon another. Not only will they burn in the greatest Fire, but moreover, in that state, they will neither die nor live. The psychological dimension of this punishment is profound. The absence of hope for an end intensifies the suffering infinitely. The verse serves as a divine warning of the utmost severity. It is meant to shake the heedless out of their complacency. The one who shuns the reminder might think that death is the end, a peaceful nothingness. The Qur’ān tells him that his denial will lead him to a state where the very nothingness he hoped for will be forever out of reach. For the believer, this verse inspires an immediate, heartfelt dua: “O Allah, protect us from the Fire where one neither lives nor dies.” It also motivates the believer to endure the temporary hardships of this life, knowing that even the worst worldly pain eventually ends, while the pain of Hell does not. The verse completes the portrait of the wretched, setting up the dramatic contrast with the successful in the following verses.
14 | Successful indeed are those who purify themselves, | After the terrifying depiction of the wretched, the sūrah shifts to the radiant opposite with a declaration of triumph in verse fourteen: “Successful indeed are those who purify themselves.” The Arabic qad aflaḥa man tazakkā uses the particle qad for emphasis and certainty. The verb aflaḥa comes from falaḥ, which means to succeed, to achieve true and lasting prosperity, to cultivate and harvest the fruits of one’s labor. The successful ones are those who engage in tazakkā. The word tazakkā is from the root zakā, meaning to purify, to grow, to increase in goodness. Tazakkā is the reflexive form, indicating an active, self-initiated process of purification. This purification encompasses both the inner and the outer. It includes the purification of the soul from sins like arrogance, greed, and envy, through repentance and the cultivation of virtues. It also includes the outward purification, which many early exegetes, including Ibn ‘Abbās, specifically linked to the payment of zakāt al-fiṭr (the charity at the end of Ramadan) and giving charity generally, thereby purifying one’s wealth and one’s self from stinginess. The verse was often recited by the Prophet (ﷺ) in the ‘Īd prayers, linking the success of the ‘Īd with the act of purification that preceded it. The concept of tazakkā is comprehensive. The one who purifies his belief from shirk and disbelief, his heart from spiritual diseases, his limbs from sinful actions, and his wealth from the right of others, is the one who has laid the foundation for eternal success. The word “successful” (aflaḥa) is the ultimate word of hope. It is the cry of the mu’adhdhin in the call to prayer: “Ḥayya ‘alā al-falāḥ,” come to success. This verse defines what that success truly is and how it is achieved. The purification is not a one-time event but a continuous process, a diligent cultivation of the self. The contrast with the previous verses is striking: the one who shuns the reminder and refuses to purify himself is the most wretched, burning in a Fire where he neither lives nor dies. The one who purifies himself is the successful, whose destination is eternal bliss. The verse thus serves as a powerful motivation to engage in the lifelong journey of tazkiyah al-nafs. It promises that the effort of fighting one’s lower desires and turning to Allah is not in vain; it is the very path to true, lasting success. The following verse will enumerate specific acts of purification and remembrance that lead to this success.
15 | remember the Name of their Lord, and pray. | The acts that flow from and constitute tazakkā are specified in verse fifteen: “remember the Name of their Lord, and pray.” The Arabic wa dhakara isma Rabbihi fa-ṣallā presents two core acts of worship that are inseparable. The first is dhikr, the remembrance of the Name of the Lord. This includes all forms of verbal remembrance, such as saying subḥāna Allāh, al-ḥamdu lillāh, Allāhu akbar, and especially the words of glorification that opened this very sūrah. To “remember the Name of the Lord” is to keep the divine names on one’s tongue and in one’s heart, to begin every action with the name of Allah, and to live in a state of constant mindfulness of His presence. The second act is ṣalāh, the formal prayer. The particle fa- (and, then) can indicate a sequence, but it also indicates that prayer is the greatest manifestation of that remembrance. The one who has purified himself will naturally turn to the remembrance of his Lord, and that remembrance will culminate in the establishment of the prayer. The verse pairs the inner and outer, the private and the public, the spontaneous and the structured. Remembrance can be done at any time, in any place; prayer has its fixed times, its specific postures, its direction towards the Ka‘bah. Both are essential. The mention of prayer specifically after purification is significant; many scholars have connected this verse to the practice of the ‘Īd prayer, where the Muslim purifies himself through ghusl and charity, remembers Allah with the takbīrāt, and then performs the prayer. The verse thus provides a complete template for a day of true success. The one who wants to be among the mufliḥūn (the successful) must engage in tazakkā, dhikr, and ṣalāh. The order is also profound: purification of the self makes the heart ready for genuine remembrance, and genuine remembrance overflows into the disciplined, humble act of prayer. The prayer itself is a form of purification, as the Prophet (ﷺ) described it as a river in which one bathes five times a day, washing away sins. The verse connects back to the opening command to glorify the Name of the Lord, the Most High. The entire sūrah is a call to a life of glorification, and here that life is summarized in its essential practices. For the believer, this verse is a daily checklist: am I purifying my soul, am I remembering my Lord, am I praying? These are the deeds of the successful, the deeds that lead to the eternal gardens and away from the greatest Fire. The next verses will contrast this with the choice of the deniers.
16 | But you ˹deniers only˺ prefer the life of this world, | The sūrah now delivers a direct and sobering diagnosis of the deniers’ condition in verse sixteen: “But you deniers only prefer the life of this world.” The Arabic bal tu’thirūna al-ḥayāta al-dunyā uses the word bal to sharply contrast with the previous description of the successful. The successful purify themselves, remember their Lord, and pray; as for the disbelievers, they instead make a deliberate, conscious choice. The verb tu’thirūna means “you prefer,” “you give priority to,” “you choose one thing over another.” It indicates an act of will, a decision based on desire. The object of their preference is al-ḥayāt al-dunyā, the life of this world, the lower life, the immediate, tangible, and fleeting pleasures of the material realm. The word dunyā itself comes from the root danā, meaning to be near or low. It is the near, lowly life compared to the elevated, eternal life of the Hereafter. The verse exposes the root cause of their shunning of the reminder. They are not intellectually incapable of seeing the truth; rather, their hearts are so attached to the immediate gratifications of the world—wealth, status, power, sensual pleasure—that they actively choose them over the deferred, greater rewards of the Hereafter. This preference is the essence of the human tragedy. It is the trade of the eternal for the temporary, the real for the illusion. The “you” is plural, addressing the Meccan disbelievers directly, but the message resonates across time. Every sin is, in essence, a preference for the dunyā over the ākhirah. The verse is a mirror held up to the soul. It forces the listener to ask: what am I truly preferring in my daily choices? Am I choosing the ease of the world over the effort of prayer? Am I choosing the pleasure of backbiting over the purification of silence? Am I choosing the accumulation of wealth over the purification of charity? The verse does not condemn the world itself, which is a place of planting for the Hereafter; it condemns the preference for the world over the Hereafter. The believer lives in the dunyā but his heart is anchored in the ākhirah. The verse is a warning and a wake-up call. It reminds the believer that the culture of materialism that surrounds us is a direct echo of the Meccan elite’s mindset, and the cure is the same: tazakkā, dhikr, and ṣalāh, which realign the heart’s priorities. The next verse will give the logical and compelling reason why this preference is a catastrophic error.
17 | even though the Hereafter is far better and more lasting. | The profound foolishness of preferring this world is laid bare in verse seventeen: “even though the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.” The Arabic wa al-ākhiratu khayrun wa abqā uses a simple, unadorned comparison that carries the weight of undeniable truth. The particle wa, “even though,” introduces a reality that makes the deniers’ preference utterly irrational. Al-ākhirah, the Hereafter, the ultimate life, is described with two superlative qualities. First, it is khayr, better, far superior in quality, goodness, and pleasure. The bliss of Paradise is not merely a slight improvement over the pleasures of the world; it is in a completely different category of excellence. The Prophet (ﷺ) reported that in Paradise there is “what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has ever conceived.” The smallest space in Paradise is better than the entire world and all it contains. Second, it is abqā, more lasting, more enduring, eternal. The world, no matter how long one lives, inevitably ends in death and decay, as illustrated by the green pasture turning to withered chaff. The Hereafter never ends. It is an infinite life. When one compares a finite, imperfect pleasure with an infinite, perfect pleasure, the rational choice is obvious. The verse is an appeal to basic human reason and self-interest. The deniers think they are being clever by seizing the immediate gain, but they are actually making the worst trade in history. The believer is called to constantly remind himself of this comparison. When faced with a temptation, the mind should echo this verse: “the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.” This is the cornerstone of zuhd, the Islamic detachment from the world. Zuhd does not mean despising the permissible pleasures of the world, but rather understanding their true, fleeting value and not allowing them to distract from the permanent and superior. The verse also serves as a comfort to the believers who were poor and oppressed in Mecca. They were denied the pleasures of the world, but they were securing the far better and more lasting pleasures of the Hereafter. This was the secret to their patience and resilience. The verse also corrects the misconception that religion is a killjoy; it is rather the ultimate guide to maximum, lasting pleasure. The believers will have the best of this world in a lawful way and the best of the Hereafter without limit. The phrase “far better and more lasting” is a divine guarantee, a banner under which the believer marches through the temptations of the dunyā. The final two verses will ground this truth in the continuity of the prophetic message, showing that this wisdom is ancient and universal.
18 | This is certainly ˹mentioned˺ in the earlier Scriptures— | The sūrah concludes by anchoring its core message in the grand narrative of divine revelation in verse eighteen: “This is certainly mentioned in the earlier Scriptures.” The Arabic inna hādhā la-fī al-ṣuḥufi al-ūlā uses multiple emphasis particles: inna and la-. The demonstrative pronoun hādhā, “this,” refers to the entire message of the sūrah—the glorification of the Most High, the reality of creation and resurrection, the success of those who purify themselves, and the preference for the Hereafter over the world. All of this, the verse declares, is not a new invention or a peculiar teaching of the Prophet Muḥammad (ﷺ) alone. It is confirmed and recorded in the earlier scriptures, the ancient scrolls of the previous prophets. The word ṣuḥuf is the plural of ṣaḥīfah, meaning pages, scrolls, written scriptures. Al-ūlā means the first, the earliest, the ancient. This refers to the scriptures that were revealed before the Qur’ān, specifically, as the next verse names, the scriptures of Abraham and Moses. The declaration serves several crucial purposes. It refutes the Meccan claim that the Prophet was bringing something bizarre and unprecedented. The core truths of his message—monotheism, moral purification, accountability, the transient nature of the world, the importance of prayer and charity—are the timeless truths that have been revealed to every prophet since the dawn of humanity. This continuity validates the Prophet’s message and comforts him. He is not alone; he stands in a long line of noble messengers delivering the same essential truth. It also challenges the pagans who had some residual respect for the ancient traditions. Their own forefather Abraham was a monotheist, and Moses is revered. The Qur’ān is telling them that the very scriptures they might acknowledge in name contain the very truths they are rejecting. For the believer, this verse instills a sense of connection to the universal community of faith that stretches across history. The religion of all the prophets is one, and its essence is the glorification of the Most High and the purification of the self for the eternal life. The mention of the earlier scriptures also emphasizes that the Qur’ān is the final, confirming authority over them, preserving what was authentic and correcting what was altered. The verse elevates the reader’s perspective from the local conflict in Mecca to the cosmic, historical sweep of divine guidance. The final verse will identify these earlier scriptures by name, giving the reference a concrete anchor.
19 | the Scriptures of Abraham and Moses. | The sūrah closes by naming those earlier scriptures in verse nineteen: “the Scriptures of Abraham and Moses.” The Arabic ṣuḥufi Ibrāhīma wa Mūsā specifies the two great prophetic figures whose scriptures were known to the People of the Book and whose names were familiar to the Arabs. Abraham (Ibrāhīm) is the father of monotheism, the patriarch who smashed the idols, built the Ka‘bah, and was given the title Khalīl Allāh, the intimate friend of Allah. He received sacred scrolls containing foundational guidance. Moses (Mūsā) is the great prophet to whom the Tawrāh, the Torah, was revealed, a detailed law and guidance for the Children of Israel. By mentioning these two, the Qur’ān connects the final revelation to both the pre-Mosaic and Mosaic dispensations. The message of the Qur’ān is the revival and perfection of the Abrahamic and Mosaic traditions. Abraham represents pure, primordial monotheism, and Moses represents the revealed law. The Qur’ān is the culmination of both. The verse also carries a subtle but powerful argument against the Meccan pagans. They claimed to follow the way of Abraham, yet they had fallen into the most corrupt form of idolatry, burying their daughters and worshipping stones. The verse tells them that the true scriptures of Abraham, the father they claim, contain the very message of purification, prayer, and the Hereafter that they are now rejecting. Similarly, the Jews and Christians who held the scripture of Moses were told that the same truths are confirmed in the Qur’ān. The naming of Moses also connects to the earlier mention of Pharaoh and Thamūd in previous sūrahs, tying the narratives together. For the believer, this final verse is a profound reminder of the unity of divine truth. The path to success has always been the same: glorify the Most High, purify the self, remember the Lord, pray, and prefer the eternal over the temporal. The sūrah began with the command to glorify the Most High, and it ends by noting that this command and its entire context were inscribed in the most ancient of scriptures. This creates a perfect, closed circle of timeless truth. The believer who recites this sūrah is partaking in a message that spans all of human history, a message from the Most High that has been sent to guide humanity from its earliest days to its final hours. The entire supplied passage of Sūrah Al-A‘lā has now been fully completed.
