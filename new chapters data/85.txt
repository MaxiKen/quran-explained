1 | By the sky full of constellations, | The sūrah opens with a magnificent oath: “By the sky full of constellations.” The Arabic word used is al-burūj, the plural of burj, which originally means a tower, a fortress, or a high, visible structure. In the context of the sky, it refers to the great constellations, the groupings of stars that form patterns across the celestial sphere, or the twelve signs of the zodiac through which the sun and moon travel. The sky is not a chaotic scattering of light; it is a structured, adorned canopy marked by these towering star-patterns. By swearing by this, Allah draws the human gaze upward to the vast, orderly heavens that testify to the power, wisdom, and majesty of the Creator. The oath prepares the listener for a matter of immense gravity. When Allah swears by any of His creation, it is to highlight the importance of what follows and to provide a sign that points to the truth of the message. The sky with its constellations is a cosmic book that the Arabs observed nightly in their desert travels, recognising the regularity of its patterns. These burūj are not deities, as some might have imagined, but created things, placed in the sky as adornments, guidance for travellers, and signs for those who reflect. The oath also serves as a subtle link to the story about to be told. Just as the constellations are high and visible, the deeds of the tyrants and the believers were witnessed by the One who is above all. The towering sky is a symbol of protection and height, yet even that will be rent asunder on the Day of Judgment. The oath therefore sets a tone of sublime seriousness. It reminds the believer that the same Lord who placed the constellations in the heavens is the One who will judge the oppressors and the oppressed. For the listener, the verse evokes a sense of awe and anticipation, drawing the heart away from the dusty earth of conflict and pointing it toward the celestial signs of the Almighty’s unchallenged authority, preparing the way for the oaths that follow.
2 | and the promised Day ˹of Judgment˺, | The second oath intensifies the gravity of the message: “and the promised Day of Judgment.” The Arabic wa al-yawmi al-maw‘ūd uses the passive participle maw‘ūd, meaning “the Day that has been promised.” This is the Day of Resurrection and Reckoning, a day that every prophet warned of, a day whose coming is an absolute, unbreakable promise from Allah. The oath by this Day draws attention to its certainty. It is not a myth or a distant possibility; it is as real as the sky they see above them. The pairing of the sky with the Day of Judgment is profound. The sky, with all its constellations, appears permanent and unchanging, yet it will one day burst open. The promised Day is the moment when the heavens and the earth will be replaced, when the graves will spill out their contents, and when the oppressed will meet the oppressors before the divine tribunal. The oath also connects directly to the story of the People of the Ditch. Those believers who were burned alive for their faith might have seemed, in the eyes of the world, to have suffered a complete and final loss. But the promised Day is the guarantee that their story is not over, that their blood is not forgotten, and that a court of absolute justice awaits. For the tyrants who sat by the fire and watched, this oath is a terrifying declaration that their worldly power was not the final word. The Day that was promised will come, and on that Day they will face a fire far greater than the one they kindled. For the believer, the oath by the promised Day instills a powerful sense of patience and hope. Whatever injustices we witness or endure in this world are temporary. The promise of a Day of Judgment means that no wrong will remain unaddressed. It also serves as a call to live in constant awareness of that Day, allowing its certainty to shape our actions and priorities. The sky and the promised Day are two witnesses, one seen and one unseen, both creations of the same Lord, both pointing to the truth that the world is moving toward a definitive, divine conclusion.
3 | and the witness and what is witnessed! | The third and final oath of the opening series is one of the most profound and multi-layered in the Qur’ān: “and the witness and what is witnessed!” The Arabic wa shāhidin wa mashhūd uses two words from the root shahida, which encompasses seeing, testifying, and being present. The “witness” (shāhid) and the “what is witnessed” (mashhūd) have been interpreted by the companions and early scholars in many complementary ways. Some said the witness is the Day of Friday, and the witnessed is the Day of ‘Arafah. Others said the witness is the Prophet Muḥammad (ﷺ), who will testify over his nation, and the witnessed is the Day of Judgment. Others said the witness is every human being’s own limbs, which will testify against them, and the witnessed is the record of their deeds. Others said the witness is the angels who record, and the witnessed is the gathering of all creation. All these interpretations converge on a single theme: testimony and presence. The scene of judgment is one where nothing is hidden. Every act will have its witness, whether an angel, a prophet, a limb, or the day itself. In the context of the sūrah, the oath by the witness and the witnessed directly prepares for the story of the makers of the ditch. The believers who were thrown into the fire were witnessed by their murderers who sat around, and by the angels who recorded, and above all by Allah, the ultimate Witness. The tyrants thought their crime was a spectacle for their entertainment, but in reality, it was a scene being witnessed by the Lord of the worlds. The oath also applies to the listener: every deed we do, hidden or open, has a witness. The day we live, the ground we walk on, our own skin, all will one day bear witness. The verse thus creates an overwhelming sense of accountability. The pairing of “the witness” and “what is witnessed” in an oath form elevates the entire concept of shahādah—testimony—to a cosmic level. It is a solemn reminder that we are always under observation, and that the ultimate trial will be based on irrefutable evidence. The stage is now fully set for the terrifying story that follows.
4 | Condemned are the makers of the ditch— | With the three great oaths complete, the object of the oaths is delivered in a devastating declaration: “Condemned are the makers of the ditch.” The Arabic qutila aṣḥābu al-ukhdūd uses the verb qutila, literally “they were killed” or “slain,” which is an expression of curse and condemnation. It means “may they be destroyed,” “cursed are they.” This is a divine verdict against the tyrants who dug a great trench and filled it with fire to persecute the believers. The story refers to a historical event that occurred in the region of Najrān, in southern Arabia, around the year 524 CE. A Jewish king named Dhū Nuwās, who ruled over the Himyarite kingdom in Yemen, launched a brutal persecution against the Christians of Najrān who refused to abandon their faith and embrace Judaism. He ordered deep ditches to be dug, filled with blazing fuel, and the believers, including men, women, and children, were thrown into the fire. This act of mass execution was witnessed by the king and his soldiers who sat around the pit. The Qur’ān immortalises this event as an example of ultimate tyranny and steadfast faith. The “makers of the ditch” are not just those who physically dug the trench, but all who participated in the crime, the leadership that planned it, and those who gleefully observed. The word ukhdūd implies a deep, long trench, a scar deliberately carved into the earth for a purpose of unimaginable cruelty. The verse’s immediate condemnation, without even yet describing the crime in full, shows the severity of the sin. The oaths by the sky, the Day of Judgment, and the witness were all leading to this judgment. For the believers being persecuted in Mecca, this story was a powerful source of comfort. It told them that their suffering was seen, that their oppressors were already condemned in the divine court, and that the steadfastness of the people of the ditch was an example to follow. For the Quraysh who mocked and tortured the early Muslims, it was a dire warning that they were walking in the footsteps of a condemned people.
5 | the fire ˹pit˺, filled with fuel— | Verse five provides the terrifying detail of the ditch: “the fire pit, filled with fuel.” The Arabic al-nāri dhāti al-waqūd specifies that the ditch was not a simple trench but one designed to contain a raging, sustained fire. The word nār means fire, and dhāti al-waqūd means “possessor of fuel” or “filled with intensely burning fuel.” The fire was deliberately fed with massive amounts of wood, oil, or whatever combustible material was available, to make it a blazing inferno that would consume the believers completely. The description is stark and horrifying. The tyrants did not just kill; they created a theatrical, prolonged death by burning, one of the most painful forms of execution. The phrase “filled with fuel” indicates that this was a planned, premeditated atrocity, with resources dedicated to the suffering of the innocent. The fire pit was the tool of a state-sponsored persecution, intended to terrorise the population and crush the spirit of faith. The historical records of the Najrān massacre describe how believers were brought in groups, given the choice to renounce their faith or be thrown into the flames, and how they chose the fire over apostasy. The fuel was plentiful, the flames high, and the victims were reduced to ashes. The verse does not shy away from the graphic reality. By mentioning the fuel, the Qur’ān immortalises the cruelty of the oppressors, so that every generation that reads these words can picture the leaping flames and the thick smoke. It is an eternal testimony to the cost of faith and the evil of tyranny. For the believer, this verse is a reminder that the path of truth has always been met with violent opposition. The fire pit of Najrān is not an anomaly; it is the extreme manifestation of the hatred that evil harbours for good. Yet the verse is also a promise: the fuel that the tyrants gathered for the believers will be answered by a fuel of Hell prepared for the tyrants, as the later verses will make clear. The fire they kindled was temporary; the fire that awaits them is eternal.
6 | when they sat around it, | The scene of horror is made even more chilling by the detail in verse six: “when they sat around it.” The Arabic idh hum ‘alayhā qu‘ūd paints a picture of a gathered audience. The tyrants, having dug the ditch and ignited the fire, did not simply throw the believers in and leave. They sat down around the burning trench as if it were a spectacle, a source of entertainment. The word qu‘ūd means sitting, remaining seated, indicating a position of leisure and observation. They made themselves comfortable to watch innocent human beings writhe in agony. This detail exposes the utter depravity of their hearts. They had crossed a line from persecution to sadistic enjoyment. Their sitting is a symbol of power and arrogance; they were in control, the believers were in the fire, and they watched with satisfaction, convinced of their own invincibility. The verse also emphasizes that they were present as witnesses themselves, but witnesses to their own crime—a crime that will testify against them on the Day of Judgment. In the same way that they sat and watched, the angels were watching and recording, and the Lord of the Throne was watching. Their sitting around the fire is a dark inversion of the believers sitting on canopied couches in Paradise, looking on at the fate of the disbelievers. The tyrants’ momentary seat of entertainment will be replaced by their seat in the Fire of Hell. For the believers enduring persecution in Mecca, this verse was both a validation of their pain and a promise of justice. The pagans who sat around and mocked the tortured Muslims were reenacting the same scene, and they too would face the consequences. The verse also serves as a powerful moral lesson for all times: the position one takes in the face of injustice defines one’s destiny. To sit and watch cruelty with approval is to share in the crime. The righteous are those who refuse to sit among the oppressors, even if they must stand in the fire.
7 | watching what they had ˹ordered to be˺ done to the believers, | The purpose of their sitting is made explicit in verse seven: “watching what they had ordered to be done to the believers.” The Arabic wa hum ‘alā mā yaf‘alūna bi-l-mu’minīna shuhūd uses the word shuhūd, which means witnesses, spectators, those who are present and observing. They were not just passive onlookers; they were the ones who had commanded this atrocity. The phrase “what they had ordered to be done” indicates a chain of command. The leaders ordered, the soldiers executed, and then all gathered to watch the results. The believers are specifically named “al-mu’minīn,” the faithful ones. This is the only description of them, and it is the reason for the persecution. Their sole identity was their faith. The verse emphasises that the crime was against believers precisely because of their belief. The tyrants were not just punishing criminals or rebels; they were torturing people for their very heart’s conviction. The word “watching” (shuhūd) also carries the legal sense of testimony. They made themselves witnesses against their own souls. On the Day of Judgment, their own presence at the scene, their own eyes that looked upon the burning believers, will be evidence against them. The believers in the fire, on the other hand, were being watched by the One who matters most. The verse is a profound meditation on the act of witnessing. What we choose to watch, and with what heart we watch it, reveals our character. The tyrants watched with malice and satisfaction. The angels watched with recording pens. Allah watched with perfect knowledge. And the believers in the fire watched the gates of Paradise open before them. For the persecuted Muslims, this verse was a reminder that their suffering was not in vain. They were the mu’minīn, and the eyes of the universe were upon them. Their tormentors were already documented as witnesses to their own evil, and the divine verdict of qutila was already passed upon them.
8 | who they resented for no reason other than belief in Allah—the Almighty, the Praiseworthy— | The sūrah now identifies the sole motive for this horrendous crime in verse eight: “who they resented for no reason other than belief in Allah—the Almighty, the Praiseworthy.” The Arabic wa mā naqamū minhum illā an yu’minū bi-Allāhi al-‘Azīzi al-Ḥamīd uses the verb naqamū, meaning they resented, took vengeance, or were wrathful. The believers had committed no crime against the state, no act of rebellion, no breach of any just law. The only thing the tyrants could hold against them was their faith in Allah. This single statement strips away every possible justification the oppressors might have fabricated. The believers did not curse their gods, they did not attack their property, they did not plot against their kingdom. They simply believed in the one true God, and that was enough to ignite the hatred of the tyrants. This exposes the fundamental conflict between faith and tyranny. Tyranny cannot tolerate the allegiance of the heart to a higher authority. The believer’s submission to Allah is a direct threat to any power that demands absolute loyalty. The verse then describes Allah with two magnificent names: al-‘Azīz, the Almighty, the All-Mighty, the One whose power cannot be resisted; and al-Ḥamīd, the Praiseworthy, the One whose actions are inherently good and deserving of all praise. The mention of these names in the context of the persecution is deeply significant. The tyrants thought they were almighty in their little kingdom, but the believers were connected to the true Almighty, whose might would ultimately prevail. The tyrants thought their deeds would go unpunished, but the Praiseworthy Lord would ensure that justice is done and that the steadfastness of the believers would be praised for eternity. The verse offers a profound theological comfort. When believers are persecuted for their faith, they are not being punished for a mistake; they are sharing in the timeless struggle between truth and falsehood. Their Lord is both ‘Azīz and Ḥamīd, so their suffering is not a sign of His weakness but a test that will culminate in a praiseworthy outcome. The verse is also a direct rebuke to the Meccan pagans who harassed the Muslims for leaving the religion of their ancestors. They had no valid reason; their resentment was purely against the belief in the Almighty, the Praiseworthy.
9 | ˹the One˺ to Whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and earth. And Allah is a Witness over all things. | The description of Allah continues in verse nine, further dismantling the arrogance of the tyrants: “the One to Whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and earth. And Allah is a Witness over all things.” The phrase “to Whom belongs the kingdom” (alladhī lahu mulku al-samāwāti wa al-arḍ) establishes the absolute sovereignty of Allah. The tyrants owned a small piece of land and a ditch; Allah owns the entire heavens and the entire earth. Their power was a tiny, borrowed fragment, while His dominion is absolute and eternal. This is a direct challenge to the mindset of any oppressor who thinks his worldly kingdom gives him impunity. The king of Himyar, with his armies and his fire pits, was nothing compared to the King of all creation. His little fire was a spark compared to the Fire of Hell that awaits him. The second part, “And Allah is a Witness over all things,” returns to the theme of the oath by the witness. The Arabic wa Allāhu ‘alā kulli shay’in Shahīd uses the intensive form Shahīd, meaning the All-Witnessing, the One who is ever-present and ever-observing. The tyrants thought their deed was done in some remote desert, hidden from the eyes of the world. The verse declares that it was never hidden. Allah was a Witness. Every cry of the burning believers was heard, every prayer they uttered was recorded, every act of sadistic watching was noted. The witness of Allah is the ultimate witness. It needs no other testimony. This is a terrifying statement for the oppressor and an immensely comforting one for the oppressed. The believers who were thrown into the fire could have had no human advocate, no international court to appeal to, but they had the Lord of the heavens and the earth as their Witness. The Meccan Muslims, beaten in the streets and boycotted in the valleys, could take heart from this: their Lord was witnessing everything. This verse also ties back to the opening oaths. The witness and the witnessed, the sky and the promised Day, all culminate in the reality that the supreme Witness is Allah. His kingdom encompasses all, and His witness is perfect. The verse is a foundational pillar of Islamic faith in divine justice, teaching that no act of oppression, however small or however large, escapes the knowledge of the King and the Witness.
10 | Those who persecute the believing men and women and then do not repent will certainly suffer the punishment of Hell and the torment of burning. | The specific story of the ditch now broadens into a general, timeless warning in verse ten: “Those who persecute the believing men and women and then do not repent will certainly suffer the punishment of Hell and the torment of burning.” The Arabic inna alladhīna fatanū al-mu’minīna wa al-mu’mināti thumma lam yatūbū falahum ‘adhābu jahannama wa lahum ‘adhābu al-ḥarīq uses the word fatanū, which means they persecuted, tested, tortured, or tried to force the believers to abandon their faith. This word is from the same root as fitnah, which refers to a trial or a severe affliction designed to make someone renounce their religion. The verse includes both men and women, highlighting that the persecution was not limited by gender; women believers were often targeted with specific cruelty. The phrase “and then do not repent” opens a door of mercy even for the worst of oppressors. If the makers of the ditch, or any persecutor, were to sincerely repent and amend their ways, Allah’s forgiveness is vast. The surah has already described Allah as the Praiseworthy and will later describe Him as the All-Forgiving, All-Loving. However, for those who do not repent, the punishment is certain and twofold: the “punishment of Hell” (adhābu jahannam), which is the general torment of the Fire, and specifically the “torment of burning” (adhābu al-ḥarīq). This is a precise recompense for their crime. They burned the believers alive, so they will be burned in a fire that is far more intense and eternal. The word ḥarīq indicates a raging, all-consuming fire. It is the exact fitting punishment for those who sat around a fire pit and enjoyed the burning of the innocent. The verse serves as a stark warning to the Meccans who were torturing the early Muslims, such as Bilāl, ‘Ammār, and his parents Yāsir and Sumayyah, who were killed. It tells them that their fitnah is seen, and if they do not repent, they face the fire of Hell and the torment of burning. For the believers, the verse is a promise of vindication. The suffering they endure is not ignored; it is being banked as a debt that the oppressors will pay in the most severe coin.
11 | Surely those who believe and do good will have Gardens under which rivers flow. That is the greatest triumph. | In perfect balance to the warning, verse eleven provides the glad tidings for the faithful: “Surely those who believe and do good will have Gardens under which rivers flow. That is the greatest triumph.” The Arabic inna alladhīna āmanū wa ‘amilū al-ṣāliḥāti lahum jannātun tajrī min taḥtihā al-anhāru dhālika al-fawzu al-kabīr is a common Qur’ānic formula, but placed here it is extraordinarily powerful. The believers who were burned in the ditch, and all believers who endure persecution, are promised Gardens. The image of “Gardens under which rivers flow” is one of coolness, shade, and everlasting refreshment—the direct opposite of the fire pit. The fire pit was a trench of fuel and flame; the Gardens are valleys of trees with rivers running beneath them. The tyrants sat around a fire; the believers will recline on canopied couches, looking at rivers of water, milk, honey, and wine. The phrase “the greatest triumph” (al-fawzu al-kabīr) emphasises that this reward is the ultimate success, far surpassing any worldly victory. The tyrants thought they had triumphed by exterminating a group of believers, but the real victory belongs to those who kept their faith and entered the Gardens. The words “believe and do good” remind us that the believers in the ditch were not passive; their very act of remaining steadfast in the fire was the greatest righteous deed. For the persecuted Muslims of Mecca, this verse was a lifeline of hope. The dunyā offered them pain and mockery, but the Hereafter offered them eternal bliss. The contrast between the two destinies could not be starker. The tyrants are warned of burning; the believers are promised flowing rivers. This verse also teaches that the “greatest triumph” is not the defeat of one’s enemies in this world, but the attainment of Allah’s pleasure and the eternal Gardens. It redirects the human desire for victory from the temporary to the everlasting. The believer who is oppressed can find peace in knowing that the final judgment has already been declared: the oppressors face Hell, and the patient believers face Paradise. That is the true triumph, a triumph that the flames of the ditch could not touch, and that the mockery of the Quraysh could not diminish.
12 | Indeed, the ˹crushing˺ grip of your Lord is severe. | With the historical example given, the sūrah turns directly to the Meccan pagans in verse twelve: “Indeed, the crushing grip of your Lord is severe.” The Arabic inna baṭsha Rabbika la-shadīd uses the word baṭsh, which means a grip, a seizure, a sudden and violent punishment. It carries the image of a mighty hand seizing someone with overwhelming force. The word is linked to the root meaning of striking down or pouncing upon prey. The “grip” of the Lord is not a gentle tap; it is a crushing, devastating seizure. The use of “your Lord” (Rabbika) addressing the Prophet (ﷺ) serves as a warning to the disbelievers: the Lord who cares for the Prophet and the believers is the same Lord whose grip is severe for the oppressors. The adjective shadīd intensifies this to an extreme degree. The verse is a direct follow-up to the story of the People of the Ditch. The tyrants who burned the believers experienced that crushing grip in this world, as historical records indicate that Dhū Nuwās and his kingdom were soon after defeated and destroyed by the Abyssinians. But the verse extends the warning beyond a single historical event. It tells the Quraysh that the same Lord who seized the makers of the ditch can and will seize them if they persist in their persecution and denial. The verse is also a comfort to the believers. It assures them that the power of their enemies is not absolute. The crushing grip of the Lord is severe, and it will fall upon the oppressors either in this world, in the grave, or on the Day of Judgment. No tyrant can escape that grip. The mention of “your Lord” personalises the relationship; the Prophet’s Lord is the One who will defend him and his followers. The verse instils a healthy fear of Allah’s punishment, a fear that leads to repentance and reform. It also reminds us that divine justice is not always instant, but it is certain and it is severe. The severity of the grip is proportionate to the enormity of the crime and the majesty of the One whose commands were violated. The following verses will balance this severity with the attributes of forgiveness and love, painting the complete picture of the Lord.
13 | ˹For˺ He is certainly the One Who originates and resurrects ˹all˺. | The severity of the divine grip is rooted in the absolute power of Allah, as explained in verse thirteen: “For He is certainly the One Who originates and resurrects all.” The Arabic innahu huwa yubdi’u wa yu‘īd uses the emphatic pronouns innahu and huwa, making it “Indeed He, He it is Who originates and repeats.” The verb yubdi’u means He begins, He originates, He brings into existence without any prior model. The verb yu‘īd means He brings back, He repeats, He resurrects. The two actions encompass the entire scope of creation and recreation. Allah initiated the creation of the heavens, the earth, and every living being from nothing, and He will just as easily bring them back to life after death for the judgment. This is the ultimate argument against those who deny the resurrection. The One who originated the magnificent sky with its constellations, who created the tyrants and the believers from a drop of sperm, is not incapable of resurrecting them. The verse also provides the foundation for divine justice. If there were no resurrection, the oppression of the People of the Ditch would be an unresolved tragedy, a crime without a day in court. But the One who originates and resurrects will certainly establish the court. The pairing of “originates” and “resurrects” also carries a moral implication: as He brought you into being the first time, He can bring you back to hold you accountable. This is a powerful response to the Meccan pagans who marveled at the idea of bones and dust being revived. The verse tells them that the initial creation, which they witness in the birth of every child and the growth of every plant, is a continuous proof of the possibility of the second creation. The mention of this attribute right after the warning of the severe grip reinforces that the grip is not an empty threat; it is backed by the power to create and recreate. For the believer, this verse deepens trust in Allah’s promise. The same Lord who originated them will resurrect them to receive the Gardens under which rivers flow. The ultimate power of origination and resurrection belongs to Him alone.
14 | And He is the All-Forgiving, All-Loving— | After the stern warning of the severe grip and the declaration of absolute power, the sūrah reveals the beautiful, merciful attributes of the Lord in verse fourteen: “And He is the All-Forgiving, All-Loving.” The Arabic wa huwa al-Ghafūru al-Wadūd uses two intensive names of Allah. Al-Ghafūr is the Oft-Forgiving, the One who repeatedly and abundantly covers sins and grants forgiveness. Al-Wadūd is the All-Loving, the One who is full of love, who loves His righteous servants and bestows love upon them. The juxtaposition of the severe grip with the All-Forgiving, All-Loving is one of the most beautiful aspects of Islamic theology. Allah’s punishment is severe, but His mercy and love are the foundational attributes. He does not punish out of a love for cruelty; He punishes because of His justice, and even then, His forgiveness is vast for those who repent. The name al-Wadūd indicates that Allah’s relationship with the believers is not one of a cold, distant judge; it is a relationship of love. He loves those who believe and do good, and He places love for them in the hearts of others. This love was manifested in the steadfastness of the People of the Ditch; their love for Allah was stronger than their fear of the fire. And in return, Allah loved them and granted them the greatest triumph. The verse also holds out an invitation to the oppressors. No matter how severe their crimes, if they turn in repentance, they will find their Lord All-Forgiving, All-Loving. The door of mercy is not closed, even to the makers of the ditch, had they repented. This is a profound message for the Meccan pagans who were persecuting the Muslims. Even now, if they cease and repent, the All-Forgiving, All-Loving will accept them. The combination of severity and love creates a perfect balance, preventing the believer from either despairing of Allah’s mercy or feeling secure from His punishment. The love of al-Wadūd is the greatest reward, greater than the Gardens and rivers. It is the intimate connection between the Lord and His servants, a connection that the fire of the ditch could not sever. The verse reassures every believer that their Lord is not an indifferent Creator; He is a loving, forgiving Lord who is intimately aware of their struggles.
15 | Lord of the Throne, the All-Glorious, | The description of Allah’s majesty continues in verse fifteen: “Lord of the Throne, the All-Glorious.” The Arabic dhū al-‘arshi al-majīd (or al-majīd as an attribute of Allah, and some readings have al-‘arshi al-majīd, meaning the Glorious Throne, but most tafsirs accept both meanings; the translation here takes al-majīd as describing Allah) uses the title “Lord of the Throne” to emphasize the absolute sovereignty and supreme authority of Allah. The Throne (al-‘Arsh) is the greatest of all created things, the symbol of divine kingship, encompassing the entire cosmos. The Lord of the Throne is the One who sits in complete control over all existence. The mention of the Throne right after the All-Forgiving, All-Loving is particularly striking. It shows that the love and forgiveness are not signs of weakness; they are the prerogatives of the absolute Sovereign. The King of all kings can forgive whom He wills and love whom He wills, and no one can question His authority. The word al-Majīd means the All-Glorious, the One who is inherently majestic, noble, and magnificent. His glory is intrinsic, not granted by anyone. This attribute reinforces the greatness of Allah and the insignificance of the earthly tyrants. The Lord of the Throne, the All-Glorious, is far above the petty kings who dug ditches and sat around fires. Their glory was fake and temporary; His glory is real and eternal. The verse also connects to the earlier mention of the sky full of constellations. The constellations are part of the heavens, and the Throne is above the heavens. The oath by the sky was a step in a ladder that leads upward to the Lord of the Throne, the All-Glorious. For the believer, this verse inspires awe and love simultaneously. The Lord of the Throne is the same All-Loving Lord who is close to His servants. The vastness of His majesty does not diminish His intimate care. The mention of the Throne also reminds the believer that the ultimate power in the universe is not held by the oppressor on his earthly seat, but by the One on the Heavenly Throne. This perspective empowers the believer to endure hardship, knowing that the All-Glorious Lord of the Throne is their guardian. The verse is a peak of Quranic rhetoric, combining majesty and mercy in a single, breathtaking line.
16 | Doer of whatever He wills. | The description of divine attributes concludes with the sweeping statement of verse sixteen: “Doer of whatever He wills.” The Arabic fa‘‘ālun li-mā yurīd uses the intensive form fa‘‘āl, meaning the One who constantly, easily, and perfectly does whatever He intends. There is no barrier, no obstacle, and no check on His will. Whatever He decides comes into being without delay and without effort. This verse serves as the summary of all the preceding attributes. Because He is the Originator and Resurrector, the All-Forgiving, All-Loving, Lord of the Throne, the All-Glorious, He is therefore the Doer of whatever He wills. His will is absolute and encompasses everything. The tyrants who thought they were doing what they willed by burning the believers were, in reality, only acting within the limits of His will. Their fire could only burn because He allowed it. The believers’ steadfastness was also by His will. The punishment of the oppressors and the reward of the faithful are both expressions of His will. The verse demolishes any notion of human autonomy apart from Allah. It also provides immense comfort to the believer. The Doer of whatever He wills has willed that those who believe and do good will have gardens, and that the persecutors who do not repent will suffer Hell. No power in the universe can alter that will. For the oppressed, this is a guarantee; for the oppressor, it is an inescapable sentence. The phrase also addresses the Meccans’ objections about the timing of punishment. They asked why they were not being destroyed immediately. The answer is that He is the Doer of whatever He wills, and He wills to give them time, or to test the believers, or to bring about other wisdoms. His will is not subject to human demands. The verse thus instills a deep sense of submission and trust in the believer. We may not understand the why and when of events, but we know that the Doer of whatever He wills is also the All-Loving and All-Forgiving, so His will is ultimately for the best. The verse stands as a majestic conclusion to the theological section, before the sūrah returns to the historical warnings in the next verses.
17 | Has the story of the ˹destroyed˺ forces reached you ˹O Prophet˺— | After establishing the attributes of Allah, the sūrah returns to warning the disbelievers by reminding them of past destroyed nations. Verse seventeen asks: “Has the story of the destroyed forces reached you O Prophet?” The Arabic hal atāka ḥadīthu al-junūd is a rhetorical question addressed to the Prophet (ﷺ), but its target is the Meccan disbelievers. The word ḥadīth means narrative, story, account. The word junūd is the plural of jund, meaning soldiers, forces, armies, hosts. So the question is: Has the news, the historical account, of the mighty armies and forces of the past reached you? The Prophet and the Quraysh were certainly aware of the stories of the Pharaoh and Thamūd, either through revelation or through the oral traditions of Arabia. The question serves to remind the disbelievers that their own knowledge of history testifies to the fate of tyrants. These were not weak, primitive tribes; they were powerful civilisations with massive armies, and yet they were utterly destroyed when they defied Allah. The rhetorical question carries a tone of warning and reflection. The disbelievers who walk among the ruins of these ancient peoples should ask themselves what happened to those mighty hosts. The verse implicitly connects the forces of the past to the armies and clans of Quraysh. The Meccans took pride in their military strength and their trading caravans guarded by armed men. The verse tells them: your forces are nothing compared to the forces of Pharaoh and Thamūd, and look at what became of them. For the believers, this verse is a reminder that the struggle between truth and falsehood is as old as humanity. The prophets were always confronted by powerful armies, and those armies were always defeated by the divine punishment. The outcome is never determined by material strength but by the will of the Doer of whatever He wills. The question also invites the listener to seek knowledge and reflect on history as a source of moral and spiritual lessons. The forces that were destroyed are not just stories; they are signs of the severe grip of the Lord. The verse sets up the specific examples in the next verse, making the warning concrete and undeniable.
18 | ˹the forces of˺ Pharaoh and Thamûd? | The question of the previous verse is answered with two prominent examples in verse eighteen: “the forces of Pharaoh and Thamūd?” The Arabic Fir‘awna wa Thamūd names two of the most notorious tyrants and their peoples mentioned in the Qur’ān. Fir‘awn, or Pharaoh, was the king of Egypt who claimed divinity for himself, saying “I am your lord, most high,” and who oppressed the Children of Israel, slaughtering their sons and enslaving them. He pursued the Prophet Mūsā and his followers with his great army, only to be drowned in the Red Sea. His body was preserved as a sign for future generations. Thamūd was an ancient Arabian tribe, successors to ‘Ād, who were given great skill in carving homes out of mountains. They were sent the Prophet Ṣāliḥ, and as a sign, a miraculous she-camel emerged from a rock. They defied the warning, hamstrung the camel, and challenged Ṣāliḥ to bring the punishment. They were destroyed by a devastating earthquake or a thunderous blast. Both of these peoples were wealthy, powerful, and technologically advanced. Pharaoh had his pyramids and his mighty army; Thamūd had their rock-hewn palaces and sophisticated engineering. Yet when the divine command came, their strength was utterly useless. By mentioning them right after the People of the Ditch, the sūrah creates a continuous line of tyrants who met the same end. The Meccans passed by the dwellings of Thamūd on their trade journeys to Syria, and they knew the stories of Pharaoh. The verse forces them to recognise that their own power is nothing compared to these great civilisations, and if those could be wiped out, so could they. For the believers, the mention of Pharaoh and Thamūd serves as a reinforcement of the pattern: the oppressors are given time, but the grip of the Lord is severe and eventually seizes them. It is also a reminder that the prophets before Muḥammad faced the same challenges—mockery, persecution, powerful enemies—and yet the truth ultimately prevailed. The verse connects the Meccan struggle to a larger divine narrative, giving the believers patience and perspective.
19 | Yet the disbelievers ˹still˺ persist in denial. | Despite all these clear signs and warnings, the sūrah notes the frustrating reality in verse nineteen: “Yet the disbelievers still persist in denial.” The Arabic bali alladhīna kafarū fī takdhīb uses the particle bal, which indicates a correction or a striking contrast. The meaning is: even after hearing the story of the People of the Ditch, the severe grip of the Lord, His attributes, the fate of Pharaoh and Thamūd, the disbelievers do not take heed. Instead, they persist in their denial. The word fī takdhīb, literally “in denial,” uses a nominal construction that suggests they are immersed, enveloped, and entrenched in their state of calling the truth a lie. It is not a momentary hesitation but a continuous, enveloping condition. The verse expresses a tone of amazement and lament. How can a rational being witness the destruction of such mighty powers, hear the vivid descriptions of Hell and Paradise, and still persist in calling it a lie? The answer lies in the spiritual sickness described in earlier surahs: their hearts are stained, they are arrogant, and they are unwilling to give up their worldly status and desires. The verse is a diagnosis of the Meccan elite’s response. They were not merely ignorant; they were actively engaged in a campaign of denial, spreading mockery and trying to suppress the message. The verse also serves as a comfort to the Prophet (ﷺ). It tells him that his experience of persistent denial is not a sign of his failure; it is a recurring pattern in human history. The forces of falsehood always persist in denial until the moment of their destruction. The believer, therefore, should not lose heart when the truth is rejected. The persistence of the disbelievers is their own choice, and it will be their own ruin. The verse also acts as a final, implicit invitation: do not be among those who persist in denial. The door of repentance is still open, and the All-Forgiving, All-Loving is ready to receive those who turn. The persistent denial, however, leads directly to being encompassed by Allah, as the next verse describes.
20 | But Allah encompasses them from all sides. | The sūrah closes the warning section with a powerful statement of divine encirclement in verse twenty: “But Allah encompasses them from all sides.” The Arabic wa Allāhu min warā’ihim muḥīṭ uses the word muḥīṭ, from the root aḥāṭa, meaning to surround, to encompass, to encircle completely. The phrase “from all sides” (min warā’ihim, literally from behind them, but idiomatically meaning from all directions) indicates that there is no escape, no gap, no place of safety outside of Allah’s control. The disbelievers may persist in denial, thinking they are free and unaccountable, but the reality is that they are completely surrounded by the knowledge, power, and will of Allah. He encompasses them just as the sky encompasses the earth, just as the womb encompasses the foetus. They are within His grip, whether they realise it or not. This verse is a terrifying image for the oppressor. The tyrants who built the ditch thought they were in control, but Allah encompassed them, and their destruction came from where they did not perceive. The Meccan pagans who plot and mock are not hidden; they are surrounded. The verse also carries a note of comfort for the believers. The same encompassing that confines the oppressors is a protective sphere for the righteous. The believers are under the encompassment of the All-Loving, the All-Glorious. They cannot be touched by any harm except by His will. The word muḥīṭ is also one of the names or attributes of Allah, indicating His comprehensive knowledge and power. Nothing escapes His encompassing. The verse thus serves as the perfect conclusion to the argument. The disbelievers deny, but their denial does not create an escape. The grip of the Lord is severe, and He surrounds them on all sides. The only way out is to turn to Him in repentance and faith, thus transforming the encompassing from a prison of wrath into a circle of love and protection. The verse leaves the listener with a profound sense of the majesty and immanence of Allah, a fitting note before the final verses that glorify the Qur’ān itself as the ultimate guiding light within this divine encompassment.
21 | In fact, this is a glorious Quran, | After describing the encompassing power of Allah and the persistent denial of the disbelievers, the sūrah shifts focus to the very source of guidance that they are rejecting: “In fact, this is a glorious Quran.” The Arabic bal huwa qur’ānun majīd uses the particle bal to correct and elevate. The disbelievers may think the revelation is poetry, magic, or ancient fables, but the truth is that it is a Qur’ān of immense glory. The word majīd is the same attribute used for Allah in verse fifteen, “the All-Glorious,” and for the Throne in some readings. By describing the Qur’ān as majīd, the verse connects the book directly to its divine source. The Qur’ān is glorious in its origin, glorious in its content, glorious in its style, and glorious in its impact. It is a book of majesty, nobility, and sublime honour. No human speech can match it. The Meccans who called it “ancient fables” were insulting not just words but a revelation that shares in the glory of the Lord of the Throne. The verse is a powerful refutation of their mockery. It tells them that what they are turning away from is not a minor thing; it is a glorious Quran, a book that would elevate them if they honoured it. The description also serves as a comfort to the Prophet (ﷺ) and the believers. The persecution they face is for the sake of this glorious Quran, and its glory will ultimately triumph. The word qur’ān itself means a recitation, something to be read and proclaimed. The adjective majīd assures the believers that every verse they recite is majestic and worthy of any sacrifice. The verse also implicitly contrasts the transient glory of the tyrants with the eternal glory of the Qur’ān. The palaces of Pharaoh, the rock-hewn homes of Thamūd, and the ditches of the Himyarite king have all crumbled to dust, but the glorious Quran remains, recited by millions across the ages. The glory of the Qur’ān is not material; it is a spiritual and intellectual glory that outlasts all earthly power. The verse thus re-centres the discussion on the revelation itself, preparing for the final verse that reveals its celestial preservation.
22 | ˹recorded˺ in a Preserved Tablet. | The sūrah concludes with a majestic statement about the Qur’ān’s heavenly origin and protection: “recorded in a Preserved Tablet.” The Arabic fī lawḥin maḥfūẓ is a prepositional phrase describing the location and status of the glorious Qur’ān. The word lawḥ means a tablet, a board, or a flat surface on which writing is inscribed. Al-lawḥ al-maḥfūẓ is the Preserved Tablet, a celestial record that exists in the highest realm, directly under the knowledge and care of Allah. It is the source of all divine decrees and scriptures. The Qur’ān, before being sent down to the lowest heaven and then revealed piecemeal to the Prophet (ﷺ), was inscribed on this Tablet. The word maḥfūẓ means preserved, protected, guarded. It is safeguarded from any alteration, corruption, loss, or satanic interference. This preservation is absolute. The disbelievers’ attempts to mock, distort, or extinguish the Qur’ān are utterly futile because the original is preserved with the Lord of the Throne. This verse is the ultimate answer to those who said the Qur’ān was a fabrication or that it could be rivaled. A book that is protected in the Preserved Tablet cannot be falsified. The verse also ties back to the opening of the sūrah, which swore by the sky full of constellations. The sky is a visible book of stars; the Preserved Tablet is the unseen book of divine decree. Both are creations of Allah, but the Preserved Tablet is higher and more protected. The mention of the Preserved Tablet also connects to the earlier theme of witnessing. The deeds of the oppressors and the steadfastness of the believers are all recorded in a divine record that is perfectly preserved. No detail is lost. The sūrah thus ends on a note of immense hope and security for the believers. The Qur’ān they hold, the words they recite, are a transcript from a Tablet that is with Allah, guarded from any change. This gives the believer absolute confidence in the authenticity and authority of the revelation. The Preserved Tablet also signifies that the fate of the tyrants and the reward of the righteous are already written in the eternal decree, and nothing can alter that final, divine script.
