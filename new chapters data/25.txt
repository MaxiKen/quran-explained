1 | Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant, so that he may be a warner to the whole world. | The sûrah opens with the declaration of blessing: "Blessed is the One Who sent down the Standard to His servant." The word tabārak, translated as "Blessed," is a divine attribute that belongs exclusively to Allah. It conveys the idea of abundant, perpetual, and ever-increasing goodness, and it declares that every blessing in existence flows from Him alone. No created being is ever described with this word, for it is the unique mark of the One Whose goodness is limitless and eternal. The verse immediately fixes the believer's gaze upon the source of all good before any other theme is introduced. The "Standard" (al-Furqān) is one of the most significant names of the Qur'an. It means the criterion, the decisive measure that separates truth from falsehood, guidance from error, and light from darkness. The Qur'an is called the Standard because it is the yardstick against which every claim, every belief, and every action is to be measured. In a world of competing voices and conflicting desires, the Qur'an provides the firm and unshakeable standard by which the sincere person distinguishes right from wrong. The choice of this name for the sûrah highlights the central theme of the chapter: the contrast between those who accept the truth and those who reject it. The verse then tells us that this Standard was sent down "to His servant," referring to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The word "servant" is itself an honour, for there is no station more noble than being a true servant of Allah. It is a reminder that the Prophet, despite his immense rank, was a servant first and foremost, and that his mission was to call all of humanity to the worship of their Lord. The purpose of the revelation is then stated: "so that he may be a warner to the whole world." The message of the Prophet was not confined to his own people or his own time; it is a universal warning addressed to every human being until the end of time. This opening verse thus establishes the divine origin of the Qur'an, the role of the Prophet, and the universal scope of his mission.
2 | ˹Allah is˺ the One to Whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth, Who has never had ˹any˺ offspring, nor does He have a partner in ˹governing˺ the kingdom. He has created everything, ordaining it precisely. | Having declared the blessing of the One Who revealed the Standard, the verse now describes His absolute sovereignty: "˹Allah is˺ the One to Whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth." This is the foundation of everything that follows. Because Allah alone owns the entire universe, He alone has the right to command, to prohibit, to reward, and to punish. No one shares in His dominion, and no one can claim any authority over any part of His creation except by His permission. The believer who internalises this truth lives in constant awareness that they are but a servant in the vast kingdom of their Lord. The verse then addresses the greatest of all falsehoods — the claim that Allah has a child: "Who has never had ˹any˺ offspring, nor does He have a partner in ˹governing˺ the kingdom." This is a direct refutation of the beliefs of those who attributed sons or partners to Allah. The idea of divine offspring is incompatible with the very concept of Allah, for a child implies need, succession, and limitation, while Allah is the Self-Sufficient, the Eternal, the One Who has no equal. Likewise, the notion of a partner in governing contradicts His absolute unity, for a shared kingdom would inevitably lead to conflict and discord. The Qur'an elsewhere declares that if there were other gods besides Allah, the heavens and the earth would have been corrupted. Finally, the verse proclaims: "He has created everything, ordaining it precisely." This affirms both the comprehensiveness of Allah's creation and the perfection of His decree. Nothing exists except by His creation, and nothing happens except by His precise ordination. The word taqdīr, ordaining, carries the sense of measuring and apportioning everything with exact wisdom — the size of the heavens, the orbit of the planets, the span of a life, and the provision of every creature. This is a source of immense comfort for the believer, for it assures them that they live not in a world of random chaos but within a perfectly ordered plan governed by an All-Wise Creator.
3 | Yet they have taken besides Him gods who cannot create anything but are themselves created. Nor can they protect or benefit themselves. Nor can they control life, death, or resurrection. | This verse exposes the folly of idolatry by describing the utter helplessness of the false gods that people worship besides Allah: "Yet they have taken besides Him gods who cannot create anything but are themselves created." The irony is devastating. Those who worship idols, false deities, or human figures have reversed the natural order of things. They worship beings who are themselves created, who had a beginning and will have an end, and who possess no power of their own. How can a created thing, itself dependent on a creator, ever deserve worship? The verse then enumerates three specific areas of helplessness that demonstrate why these false gods are unworthy of worship: "Nor can they protect or benefit themselves. Nor can they control life, death, or resurrection." First, they cannot bring themselves any benefit or ward off any harm. An idol cannot heal itself if broken, cannot save itself if thrown into a fire, and cannot move or speak. Second, they have no control whatsoever over life and death. They cannot give life to a single being, nor can they delay the death of anyone, nor can they resurrect the dead. These are the exclusive prerogatives of the Creator, the One Who gives life, causes death, and will raise all of creation for judgment. The verse is a powerful argument for pure monotheism. It invites the human intellect to reflect: why worship something that cannot create, cannot protect itself, and cannot control the most fundamental realities of existence? True worship belongs only to the One Who possesses all power and all authority. The believer is reminded that turning to anyone or anything besides Allah — whether an idol, a saint, a desire, or one's own ego — is to entrust oneself to that which is utterly incapable of helping. The verse thus calls the heart back to the worship of Allah alone, the only true God, the only true protector, and the only One Who holds the keys of life, death, and resurrection.
4 | The disbelievers say, "This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others." Their claim is totally unjustified and untrue. | This verse records the accusation that the disbelievers of Mecca levelled against the Prophet: "The disbelievers say, 'This ˹Quran˺ is nothing but a fabrication which he made up with the help of others.'" Unable to deny the eloquence, power, and truth of the Qur'an, they resorted to the claim that the Prophet had invented it, and that he had received assistance from certain people. Historically, they pointed to individuals from among the People of the Book, or to a Christian servant, or to poets and storytellers, alleging that these had helped him compose the Qur'an. The accusation was as absurd then as it is now, for the Qur'an's knowledge of previous scriptures, its prophecies, and its unparalleled style could not have come from any human source. The verse then delivers the verdict on this accusation: "Their claim is totally unjustified and untrue." The word translated as "unjustified" (ẓulm) literally means wrongdoing or injustice, indicating that their accusation was not merely mistaken but was itself an act of oppression — an oppression of the truth and of the one who brought it. The word "untrue" (zūr) refers to falsehood and fabrication, the very crime they were attributing to the Prophet. In a beautiful turn of justice, the Qur'an declares that it is they who are the fabricators, and that their claim is itself the lie they accused him of. This verse teaches the believer how falsehood operates: when people cannot refute the truth, they attack its source. The disbelievers could not produce a chapter like the Qur'an, so they attacked the character of the one who brought it. The believer should neither be surprised nor discouraged by such attacks, for every prophet faced them. The verse also reassures the believer of the Qur'an's divine origin, for a book of such truth and majesty could never be the product of human fabrication, and its survival and consistency across fourteen centuries stand as an enduring testimony against every accusation.
5 | And they say, "˹These revelations are only˺ ancient fables which he has had written down, and they are rehearsed to him morning and evening." | This verse records a second accusation made by the disbelievers: "And they say, '˹These revelations are only˺ ancient fables which he has had written down, and they are rehearsed to him morning and evening.'" This accusation was meant to explain away the Qur'an by claiming that its stories of previous nations, its warnings, and its teachings were nothing new, but merely old tales that the Prophet had copied from earlier sources and had others write down for him, and which were then recited to him regularly so that he could memorise them and present them as revelation. The accusation attempted to reduce the Qur'an to a collection of borrowed legends. The accusation is self-contradictory and was easily refuted. First, the Prophet was known to be unlettered — he could neither read nor write — so the claim that he had things "written down" and then read them was baseless. Second, the Qur'an was revealed gradually over twenty-three years, in response to unfolding events, with verses that addressed the very accusations being made against it. A collection of copied fables could not have answered its critics in real time with such precision. Third, the Qur'an contains knowledge — historical, scientific, and prophetic — that was unknown to the people of the time and could not have been gathered from any available source. This verse reveals the desperation of those who reject the truth. They grasp at one explanation after another — first fabrication, now borrowed fables — each as weak as the last. It also highlights a profound truth: the Qur'an's message is not an innovation but a renewal of the eternal truth that all prophets brought. Its stories of past nations are not "fables" but true accounts, preserved by the One Who witnessed all of history, and presented as lessons for humanity. The believer receives these accounts not as entertainment but as divine instruction, learning from the rise and fall of those who came before.
6 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "This ˹Quran˺ has been revealed by the One Who knows the secrets of the heavens and the earth. Surely He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful." | This verse gives the decisive answer to the accusations of the previous two verses: "Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'This ˹Quran˺ has been revealed by the One Who knows the secrets of the heavens and the earth.'" The Prophet is instructed to respond not by defending himself but by pointing to the divine origin of the Qur'an. The One Who revealed it is the One Who possesses complete knowledge of everything hidden in the heavens and the earth — every secret, every unseen reality, every past event, and every future occurrence. Such a Book, coming from such a source, cannot be a fabrication or a collection of fables, for its knowledge transcends all human knowledge. The argument is powerful in its simplicity. The Qur'an speaks of the unseen — of the angels, of the Hereafter, of the hidden realities of creation — with a certainty and precision that no human being could possess. It recounts the stories of past nations with details that were not known to the people of Arabia. It foretells future events that later came to pass exactly as described. All of this points to a source of knowledge that is beyond human reach, namely the All-Knowing Creator. The accusation of fabrication collapses in the face of this reality. The verse then concludes with a reassurance and an invitation: "Surely He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful." Even as the Qur'an refutes the disbelievers, it does not close the door on them. The mention of Allah's forgiveness and mercy here is a gentle call to those who had accused the Prophet, inviting them to repent and be forgiven. It teaches that the purpose of refuting falsehood is not to condemn but to guide, and that the mercy of Allah remains open even to those who have wronged His Messenger. The believer is reminded that the Qur'an is a message of mercy as much as a message of warning.
7 | And they say ˹mockingly˺, "What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner | This verse records yet another objection raised by the disbelievers: "And they say ˹mockingly˺, 'What kind of messenger is this who eats food and goes about in market-places ˹for a living˺? If only an angel had been sent down with him to be his co-warner!'" Their objection was that a true messenger should not be an ordinary human being who eats, drinks, and walks in the markets like everyone else. In their minds, a messenger should be something extraordinary — an angel, or a person accompanied by an angel — not a man who shares in the daily life of ordinary people. This objection was rooted not in reason but in arrogance, for it presumed that Allah could not choose a human being as His messenger. The Qur'an answers this objection throughout its pages. The prophets were sent as human beings precisely so that they could serve as living examples for humanity. An angel could not be a model for human beings, for the angel does not experience hunger, desire, temptation, or the struggles of daily life. A human messenger, on the other hand, demonstrates that it is possible to live a life of complete devotion to Allah amid the ordinary realities of eating, working, and interacting with others. His humanity is not a defect but a mercy, for it makes the path of righteousness attainable and imitable. The verse also exposes a deeper issue: those who reject a human messenger are often rejecting the very idea of accountability. They want a messenger who is so distant and extraordinary that his message can be dismissed as irrelevant to their own lives. But a messenger who eats and walks among them brings a message that speaks directly to their condition. The believer is reminded to look past the surface and to recognise the wisdom of Allah in sending human messengers, and to beware of the arrogance that demands signs on one's own terms rather than accepting the guidance Allah has sent.
8 | or a treasure had been cast down to him, or he had had a garden from which he may eat!" And the wrongdoers say ˹to the believers˺, "You are only following a bewitched man." | This verse continues the disbelievers' list of demands: "or a treasure had been cast down to him, or he had had a garden from which he may eat!" Having objected that the Prophet was merely human, they now demanded that he be accompanied by miraculous wealth — a treasure sent down from heaven, or a garden that would provide for him — as a condition of accepting him. They imagined that a true messenger should be wealthy and free from the need to work, and that his material prosperity would be proof of his truthfulness. This demand reveals their fundamental misunderstanding of what a messenger is, for prophethood is not measured by wealth but by truth. The verse then records their conclusion: "And the wrongdoers say ˹to the believers˺, 'You are only following a bewitched man.'" Having exhausted their demands, they turned to mocking both the Prophet and his followers, accusing him of being bewitched or under a spell, and dismissing the believers as deluded for following him. The word "wrongdoers" (ẓālimūn) is significant, for it identifies the root of their problem: their rejection was not based on genuine doubt but on wrongdoing — the arrogance, injustice, and self-interest that made them unwilling to accept a truth that would require them to change their lives. This verse teaches an enduring lesson about the nature of denial. Those who do not want to accept the truth will always find new demands and new objections, no matter how much evidence they are given. Their demands for treasure and gardens were not sincere conditions but excuses, as shown by their final resort to mockery and accusation. The believer is reminded not to be shaken by such objections, for they stem not from a lack of evidence but from a refusal to submit. The truth stands on its own, and no amount of mockery can diminish it.
9 | See ˹O Prophet˺ how they call you names! So they have gone so ˹far˺ astray that they cannot find the ˹Right˺ Way. | This verse addresses the Prophet directly, drawing his attention to the absurdity of the accusations against him: "See ˹O Prophet˺ how they call you names!" The word translated as "call you names" (amthāl) refers to the parables, comparisons, and labels that the disbelievers applied to the Prophet — calling him a poet, a soothsayer, a madman, and a bewitched man. They could not agree on a single description because none of them fit, and each accusation was as empty as the last. The verse invites the Prophet to reflect on the sheer inconsistency and desperation of their attacks. The verse then diagnoses the result of their condition: "So they have gone so ˹far˺ astray that they cannot find the ˹Right˺ Way." Their misguidance was not a matter of minor error but of complete deviation. The more they attacked the truth, the further they wandered from it, until they reached a state in which they could no longer even perceive the right path. This is the tragic consequence of persistent rejection: the heart that turns away from the truth repeatedly becomes hardened, and the capacity to recognise guidance is gradually lost. Their straying was so complete that returning became impossible without a complete change of heart. The verse is both a consolation to the Prophet and a warning to every reader. To the Prophet, it assures him that the insults of his opponents are a sign not of his weakness but of their own confusion, for truth is never refuted by name-calling. To the believer, it warns against the danger of letting arrogance and stubbornness close the door to guidance. The one who persists in rejecting the truth does not merely remain where they are; they sink further into error, until the path of return becomes obscured. The verse thus calls for sincerity and humility in the search for truth, and for gratitude to Allah for the guidance of the Qur'an.
10 | Blessed is the One Who—if He wills—can give you far better than ˹all˺ that: gardens under which rivers flow, and palaces as well. | After refuting the disbelievers' demands for treasure and gardens, this verse declares: "Blessed is the One Who—if He wills—can give you far better than ˹all˺ that: gardens under which rivers flow, and palaces as well." The verse redirects the Prophet's attention, and the attention of the believers, from the petty demands of the disbelievers to the boundless generosity of Allah. The very things they demanded as proof — treasure and gardens — are nothing compared to what Allah can bestow. His power is not limited to this world, and His gifts are not confined to earthly wealth. The verse describes what Allah can give as "gardens under which rivers flow, and palaces as well." These are the delights of Paradise, the eternal gardens and lofty palaces that await the believers in the Hereafter. By mentioning them here, the verse teaches that the true reward of the Prophet and his followers is not worldly wealth but the everlasting bliss of Paradise, which is "far better" than anything the disbelievers could ever imagine or demand. The comparison is not even close, for the treasures of this world fade and perish, while the gardens and palaces of Paradise endure forever. The phrase "if He wills" is significant, for it affirms that Allah is free to bestow His blessings as He pleases, and that no demand from His creation binds Him. Yet His will is always aligned with His wisdom and mercy, and He has prepared for His righteous servants that which no eye has seen and no ear has heard. The verse is a profound consolation for the believer who faces the mockery of the world: the apparent poverty of the believers is not their true state, for they are the heirs of an eternal treasure. It turns the gaze from the fleeting to the everlasting, and fills the heart with trust in the generosity of Allah.
11 | In fact, they deny the Hour. And for the deniers of the Hour, We have prepared a blazing Fire. | This verse identifies the root cause of the disbelievers' rejection: "In fact, they deny the Hour. And for the deniers of the Hour, We have prepared a blazing Fire." All their accusations against the Prophet — their demands for treasure, their mockery, their claims of fabrication — were ultimately rooted in a deeper denial: their rejection of the Day of Judgment. It was not the Prophet they could not accept, but the message of accountability that he brought, for belief in the Hour would require them to abandon their arrogance, their injustice, and their attachment to the pleasures of this world. The verse reveals the psychology of denial. People often object to the details of the message — the messenger, the style, the timing — when their real objection is to the message itself. The disbelievers of Mecca did not want to believe in a Day when they would be held accountable, so they found endless excuses to reject the one who warned them of it. Their rejection of the Hour was not intellectual but moral, a refusal to face the consequences of their actions. The verse then declares the consequence of this denial: a blazing Fire prepared for those who deny the Hour. The word "prepared" (aʿtadnā) indicates that this punishment is not an afterthought but a determined reality, established and awaiting its appointed time. The Fire is described as "blazing" (saʿīr), emphasising its intensity and fury. The verse is a stark warning that the denial of the Hereafter does not make it disappear; it only guarantees the punishment of those who persist in it. The believer is reminded to live with the awareness of the Hour, for this awareness is the foundation of all righteousness.
12 | Once it sees them from a distance, they will hear it fuming and growling. | This verse describes the fury of the Fire as it catches sight of the deniers: "Once it sees them from a distance, they will hear it fuming and growling." The image is one of terrifying intensity. The Fire is personified as a living, breathing entity that perceives its victims from afar and responds with rage at their approach. It "fumes" (taghayyuẓ) with boiling anger and "growls" (zafīr) with a deep, roaring sound, as though it is a wild beast straining to devour its prey. The description is not metaphorical flourish but a vivid depiction of the reality of Hell, whose fury is beyond human comprehension. The verse conveys two important truths. First, the punishment of Hell is not merely physical but also psychological, for the very sight and sound of the Fire, from a distance, will fill the deniers with terror before they even reach it. The anticipation of the punishment is itself part of the punishment, and the awareness of what awaits them will multiply their dread. Second, the Fire is presented as an active agent of divine justice, not a passive place. It is as though the Fire itself is angry at those who defied their Lord, and it waits eagerly to exact the retribution they have earned. The verse also stands in stark contrast to the description of Paradise, where the believers will be greeted with peace and welcomed with honour. The same distance from which the Fire rages at the deniers is the distance from which Paradise will beckon to the righteous. The believer is invited to reflect on which of these two destinations their life is leading them towards, and to live in a way that makes their final approach one of welcome rather than one of terror. The verse is a powerful call to heed the warning while there is still time.
13 | And when they are tossed into a narrow place inside ˹Hell˺, chained together, then and there they will cry out for ˹instant˺ destruction. | This verse describes the moment of the deniers' entry into Hell: "And when they are tossed into a narrow place inside ˹Hell˺, chained together, then and there they will cry out for ˹instant˺ destruction." The word "tossed" conveys the violence and contempt with which they will be thrown into the Fire, without dignity or ceremony. The "narrow place" (makān ḍayyiq) describes a cramped, constricted space within Hell where the sinners will be crushed together, adding physical confinement to the burning torment. The image of being "chained together" further emphasises their utter helplessness and their shared fate. The verse then records their cry: "then and there they will cry out for ˹instant˺ destruction." The word thubūr refers to destruction, ruin, and perdition. In their unbearable torment, they will call out for their own destruction, wishing for an end to their suffering. This is the ultimate expression of despair — a person so overwhelmed by pain that they long for annihilation. Yet, as the next verse will show, their wish will not be granted, for the punishment of Hell is eternal. The verse teaches the believer to reflect on the reality of Hell and to seek Allah's protection from it. The cramped confinement, the chains, and the cry for destruction all paint a picture of suffering that the human mind can barely grasp, and they stand as a warning against the sins of arrogance, denial, and rebellion that lead to such a fate. At the same time, the verse highlights the mercy of Allah, who has warned His servants so clearly and given them every opportunity to avoid this end. The believer is urged to take the warning to heart and to live in a way that leads not to the narrow place of Hell but to the vast gardens of Paradise.
14 | ˹They will be told,˺ "Do not cry only once for destruction, but cry many times over!" | This verse records the response to the deniers' cry for destruction: "˹They will be told,˺ 'Do not cry only once for destruction, but cry many times over!'" The word thubūr, translated as "destruction," appears again, and the command is delivered with a bitter irony: they asked for one destruction, but they will be told to ask for many, for their suffering will not end and their wish for annihilation will never be granted. The mockery they once directed at the Prophet is now turned back upon them, as they are told to continue crying out in a futile plea that will never be answered. The verse conveys the finality and permanence of the punishment of Hell. There is no escape, no relief, and no end. The deniers will cry out in their agony, but their cries will only add to their torment, for they will be met with the cruel reminder that their destruction will not come. This is the reality of the Hereafter for those who rejected the truth in this world: an eternal state from which there is no exit, and in which the very hope of an end is denied. The verse also serves as a profound warning to the living. In this world, a person may wish for an end to their troubles, and death or relief may come. But in the Hereafter, the cry for relief from the one who rejected their Lord will be answered only with more suffering. The believer is reminded to seize the opportunity of this life, where repentance is still possible and the door of mercy is still open, before the time comes when even the wish for destruction will be denied. The verse is a call to urgency in turning to Allah while the chance remains.
15 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ "Is this better or the Garden of Eternity which the righteous have been promised, as a reward and ˹an ultimate˺ destination | This verse poses a direct question: "Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ 'Is this better or the Garden of Eternity which the righteous have been promised, as a reward and ˹an ultimate˺ destination?'" The Prophet is instructed to challenge the disbelievers with a comparison between two destinies: the blazing Fire described in the preceding verses, and the Garden of Eternity prepared for the righteous. The question is rhetorical, for the answer is obvious, yet it forces the listener to confront the choice before them. Which is better — the narrow, chained torment of Hell, or the eternal Garden of bliss? The "Garden of Eternity" (jannat al-khuld) is described as "a reward and ˹an ultimate˺ destination." It is a reward (jazā') for the righteous, granted to them by the grace of their Lord, and it is an ultimate destination (maṣīr), the final resting place to which their journey leads. The word "eternity" emphasises that this Garden, unlike the pleasures of this world, will never end and never diminish. Its inhabitants will dwell there forever, in a state of perfect and everlasting contentment, having completed their journey through this world and arrived at the home their Lord has prepared for them. The verse teaches the believer to weigh the two paths with clear eyes. The choice between the Fire and the Garden is not a distant abstraction but the most immediate and consequential decision of every life. Every action, every choice, and every turning of the heart is a step towards one destination or the other. The believer is invited to reflect on which path their daily choices are leading them towards, and to orient their life towards the Garden of Eternity, which is the reward of the righteous and the ultimate destination of those who live in the awareness of their Lord.
16 | There they will have whatever they wish for, forever. That is a promise ˹to be sought after˺, binding on your Lord." | This verse completes the description of the Garden of Eternity: "There they will have whatever they wish for, forever. That is a promise ˹to be sought after˺, binding on your Lord." The first phrase captures the essence of Paradise: complete and total fulfilment. Unlike this world, where desires are often frustrated and wishes remain unfulfilled, the inhabitants of Paradise will have whatever their hearts desire, without limitation, without delay, and without any harmful consequence. Every longing of the purified soul will be satisfied, and every joy will be complete. The verse then emphasises the certainty of this promise with the word "forever" and the description of it as "binding on your Lord." The promise of Paradise is not a hopeful wish but a divine commitment that Allah has made binding upon Himself out of His grace and generosity. The phrase "binding on your Lord" is remarkable, for it shows that Allah, in His mercy, has obligated Himself to fulfil this promise to His righteous servants. It is a promise that cannot fail, for it rests upon the One Who never breaks His word. The description of it as "to be sought after" (mas'ūlan) indicates that it is a promise worth striving for, a goal deserving of every effort. This verse is a source of profound hope and motivation for the believer. It assures them that the sacrifices, patience, and devotion of this life are not in vain, but are directed towards a reward of unimaginable completeness and certainty. The believer is encouraged to seek this promise with determination, to keep it before their eyes amid the trials of life, and to trust that the One Who has bound Himself to fulfil it will not let them down. The verse transforms the pursuit of the Hereafter from a vague hope into a confident striving towards a guaranteed destination.

On that Day, the idolaters will be gathered together with the very objects of their worship — their idols, their false gods, and their invented deities. Allah will then address these objects, exposing their helplessness and placing the blame squarely where it belongs. The question posed to the objects of worship is devastating in its implication. They are asked whether they misled the idolaters, or whether the idolaters strayed on their own. The question exposes the truth: the idols never misled anyone, for they were lifeless and powerless, unable to guide or misguide. The idolaters strayed entirely on their own, following their own desires and the whispers of Satan, while projecting their own delusions onto objects that could neither help nor harm. The scene is one of profound humiliation, as the falseness of their worship is laid bare before all of creation. The verse teaches the believer about the reality of false worship and the accountability that awaits on the Day of Judgment. Every human being is responsible for their own choices, and no excuse — whether the influence of others, the pressure of society, or the claims of false gods — will avail them on that Day. The believer is reminded to keep their worship pure for Allah alone, to avoid attaching their heart to anything besides Him, and to remember that they will stand before their Lord alone, with no one to answer for them but themselves. The verse is a call to sincerity in worship and to personal accountability.

The reward is explicitly tied to patience. This is because every quality described in the preceding verses — moderation in spending, purity from grave sins, honesty, dignity in the face of vanity, receptiveness to guidance, and devotion in prayer — requires patient, persistent effort against the inclinations of the self. Patience is the thread that binds all virtues together. It is the strength to restrain desire, to endure difficulty, to forgive others, and to remain steadfast upon the path of Allah over a lifetime. Paradise is described elsewhere as the reward of the patient, and here the highest chambers of Paradise are promised to those who embody patience in its fullest sense. The verse then describes how they will be received: with greetings and peace. The angels will greet them with salām, and they will be met with words of honor, welcome, and reassurance after the trials of this world. This greeting is the culmination of their lives of peace — lives in which they spread peace among people, avoided conflict and falsehood, and remained in a state of inner tranquility with their Lord. Now that peace is returned to them in its most perfect form, multiplied and made eternal. This verse connects the struggle of this world directly to the reward of the next. Every act of patience is an investment in that high chamber, and every difficulty endured for the sake of Allah is a brick in that lofty dwelling. The believer is thus encouraged to persevere, knowing that what awaits them is far greater than anything they have left behind.

What an excellent place to settle and reside! | This verse concludes the description of the reward of the servants of the Most Merciful by declaring that they will abide in Paradise forever, and that it is an excellent settlement and residence. The word khālidīn, abiding forever, is the ultimate promise: this bliss is not temporary, not conditional, and not subject to decline. Every joy in this world is shadowed by the fear of its ending, but the joy of Paradise is eternal, growing ever more perfect without the slightest diminution. This is the truest and most complete happiness, for it combines the perfection of the place with the permanence of the state. The phrase “excellent settlement and residence” deliberately echoes the earlier description of Hell as an “evil settlement and residence.” The Qur'an thus places the two final destinations side by side, inviting the listener to compare and to choose. Hell is the worst of homes, where the inhabitants find torment, humiliation, and despair without end. Paradise is the best of homes, where the inhabitants find peace, honor, and delight without end. The believer who has been described throughout this passage is the one who has chosen wisely, and whose choice is now confirmed and rewarded. The repetition of the word “settlement” and “residence” emphasizes that human beings are, by nature, seekers of a home. Everyone desires a place of belonging, security, and rest. The servants of the Most Merciful have understood that the true home is not the house of this world, which is temporary and will inevitably be left behind, but the abode of the Hereafter, which is prepared for them by their Lord. Their lives in this world are spent in preparation for that home, and their hearts are attached to it rather than to the fleeting dwellings of this life. This verse thus brings the entire passage to a beautiful resolution, assuring the believer that their patience, their purity, and their devotion will culminate in an eternal home of excellence, a dwelling whose pleasure never fades and whose inhabitants never depart.

Human beings, in themselves, are of no consequence to their Lord. Their worth lies entirely in their relationship with Him, expressed through supplication, worship, and the turning of the heart towards Him. Without this, a person is, in the divine scales, of no more weight than dust. The word “supplication” here is understood by the scholars to encompass both du'ā' in the specific sense of calling upon Allah for one's needs, and ibādah in the broader sense of worship as a whole. The servant's value is found in their recognition of their need for Allah and their humble turning towards Him. It is this that elevates the human being above the heedless and makes them precious in the sight of their Lord. When a person abandons this, they abandon the very thing that gives their existence meaning. The verse then declares that since the disbelievers have denied the truth, the consequence of that denial will be incumbent upon them — literally, it will cling to them and be “attached” to them without escape. The Arabic word lizām conveys the idea of something that adheres firmly and cannot be shaken off. Their denial will follow them into the Hereafter and become the very thing that seals their fate, just as their rejection of the Prophet's warning will become their eternal regret. This verse is a fitting conclusion to a sûrah that has contrasted the arrogance of the disbelievers with the humility of the servants of the Most Merciful. It reminds every reader that true honor lies not in wealth, lineage, or worldly status, but in the sincere worship of Allah. The sûrah ends where true religion begins: with the recognition that we are nothing without our Lord, and that our only value lies in turning to Him.
