1 | The command of Allah is at hand, so do not hasten it. Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate ˹with Him in worship˺! | This opening verse of Sūrat An-Naḥl delivers a powerful warning to the Quraysh while simultaneously comforting the Prophet Muḥammad, peace be upon him. The expression “The command of Allah is at hand” refers to the divine decree of punishment that the disbelievers were impatiently challenging the Prophet to bring. The word “command” here translates the Arabic amr, which carries the sense of Allah’s irresistible decree, particularly the judgement that will overtake those who reject the truth. The Meccan pagans would mockingly ask when the promised punishment would come, demonstrating their disbelief in the Hereafter and their arrogance towards the Messenger. The phrase “so do not hasten it” is a direct address, first and foremost to the disbelievers who thought that the delay of punishment meant it would never arrive, and also a lesson for believers never to become impatient with Allah’s timing. Throughout the Qur’an, Allah reminds humanity that what seems slow according to human reckoning is precise in the divine plan; a day with your Lord is like a thousand years of your count. The verse then shifts to glorification: “Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate with Him in worship.” This immediately connects the coming judgement to the crime of shirk, associating partners with Allah. The Meccans not only worshipped idols but also claimed that angels were Allah’s daughters and that their false gods could intercede for them. By declaring Allah glorified and exalted above such fabrications, the verse affirms His absolute transcendence and uniqueness. The verb “glorified” implies declaring Allah free from every imperfection, while “exalted” emphasises His supreme highness above anything they falsely attribute. This opening thus sets the tone for the entire sūrah, which repeatedly demonstrates Allah’s exclusive power over creation and provision, dismantling any justification for worshipping other beings. It teaches the believer that trust in Allah’s promise must be absolute; the delay of punishment is not a sign of weakness but an act of mercy allowing time for repentance, yet the arrival of His command is absolutely certain. In modern life, this verse encourages patience when we see injustice prevailing; we must not hasten Allah’s judgement upon others, for His timing is perfect and His punishment, when it comes, will be just.
2 | He sends down the angels with revelation by His command to whoever He wills of His servants, ˹stating:˺ “Warn ˹humanity˺ that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me, so be mindful of Me ˹alone˺.” | After the warning of judgement, this verse shifts the focus to Allah’s mercy and guidance through revelation. It describes how Allah “sends down the angels with revelation by His command.” The mention of angels being sent down with the revelation highlights the heavenly origin of the message delivered by the Prophet Muḥammad. The angel Jibrīl, peace be upon him, is the chief emissary entrusted with carrying the Qur’an from the Preserved Tablet to the heart of the Messenger. The phrase “by His command” emphasises that this sending is not a random event; it is a deliberate, purposeful act of the Almighty. The destination is “whoever He wills of His servants,” and that chosen servant in this context is the Prophet Muḥammad. The core message entrusted to him is captured in the direct quote: “Warn humanity that there is no god worthy of worship except Me, so be mindful of Me alone.” This is the essence of tawḥīd, the absolute oneness of God, which every prophet proclaimed. The instruction to warn carries an urgency; humanity is in a state of heedlessness and needs to be alerted to the reality of accountability. The warning is inextricably linked to the declaration of God’s oneness, because the greatest wrong that requires warning is associating partners with Allah. The second part, “so be mindful of Me alone,” translates the command ittaqūn, which embodies God-consciousness, fear of His displeasure, and a life of obedience and devotion. It is not sufficient to simply believe intellectually that God is one; that belief must translate into exclusive worship, love, fear, and hope directed to Him alone. Historically, this verse was a direct challenge to the Quraysh, whose polytheism had corrupted the original monotheistic legacy of Abraham and Ishmael. They had filled the Kaʿbah with idols and invented rituals that had no divine sanction. The message brought by the angels was therefore a restoration of the primordial religion. A practical lesson for every generation is that true piety is built upon correct belief; no amount of devotion to false gods or half-hearted acknowledgement can replace the exclusive mindfulness of Allah. The verse also calls us to reflect on the honour given to the prophets as chosen servants, teaching us to receive their warnings with humility and gratitude rather than pride and rejection.
3 | He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose. Exalted is He above what they associate with Him ˹in worship˺. | This verse presents the second great favour mentioned in the sūrah, the creation of the heavens and the earth, and links it directly to the refutation of polytheism. The statement “He created the heavens and the earth for a purpose” corrects the materialist view that existence is a cosmic accident without meaning. The Arabic word used for purpose is al-ḥaqq, which means truth, justice, and a definite intended goal. Thus creation is neither a game nor a meaningless pastime; it is an expression of divine wisdom, a theatre for moral testing, and a manifestation of Allah’s attributes. The vastness of the heavens with their stars and galaxies, and the intricate design of the earth with its ecosystems, all point to a wise Creator who does nothing in vain. Immediately following this, the verse declares, “Exalted is He above what they associate with Him in worship.” The connection is profound: if Allah alone created such an immense and purposeful universe, how could any created thing share in His divinity? The idols that the polytheists worshipped were themselves part of that creation; they were crafted from stone and wood taken from the earth, and they had no hand in the formation of the skies or the mountains. This logical argument pervades the Qur’an: the Creator of everything must be the sole object of worship. The repetition of the declaration of Allah’s transcendence over their associations reinforces that shirk is not a small error but a cosmic insult, attributing the Creator’s unique rights to powerless fragments of His own creation. The verse also contains a subtle comfort for believers: they are aligned with the very purpose for which the universe was made, while the polytheists are in fundamental contradiction with reality. In contemporary life, this verse challenges the modern forms of idolatry where people devote their ultimate concern to wealth, power, or ideologies, treating them as the central purpose of existence. Reflecting on the purposeful creation of the heavens and the earth restores perspective, helping a believer see that their own life must be lived with clear intention and directed towards the One who created it all. The grandeur of the universe becomes a daily sign that calls the heart back to tawḥīd and away from every false object of worship.
4 | He created humans from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Him˺. | Having mentioned the creation of the macrocosm, the heavens and the earth, the sūrah now draws attention to the microcosm of human creation. “He created humans from a sperm-drop” reminds every person of their lowly biological origin. The sperm-drop, a tiny, insignificant fluid that people normally regard with disgust, is the very substance from which Allah fashioned the human being with all its complexity, intelligence, and beauty. This sequence highlights the immense gap between the humble beginning of human life and the finished form of a fully conscious person. The verse then employs the Arabic particle thumma, rendered as “then—behold!”, to indicate a surprising turn: out of that despised drop emerges a creature who “openly challenges” his Creator. The word translated as “openly challenges” carries the sense of being argumentative, hostile, and contentious. Instead of responding with gratitude and humility, many humans use the faculties Allah gave them to dispute His signs, deny His oneness, and question His wisdom. Historically, this describes the Meccan leaders who, despite acknowledging that Allah created them, refused to worship Him alone and argued stubbornly against the Prophet. But the description applies universally to every age. A person who was nothing, brought into existence from a mere drop, dares to stand in arrogance before the Almighty, as if he were a self-sufficient partner. The verse powerfully exposes the irrationality of human pride. When a person reflects that their own origin is a microscopic cell completely dependent on divine power for its development, their pretensions of self-sufficiency crumble. The verse also serves as a call to humility: the same God who turned that drop into a full human being can surely recreate the person after death. In a modern context, when people boast of scientific and technological achievements as if they have conquered nature, this verse reminds them of their biological reality. Every human achievement is built upon a body that began as a fragile drop, a constant testament to Allah’s creative power. The appropriate response to such a humble beginning is not quarrelsome denial but profound thankfulness and submission to the One who dignified that drop by shaping it into a human being capable of knowing Him.
5 | And He created the cattle for you as a source of warmth, food, and ˹many other˺ benefits. | After establishing human creation, the sūrah moves to another major favour: the creation of cattle, meaning camels, cows, sheep, and goats. The verse begins with “And He created the cattle for you,” immediately highlighting that these animals are a deliberate provision from Allah, not a random development of nature. The primary benefits listed are “warmth, food, and many other benefits.” The mention of warmth comes first because the Arab society of the time relied heavily on animal wool, fur, and hair to make tents, blankets, and clothing that protected them from the cold desert nights and the harsh winds. This order teaches the lesson that Allah provides not only for our basic survival needs but also for our comfort. Food refers to their meat, and for some animals like goats and cows, also their milk, which is pure and nourishing. The phrase “many other benefits” is a broad term that opens the door to countless uses: leather for water skins and footwear, bones for tools, dung for fuel, and the animals themselves as mounts and beasts of burden. The verse implies that Allah placed these animals under human control, a phenomenon known in the Qur’an as taskhīr, meaning they are subjected to serve human needs by divine arrangement, not by human power alone. A camel, which is much stronger than a man, is docile and obedient; this is a sign of the Creator’s mercy. The absence of any mention of their own power over these animals instills gratitude towards Allah every time a person benefits from them. This verse also indirectly refutes the pagan practice of dedicating certain cattle to idols, because it reminds them that the cattle themselves are gifts from the One true God, not independent sources of provision. In contemporary life, while many of us no longer rely on cattle for warmth in the same direct way, the principle remains: animal-derived products like leather, wool, and dairy are part of an intricate system of provision that we did not create. Reflecting on this makes the believer more aware of the countless hidden bounties in daily life and fosters a habit of thanking Allah for the simple glass of milk or the warm jacket, which traces its origin to His creative wisdom.
6 | They are also pleasing to you when you bring them home and when you take them out to graze. | This verse continues the reflection on cattle but shifts from their material benefits to their aesthetic and emotional value. The verse mentions two specific times of day: “when you bring them home” in the evening and “when you take them out to graze” in the morning. These are the two daily moments when the herds are most visible, moving as a group. The verse states that in these movements there is jamāl, a beauty that is pleasing to the soul. The sight of healthy, well-fed animals returning from pasture with full udders, and the sight of them setting out energetically in the morning, stirs a sense of satisfaction, pride, and joy in the owner’s heart. This is not a trivial mention; Allah is drawing attention to the fact that He did not merely create cattle as utilitarian machines, but placed within them a beauty that brings psychological delight. The rhythm of pastoral life, moving livestock to and from grazing lands, is itself a source of tranquillity. For the Arabs of the time, the camel and the flock were not just a source of wealth; they were a source of honour and aesthetic pleasure. The verse teaches that appreciating natural beauty and the elegant design of animals is a valid spiritual experience, provided it leads to gratitude to the Creator rather than pride in ownership. It also serves as a subtle reminder that even these beautiful possessions are entirely under Allah’s control; the joy they bring is a gift from Him. The early morning and evening are specifically mentioned perhaps because they correspond to times of prayer, subtly linking the believer’s daily routine of worship with the natural rhythms of the creation around them. In a modern, urbanised setting, many people have lost this direct contact with livestock, yet the principle can be applied to any part of creation we find beautiful, whether it be the family pet, a garden, or a landscape. Whenever we feel a sense of pleasure looking at something in nature, that feeling should redirect our hearts to the One who placed that beauty there. The verse thus cultivates an attitude of mindful appreciation, seeing the divine hand behind every pleasing sight rather than taking it for granted.
7 | And they carry your loads to ˹distant˺ lands which you could not otherwise reach without great hardship. Surely your Lord is Ever Gracious, Most Merciful. | This verse highlights another critical function of cattle, particularly camels and donkeys, as beasts of burden. “They carry your loads to distant lands” refers to the long trade journeys that were the economic lifeline of the Quraysh, such as the winter caravan to Yemen and the summer caravan to Syria. The Arabic imagery emphasises the weight of the goods and the remoteness of the destinations, places “which you could not otherwise reach without great hardship.” The phrase “great hardship” translates a word that implies extreme strain, to the point of breaking the soul. Imagine crossing hundreds of miles of barren desert carrying heavy merchandise on your own back. The camel, uniquely designed by Allah with its ability to store water and endure extreme conditions, transformed an impossible journey into a manageable one. The verse closes the enumeration of these cattle-related blessings with the beautiful divine names: “Surely your Lord is Ever Gracious, Most Merciful.” The pairing of ra’ūf (Ever Gracious) and raḥīm (Most Merciful) is significant. Ra’ūf denotes an intense, tender kindness that anticipates needs and gently provides, while Raḥīm points to an expansive, continuous mercy. This teaches that the subjection of animals to human service is not a result of human cleverness but a direct manifestation of Allah’s compassion. He knew His servants would need to travel and trade, so He provided a living ship of the desert before they even existed. This verse also refutes the pagan notion that their idols had any part in making trade prosperous. The real facilitator of their commercial success was the One who created the camel and inspired them to domesticate it. For contemporary reflection, the lesson is about recognising divine mercy in the infrastructure that makes modern life possible. Whether it is the vehicles we use today or the animals of burden in past and present agrarian societies, the principle remains: Allah has made available means of transport that spare us unbearable hardship. This recognition should prevent arrogance and foster a spirit of humility and gratitude on every journey, reminding us that our Lord’s grace surrounds us even in the most mundane activities.
8 | ˹He also created˺ horses, mules, and donkeys for your transportation and adornment. And He creates what you do not know. | After detailing the blessings of cattle, the sūrah now mentions other riding animals: horses, mules, and donkeys. The phrase “for your transportation and adornment” identifies a dual purpose. Horses, mules, and donkeys were the primary means of personal transport and cavalry in ancient times. The word “transportation” refers to riding them to reach destinations, while “adornment” refers to their beauty and the prestige associated with owning fine steeds. In pre-Islamic Arabia, horses were a symbol of pride and tribal honour, yet the verse redirects that appreciation by declaring that Allah created them for these purposes. This reframes the ownership of such noble animals as a sign of divine favour rather than a purely human achievement. The verse then closes with a profound statement: “And He creates what you do not know.” This short sentence opens the mind to the limitless scope of Allah’s creation. At the time of revelation, the Arabs knew only the animals of their region. This statement hints that there exist countless other creatures, forms of transport, and means of adornment that are entirely beyond their current knowledge. This includes not only undiscovered animal species but also the future inventions of cars, planes, and other technologies that would one day serve humanity. Although the verse directly refers to animals, the principle extends to all means of conveyance and beautification that Allah’s creative power brings into existence through human ingenuity, which itself is a gift from Him. The statement keeps the believer humble; no matter how advanced human technology becomes, it remains part of “what you do not know” that Allah knew and created in its appointed time. It also serves as a rebuttal to those who think that human innovation makes them independent of God. Every new invention is ultimately part of Allah’s creation, using materials and laws of physics He established. This verse therefore fosters an attitude of wonder and anticipation, recognising that Allah’s creative activity is continuous and that future discoveries will continue to reveal His wisdom. It encourages the pursuit of knowledge while firmly anchoring all progress in the acknowledgement of the Creator’s prior and encompassing creative power.
9 | It is upon Allah ˹alone˺ to ˹clearly˺ show the Straight Way. Other ways are deviant. Had He willed, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you. | After detailing physical provisions like animals, the sūrah now turns to the greatest favour: spiritual guidance. The verse begins with the phrase, “It is upon Allah alone to clearly show the Straight Way.” This establishes that guiding humanity to the path that leads to Him is a self-imposed obligation of divine mercy. Allah has taken it upon Himself out of His grace to make the truth evident. He did not leave people to wander in darkness, groping for the way; rather, He sent prophets, revealed scriptures, and placed within the human soul a natural disposition to recognise truth. The “Straight Way” is the path of tawḥīd and obedience that leads to eternal happiness. In contrast, “other ways are deviant.” Any path that deviates from the way shown by Allah is crooked, leading away from the destination and into destruction. The verse anticipates a possible question: if Allah is so merciful, why then do people go astray? The answer comes immediately: “Had He willed, He would have easily imposed guidance upon all of you.” This clarifies that Allah’s will is not limited; He could have created all humans as automatically guided beings without free choice, like angels. However, in His wisdom, He granted humans the dignity of free will, making the test meaningful. The guidance is clearly shown, but the choice to walk the path is left to the individual. This is not because Allah is unable to guide them, but because forcing belief would contradict the very purpose of a moral test. The Meccan pagans could not argue that they were not shown the way; the Prophet was among them with a clear revelation. Their own deviance was a consequence of their arrogant rejection, not a lack of clarity on Allah’s part. This verse is immensely practical for contemporary believers. It teaches that while we must tirelessly convey the message and clarify the Straight Way, ultimate guidance rests with Allah, and we should not become frustrated when people reject it. It also serves as a warning to never treat the clarity of Islam as a reason for complacency; having the path made clear increases our responsibility to walk it sincerely. It reassures that Allah’s justice is perfect, and no one will be punished without having been shown the truth in a way sufficient for them.
10 | He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky, from which you drink and by which plants grow for your cattle to graze. | Transitioning to another physical blessing, this verse highlights the miracle of water. “He is the One Who sends down rain from the sky” focuses the listener on the singular divine act. Rain is not an autonomous natural process; it descends by the precise command and measure of Allah. The phrase “from the sky” points upwards, reminding the original audience who had no weather control that the life-giving water comes from a realm entirely beyond human reach. The benefits are then broken into two essential categories: “from which you drink” and “by which plants grow for your cattle to graze.” Drinking water is the most immediate, personal human need. The direct address, “you drink,” makes each listener realise the intimate dependence on this rainfall. The second benefit shows the interconnectedness of provision: rain causes vegetation to sprout, which then becomes fodder for the cattle already mentioned in earlier verses. Thus the water sustains both humans and their livestock, the animals that provide food, clothing, and transport. This chain of provision demonstrates the coherence of Allah’s creation; every element works in harmony to support life. The verse implicitly refutes the belief of the polytheists that their idols or natural forces independently provided rain. In pre-Islamic Arabia, some believed that certain stars brought rain, while others performed rituals to idols seeking water. By asserting that Allah alone sends down rain, the sūrah reclaims even this natural phenomenon as a sign pointing exclusively to the one true God. The verse also contains an environmental and ethical dimension: water is a divine gift, not a commodity to be wasted or polluted arrogantly. When a believer drinks a glass of water or sees rain falling on a garden, this verse trains the heart to trace the blessing back to the Sender, fostering a state of continuous thankfulness. In modern times, when tap water is readily available, it is easy to forget the celestial origin of all fresh water through the hydrological cycle, a cycle designed and sustained by the Creator. Reflecting deeply on a simple rain shower can become an act of worship by recognising the mercy, wisdom, and power that each drop carries.
11 | With it He produces for you ˹various˺ crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect. | This verse continues the theme of rain’s blessings by zooming in on the diverse produce that springs from the earth as a result of water. “With it He produces for you” connects the growth directly to the rain mentioned in the previous verse. The phrase “for you” personalises the blessing again; these are not random botanical events but provisions deliberately created for human benefit. The verse then lists specific items: “crops, olives, palm trees, grapevines, and every type of fruit.” The selection is culturally and nutritionally significant. Crops refer to grains like wheat and barley, the staple food. Olives provide oil, which was and remains a central element of Middle Eastern diet, used for cooking, lighting lamps, and anointing. Palm trees yield dates, a high-energy food that can be stored for long periods and was fundamental to Arabian survival. Grapevines produce grapes that can be eaten fresh, dried as raisins, or used in some contexts for wholesome provision as the sūrah later clarifies. The mention of “every type of fruit” expands the scope beyond the Arabian Peninsula to all the fruits of the world, hinting that the blessing of rain and plant growth is universal. The verse then concludes, “Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect.” The sign is that from the same water, an endless variety of colours, tastes, shapes, and nutritional properties emerge from the earth. The soil and water are uniform, yet the produce is astonishingly diverse. This botanical miracle points to a single wise Creator who programmed the genetic information within each seed and tree. The call to “those who reflect” encourages the use of intellect to move from observing the created thing to the Creator. Reflection here is not a passive glance but a deep, analytical pondering that asks “how” and “why” and ultimately finds the answer in Allah. This verse offers a model for Islamic engagement with the natural sciences: observation of the natural world should lead to increased faith. A modern believer who studies botany or agriculture can see in the verse a validation of their effort to understand the signs of Allah. The practical lesson is to avoid eating and drinking mindlessly; every meal is an opportunity to recognise the divine artistry, give thanks, and strengthen one’s conviction through reflection.
12 | And He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon. And the stars have been subjected by His command. Surely in this are signs for those who understand. | After discussing the blessings of rain and plants, the sūrah lifts the gaze further to the celestial phenomena that govern life on earth. “He has subjected for your benefit the day and the night, the sun and the moon.” The key word is “subjected,” which implies that these vast, powerful cosmic entities are placed under a system of service for humanity. The alternation of day and night provides the rhythm for work and rest. The sun is the source of light and energy that drives the climate, enables agriculture, and makes life possible. The moon provides a gentler light for the night, regulates the tides, and serves as the basis for the lunar calendar that organises many acts of worship. The phrase “and the stars have been subjected by His command” points to the stars’ multiple functions: navigation, beautification of the sky, and according to some classical commentators, meteorological influences and even as projectiles against devils who try to eavesdrop on the heavens. The stars are not deities that control fate, as the pagans believed; they are mere created objects executing the command of Allah. The verse ends with, “Surely in this are signs for those who understand.” The word translated as “understand” implies a deeper, more penetrating intellect that grasps the underlying wisdom behind phenomena. It is not enough to see the sun rise and set; a person of understanding sees the sign of the Creator’s power, precision, and mercy in that regularity. The sun does not overstep its orbit; the night does not outstrip the day. Such flawless order and subjection to human welfare could not be the result of blind chance. The verse challenges the pre-Islamic Arab beliefs that the sun and moon were deities, or that the stars had independent power over human destiny. In a contemporary context, as we learn more about the precise astronomical measurements and the delicate balance that makes earth habitable, this verse’s sign becomes even more striking. The exact tilt of the earth, the distance from the sun, the size and orbit of the moon – all point to a deliberate design for the benefit of life. The call is to use our understanding to see through the illusion of self-sustaining nature and to recognise the continuous divine command that keeps the cosmos in service to humanity, a service that should evoke profound gratitude and worship.
13 | And ˹He subjected˺ for you whatever He has created on earth of varying colours. Surely in this is a sign for those who are mindful. | The sūrah now broadens the scope from celestial bodies to everything on the earth. The verse begins with an implied verb from the previous context: “And He subjected for you whatever He has created on earth of varying colours.” The subjection of the entire earthly creation to human use is a massive declaration of Allah’s favour. Everything created on the planet, from minerals and metals to plants, animals, landscapes, and climates, is placed at the service of humanity. The mention of “varying colours” is particularly striking. It draws attention to the diversity and aesthetic beauty in creation. The colours of fruits, flowers, soils, mountains, oceans, and even the different skin tones of human beings are part of this subjection. Colour is not merely a functional property; it is a source of pleasure and a sign of the Artist’s creativity. A world of monochrome grey would be functionally adequate but aesthetically impoverished. Allah, in His grace, made the world visually beautiful. The phrase “surely in this is a sign for those who are mindful” links the observation of this diversity to a specific spiritual quality: mindfulness, or dhikr. The word implies an active, continuous remembrance of Allah that leads to consciousness of His presence and commands. Those who are mindful see the varying colours and immediately recall the Maker. Their mindfulness leads them to use these subjected resources responsibly, as trustees, not as reckless exploiters. The verse also contains a subtle refutation of the polytheistic practice of attributing some parts of creation to different gods. Since all creatures on earth are subjected to humanity by one God, there is no division of divine labour. The unity of subjection points to the unity of the Subjector. In a contemporary setting, this verse provides a basis for an Islamic environmental ethic. The earth and its diverse contents are a sign and a trust; they are subjected for our benefit, but we must exercise that benefit with the mindfulness the verse demands, avoiding waste, pollution, and destruction that would betray the trust. When a believer sees a landscape, a garden, or even the colours of a market display, this verse invites them to translate that visual experience into a conscious remembrance of the One who created all those colours and placed them at our service.
14 | And He is the One Who has subjected the sea, so from it you may eat tender seafood and extract ornaments to wear. And you see the ships ploughing their way through it, so you may seek His bounty and give thanks ˹to Him˺. | This verse highlights the blessing of the sea, a sign that was particularly awe-inspiring for the land-based Arabs of Mecca, although some engaged in trade voyages. “He is the One Who has subjected the sea” begins with the same emphasis on divine subjection. The vast, powerful ocean, which can swallow ships and flood lands, is by Allah’s command made a realm that serves humanity. The first benefit mentioned is food: “from it you may eat tender seafood.” The word “tender” describes fresh, soft, delicious meat that is easily digestible, a delicacy that emerges from the saline waters. The second benefit is adornment: “extract ornaments to wear,” referring primarily to pearls and coral. Pearls were highly prized items of jewellery in ancient times and represented wealth and beauty. The verse then shifts to commerce: “And you see the ships ploughing their way through it, so you may seek His bounty.” The image of ships cleaving through the waves, carrying goods and people, is a visible sign of Allah’s favour in enabling international trade. The phrase “ploughing their way” conveys a sense of purpose and effort, yet it is ultimately made possible by the water’s buoyancy and the wind’s driving force, both under divine command. The goal of this maritime activity is to “seek His bounty,” which encompasses all lawful commercial gain, as well as the spiritual journey of pilgrimage and seeking knowledge. The verse concludes, “and give thanks to Him.” This makes it clear that the intended final outcome of enjoying these sea-derived blessings is gratitude. The logical sequence is: Allah subjected the sea, He placed in it food and ornaments, He enabled ships to sail, all so that humans might benefit materially and then turn those material benefits into spiritual thanks. This verse has a strong contemporary resonance in the global fishing and shipping industries. The modern believer who eats fish, wears pearls, or benefits from goods transported by sea should trace all of that back to the One Who subjected the sea. It also prompts reflection on the delicate marine ecosystem: pollution and overfishing harm a domain that is a divine sign and a source of provision, and thus contradict the grateful attitude the verse seeks to cultivate. The sight of a ship at sea or a pearl on a necklace can thus become a trigger for a moment of sincere gratitude to the Lord of the worlds.
15 | He has placed into the earth firm mountains, so it does not shake with you, as well as rivers, and pathways so you may find your way. | The sūrah continues its catalogue of terrestrial blessings. “He has placed into the earth firm mountains” uses the image of mountains as pegs or stabilisers. The verb “placed” implies a deliberate, calculated insertion. The purpose is stated explicitly: “so it does not shake with you.” The mountains, with their deep roots extending into the earth’s crust according to modern understanding, play a crucial role in stabilising the earth’s surface. While the early Arabs did not know the geological details, they observed the immovable solidity of mountains and could appreciate that they make the earth stable and habitable, preventing constant catastrophic tremors. The verse then mentions “as well as rivers.” Mountains are the source of many rivers; snowmelt and rainfall captured on mountain peaks flow down to carve valleys and provide fresh water across continents. The mention of rivers immediately after mountains highlights the interconnected design. Then, “and pathways so you may find your way” adds another layer. The natural contours of the land, the mountain passes, the river valleys, all create pathways that guide travellers. The earth is not a flat, featureless expanse; it is sculpted with landmarks that enable navigation. All of these features are described as deliberate placements by Allah. The polytheistic worldview often attributed such features to nature spirits or the doings of various gods. This verse reclaims the entire topography of the earth as the handiwork of the One God, designed for human benefit. The phrase “so it does not shake with you” also carries a metaphorical layer. The mountains of divine law and the steadfast believers are sometimes described as stabilisers of society, preventing moral and social earthquakes. However, the primary meaning is the physical stabilization. In a modern context, this verse invites believers to see geological features not as brute, meaningless matter, but as purposeful creations. When driving through a mountain pass, crossing a river, or following a path, the heart can be directed to the One who shaped the earth for our guidance and stability. The verse fosters a sense of security and gratitude: the very ground beneath our feet has been made firm by a merciful Creator, and the routes we travel were inscribed on the earth long before we existed.
16 | Also by landmarks and stars do people find their way. | This short verse completes the theme of guidance in travel started in the previous verse, which mentioned pathways. Now it adds two more means of navigation: “landmarks and stars.” The conjunction “also” links this directly to the pathways created by mountains and rivers. Landmarks refer to prominent features of the landscape such as tall mountains, distinctive rock formations, valleys, oases, or even human-made structures that serve as reference points during a journey. In the vast featureless deserts of Arabia, any distinctive hill or cluster of trees was crucial for orientation. The stars, on the other hand, are the navigational tools of the night sky and the open sea. Before compasses and GPS, travellers relied on the fixed positions of stars and constellations to determine direction, especially the North Star. The Bedouin and the seafaring traders of Arabia had an intricate knowledge of the night sky, and this verse acknowledges that this knowledge is part of Allah’s provision for them. By mentioning both earthly and celestial guides, the verse encompasses all types of travel: land and sea, day and night. Importantly, the verse says “by landmarks and stars do people find their way,” which is a statement of fact that simultaneously serves as a reminder of the Giver of those guides. It is Allah who created the stars and shaped the landmarks; thus, whenever a person uses them to find the right path, they are indirectly benefiting from divine guidance. This physical navigation becomes a metaphor for spiritual navigation. Just as Allah has provided material signs in the heavens and the earth to guide us to our physical destinations, He has also provided spiritual signs—the Qur’an and the Sunnah—to guide us to our eternal destination. The surah will later elaborate on the Straight Way. This verse, though brief, is rich in its call to reflection. When a traveller today looks at a map, a mountain peak, or the stars, this verse invites them to see these not as neutral tools but as part of Allah’s merciful arrangement. The verse also carries a subtle caution: if we can so readily use landmarks and stars to avoid getting lost in the desert, why do we so often ignore the much clearer spiritual landmarks sent down by Allah to avoid losing our way in the journey of life? The one who reflects on the stars should be led to the Creator of the stars, and then to the guidance He has revealed.
17 | Can the One Who creates be equal to those who do not? Will you not then be mindful? | After the extensive list of divine favours and creative acts in the preceding verses, this verse delivers the logical conclusion as a rhetorical question. “Can the One Who creates be equal to those who do not?” The contrast is stark. On one side is Allah, the Creator, who brought the heavens, earth, rain, cattle, sea, mountains, rivers, landmarks, and stars into existence and subjected them all to human benefit. On the other side are the idols and false gods worshipped by the polytheists, which are themselves created, lifeless objects that cannot create even a fly. The verse uses the most fundamental dividing line in existence: the Creator versus the created. It is a self-evident truth that the Creator cannot be on the same level as His creation, and that the one who grants every blessing cannot be equated with those who are themselves utterly dependent and powerless. The rhetorical question demands an obvious answer: no, they are not equal. The second part, “Will you not then be mindful?” drives the point home. The word translated as “be mindful” is tadhakkarūn, which means to remember, to take heed, to use one’s mind and heart to recognise the truth. The verse is calling the polytheists to wake up from their heedlessness. They had never seen their idols create a single raindrop, a single grain of wheat, or a single camel. Yet they continued to call upon them, offer sacrifices to them, and fear them. This is a profound mental contradiction. The verse is not merely an argument; it is a compassionate appeal to innate human reason and the fitrah, the natural disposition to recognise the superiority of the Creator. Historically, the Quraysh themselves acknowledged that Allah was the Creator of the heavens and the earth, yet they associated partners with Him. This question exposes the irrationality of that position. In contemporary times, the idol of materialism, the worship of human intellect, or the devotion to charismatic leaders can similarly be challenged by this verse: can the one who creates and sustains the universe be equal to these created things that people worship? The question “Will you not then be mindful?” echoes through the ages, asking every person to stop, think, and realign their devotion to the only One worthy of it.
18 | If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | This verse follows the rhetorical question of the previous one with a statement that is both humbling and awe-inspiring. “If you tried to count Allah’s blessings, you would never be able to number them.” The address is universal, to every human being. The word for “blessings” here encompasses all favours, from the most obvious—like the senses, health, and provision—to the most hidden—like the immunity system functioning within the body, the precise orbit of the earth, or the timely arrival of a helpful thought. The verse declares that even if one were to spend a lifetime attempting to enumerate these blessings, they could not encompass them. The human mind simply does not have the capacity to perceive, let alone count, all the favours that sustain a single moment of life. This statement perfectly complements the long list of blessings described earlier in the sūrah. Those verses mentioned specific categories of favours, and now verse 18 tells us that those were merely examples; the full inventory is beyond human comprehension. Immediately after this declaration of the infinitude of blessings, the verse says, “Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The connection between the two parts is profoundly beautiful. After reminding us that His blessings are uncountable, Allah does not say “therefore He is severe in punishment” for our failure to thank Him, but rather “He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” This teaches that despite our inability to adequately appreciate or even recognise His blessings, Allah’s primary relationship with His creation is one of mercy and forgiveness. He overlooks our shortcomings and continues to shower us with gifts. The polytheists of Mecca were enjoying these countless blessings while worshipping idols, yet Allah was still All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, giving them time to repent and sending them a prophet. This verse provides immense hope for the sinner. When one reflects on their lack of gratitude, which is itself a great ingratitude, this verse prevents despair by redirecting the heart to divine forgiveness. In daily life, a believer can use this verse as a meditation: instead of trying to count blessings, which is impossible, simply acknowledge the impossibility and then seek the forgiveness of the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. This fosters a state of humble gratitude mixed with hopeful repentance, which is the essence of the believing heart’s posture before its Lord.
19 | And Allah knows what you conceal and what you reveal. | After the sweeping statement about the uncountable blessings and Allah’s forgiveness and mercy, this verse introduces an essential attribute of Allah: His perfect knowledge. “And Allah knows what you conceal and what you reveal.” The address “you” reaches both the believers and the disbelievers. The polytheists of Mecca would often conceal their plots against the Prophet, their secret mockery, and their hidden resentment, while they would openly reveal their objections, their idol worship, and their public defamation of the new faith. This verse tells them that there is no hidden thought, no whispered conspiracy, no inner doubt, no secret charity, and no suppressed belief that escapes Allah’s knowledge. The contrast between what is concealed and what is revealed covers the entire spectrum of human action and intention. It also serves as a crucial link to the surrounding verses. Verse 18 spoke of the impossibility of counting blessings, implying that many blessings are hidden and unknown to us; verse 19 declares that nothing of our deeds, hidden or open, is unknown to Allah. Thus, while we are ignorant of most of His favours, He is perfectly aware of all of our states. This asymmetry should produce a deep sense of humility and accountability. If a person knows that their Lord is fully aware of their secret sins, hypocritical thoughts, or their genuine faith and secret good deeds, that knowledge becomes a powerful moral compass. For the disbelievers, it is a warning: their secret mocking of the revelation and their hidden plots are not hidden from Allah, and they will be held accountable. For the believers, it is both a comfort and a motivation. It comforts the one who suffers in silence, does good in secret, or struggles with doubts privately, that Allah sees and will reward or assist accordingly. It motivates the believer to purify the inner self, because outer piety is insufficient if the concealed inner state is corrupt. In a modern context where privacy is highly valued and much of our life happens behind screens and closed doors, this verse reminds us that there is no true secrecy from the Creator. This constant awareness is an antidote to hypocrisy and a source of sincerity, encouraging the believer to align their private life with their public one, all for the sake of the One who knows both what they conceal and what they reveal.
20 | But those ˹idols˺ they invoke besides Allah cannot create anything—they themselves are created. | This verse marks a decisive shift back to the critique of idolatry after the parenthetical emphasis on divine knowledge. It begins with “But,” contrasting the all-knowing, all-providing Creator with the powerless objects of polytheistic worship. “Those idols they invoke besides Allah” refers to the statues, images, and other entities that the Meccans called upon. The pronoun “they” here includes not just the Quraysh but all idolaters across time. The verse then delivers a twofold description that exposes the utter absurdity of idol worship. First, “cannot create anything.” The idols lack the fundamental attribute of divinity, which is the power to create. They did not bring into existence the heavens, the earth, a single drop of water, or even a tiny ant. This echoes the rhetorical question of verse 17 and drives the point home conclusively. If an object is incapable of creation, it is part of creation, and thus it is not God. The second part, “they themselves are created,” takes the argument further. Not only do the idols fail to create, but they are themselves products of creation. A human being carves a statue out of wood or stone, shapes it from metal, or forms it from clay. The raw materials are part of Allah’s creation, and the act of manufacturing is itself enabled by faculties that Allah created in the artisan. Thus, the idol is a created thing twice over. The worshipper essentially takes a piece of the creation, re-forms it, and then bows down to it as though it were the Creator. This is the height of irrationality. The verse dismantles any supposed divinity of idols by reducing them to their true ontological status: mere artefacts. In the Meccan context, this was a direct assault on the very foundation of the Quraysh’s religion and economy, as idol production and pilgrimage to the Kaʿbah filled with idols were central to their power. The verse also applies to more abstract idols in the modern age, such as the deification of human leaders, ideologies, or material possessions. Whatever a person centres their life upon besides Allah is a created thing that can create nothing. The logical conclusion is that only the uncreated Creator, who creates all things and is not Himself created, deserves worship. This verse serves as a clear criterion for tawḥīd, and understanding it deeply is the starting point for genuine faith.
21 | They are dead, not alive—not even knowing when their followers will be resurrected. | This verse continues the demolition of idol worship by adding another layer to the description of the false gods. After declaring that the idols cannot create anything and are themselves created, the sūrah now describes them as “dead, not alive.” This simple contrast strips away any lingering illusion of power or personality that idolaters might attribute to their objects of worship. Life is the most fundamental quality we associate with agency, awareness, and the ability to respond. A dead thing possesses none of these. The idols, whether made of stone, wood, or metal, are utterly lifeless. They do not hear the prayers of their worshippers, they do not see their acts of devotion, and they cannot respond with help or harm. The verse then adds a further dimension of their impotence: “not even knowing when their followers will be resurrected.” This is a devastating point. The ultimate destiny of every human being is resurrection and judgement. Even if a person could find some worldly benefit from an idol, the idol is completely ignorant of the most critical event in that person’s existence. How can a worshipper rely on something that does not even know when its own devotees will be raised to face their Lord? The idol cannot intercede, cannot warn, and cannot prepare its follower for that Day. This echoes the Qur’anic theme that on the Day of Judgement, the false gods will disown their worshippers, and the worshippers will realise the utter futility of their devotion. The verse serves a powerful pedagogical purpose: it invites the polytheist to compare the living, all-knowing Creator, who is aware of every hidden thing and has power over resurrection, with these dead, ignorant objects. The logical conclusion is inevitable. The idol is worse than a dead animal; an animal at least once possessed life and senses. The idol never possessed life at all. In a modern context, the principle extends to any dead thing that people devote their lives to, whether it is the dead matter of money, lifeless technology, or expired ideologies. Only a living, eternal God is worthy of ultimate concern. The believer who understands this verse will feel a profound gratitude that they are connected to the Ever-Living, the Self-Subsisting, who knows all their states and holds their resurrection in His hands. The verse also strengthens the heart against the fear of false powers, because it reminds us that all such powers are essentially dead before Allah.
22 | Your God is ˹only˺ One God. As for those who do not believe in the Hereafter, their hearts are in denial, and they are too proud. | After the sustained critique of idols, this verse returns to the positive proclamation of tawḥīd. “Your God is only One God.” The address “your” is singular, directed at the Prophet or at each individual listener, making the declaration deeply personal. The exclusivity of divinity is absolute; there is no room for a second, a partner, or an intermediary. The one God is the same God of Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets. The verse then immediately shifts to a description of the rejecters: “As for those who do not believe in the Hereafter, their hearts are in denial, and they are too proud.” This identifies the root cause of polytheism and disbelief. The rejection of the Hereafter is directly linked to the corruption of the heart. The phrase “their hearts are in denial” indicates a deep-seated refusal to accept the truth, not because of a lack of evidence, but because of a spiritual disease. The word for denial, munkir, carries the sense of actively rejecting something they actually recognise inwardly. Their innate nature, the fitrah, testifies to the oneness of God, but their hearts have been overcome by denial. The second characteristic, “and they are too proud,” reveals the deeper motive: arrogance. Believing in the Hereafter means accepting accountability, judgement, and the ultimate authority of a higher power. The proud person despises the idea of bowing to a Lord, of being held responsible for their actions, and of being told what is right and wrong. Idol worship, by contrast, allowed them to invent their own gods, which they could manipulate and which placed no moral demands upon them. They could live as they pleased, with their idols serving their desires rather than guiding them. The verse thus diagnoses the spiritual psychology of disbelief as a combination of intellectual dishonesty and moral arrogance. The heart knows the truth but denies it, and the ego refuses to submit. This diagnosis remains profoundly relevant today. Many reject faith not because they have examined the evidence and found it lacking, but because accepting it would require a radical reorientation of their lives, a humility they are unwilling to embrace. The verse is also a comfort to the believer who sees people rejecting clear truth; it explains that the issue lies not in the message but in the diseased state of the rejecter’s heart. The cure is a sincere turning to Allah to remove the pride and soften the heart towards His oneness and the reality of the Hereafter.
23 | Without a doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and what they reveal. He certainly does not like those who are too proud. | This verse follows directly from the diagnosis of the disbelievers’ state in the previous verse. Their hearts are in denial and they are arrogant, but their outward behaviour and their inner secrets are fully known to Allah. “Without a doubt, Allah knows what they conceal and what they reveal.” The phrase “without a doubt” is an affirmation that dispels any illusion of hiddenness. The disbelievers might hide their plots, their mockery of the Prophet, their secret fears of the truth, or even a suppressed recognition of Allah’s oneness. They reveal their idol worship, their arguments against the Qur’an, and their open hostility. Both categories, the concealed and the revealed, are equally exposed to divine knowledge. This serves as a warning: there is no secret chamber of the heart or private meeting where they can escape Allah’s awareness. The verse then adds a severe judgement: “He certainly does not like those who are too proud.” The Arabic word for pride here is mustakbirīn, those who actively seek to magnify themselves and refuse to submit to truth. This statement is devastating because divine dislike is the precursor to divine punishment. Allah’s love is the source of all good, and His dislike portends utter ruin. The proud person is the opposite of the grateful, humble servant who recognises their total dependence on Allah. The pride of the disbelievers prevents them from accepting tawḥīd and the Hereafter. They consider themselves too great to follow a mere human messenger, too great to abandon the religion of their forefathers, too great to submit their desires to a revealed law. The verse teaches that pride is not a minor character flaw; it is the fundamental barrier between a soul and its Lord. In the context of the sūrah’s flow, this verse links the knowledge of Allah to the rejection of the proud. Because Allah knows what they conceal, He knows their arrogance, their inner mockery, and their stubborn refusal, and He does not like them for it. This should terrify anyone harbouring pride and encourage them to cultivate humility. For the believer, this verse prompts a constant examination of the heart: are we secretly proud of our piety, knowledge, or lineage? Such hidden pride is known to Allah and is disliked by Him. The remedy is to remember one’s humble origin from a sperm-drop and to attribute all good to Allah’s grace. The verse also reassures the oppressed believers that Allah is fully aware of the hidden arrogance of their persecutors and will deal with them justly.
24 | And when it is said to them, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “Ancient fables!” | This verse provides a concrete example of the arrogance and denial described in the previous verses. The question “What has your Lord revealed?” was likely posed by the Quraysh to one another, or by newcomers to Mecca asking about the Prophet’s message, but it is framed here as a test. The disbelievers are asked to identify what their Lord—acknowledging that they did believe in a supreme Lord—had sent down. Their response is not a denial of revelation per se, but a dismissive characterisation of its content: “Ancient fables!” The Arabic term asāṭīr al-awwalīn literally means the writings or tales of the ancient people. By calling the Qur’an ancient fables, they sought to reduce its authority in several ways. First, they implied that it was not a fresh divine revelation but a collection of old stories recycled by Muḥammad. Second, they suggested it was mythical and unworthy of serious attention, belonging to the realm of bedtime stories, not truth. Third, they attempted to strip it of its moral and legal weight; if it was just old tales, no one was obliged to obey its commands. This mockery was an expression of their pride and denial. Historically, the Quraysh knew that the stories of previous prophets like Noah, Hūd, and Ṣāliḥ were not fabrications; they were part of the cultural memory of Arabia. But to acknowledge them as true would mean acknowledging the truth of Muḥammad’s prophethood and the coming judgement, which they were too proud to do. So they resorted to ridicule. The verse exposes the bankruptcy of their response; they had no rational argument, so they attacked with derision. This tactic is common among those who reject truth across ages. When they cannot refute the message, they mock it, calling it outdated, primitive, or mythical. This verse is a comfort to the Prophet and to all believers who face similar mockery. The response of the disbelievers is not a reflection on the message but on their own diseased hearts. It also provides a lesson for believers today: when we hear the truth being mocked, we should recognise the arrogance behind the mockery and not let it shake our faith, because the same was said to the Prophet. The verse sets up a contrast that will follow in the sūrah between the response of the disbelievers and that of the mindful believers when asked the same question, showing that the difference lies entirely in the state of the heart.
25 | Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge. Evil indeed is what they will bear! | This verse announces the consequences of the mockery and the active misguidance of others. “Let them bear their burdens in full on the Day of Judgment” refers to the full weight of their own sins, particularly the sin of disbelieving and ridiculing the revelation. The imagery of bearing burdens evokes the crushing weight of sin, which will be an actual, felt reality on that Day. But their punishment is not limited to their own misdeeds. The verse adds, “as well as some of the burdens of those they mislead without knowledge.” This is a principle of tremendous gravity: a person who leads others astray will carry, in addition to their own sins, a portion of the sins of those they misguided. The phrase “without knowledge” indicates that these leaders of misguidance actively led others astray through false arguments, propaganda, or social pressure, preying on the ignorance of their followers. However, this does not absolve the followers of their own responsibility; they also bear the burden of their own misdeeds, but the misleaders share in it because they caused it. The burden of the misled person is not reduced; rather, the misleader receives an additional equivalent portion. A well-known hadith of the Prophet Muḥammad confirms this principle: “Whoever calls to guidance will have a reward similar to the reward of those who follow him, without their rewards being diminished in any respect. And whoever calls to misguidance will have upon him sin similar to the sins of those who follow him, without their sins being diminished in any respect.” This verse and this hadith together form a powerful deterrent for anyone in a position of influence, whether a political leader, a scholar, a media personality, or even a parent or friend. It also serves as a warning against spreading false ideologies, misleading content, or immoral lifestyles. In the Meccan context, the chieftains who actively worked to prevent people from embracing Islam would bear not only their own disbelief but also some of the burdens of those they successfully kept in idolatry. The verse concludes with an exclamation: “Evil indeed is what they will bear!” This emphasises the horror of that load. The word “evil” here is a judgement on the terrible nature of the burden itself. Reflecting on this verse should make a believer deeply cautious about what they call others to, and should inspire a sense of responsibility to only promote that which brings true guidance. Conversely, it offers hope to those who call to good, knowing that their reward multiplies without diminishing the rewards of others.
26 | Indeed, those before them had plotted, but Allah struck at the ˹very˺ foundation of their structure, so the roof collapsed on top of them, and the torment came upon them from where they did not expect. | This verse provides a historical warning by referencing the fate of previous nations who plotted against their messengers. “Those before them” refers to earlier peoples like the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and Pharaoh’s folk, who conspired to undermine the prophets’ missions and maintain their systems of disbelief. The Quraysh, who were actively plotting against Muḥammad, are reminded that they are not the first to try such schemes. The nature of their plot is described with a powerful metaphor: “Allah struck at the very foundation of their structure.” The schemers built their power, their arguments, and their society upon a foundation of falsehood. They thought their structure was solid, but Allah targeted the very base of it, which represents the core assumptions and pillars of their system. By destroying the foundation, the entire edifice collapsed. “So the roof collapsed on top of them” indicates that the destruction came from their own structure turning against them. The thing they had built for their protection and dominance became the instrument of their ruin. This is a precise depiction of how divine punishment often operates: it turns the schemes of the wicked back upon themselves. The verse then adds, “and the torment came upon them from where they did not expect.” Their calculations never included the possibility of divine intervention. They had planned for every human threat but were completely blindsided by the judgement of Allah, which arrived from an angle they had not considered. This was literally true for many past nations that were destroyed by earthquakes, storms, or plagues, and metaphorically true for the Quraysh, who would eventually face the Muslim conquest, a turn of events they never imagined. The verse serves as both a warning and a comfort. It warns the plotters of Mecca that their intelligence and resources are useless if Allah opposes them. It comforts the Prophet and the believers that no matter how elaborate the schemes of the enemy appear, Allah can destroy them from their foundations in an instant and from a direction they never anticipated. In a modern context, it cautions those who place their trust in elaborate worldly systems built on injustice; these systems carry the seeds of their own destruction, and when Allah’s judgement comes, it will collapse upon its architects in ways they could never have foreseen.
27 | Then on the Day of Judgment He will humiliate them and say, “Where are My ˹so-called˺ associate-gods for whose sake you used to oppose ˹the believers˺?” Those gifted with knowledge will say, “Surely disgrace and misery today are upon the disbelievers.” | This verse moves from the worldly punishment of past plotters to the eschatological punishment awaiting all who follow their path. “Then on the Day of Judgment He will humiliate them” uses a verb that implies open, public disgrace. The humiliation is not merely physical pain but psychological and social degradation before all of creation. The divine question, “Where are My so-called associate-gods for whose sake you used to oppose the believers?” is a devastating rebuke. The phrase “My so-called associate-gods” is deeply ironic; they are called “associate-gods” with a sarcastic reference to the polytheists’ false claim, and the possessive “My” mocks the idea that Allah has any partners. The question reminds them that their entire worldly conflict with the believers was rooted in their insistence on defending gods that do not exist. They persecuted the believers, opposed the truth, and waged war against the Prophet for the sake of these fabricated deities. Now, in their hour of greatest need, those gods are nowhere to be found. The believers, who were once the objects of their opposition, will witness this humiliation, turning the tables completely. The verse then introduces the statement of “those gifted with knowledge,” referring to the prophets, the angels, or the knowledgeable believers. They will declare, “Surely disgrace and misery today are upon the disbelievers.” This is not a personal gloating but a factual recognition of divine justice. The knowledge they possessed in the world about the truth of tawḥīd and the Hereafter is now vindicated. Their statement serves as a divine seal upon the disbelievers’ state. The disgrace is the inner shame and external dishonour, and the misery is the suffering of the Fire. This verse offers profound comfort to believers who face mockery and opposition for their faith. It assures them that the present situation, where the arrogant appear powerful and the believers appear weak, is temporary. The Day will come when the roles are reversed in the most absolute sense, and the humiliation of the disbelievers will be a public, eternal reality. It also instills in the believer a sense of the worthlessness of the false gods of this world—whether idols, ideologies, or egos—for which so many conflicts are fought. All of them will vanish, leaving their devotees in disgrace.
28 | Those whose souls the angels seize while they wrong themselves will then offer ˹full˺ submission ˹and say falsely,˺ “We did not do any evil.” ˹The angels will say,˺ “No! Surely Allah fully knows what you used to do. | This verse zooms in on the moment of death for the disbelievers described earlier. “Those whose souls the angels seize while they wrong themselves” paints a terrifying scene. The seizing of the soul is the moment of death, and it is carried out by the angels of death. The phrase “while they wrong themselves” indicates that at the moment of death, they are still in a state of persistent wrongdoing. They spent their lives in disbelief, oppression, and sin, and they die without having repented. The Arabic verb for seizing implies a forceful, harsh extraction, unlike the gentle taking of the believers’ souls that will be described later in the sūrah. As death arrives and the reality of the Hereafter becomes undeniable, the disbeliever makes a desperate attempt at denial and false repentance: “they will then offer full submission and say falsely, ‘We did not do any evil.’” Their sudden submission is not sincere faith; it is the compelled recognition of the truth when it is too late. Their claim that they did no evil is a blatant lie, an attempt to escape the consequences by denying the very actions that are now about to be judged. However, the angels reply with a decisive rejection: “No! Surely Allah fully knows what you used to do.” The angels function as divine agents who speak the truth that cuts through the disbeliever’s falsehood. The phrase “fully knows” emphasises the completeness and perfection of Allah’s knowledge; every deed, every word, every hidden intention is recorded. There is no room for argument or appeal. This verse exposes a tragic human tendency: to deny one’s sins until the last moment, and even then to try to wriggle out of responsibility. It is a stark warning that deathbed repentance, when the soul is being seized and the unseen becomes seen, is not accepted, because it is not voluntary faith but forced confession. True repentance must occur before the throes of death. The verse also highlights the mercy inherent in Allah’s system; the fact that He fully knows means that justice will be perfect. No one will be wronged, and no cunning liar will escape. For believers, the verse is a powerful motivator to live with constant awareness of death and to avoid the false comfort of thinking one can postpone reform until old age, because death might seize them while they are still wronging themselves.
29 | So enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever. Indeed, what an evil home for the arrogant! | This verse delivers the final sentence of the scene begun in the previous verse. After the angels refute the disbeliever’s false denial, they pronounce the judgement: “So enter the gates of Hell, to stay there forever.” The command “enter” is one of utter contempt and finality. There is no delay, no second chance, no court of appeal. The “gates of Hell” indicates that Hell has multiple entrances, perhaps corresponding to different categories of sinners or different levels of punishment. The phrase “to stay there forever” seals their fate eternally. The punishment is not temporary reformatory discipline; it is permanent banishment and torment because their state at death was one of irreversible rejection and arrogance. The verse then closes with a powerful exclamation: “Indeed, what an evil home for the arrogant!” This explicitly links the eternal punishment to the sin of arrogance, which has been a recurring theme. The disbelievers did not merely make an intellectual error; they were mustakbirīn, those who thought themselves too great to bow to their Creator. Their arrogance made them reject the truth when it came, mock the messengers, and persecute the believers. Now, the home prepared for them is described as “evil,” a word that encompasses all the horror, suffering, and degradation of Hell. The rhetorical exclamation invites the listener to truly contemplate the awfulness of that destination. This verse serves as the ultimate warning against pride. In Islam, even a particle of arrogance is enough to bar one from Paradise, as stated in a hadith. The arrogant person’s insistence on their own greatness is the direct opposite of the attitude required to enter Paradise, which is humility, submission, and recognition of one’s utter dependence on Allah. In the context of the sūrah, this verse concludes the section on the fate of the disbelievers who denied and mocked, showing that their worldly arrogance will result in eternal humiliation. For the believer, this should prompt a constant internal struggle against arrogance in all its forms, whether it is pride in knowledge, wealth, lineage, or piety. The realisation that Hell is the evil home for the arrogant should make the heart tremble and seek refuge in humility, which is the true station of the servant before the Master.
30 | And ˹when˺ it is said to those mindful ˹of Allah˺, “What has your Lord revealed?” They say, “All the best!” For those who do good in this world, there is goodness. But far better is the ˹eternal˺ Home of the Hereafter. How excellent indeed is the home of the righteous: | This verse presents the beautiful contrast to verse 24, where the disbelievers called the revelation “ancient fables.” The same question is posed: “What has your Lord revealed?” But this time it is asked to “those mindful of Allah,” the muttaqīn, the God-conscious believers. Their answer is immediate and exuberant: “All the best!” The word khayr, translated as “all the best,” encompasses every type of goodness: truth, guidance, mercy, wisdom, justice, and blessing. The believers recognise the revelation for what it truly is because their hearts are pure and receptive. They do not parse it to find fault; they embrace it wholly as a gift from their Lord. The difference in responses is entirely a function of the state of the heart. The verse then expands on the reward of such people: “For those who do good in this world, there is goodness.” The phrase “do good” translates aḥsanu, which implies excellence in action, doing things beautifully and with full dedication. For them, in this very world, there is ḥasanah, a good life. This includes inner peace, contentment, provision, and the sweetness of faith. The verse immediately directs attention upward: “But far better is the eternal Home of the Hereafter.” The goodness of this world, however wonderful, is nothing compared to the reward of the next life. The comparative “far better” dismisses any attachment that might make a believer satisfied only with worldly goodness. The Hereafter is the ultimate goal. The verse closes with an exclamation: “How excellent indeed is the home of the righteous!” The word for “righteous” connects back to mindfulness. The home is the dwelling place of the righteous, which the next verses will describe. This exclamation is an invitation to marvel at and yearn for that home. The entire verse is a model of the believer’s psychology: immediate recognition of revealed truth, a life of excellence that brings worldly goodness, but a constant orientation towards the far greater reward of the Hereafter. The believer is not a pessimist who despises all worldly good, nor a hedonist who is satisfied with it. They receive the good of this life with gratitude, but their hearts are always beating for the eternal home.
31 | the Gardens of Eternity which they will enter, under which rivers flow. In it they will have whatever they desire. This is how Allah rewards the righteous— | This verse begins with a description of that excellent home mentioned at the end of the previous verse. “The Gardens of Eternity” translates Jannātu ʿAdn, the gardens of perpetual residence. The word ʿAdn implies a settled, enduring abode from which one is never expelled. This contrasts starkly with the temporary homes of this world and with the eternal but horrific home of the arrogant in Hell. The phrase “which they will enter” indicates full and unhindered access. In this world, even the most beautiful gardens have gates and restrictions; in Paradise, the righteous are welcomed as rightful inheritors. The imagery “under which rivers flow” is a recurring Qur’anic description of Paradise. It evokes coolness, abundance, and life. The rivers flow beneath their palaces and gardens, providing a continuous, effortless source of water, milk, honey, and wine of a pure nature. This was especially appealing to the desert-dwelling Arabs, for whom a garden with flowing rivers represented the pinnacle of comfort and luxury. The verse then adds, “In it they will have whatever they desire.” This is a staggering promise. Every legitimate desire of the human soul, from the most basic to the most sublime, will be fulfilled. There is no deprivation, no unrequited longing, and no boredom. The human capacity to desire is vast, and Paradise will satisfy it completely. Importantly, the desires of the righteous in Paradise will themselves be purified, so they will only desire what is beautiful and good. The verse concludes the description by stating, “This is how Allah rewards the righteous.” The word “righteous” again references the muttaqīn. The reward is not an arbitrary favour; it is the just and generous recompense for their mindfulness and good deeds in the world. The phrase “this is how” emphasizes that the magnificent reward just described is the consistent pattern of Allah’s treatment of the righteous; it is His sunnah. The verse functions as a powerful motivator: when the believer hears that the righteous will have whatever they desire in eternal gardens, the temporary allure of sinful desires in this world loses its grip. The delayed gratification of Paradise is infinitely better than the immediate gratification of forbidden things. It also instills a profound sense of hope and longing, making the daily struggle to be mindful of Allah feel light, because the destination is so overwhelmingly beautiful.
32 | those whose souls the angels take while they are virtuous, saying ˹to them˺, “Peace be upon you! Enter Paradise for what you used to do.” | This verse continues the description of the righteous’ reward by shifting back to the moment of death, creating a direct parallel with the earlier description of the disbelievers’ death in verse 28. “Those whose souls the angels take while they are virtuous” presents a scene of serenity and honour. The Arabic word for “virtuous,” ṭayyibīn, means pure, good, and pleasant. The believers die in a state of spiritual purity, free from the major sins from which they have repented, and full of faith. The angels who take their souls come with gentleness and mercy. This is a stark contrast to the harsh seizure of the disbelievers’ souls. The angels then greet the believing soul with “Peace be upon you!” This is the salutation of Paradise, a word that conveys safety, peace, and the removal of all fear and grief. The angels are not accusers but welcomers, announcing the soul’s secure passage. Then comes the invitation: “Enter Paradise for what you used to do.” The command to enter Paradise is given even at the moment of death, as the soul begins its journey to the Barzakh, the intermediate realm, where it will experience a foretaste of the eternal bliss. The phrase “for what you used to do” establishes the principle of reward based on deeds, all within the framework of Allah’s mercy. Their entry is not arbitrary; it is the direct consequence of their righteous actions, which were themselves enabled by Allah’s grace. This does not contradict the hadith that no one enters Paradise by deeds alone, but by Allah’s mercy; rather, deeds are the visible expression of faith and the means by which mercy is manifested in degrees of reward. This verse brings immense comfort to believers. The thought that the angels, those noble and pure beings, will greet a dying believer with peace and an invitation to Paradise eases the fear of death. It encourages the believer to strive to be among the ṭayyibīn, those whose inner and outer states are pure, so that when the angels come, they find a soul that is pleasant to receive. The stark contrast between this scene and the previous scene of the angels seizing the disbelievers highlights the dual outcome and reinforces the sūrah’s central theme: gratitude and mindfulness lead to peace and eternal bliss, while arrogance and denial lead to humiliation and eternal punishment.
33 | Are they only awaiting the coming of the angels or the command of your Lord ˹O Prophet˺? So were those before them. And Allah never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves. | After detailing the contrasting fates of the disbelievers and the righteous, the sūrah now directly challenges the disbelievers’ complacency. “Are they only awaiting the coming of the angels or the command of your Lord?” The question is rhetorical and carries a tone of warning. The disbelievers are not expecting the truth to come to them through reflection on the signs; they are simply waiting, in their heedlessness, for one of two things: the arrival of the angel of death to seize their souls, or the final command of Allah, which could be the general judgement of the Hour or a specific punishment in this world. Both of these events will mark the end of their opportunity to believe. The question implies that they are living on borrowed time, postponing faith until a moment when it will be too late. The verse then adds, “So were those before them.” This is a brief but heavy statement. Previous nations also delayed, mocked, and waited, and then the angels or the divine command came upon them suddenly and destroyed them. The Quraysh were walking the same path. Then the verse establishes a crucial principle of divine justice: “And Allah never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.” The punishment that overtakes them is not an act of injustice from Allah. He does not wrong anyone, not even by an atom’s weight. The wrong they suffer is entirely self-inflicted. They wronged themselves by rejecting the truth, by worshipping false gods, by burying their daughters alive, and by mocking the messengers. They were given clear signs, sound intellect, and free will. Their destruction is the natural consequence of their own choices. This principle is vital because some might see the punishment described in the sūrah and mistakenly think of Allah as vengeful or cruel. The Qur’an repeatedly clarifies that Allah’s punishment is a manifestation of justice and is always preceded by ample warning and opportunity. For contemporary believers, this verse is a call to urgency. We do not know when the angels will come for us personally or when the major signs of the Hour will commence. The attitude of “awaiting” while remaining in sin is spiritually deadly. We must not postpone repentance and reform. The verse also provides comfort: we can trust that Allah’s judgement, however severe, is perfectly just, and no one is ever punished unfairly. This frees the believer from resentment against divine decree and encourages a focus on rectifying one’s own self, which is the only source of potential ruin.
34 | Then the evil ˹consequences˺ of their deeds overtook them, and they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule. | This verse summarises the fate of the past nations and, by extension, warns of what awaits the Quraysh. “Then the evil consequences of their deeds overtook them.” The word “consequences” translates sayyi’āt, which can mean the bad results or the punishment for evil deeds. Their own actions generated a destructive force that eventually caught up with them. The verb “overtook” implies that they could not outrun it; it enveloped them completely. No amount of wealth, power, or plotting could shield them once the appointed time arrived. The second part of the verse adds a layer of poetic justice: “and they were overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule.” The very thing they mocked—the warnings of the prophets about divine punishment—became their reality. They used to laugh at the idea that stones from the sky or an earthquake would destroy them. They ridiculed the notion of a Day of Judgement. Then that mockery turned into their actual, lived nightmare. This is a recurring theme: the thing a people mockingly dismiss often becomes the instrument of their destruction, because their mockery reflects a profound lack of seriousness about truth, which leads them to ignore all warnings until it is too late. For the Quraysh, they ridiculed the Prophet’s warnings of punishment, calling them ancient fables. This verse foretells that the same will happen to them, and indeed, the Battle of Badr saw the Quraysh leaders who most viciously mocked Islam being killed and humiliated. In a broader sense, the verse describes a universal law of history. Civilisations that become arrogant and mock the moral laws of Allah eventually collapse under the weight of their own corruption. The believer takes this as a sobering reminder never to mock the signs of Allah, the warnings of scripture, or the idea of accountability. Even a casual joke about hellfire or judgement can be a symptom of a diseased heart. The verse also teaches that sin is not just a private matter; it has accumulating consequences that can overtake a person or a society, often when they least expect it. The only protection is sincere faith and constant turning to Allah, so that one is not among those overwhelmed by what they once ridiculed.
35 | The polytheists argue, “Had Allah willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have worshipped anything other than Him, nor prohibited anything without His command.” So did those before them. Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly? | This verse records one of the most famous and deceitful arguments used by the Quraysh to justify their polytheism. They argued using divine decree as an excuse: “Had Allah willed, neither we nor our forefathers would have worshipped anything other than Him, nor prohibited anything without His command.” The logic, on the surface, suggests that their idol worship must be acceptable to Allah because Allah, being all-powerful, could have prevented it if He disliked it. This is a classic case of misusing the concept of divine will to evade moral responsibility. They attempted to shift the blame onto Allah, implying that He approved of their shirk. The verse immediately undercuts this argument with two responses. First, “So did those before them.” This argument is not new; previous nations used the exact same excuse to reject their messengers. This shows that it is a hollow, recycled justification rather than a sincere search for truth. Second, the verse asks rhetorically, “Is not the messengers’ duty only to deliver the message clearly?” This clarifies the true role of the prophets. The messengers were sent to convey the divine message with clarity, to warn and to give glad tidings. Allah’s will to permit something to occur is not the same as His approval of it. Allah wills, by His universal creative will, the existence of disbelief and idol worship as a test for humanity, but He does not love it or command it. The sending of messengers to prohibit these very practices is the proof that Allah disapproves of them. The presence of a clear prohibition through the messengers completely refutes their argument. If Allah were pleased with their idol worship, He would not have sent a messenger commanding them to stop it. The verse thus distinguishes between Allah’s permissive will and His legislative will. This argument is still used today by some who claim that everything that happens is God’s will, so they are not responsible for their actions. The Qur’anic response is that God’s giving us free will and allowing us to choose does not mean He endorses our choices; He has clearly communicated what is right and wrong through revelation, and we will be judged by that standard. The verse also liberates the Prophet from feeling responsible for their rejection, reminding him that his task is only clear delivery.
36 | We surely sent a messenger to every community, saying, “Worship Allah and shun false gods.” But some of them were guided by Allah, while others were destined to stray. So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers! | This verse expands on the principle of clear delivery mentioned in the previous verse. “We surely sent a messenger to every community” establishes the universality of divine guidance. No nation in history was left without a warner, a messenger who spoke their language and conveyed the core message. The content of that universal message is succinctly stated: “Worship Allah and shun false gods.” The Arabic for false gods is ṭāghūt, which encompasses everything worshipped or obeyed besides Allah, including idols, tyrants, and satanic forces. The command is a positive call to worship Allah and a negative call to avoid all false objects of worship. This is the essence of every prophetic mission. The verse then describes the two-fold response: “But some of them were guided by Allah, while others were destined to stray.” The guidance mentioned is the special guidance that results from a person’s sincere openness to the truth. Allah guided them because they were receptive. The straying of others is described as a just consequence of their rejection and arrogance; they became subject to misguidance because of their own choices. This does not imply that Allah randomly misguides people; rather, those who persistently reject the clear message are left to stray by Allah’s just decree. The verse then invites the skeptical Quraysh to empirical investigation: “So travel throughout the land and see the fate of the deniers!” The ruins of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Lot were visible along the Arabian trade routes. The Quraysh passed by these ruins during their winter and summer journeys. This is an appeal to tangible historical evidence. The fate of the deniers is written in the archaeological record of the desert. The command to travel and observe is a recurring Qur’anic method of proof. It turns history into a teacher. In a contemporary context, while we may not see the exact ruins described, the principle of studying history to see the consequences of denying truth remains valid. The rise and fall of civilizations that rejected moral and spiritual truth is evident. The verse also instills a sense of humility: the same message of tawḥīd that we have received has been sent to all humanity across time. We are part of a long chain of communities tested with the same call, and we should learn from the fate of those before us rather than repeating their mistakes.
37 | Even though you ˹O Prophet˺ are keen on their guidance, Allah certainly does not guide those He leaves to stray, and they will have no helpers. | This verse is a direct address to the Prophet Muḥammad, peace be upon him, comforting him and explaining a fundamental reality of guidance. The Prophet was deeply grieved by the stubborn rejection of his people. He was ḥarīṣ, intensely eager and keen, for their guidance. His compassion for his community drove him to exhaust himself in calling them to the truth. The verse acknowledges this sincere concern but then draws a divine boundary: “Allah certainly does not guide those He leaves to stray.” The Arabic construction implies that when Allah, as a result of their persistent, defiant rejection, has decreed misguidance for a person, that misguidance becomes a fixed state that the Prophet’s keenness cannot reverse. The verse is not saying that the Prophet should stop caring or preaching; it is saying that the outcome is ultimately in Allah’s hands, not his. This prevents the preacher from falling into despair or taking personal responsibility for the rejection of others. The phrase “and they will have no helpers” underlines the finality of their situation. On the Day of Judgement, or even in this world when punishment descends, they will find no one to assist them against Allah. Their idols, their wealth, their allies—all will be powerless. This verse is crucial for understanding the Islamic doctrine of guidance. It balances the emphasis on human effort with the absolute sovereignty of Allah. The Prophet is commanded to strive and convey, but he cannot force faith into hearts. The verse is also a warning to the rejecters: there is a point of no return where the heart becomes so sealed that even the most sincere teacher cannot affect it. The hope for change lies in oneself, in softening the heart before it hardens completely. For modern duʿāt, preachers, and concerned believers, this verse is a source of solace. It teaches that one can care deeply for the guidance of others, but one must not transgress the limits of one’s role. The result is in Allah’s hands. This fosters a healthy detachment that prevents burnout and allows one to continue one’s work with a balanced spirit. It also warns against the arrogance of thinking that we can guide whom we love; even the Prophet could not guide his beloved uncle Abū Ṭālib, despite his deep keenness. Guidance is a treasure that Allah bestows upon whom He wills.
38 | They swear by Allah their most solemn oaths that Allah will never raise the dead to life. Yes ˹He will˺! It is a true promise binding on Him, but most people do not know. | This verse exposes one of the core doctrines of the Meccan pagans: their denial of the resurrection. They would “swear by Allah their most solemn oaths,” meaning they invoked Allah’s name with the strongest possible emphasis, to assert that there is no life after death. The irony is that they swore by Allah to deny a power of Allah. They used the name of the Creator to claim that the Creator cannot recreate His creation. Their oath was not based on evidence but on a materialist assumption that the decay of the body made resurrection impossible. The verse responds with a single emphatic word: “Yes He will!” The Arabic balā is a forceful affirmative that refutes their negation. The resurrection will most certainly occur. Then the verse describes it as “a true promise binding on Him.” The resurrection is a divine promise, and Allah’s promise is truth. The phrase “binding on Him” does not mean that anyone forces Allah; it means that Allah, in His perfect wisdom and justice, has obligated Himself to fulfill this promise. This is an act of His mercy and justice, for without resurrection, the moral order of the universe would be incomplete. The verse closes with “but most people do not know.” The knowledge referred to is not mere intellectual recognition but a deep, transformative awareness that shapes behaviour. Most people, including the oath-swearers, do not truly grasp the reality of the resurrection. If they did, they would not swear against it, and they would live their lives in preparation for it. The ignorance is both of Allah’s power—that He who created from nothing can easily recreate—and of the purpose of life. This verse is a recurring theme in the Meccan sūrahs, which place great emphasis on establishing the reality of the Hereafter. The denial of resurrection was not just a theoretical error; it was the linchpin of their immoral lifestyle. If there is no accountability, one can live selfishly and oppress others without consequence. The affirmation of the resurrection is therefore the foundation of moral responsibility. For the modern believer, this verse strengthens conviction in the face of materialist worldviews that reduce existence to what is observable. The resurrection is a promise of the All-Powerful, and reflecting on the signs in creation, as the sūrah does, reinforces that the One who fashioned the universe from nothing can and will recreate humanity.
39 | ˹He will do that˺ to make clear to them what they disagreed on, and for the disbelievers to know that they were liars. | This verse provides the wisdom behind the resurrection. The pronoun “He will do that” refers back to the act of raising the dead. The primary purpose is “to make clear to them what they disagreed on.” Humanity has been in perpetual disagreement over the most fundamental truths: the nature of God, the purpose of life, the reality of the afterlife, and moral values. In this world, these disagreements persist, and the truth, though established by revelation, is often obscured by argument, propaganda, and stubbornness. The Day of Judgement is the day of ultimate clarity. Every hidden truth will become manifest. Those who worshiped false gods will see the falsehood of their beliefs. Those who denied the prophets will see their vindication. The disagreements that filled the earth with conflict and confusion will be resolved not by debate but by undeniable reality. The second purpose is specifically directed at the disbelievers: “and for the disbelievers to know that they were liars.” During their worldly life, they called the messengers liars, they called the revelation lies, and they themselves fabricated lies about Allah, claiming He had partners or daughters. On that Day, their own inner state and the manifest reality will testify that they were the true liars all along. This is the poetic justice mentioned earlier. Their false claims will be exposed before all of creation, adding public humiliation to their private regret. The verse highlights that divine punishment is not arbitrary; it is integrally connected to truth and justice. The resurrection is the great clarifying event that resolves the ambiguities of the world. For believers, this verse provides immense comfort. In a world where falsehood often appears triumphant and truth is maligned, the promise that one day everything will be made clear and the liars will be exposed is a source of patience and hope. It also motivates the believer to be scrupulously truthful in all matters, knowing that every statement and belief will one day be subjected to that ultimate clarification. The fear of being exposed as a liar on that Day should be enough to make a person flee from falsehood in all its forms.
40 | If We ever will something ˹to exist˺, all We say is: “Be!” And it is! | This powerful verse concludes the discussion on resurrection by addressing the core doubt of the deniers: how can decayed bodies be brought back to life? The answer is a profound statement about the nature of divine creative power. “If We ever will something to exist” indicates that whenever Allah intends a thing, no matter how impossible it may seem to human minds, the process of its creation is effortless. “All We say is: ‘Be!’ And it is!” The command “Be” (kun) is a single word that brings a thing into existence instantaneously. There is no gradual process, no raw material requirement, no tool, no assistance. This command is not a vocal utterance in the human sense; it is the expression of His will that directly translates into the existence of the intended thing. The verb “it is” (fayakūn) indicates immediate, inevitable realisation. The difficulty of recreating a decomposed body, which the polytheists considered impossible, is no obstacle to the One whose mode of creation is the simple command “Be.” This verse is one of the most concise and powerful expressions of Allah’s omnipotence in the Qur’an. It demolishes the materialist objection by establishing that the creation of the universe and the resurrection of humanity operate on the same principle: the divine will. The creation of the heavens and the earth from nothing, which was even more staggering than resurrection, was accomplished by the same “Be.” The verse also has a deep theological implication: it shows that the relationship between cause and effect in the natural world is not autonomous; it is sustained moment by moment by Allah’s command. Natural processes are simply the habitual pattern of the “Be” command. When Allah wills a miracle or the breaking of the natural order, the same “Be” suffices. For the believer, this verse is a source of immense hope and trust. When facing a seemingly impossible situation, one remembers that the One who merely says “Be” to bring things into existence can resolve any difficulty. It also fosters humility: all of creation is utterly contingent, existing only because Allah says “Be”. If He withheld that command for an instant, everything would cease to be. The verse thus invites the mind to transcend the limits of human causality and rest in the unlimited power of the Creator, a power that guarantees the resurrection of the dead with the same effortless command that first brought them to life.
41 | As for those who emigrated in ˹the cause of˺ Allah after being persecuted, We will surely bless them with a good home in this world. But the reward of the Hereafter is far better, if only they knew. | After the powerful affirmation of divine creative power and the certainty of resurrection, the sūrah now turns to a practical example of true faith in action, the emigration of the oppressed believers. The verse begins, “As for those who emigrated in the cause of Allah after being persecuted,” pointing directly to the early Muslims in Mecca who suffered severe torture, social boycott, and economic strangulation at the hands of the Quraysh. The phrase “in the cause of Allah” makes it clear that their emigration was not for worldly gain, trade, or personal comfort, but solely to preserve their faith and worship their Lord freely. This refers first and foremost to the group of Muslims who migrated to Abyssinia, seeking refuge under the just Christian king, the Negus. Later, this verse would also apply to the Hijrah to Medina. “After being persecuted” highlights that they did not leave out of weakness or cowardice; they endured immense hardship until it became unbearable and they were commanded or permitted to leave. The divine promise follows immediately: “We will surely bless them with a good home in this world.” The Arabic word for a good home, mubawwa’an ḥasanan, indicates a safe, pleasant dwelling place where they can practice their religion without fear. For the emigrants to Abyssinia, they found exactly that: protection and peace under a benevolent ruler who respected their faith. For those who migrated to Medina, the city became their blessed new home and the launching place of the Islamic state. This promise was a direct counter to the Quraysh’s attempts to uproot Islam by making life impossible for its adherents. Yet the verse immediately elevates the believer’s perspective: “But the reward of the Hereafter is far better, if only they knew.” The good home in this world is a foretaste and a blessing, but it pales in comparison to the eternal gardens awaiting them. The phrase “if only they knew” refers to those whose vision is limited to this world, including the Quraysh who thought they were inflicting a great defeat on the Muslims. In reality, the emigrants were trading temporary hardship for eternal bliss. This verse establishes a core principle for all generations: when a believer sacrifices worldly comfort for the sake of Allah, He compensates them generously in this life and immeasurably in the next. The migration of oppressed Muslims today, whether from war or persecution, echoes this promise. The good home might be a place of physical safety, but the far better reward remains the ultimate hope, keeping the heart steady amid the trials of displacement.
42 | ˹It is˺ they who have patiently endured, and in their Lord they put their trust. | This verse provides the spiritual profile of the emigrants mentioned in the previous verse, linking their reward directly to two foundational qualities of the believer. “It is they who have patiently endured” begins with the word “they,” emphasizing that the ones receiving the good home in this world and the far better reward in the Hereafter are precisely those characterised by patience and trust. The Arabic word for “patiently endured” is ṣabarū, derived from ṣabr, a comprehensive term that includes perseverance in obedience, restraint from sin, and steadfastness during hardship. The emigrants demonstrated all three. They patiently endured the physical torture, the verbal abuse, the economic boycott, and the emotional pain of leaving their homeland, their families, and their property behind. They did not retaliate in kind when they were weak, they did not abandon their faith when pressured, and they remained obedient to Allah’s command to migrate despite the immense difficulty. This patience was not passive resignation but active, hopeful steadfastness. The second quality is “and in their Lord they put their trust.” This tawakkul, or complete reliance upon Allah, was the engine that powered their patience. They trusted that Allah’s command to emigrate, whether to an unknown land or to face an uncertain future, was for their ultimate good. They placed their trust in their Lord alone, not in their own strength, their tribal affiliations, or even in the protection of a foreign king, though they used these means. Their hearts were anchored in the certainty that Allah’s plan is perfect. The combination of ṣabr and tawakkul is the hallmark of the successful believer throughout the Qur’an. Patience without trust can become bitter and despairing; trust without patience can be a shallow claim that collapses under pressure. Together they form an unshakeable foundation. For the Prophet’s Companions, this verse was a badge of honour, a divine commendation that validated their suffering. For contemporary Muslims facing trials, whether personal, social, or political, this verse offers the blueprint for navigating hardship: couple patient endurance with absolute trust in Allah, and the promise of a good outcome in both worlds will be fulfilled. The verse also serves as a quiet rebuke to those who claim faith but panic or abandon principles at the first sign of difficulty. True emigration of the soul from sin to obedience also requires this patient trust, making the verse applicable far beyond physical migration.
43 | We did not send ˹messengers˺ before you ˹O Prophet˺ except mere men inspired by Us. If you ˹polytheists˺ do not know ˹this already˺, then ask those who have knowledge ˹of the Scriptures˺. | The sūrah now addresses a primary objection of the Meccan polytheists to the Prophet Muḥammad’s message: their insistence that a messenger should be an angel, not a mere human being who eats, walks in the markets, and has a family. The verse begins with the clear, universal principle: “We did not send messengers before you O Prophet except mere men inspired by Us.” This statement refutes their objection by placing Muḥammad squarely in the line of all previous prophets. Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, peace be upon them all, were human beings, not angels. They ate food, married, and lived among their people. The distinguishing feature of a messenger is not a non-human nature but divine inspiration, the waḥy that descends upon them. The phrase “inspired by Us” emphasizes that the source of their authority and knowledge is not their own human intellect but the revelation from Allah. The verse then turns the argument back on the polytheists: “If you polytheists do not know this already, then ask those who have knowledge of the Scriptures.” This refers to the Jewish and Christian scholars who were present in Arabia and with whom the Quraysh had trade contact. These “People of the Book” could confirm that all prophets sent by God were human beings. The Quraysh, being an unlettered people without a revealed scripture of their own, could not claim expertise in matters of prophethood. By directing them to ask the knowledgeable, the Qur’an appeals to an external, historically verifiable source. It also implicitly acknowledges the continuity between the Abrahamic faiths on this fundamental point. The verse thus uses a rational and historical argument to dismantle the objection. It also carries a subtle lesson about the nature of prophethood: humanity is guided by one of their own, someone who understands their struggles, speaks their language, and serves as a relatable model. An angel could not be the model for human conduct. The verse reinforces the status of the Prophet Muḥammad while humbling the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their intelligence, by telling them to learn from others if they are ignorant. In modern times, the objection that religious leaders are “just men” is similarly answered by the fact that divine guidance has always been delivered through human channels, which is a mercy, not a deficiency.
44 | ˹We sent them˺ with clear proofs and divine Books. And We have sent down to you ˹O Prophet˺ the Reminder, so that you may explain to people what has been revealed for them, and perhaps they will reflect. | This verse continues the description of the messengers, adding the substance of their mission. “We sent them with clear proofs and divine Books.” The “clear proofs” refer to the miracles and compelling signs that validated their prophethood, such as the staff of Moses or the healing of Jesus, and also the rational and moral arguments contained in their messages. The “divine Books” are the scriptures revealed to them, such as the Torah, the Gospel, and earlier scrolls. Thus, every messenger came with both tangible signs and written guidance. The verse then transitions to the Prophet Muḥammad: “And We have sent down to you O Prophet the Reminder, so that you may explain to people what has been revealed for them.” The “Reminder” is a beautiful name for the Qur’an, implying that it awakens the primordial knowledge of God that is embedded in the human soul. The primary function of the Prophet is here defined as explaining the revelation. This is the foundation of the Sunnah: the Prophet’s words, actions, and approvals are the divinely authorised clarification of the Qur’anic text. Without the Prophet’s explanation, many commands would remain ambiguous. For example, the Qur’an commands prayer and charity, but the details of how many prayers, at what times, and the exact amounts of zakah are provided by the Prophet’s teaching. The verse also clarifies the purpose of this entire revelatory process: “and perhaps they will reflect.” The revelation and its explanation are given so that people might engage in deep thought, contemplation, and internalization. The goal is not blind acceptance but reflective understanding that leads to faith and action. The word “perhaps” in the Arabic context expresses hope and expectation, not doubt. Allah sends down the Reminder and the explainer with the confident hope that receptive hearts will reflect and be guided. This verse elevates the role of the Prophet as the divinely appointed exegete and establishes the inseparable link between the Qur’an and the Sunnah. It also calls contemporary believers to approach the Qur’an with the explanation of the Prophet, not relying solely on private interpretation detached from the prophetic context. The ultimate aim remains reflection, a deep engagement of the mind and heart that transforms the reader, making the Reminder a living source of guidance rather than a merely recited text.
45 | Do those who devise evil plots feel secure that Allah will not cause the earth to swallow them? Or that the torment will not come upon them in ways they cannot comprehend? | The sūrah returns to warning the Meccan plotters with a series of rhetorical questions that instil fear and uncertainty. The verse begins, “Do those who devise evil plots feel secure…” This refers directly to the Quraysh leaders who were constantly scheming against the Prophet and the Muslims. Their plots included plans to imprison, exile, or kill the Prophet, as well as efforts to discourage pilgrims from listening to his message. The word “evil plots” carries the connotation of secret, malicious planning done under the cover of darkness. The verse challenges their false sense of security: do they think that their cunning will protect them from divine retribution? The first threatened punishment is “that Allah will not cause the earth to swallow them.” This is not a hypothetical fear but a reference to historical precedents, such as Korah (Qārūn) who was swallowed by the earth for his rebellion against Moses. The earth, which appears stable and solid under their feet, could at any moment become their grave by Allah’s command. The second threat adds: “Or that the torment will not come upon them in ways they cannot comprehend?” This expands the scope of possible punishment beyond the specific image of an earthquake. The torment could come from the sky, from the sea, from a sudden plague, or from an unexpected military defeat, anything that their human minds have not anticipated and against which they have erected no defense. The key phrase is “in ways they cannot comprehend”; their plots are limited to what they can foresee, but Allah’s punishment can come from angles entirely outside their calculation. This is a deeply unsettling warning. It strips the oppressors of the illusion of control. No amount of human planning can guarantee safety from the Creator’s grasp. For the believers, this verse is a source of comfort: the seemingly powerful schemers are utterly vulnerable before Allah. It also serves as a deterrent for anyone who would harm others through secret plots, reminding them that while they may escape human justice, they cannot escape the divine grasp, which may manifest in the most unexpected ways. The verse encourages the believer to live with a sense of awe and caution, never feeling secure from Allah’s plan, for such security is the hallmark of the spiritually heedless.
46 | Or that He will not seize them while they go about ˹their day˺, for then they will have no escape? | This verse adds a third terrifying scenario to the list of possible divine punishments. “Or that He will not seize them while they go about their day” shifts the focus to the suddenness and the mundane context of punishment. The people targeted are those who are going about their daily business, travelling for trade, moving from one place to another, fully absorbed in their worldly affairs, feeling completely safe and secure. The Arabic word taqallubihim suggests their active movement, to and fro, their bustling about in their commercial journeys or their daily routines. It is precisely in the midst of this perceived normalcy and busyness that Allah’s seizure could occur. The verse says, “for then they will have no escape.” The suddenness of the punishment when they are dispersed and unaware makes escape impossible. Unlike a visible advancing army against which one might prepare defences, a divine seizure gives no warning and leaves no room for flight or repulsion. This would resonate deeply with the Quraysh, whose trade caravans were the lifeblood of their economy. The verse implies that on one of their seemingly routine journeys, perhaps through the very deserts they knew so well, Allah’s punishment could overtake them, and their wealth, knowledge of the terrain, and tribal alliances would offer no refuge. This is linked to the preceding verse’s theme: the plotters feel secure, but that security is an illusion. Punishment does not require a dramatic build-up; it can arrive in the midst of the most ordinary moment. The verse is a powerful reminder of human fragility. We tend to feel safe in our routines, assuming that tomorrow will be like today. The Quraysh’s confidence in their trade and their plotting made them deaf to the warning. This verse teaches that no place and no activity provides protection from Allah’s decree. For the believer, this awareness is not meant to induce crippling fear but to cultivate a state of constant humility and dependence. One should not feel immune from trials or punishment simply because life seems calm. The phrase “no escape” underscores the finality of divine judgement when it arrives. The call is to repent and reform before that sudden seizure, from which there is no return, overtakes a person in the middle of their daily business, leaving them with nothing but regret.
47 | Or that He will not destroy them gradually? But your Lord is truly Ever Gracious, Most Merciful. | This verse presents a fourth and psychologically distinct mode of punishment. “Or that He will not destroy them gradually?” The Arabic word takhawwuf, translated as “destroy them gradually,” carries the nuance of causing them to diminish bit by bit, accompanied by fear. It refers to a slow, creeping punishment that erodes their power, wealth, and security over time, rather than a single sudden strike. This could manifest as successive losses, internal divisions, the gradual defection of allies, economic decline, or a series of small defeats that collectively lead to their ruin. The psychological aspect of fear is crucial: they live in a state of constant anxiety, witnessing their own decline without being able to stop it. This gradual punishment can be more agonizing than a swift end, as it gives them time to reflect and perhaps repent, yet their arrogance prevents them, so they suffer prolonged deterioration. After listing these terrifying possibilities, the verse immediately follows with a profound attribution of mercy: “But your Lord is truly Ever Gracious, Most Merciful.” This juxtaposition is stunning. The statement could be read as explaining why the punishment is delayed or graduated; Allah’s ra’fah (ever-graciousness) and raḥmah (mercy) are the reasons the polytheists are not instantly annihilated. Every moment of respite is an act of mercy, an opportunity to turn back. Alternatively, it could mean that despite the severity of the warnings, the overarching attribute governing Allah’s relationship with creation is mercy, and believers should not despair. The combination of warning and mercy is typical of the Qur’an; it balances fear and hope. The Meccan plotters are warned that their continued existence is not a sign of divine approval but of divine forbearance, giving them time to abandon their evil. For the believer, this verse instils a profound appreciation for Allah’s mercy in the very fact that we are given time to repent, that punishment is not immediate. It also warns against misinterpreting a period of ease for the wrongdoers as proof of their safety; they may be on a path of gradual destruction, all while being given the chance to change. The verse fosters a mindset of perpetual vigilance and gratitude, never taking Allah’s forbearance for granted and rushing to repent before the gradual destruction becomes complete.
48 | Have they not considered how the shadows of everything Allah has created incline to the right and the left ˹as the sun moves˺, totally submitting to Allah in all humility? | The sūrah now shifts from warning to a profound reflection on a universal sign of creation that testifies to tawḥīd. The verse asks, “Have they not considered how the shadows of everything Allah has created incline to the right and the left as the sun moves?” The question is an invitation to observe a daily, ordinary phenomenon with spiritual insight. Shadows are formed by objects blocking sunlight, and they shift their direction and length throughout the day, extending to the right and to the left. This movement is not random; it follows the precise laws set by Allah for the sun’s course. The verse states that these shadows are “totally submitting to Allah in all humility.” The submission of a shadow is metaphorical but profound. The shadow has no will of its own; it is completely subjected to the Creator’s command, following the sun’s path in perfect obedience. It prostrates, extending itself on the ground in a posture of humility, and then rises again. This is a silent, visible act of worship performed by all physical objects. The polytheists worshipped created things like the sun and idols, yet here the very shadows of those created things, and even the shadows of the idols themselves, are submitting to Allah alone. The shadow of an idol bows to Allah, not to the idol. This is a powerful refutation of their shirk. The act of noticing shadows and seeing them as signs of divine submission elevates the believer’s daily experience. Every time one sees a shadow, it can be a reminder that all creation is in a state of constant, involuntary worship of the Creator. The phrase “in all humility” translates sujjadan, which is also the word for prostration. The shadows prostrate themselves, shifting from right to left, similar to the movements of a worshipper in prayer. This verse therefore connects the physical world directly to the act of Islamic prayer, showing that the believer, when prostrating, is joining the entire universe in its submission to Allah. The call to “consider” is a call to a conscious, reflective gaze upon nature, transforming the mundane into a source of faith. The verse encourages the scientific observation of nature not for its own sake but to see the Creator’s hand and to take lessons for one’s own voluntary submission.
49 | And to Allah ˹alone˺ bows down ˹in submission˺ whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures, as do the angels—who are not too proud ˹to do so˺. | This verse broadens the scope of submission from shadows to all living creatures and angels. “And to Allah alone bows down in submission whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth of living creatures.” The verb “bows down” (yasjudu) is the same used for ritual prostration. The verse declares that every living creature, from the birds in the sky to the fish in the sea and the beasts on land, is in a constant state of prostration and submission to Allah. This submission is not by rational choice for animals but by their very nature; they follow the instincts and laws programmed into them by their Creator, never deviating from their assigned role. The heavens and the earth encompass all realms, so the entire cosmos is engaged in this worship. The verse then singles out the angels: “as do the angels—who are not too proud to do so.” The mention of angels serves a dual purpose. First, it highlights that even these pure, mighty beings, who are far more powerful and closer to Allah than humans, are not too proud to prostrate. This is a direct contrast with the Meccan pagans, who were too proud to bow to Allah but were willing to bow to idols. Second, the verse refutes their claim that angels are daughters of Allah or independent deities; the angels themselves are humble worshippers of the One God. The phrase “not too proud” echoes the earlier characterisation of the disbelievers as arrogant. The entire universe, animate and inanimate, including the greatest creatures like angels, submits without a trace of pride. Only some humans and jinn, those who choose disbelief, arrogantly refuse to join this universal chorus of submission. This verse profoundly humbles the human being. When a person prostrates in prayer, they are not doing something strange or demeaning; they are finally aligning themselves with the rest of creation. Their refusal to prostrate is the anomaly, the act of rebellion against the natural order. The verse also implicitly argues against any worship directed to these creatures; if everything bows to Allah alone, then nothing else deserves worship. For the believer, observing animals or contemplating the heavens becomes a spiritual exercise, recognising the silent prostration happening all around and joining it willingly and lovingly, not out of compulsion but out of conscious choice.
50 | They fear their Lord above them, and do whatever they are commanded. | This verse specifically refers to the angels mentioned in the previous verse, though some commentators extend the meaning to all creation that submits. The subject “They” points to the angels as the immediate context requires, but it also encompasses the broader reality of all obedient creation. “They fear their Lord above them” describes the angels’ constant state of awe and reverential fear of their Lord, who is above them in rank, power, and majesty. The phrase “above them” signifies Allah’s transcendence and supreme authority over all creatures. The fear mentioned here is not a terror that paralyses but a profound awe and reverence that motivates perfect obedience. This fear is born of deep knowledge of Allah’s greatness and their own absolute dependence. The second part of the verse, “and do whatever they are commanded,” emphasises the completeness and immediacy of their obedience. The angels do not hesitate, question, or alter the divine commands. Their submission is total and unwavering. This serves as a model for the believers, who are called to emulate the angels in their unquestioning obedience to Allah’s revealed commands. The contrast with the disbelievers is again stark: the angels, despite their immense power and purity, fear their Lord and obey completely, while the Meccan polytheists, weak and dependent, act arrogantly and disobey. The verse also implicitly answers the pagan belief that angels have any independent power or will; they are mere servants, bound by fear and obedience to Allah. For the believers, this verse instills a sense of the proper attitude towards divine commands. The angels’ fear is not pathological but a natural consequence of recognizing the majesty of God. When a believer hears a command from the Qur’an or Sunnah, the ideal response is that of the angels: a fear of transgressing, and an immediate effort to comply. The mention of “above them” also reinforces the Islamic concept of Allah’s elevation and transcendence over His creation, a doctrine that counters any notion of pantheism or incarnation. This verse, along with the preceding ones, builds a cosmic picture where all of existence is in a state of obedient worship, and the only discordant note is struck by the arrogant human who refuses to join this universal harmony. The call, therefore, is to humble oneself, fear the Lord, and do what is commanded, thus finding one’s place in the symphony of creation.
51 | And Allah has said, “Do not take two gods. There is only One God. So be in awe of Me ˹alone˺.” | After the cosmic description of universal submission, the sūrah returns to the direct, verbal command of Allah. “And Allah has said” gives this statement the weight of direct divine speech. The command begins with a prohibition: “Do not take two gods.” The minimum number of plurality is mentioned—two—to negate any form of multiplicity in divinity. If two gods existed, the universe would fall into chaos, as the Qur’an argues elsewhere. This simple prohibition covers all forms of polytheism, whether it involves two, three, or a pantheon of deities. The core of the message is that divinity is indivisible. Then comes the affirmation: “There is only One God.” This is the positive declaration of tawḥīd. The Arabic construction stresses exclusivity: there is no God but He. The logical conclusion of this exclusive oneness is the command: “So be in awe of Me alone.” The word translated as “be in awe” is irhabūn, which carries meanings of fear, awe, and reverence, but also implies a sense of being vigilant and on guard. The object of this awe is exclusively Allah, not any idol, not poverty, not the enemy, not the schemes of the oppressors. The believer’s heart should be filled with such reverence for Allah that all other fears become insignificant. The verse thus connects correct belief with the correct emotional and spiritual response. Monotheism is not merely an intellectual position; it is a state of the heart where Allah alone becomes the focal point of all ultimate concern. The sequence is beautiful: the prohibition of shirk, the affirmation of oneness, and the command to exclusive awe. This verse encapsulates the entire prophetic mission in a single, powerful sentence. For the early Muslims in Mecca, this was a revolutionary statement that directly challenged the entire religious, social, and economic structure of their society, which was built around the worship of multiple idols. To adopt this belief was to invite persecution, yet it was the liberating truth. In contemporary times, the “two gods” may not be physical idols but rather the dual worship of God and money, God and ego, God and ideology. The verse challenges every heart to examine its ultimate allegiances. The command “be in awe of Me alone” also addresses common anxieties; people often fear losing their livelihood, their status, or their lives. This verse redirects that fear to the only One who truly holds power, liberating the believer from all other fears.
52 | To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth, and to Him ˹alone˺ is the everlasting devotion. Will you then fear any other than Allah? | This verse provides the logical foundation for the command to fear only Allah, which was stated in the previous verse. “To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth” establishes Allah’s absolute ownership and sovereignty. Every single thing in the entire universe, from the largest galaxy to the smallest particle, belongs to Him. Human beings may claim temporary ownership of property, but that ownership is a trust, not an absolute right. The true Owner is Allah alone. This has direct implications for worship: since He owns everything, He alone deserves thanks, praise, and obedience. The polytheists of Mecca acknowledged that Allah created the heavens and the earth, yet they worshipped other things that owned nothing. The second part, “and to Him alone is the everlasting devotion,” uses the Arabic word dīn, which can be translated as religion, obedience, or recompense. Here it is interpreted as the perpetual, unwavering obedience and worship that is due to Allah at all times. The word “everlasting” indicates that this devotion is constant, not intermittent or situational. He alone is the object of the eternal religion of truth that never changes. All other systems of worship are transient falsehoods. The verse then closes with a rhetorical question that challenges the very basis of polytheism: “Will you then fear any other than Allah?” If Allah is the sole Owner and the only One deserving constant worship and devotion, what rational basis is there to fear idols, people, poverty, or the future? All those things are themselves owned and controlled by Allah. Fear of anything else is a misplacement of the heart’s natural awe, which belongs only to the Creator. This question is a powerful psychological tool; it forces the listener to confront the irrationality of their fears. For the believer, this verse offers a comprehensive remedy for anxiety and worldly fear. When one truly internalises that everything belongs to Allah and that He is the only one owed ultimate devotion, the fear of creatures dissolves. The verse also has a political dimension: the Quraysh feared that accepting Islam would lead to loss of tribal support and economic boycott. This verse asks them, why fear those things when the Owner of all is with you? The lesson is timeless: align your devotion entirely with Allah, and you will be freed from the tyranny of lesser fears.
53 | Whatever blessings you have are from Allah. Then whenever hardship touches you, to Him ˹alone˺ you cry ˹for help˺. | This verse begins a new passage that exposes the psychological inconsistency of the polytheists. “Whatever blessings you have are from Allah” is a comprehensive and indisputable statement. Every blessing—health, wealth, children, safety, senses, intellect, and the very air one breathes—originates solely from Allah. The word “whatever” leaves nothing out. Even the blessings a person thinks they earned through their own effort are ultimately from Allah, who created their body, their mind, and the means to achieve. This is a truth that the Quraysh themselves would not deny, as they acknowledged Allah as the supreme Creator and Provider. The verse then describes a common human phenomenon: “Then whenever hardship touches you, to Him alone you cry for help.” When a crisis strikes—a storm at sea, a severe illness, a sudden danger—the human being’s innate nature surfaces. In that moment of extreme distress, the polytheist forgets his idols. He does not call upon Lāt, ʿUzzā, or Manāt. He cries out directly to Allah, the One true God. This is because the fiṭrah, the primordial human disposition, knows that only the supreme power can remove genuine calamity. The idols are recognized, even if unconsciously, as powerless. The polytheist’s duʿāʾ in hardship is a sincere, desperate, and exclusive call to Allah. The verse exposes this as a contradiction: if Allah is the only one you turn to in hardship, why do you associate partners with Him in times of ease? This is a powerful argument against all forms of conditional or temporary monotheism. The verse also serves as a profound comfort to believers: hardship itself can be a mercy because it forces the soul to turn back to its Lord with a sincerity that may be lacking in comfort. For many, spiritual awakening begins in the crucible of suffering. The verse encourages believers to maintain the same sincerity in ease that they naturally have in hardship. The hypocritical human tendency is to remember God intensely when in trouble and forget Him when the trouble passes, a theme the following verses will develop. The phrase “to Him alone you cry” uses a grammatical structure that emphasizes exclusivity, capturing the essence of the natural, untainted prayer of a soul in distress.
54 | Then as soon as He removes the hardship from you, a group of you associates ˹others˺ with their Lord ˹in worship˺, | This verse exposes the tragic and all-too-common human response to divine mercy. “Then as soon as He removes the hardship from you” describes the moment of relief. Allah, in His mercy, answers the desperate cry and lifts the calamity—the storm subsides, the illness is cured, the danger passes. The immediate response one would logically expect is overwhelming gratitude, a strengthening of exclusive worship, and a life dedicated to the One who saved them. But the verse describes a different reality: “a group of you associates others with their Lord in worship.” The phrase “a group of you” indicates that not all fall back into this error, but a significant portion do. Once they feel safe and secure again, they return to their old habits of shirk, setting up partners alongside Allah, the very God who just delivered them. This is the height of ingratitude and irrationality. The imagery is of a drowning man who calls upon God to save him, promising devotion, and then, upon reaching the shore, turns his back and bows to a statue. This pattern was visible among the Quraysh, who would invoke Allah sincerely when in distress at sea or in the desert, but upon returning safely to Mecca, would resume their idol worship. Yet the verse is universal in its application. It describes a human tendency that persists across generations. Many people turn to God in foxhole prayers but become secular or idolatrous once the crisis is over. The verse serves as a mirror for the reader to examine their own life: do we remember Allah more intensely when we are ill or in trouble, and then become heedless when we are healthy and prosperous? The word “associates” shows that they are not abandoning Allah entirely but mixing His worship with that of others, which is exactly what the Quraysh did. This is a severe warning that such behaviour is not a small slip but a fundamental betrayal of the natural covenant made in the moment of distress. The verse also highlights the patience and forbearance of Allah, who, despite this repeated ingratitude, continues to rescue people when they call. However, the following verse will make clear that this pattern cannot continue forever without consequence.
55 | only returning Our favours with ingratitude. So enjoy yourselves, for you will soon know. | This verse explains the motivation behind the behaviour described in the previous verse and delivers a stern warning. “Only returning Our favours with ingratitude” clarifies that the idolatry committed after the removal of hardship is a direct act of ingratitude for the very favour of deliverance. The Arabic word for ingratitude is yakfurū, which is also the root for disbelief. This linguistic link shows that ingratitude and disbelief are deeply intertwined. The favour that saved their lives, restored their health, or secured their safety is repaid not with thanks but with the worship of others, which is the ultimate form of kufr. This is not a passive forgetting; it is an active recompensing of good with evil. Allah describes it as “returning Our favours,” meaning that they take the blessing and use it as a foundation for further rebellion. The verse then concludes with a chilling, sarcastic imperative: “So enjoy yourselves, for you will soon know.” This is not a permission but a threat. The command “enjoy” is a form of istidrāj, a divine strategy where a sinner is given increasing blessings and time, which only increases their heedlessness, leading them step by step to their ultimate destruction. The phrase “for you will soon know” points to the certainty of future punishment. Whether in this world, at the moment of death, or on the Day of Judgment, they will come to know the reality of what they denied. The word “soon” indicates the brevity of worldly life; compared to eternity, even a long life is a fleeting moment of enjoyment before the inevitable reckoning. This verse is profoundly sobering. It warns believers never to become complacent or to misinterpret a comfortable life as a sign of Allah’s pleasure if it is accompanied by disobedience. The enjoyment of this world is short, and its consequences are eternal. The verse also teaches that ingratitude is a serious sin that can invite divine punishment. The proper response to blessings and relief from hardship is not a temporary turning to God but a permanent transformation of life, marked by continuous gratitude and exclusive worship. Every time a believer experiences relief from a difficulty, they should remember this verse and ensure that their behaviour does not fall into the pattern of those who “enjoy” and forget.
56 | And they ˹even˺ assign to those ˹idols˺—who know nothing—a share of what We have provided for them. By Allah! You will certainly be questioned about whatever ˹lies˺ you used to fabricate ˹against Allah˺. | This verse exposes a specific, egregious practice of the polytheists. “And they even assign to those idols—who know nothing—a share of what We have provided for them.” The Quraysh would dedicate a portion of their crops, livestock, and produce to their idols, setting it aside as an offering. At the same time, they would allocate another portion to Allah. This division of provision was a direct act of shirk in the economic sphere. They treated their idols as partners in the blessings that Allah alone had provided. The parenthetical phrase “who know nothing” emphasizes the absurdity: these idols are utterly ignorant, yet the polytheists grant them a share in the sustenance that the All-Knowing Creator bestowed. The idols cannot benefit from these offerings, nor do they know the intentions behind them. This practice also involved arbitrary prohibitions, where they declared certain animals or crops forbidden based on their own fabricated rules, attributing these prohibitions to the idols. The verse then swears by Allah: “By Allah! You will certainly be questioned about whatever lies you used to fabricate against Allah.” This divine oath adds the highest degree of solemnity and certainty to the threat. The questioning will not be a casual inquiry but a severe interrogation on the Day of Judgment, where they will have to account for every lie they invented—claiming that Allah has partners, that certain foods are divinely forbidden, and that their idols deserve a share of their wealth. The word “fabricate” indicates that they were not merely mistaken but actively constructed falsehoods. This verse is a powerful warning against all forms of religious innovation and false attribution to Allah. In a modern context, it condemns any act of dedicating one’s wealth or resources to causes or entities that are set up as rivals to God, as well as making unlawful what Allah has made lawful or vice versa based on human whims. The verse also teaches that believers will be questioned about their financial choices: did they spend their provision in ways that please Allah, or did they assign shares to false gods, whether materialistic ideologies, ego, or corrupt institutions? The oath “By Allah!” brings a sense of urgency and fear, reminding believers to audit their allocation of resources and ensure that everything is directed solely towards the One who provided it all.
57 | And they attribute ˹angels as˺ daughters to Allah—glory be to Him!— the opposite of what they desire for themselves. | This verse shifts to another blasphemous claim of the Meccan pagans. Some Arab tribes, particularly the Quraysh and others, believed that the angels were female beings, and they claimed these angels were the daughters of Allah. “And they attribute angels as daughters to Allah” exposes this absurdity. They took the most honoured, pure, and obedient servants of Allah—the angels—and invented a familial relationship, calling them daughters of God. The immediate interjection “glory be to Him!” is a declaration of Allah’s absolute transcendence and freedom from such false attributions. He is far above having children, whether sons or daughters, as He is the Self-Sufficient, the Eternal, who neither begets nor is begotten. The verse then demolishes their claim with a simple logical point: this attribution is “the opposite of what they desire for themselves.” The pagan Arabs despised having daughters. They considered daughters a source of shame and disgrace, as the following verses will graphically illustrate. They would bury their baby girls alive out of fear of dishonour. Yet they had the audacity to assign to Allah what they themselves detested. This is a double injustice: first, claiming that Allah has offspring at all, and second, claiming that His offspring are of the gender they consider inferior and shameful. The argument is a rational and psychological slam against their own inconsistency. If daughters are so despised, why would they insult the Creator by attributing them to Him? The verse exposes the moral and intellectual bankruptcy of their theology. For Muslims, this verse reinforces the core doctrine of tawḥīd in Allah’s essence: He is unique, without any partner, parent, or child. The practice of ascribing human qualities or family structures to God is a pagan residue that must be rejected. The verse also critiques cultural misogyny by highlighting that their own disdain for daughters made their attribution to Allah even more heinous. It implicitly calls for a revaluation of the status of women: the very attitude that makes a man ashamed of a daughter is what makes his claim about God so monstrous. The “glory be to Him!” reminds the believer to constantly exalt Allah above all human projections and limitations.
58 | Whenever one of them is given the good news of a baby girl, his face grows gloomy, as he suppresses his rage. | Having exposed the contradiction of attributing daughters to Allah while hating them for themselves, the sūrah now paints a vivid, unflinching portrait of the pagan attitude towards female infants. “Whenever one of them is given the good news of a baby girl” uses the phrase “good news” with deep irony. The birth of a child is conventionally a cause for celebration, something for which one is congratulated. But for the pre-Islamic Arab, the news of a daughter was the opposite of good. The verse describes the immediate physical reaction: “his face grows gloomy.” The Arabic muswaddan literally means “turned black,” reflecting the darkness of anger, shame, and depression that visibly overcomes his face. This is a physiological manifestation of his inner turmoil. He receives the news not with joy but with a feeling of disgrace. The second phrase, “as he suppresses his rage,” is crucial. The word kaẓīm means someone who is choking with anger, filling with suppressed fury. He is not just sad; he is enraged by the birth of a daughter. He feels humiliated before his peers because a daughter is seen as a liability, a potential source of scandal, and an economic burden in a society that valued male warriors and sons to carry on the family name. He suppresses this rage only because of social constraints; the full expression of his anger might bring even more shame. This verse is a damning indictment of a culture that valued women so little that their very existence was considered a misfortune. It sets the stage for the next verse’s description of the horrific consequence of this mindset. By describing the scene with such emotional precision, the Qur’an calls its listeners to witness the ugliness of this custom and to feel revulsion towards it. It prepares the ground for the Islamic reform that would elevate the status of women, condemn female infanticide, and declare that the birth of a daughter is a divine blessing. For contemporary readers, this verse is a historical mirror that shows how far a society can deviate from natural human affection when it is governed by false concepts of honour and worth. It also reminds believers that every child, male or female, is a gift from Allah, and the only appropriate response is gratitude, not gloom or suppressed rage.
59 | He hides himself from the people because of the bad news he has received. Should he keep her in disgrace, or bury her ˹alive˺ in the ground? Evil indeed is their judgment! | This verse continues the tragic narrative of the father who received news of a daughter’s birth. “He hides himself from the people because of the bad news he has received.” His shame is so profound that he withdraws from public view. He cannot face his community with what he perceives as a humiliating event. The “bad news” is the birth of the baby girl, which to him is a catastrophe. In his isolation, he wrestles with a terrible dilemma, captured in the question: “Should he keep her in disgrace, or bury her alive in the ground?” The first option, to keep her, is equated with disgrace. He would have to endure the social stigma and the supposed burden of raising a daughter. The second option, burying her alive, was the horrifying practice of wa’d al-banāt, female infanticide, which was prevalent among some Arab tribes. Fathers would take their newborn daughters and bury them in the sand until they died, a practice driven by fear of poverty and a twisted sense of honour. The fact that this is presented as a genuine, agonising choice reveals the depth of the societal depravity. The verse then delivers the divine verdict: “Evil indeed is their judgment!” The word “evil” (sā’a) is a categorical condemnation. Whatever choice they make within this framework is evil; even keeping her in disgrace is not a righteous act if it is done with resentment and a sense of humiliation. The ultimate evil is, of course, the murder. This Qur’anic passage is one of the most powerful denunciations of female infanticide and misogyny in any scripture. It does not merely prohibit the practice; it exposes the inner ugliness of the mindset behind it, the gloom, the rage, the shame, and the monstrous deliberation. By revealing this divine disgust, the verse planted the seeds for a radical transformation. Islam categorically forbade the killing of children and declared the birth of a daughter a mercy. The Prophet Muḥammad famously said that whoever raises daughters with kindness will be with him in Paradise like two fingers. This verse remains profoundly relevant in societies where daughters are still viewed as inferior or where gendercide occurs. It stands as an eternal witness against the devaluation of female life and a call to honor every child as a trust from Allah.
60 | To those who disbelieve in the Hereafter belong all evil qualities, whereas to Allah belong the finest attributes. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise. | This verse concludes the section on the pagan misogyny and false attributions to Allah by drawing a sharp contrast between the character of disbelievers and the character of God. “To those who disbelieve in the Hereafter belong all evil qualities” translates the Arabic mathal as-saw’, meaning the worst possible description or attribute. Their entire worldview, built on the denial of accountability, produces evil outcomes: female infanticide, idol worship, ingratitude, arrogance, and the attribution of daughters to God while despising daughters for themselves. The root cause is their disbelief in the Hereafter. Because they do not believe they will be judged, they have no moral compass, and their values become inverted. The phrase “all evil qualities” could also refer specifically to their own statement that daughters are a curse and a shame; this evil description belongs to them, not to Allah. In contrast, “whereas to Allah belong the finest attributes.” The word mathal al-aʿlā, the highest description, refers to the attributes of absolute perfection, such as power, knowledge, wisdom, mercy, and transcendence. Allah is free from every imperfection they ascribe to Him. He is far above having children, feeling shame, or possessing any quality that is deficient. The verse then seals this contrast with two of His names: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” The Almighty (al-ʿAzīz) signifies that He is fully capable of punishing those who attribute such evil to Him, yet His punishment is delayed. The All-Wise (al-Ḥakīm) indicates that all His actions, including the respite given to the wrongdoers, are based on perfect wisdom. This concluding statement serves as both a warning and a comfort. The disbelievers’ evil descriptions and actions will not go unpunished, but the timing and method are determined by divine wisdom. For the believer, this verse invites a deep reflection on the relationship between belief in the Hereafter and moral character. When the Hereafter is denied, moral standards plummet to accommodate human desires. When it is affirmed, the believer strives to emulate the divine attributes of mercy, justice, and generosity, as far as humanly possible. The verse also teaches that one’s concept of God must be purified, attributing to Him only the highest perfection and rejecting any anthropomorphic or degrading descriptions, whether from ancient paganism or modern misconceptions.
61 | If Allah were to punish people ˹immediately˺ for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth. But He delays them for an appointed term. And when their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it. | Having exposed the evil qualities of those who disbelieve in the Hereafter and affirmed Allah's perfect attributes, the sūrah now addresses a question that naturally arises: why do wrongdoers seem to escape immediate punishment? The verse begins, “If Allah were to punish people immediately for their wrongdoing, He would not have left a single living being on earth.” The word for wrongdoing is ẓulm, which encompasses every form of injustice, sin, and transgression, from the greatest crime of shirk to the smallest disobedience. If Allah were to seize every wrongdoer the moment they committed an offense, the earth would be swept clean of life. This is because no human being is entirely free from wrongdoing. Even the believers commit sins, and the general human condition involves some degree of shortcoming. The immediate, automatic punishment of every wrong act would therefore result in the extinction of all people, and perhaps even animals and plants that suffer as a consequence of human injustice. This statement is a profound testament to Allah's forbearance. The fact that we are alive, despite our countless sins, is proof of His mercy. He does not approve of the wrongdoing, but He withholds the punishment out of grace, giving time for repentance, correction, and growth. The verse then provides the explanation: “But He delays them for an appointed term.” Every individual, every community, and indeed humanity as a whole has an appointed term (ajal musammā). This term is fixed in divine knowledge. The delay is not a sign of neglect or helplessness; it is a deliberate, merciful reprieve. Within this term, the wrongdoer has the opportunity to turn back, while the believer can increase in good deeds and purification. However, the verse immediately balances this reassurance with a warning: “And when their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it.” When the appointed term comes, whether for an individual at death or for a nation facing its destruction, there is absolutely no flexibility. No one can bargain for an extra hour, and the punishment cannot be brought forward before its designated time. The divine timetable is precise and unalterable. This verse provides a complete answer to the problem of evil and divine justice. It teaches that the apparent prosperity of wrongdoers is not a sign of divine approval but of divine respite. It instills in the believer a sense of urgency: the appointed term is approaching, and one must not waste the delay in continued wrongdoing. It also comforts those who suffer injustice, assuring them that the oppressor's time is limited and divinely measured. The perfect balance between mercy and justice in this verse reflects the two divine names mentioned at the end of the previous passage: the Almighty and the All-Wise.
62 | They attribute to Allah what they hate ˹for themselves˺, and their tongues utter the lie that they will have the finest reward. Without a doubt, for them is the Fire, where they will be abandoned. | This verse continues the critique of the pagan mindset, building on the earlier discussion of their attributing daughters to Allah and their evil judgments. “They attribute to Allah what they hate for themselves” refers back to their detestation of daughters while claiming that Allah has daughters. More broadly, it encompasses every false and deficient quality they ascribe to God while being unwilling to accept such qualities for themselves. They claim Allah has partners, yet they would hate to have partners in their own businesses or households who share equally in their authority. They set aside a portion of their provision for idols, yet they would resent anyone taking a share of their own wealth without permission. Their theology is a projection of their own flaws, riddled with inconsistencies. The second part of the verse says, “and their tongues utter the lie that they will have the finest reward.” Despite their evil deeds and beliefs, they audaciously claim that they will receive a good outcome in the Hereafter, or that their idols will intercede for them and grant them paradise. Some commentators explain that when the Quraysh heard the descriptions of the believers' reward in Paradise, they boasted that if there is a resurrection, they would receive even better because of their wealth and status in this world. This is a lie they fabricate and speak with their tongues, a self-deception that compounds their disbelief. The verse then delivers the definitive verdict: “Without a doubt, for them is the Fire, where they will be abandoned.” The word “abandoned” translates mufraṭūn, which some scholars interpret as being left there eternally, forgotten by any helper, or as being pushed forward into the Fire with contempt. There is no hope of rescue, no intercessor, no second chance. Their fine words and false hopes will collapse into the reality of eternal abandonment. This verse dismantles the psychology of self-justification. Sinners often comfort themselves with the idea that everything will be fine in the end, despite their persistence in wrongdoing. The verse warns that such a claim is merely a lie spoken by the tongue, not a reality grounded in divine promise. For the believer, it is a call to examine one’s own assumptions: am I relying on Allah’s true promise or on my own wishful thinking? The stark contrast between the “finest reward” they claim and the “Fire” that awaits them underscores the danger of spiritual arrogance. Only through sincere faith and righteous deeds, as defined by revelation, can one legitimately hope for a good reward.
63 | By Allah! We have surely sent messengers to communities before you ˹O Prophet˺, but Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them. So he is their patron today, and they will suffer a painful punishment. | The sūrah now takes a divine oath to comfort the Prophet and warn the Quraysh. “By Allah! We have surely sent messengers to communities before you O Prophet.” The oath emphasizes the certainty of the historical pattern. The Prophet Muḥammad is not the first to face rejection; every preceding community received its messengers. The verse explains the common cause of rejection: “but Satan made their misdeeds appealing to them.” The word “misdeeds” refers to their idolatry, arrogance, and rejection of the prophets. Satan beautified these sins in their eyes, making them seem reasonable, noble, or even righteous. They followed their whims and cultural traditions, convinced that they were on the correct path while actually being deceived by the enemy of mankind. This is the consistent method of Satan: to take evil and wrap it in attractive packaging so that the sinner feels no need to repent. The verse then says, “So he is their patron today.” The word “patron” (walī) means a close ally, protector, and guide. Because they followed Satan in the world, he has become their guardian and companion on the Day of Judgment, leading them to their doom. On that Day, Satan will disown his followers, as the Qur'an describes elsewhere, saying he only called them and they responded; he has no power to save them from Allah's punishment. Their alliance with him in this world results in complete abandonment and enmity in the Hereafter. The verse concludes, “and they will suffer a painful punishment.” The consequence of being under Satan's patronage is the inevitable painful torment of Hell. This verse serves multiple purposes. For the Prophet, it is a consolation: the rejection he faces is not due to any deficiency in his message but follows a long history of satanic deception. For the Quraysh, it is a warning that their perceived correctness is a satanic illusion, and their alliance with their forefathers' religion is actually an alliance with Satan. For believers of every era, it highlights the crucial necessity of self-examination. Our own inclinations and cultural norms can be tainted by satanic beautification. The only safeguard is to measure everything against the clear revelation. The verse also exposes the deceptive nature of sin; it may appear pleasant and justified, but its end is a painful punishment. Seeking Allah's refuge from Satan is therefore not a mere ritual but a vital spiritual necessity to avoid having the devil as one's ultimate patron.
64 | We have revealed to you the Book only to clarify for them what they differed about, and as a guide and mercy for those who believe. | This verse presents the Qur'an as the divine antidote to the satanic deception and disputes mentioned in the previous verse. “We have revealed to you the Book” directly addresses the Prophet and emphasizes the divine origin of the revelation. The phrase “only to clarify for them what they differed about” states the primary purpose of the Qur'an in its function as a decisive arbiter. Humanity, including the People of the Book and the polytheists, had fallen into deep disagreements over fundamental truths—the nature of God, the status of prophets, the reality of the Hereafter, moral laws, and ritual practices. The Qur'an comes as the final, clear criterion that separates truth from falsehood and resolves those disputes with absolute authority. It clarifies the true stories of the past, the correct beliefs about God, and the laws by which human life should be governed. For the Quraysh, who disputed about the resurrection, about the angels, and about the legitimacy of the Prophet's message, the Book provides unambiguous answers. Then the verse adds, “and as a guide and mercy for those who believe.” The clarification is universal in its presentation, but the benefit of being a guide and a mercy is specifically for those who believe. The Qur'an guides them to the straight path in all aspects of life, leading them through the darkness of ignorance and confusion into the light of certainty. It is also a mercy because it saves them from eternal punishment, grants them peace of mind, and connects them to their Creator. The combination of “guide” and “mercy” indicates that following the Qur'an is not a burdensome duty but a compassionate gift that brings about the best of this life and the next. This verse elevates the status of the Qur'an as the ultimate reference point. In the face of endless human arguments and sectarian divisions, the believer returns to the Book to find clarity. The verse also implies that those who do not believe, despite the Book's clarity, are rejecting not just a text but a divine mercy that could have saved them. For contemporary Muslims, this verse is a call to approach the Qur'an with the right attitude: not as a book of mere recitation, but as the clarifier of disputes, the guide for life, and the mercy that heals the heart. It instills confidence that in the Qur'an, one has access to the final word on the matters that divide humanity, and that adhering to it is the path to receiving Allah's special mercy.
65 | And Allah sends down rain from the sky, giving life to the earth after its death. Surely in this is a sign for those who listen. | Following the mention of the Book as a guide and mercy, the sūrah returns to the signs in the natural world, drawing a parallel between the life-giving rain and the life-giving revelation. “And Allah sends down rain from the sky” redirects the gaze to a familiar, yet miraculous, phenomenon. The rain descends from above, beyond human control, by the precise command and measure of the Creator. The phrase “giving life to the earth after its death” paints a vivid picture. A dry, barren land, cracked and lifeless, is transformed by the rainwater into a vibrant, green landscape teeming with plants and life. This natural resurrection, repeated every season, is a tangible sign that the One who revives the dead earth can and will revive the dead bones. The verse then concludes, “Surely in this is a sign for those who listen.” The specific mention of “those who listen” rather than “those who see” is significant. While the effect of rain is visible, the sound of rain, the thunder, and the message it carries require a different kind of attentiveness. Listening implies receptivity, understanding, and willingness to heed. A person can see rain and merely remark on the weather, but one who truly “listens” hears in the raindrops the message of divine mercy, power, and the promise of resurrection. The connection to the preceding verse is profound: just as rain revives the dead earth, the Qur'an revives dead hearts. The revelation is often compared to rain in the Qur'an; it descends from the sky of divine will and transforms barren souls into flourishing gardens of faith and good deeds. The polytheists who saw rain as a natural cycle missed the sign, while those who listen with the ears of faith perceive the Creator's hand. This verse is an invitation to a deeper engagement with the natural world. Every rainfall can be a moment of spiritual reflection, a reminder of our own resurrection, and an occasion to thank Allah for His mercy. It also teaches that the signs are abundant and clear, but their benefit is only for those who have the quality of sincere listening—an active, humble attentiveness to the truth. For the modern believer, surrounded by scientific explanations, the verse does not deny those explanations but points through them to the ultimate Cause, transforming a meteorological event into a spiritual experience.
66 | And there is certainly a lesson for you in cattle: We give you to drink of what is in their bellies, from between digested food and blood: pure milk, pleasant to drink. | The sūrah now returns to the signs in cattle, building on the earlier mentions of their benefits, but here focusing on a specific physiological miracle. “And there is certainly a lesson for you in cattle” uses the word ʿibrah, a lesson that bridges one reality to another, causing the observer to cross over from the physical to the spiritual. The specific lesson is about milk: “We give you to drink of what is in their bellies, from between digested food and blood: pure milk, pleasant to drink.” This description is a remarkable piece of Qur'anic science and spiritual reflection. The cattle consume fodder, which becomes digested material in the stomach and intestines, and blood circulates in their veins. From between these two substances—the partially digested food and the blood—Allah extracts milk. Milk is neither the waste product of digestion nor blood, yet it emerges from a process that involves both. The verse describes it as “pure” (khāliṣan), free from any impurity, and “pleasant to drink” (sā’ighan), palatable and smooth, causing no revulsion despite its origin. The lesson is the divine power that can bring forth something so pure, nutritious, and delightful from within the complex, messy interior of an animal. If a human were to look at digested food and blood, the last thing they would expect to derive from them is a delicious, clean beverage. Yet Allah's creative wisdom accomplishes this for the benefit of humanity. This sign points to the resurrection as well: the ability to extract the pure from the mixed, the living from the dead. It also serves as a metaphor for spiritual purification; Allah can bring forth a pure, righteous soul from a person previously immersed in the filth of sin, just as He brings forth pure milk from between blood and waste. The cattle, which the polytheists sometimes dedicated to idols, are in fact a standing testimony to the oneness and mercy of the Creator. The verse invites the believer to contemplate the daily glass of milk with profound gratitude and awe. In a contemporary context, where milk production is industrialised and taken for granted, this verse restores the sense of wonder. The complex biological processes that modern science details—the role of the mammary glands, the filtering of nutrients from the blood—are precisely the workings of the divine sign. The believer who understands this lesson will see in every sip of milk a reason to praise the One who designed this incredible system.
67 | And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision. Surely in this is a sign for those who understand. | This verse continues with the theme of food and drink, but with a significant nuance. “And from the fruits of palm trees and grapevines you derive intoxicants as well as wholesome provision.” The verse mentions two of the most valued fruits in Arabia: dates from palm trees and grapes from grapevines. From these, people produce two very different types of products. “Intoxicants” (sakaran) refers to alcoholic beverages such as date wine and grape wine, which were widely consumed by the Arabs before Islam. “Wholesome provision” (rizqan ḥasanan) refers to the good, lawful foods and non-intoxicating drinks that can be derived from the same fruits, such as fresh dates, raisins, date syrup, and vinegar. The juxtaposition of intoxicants and wholesome provision, without an immediate explicit prohibition (the prohibition of alcohol came later), is a subtle moral lesson. The phrase “wholesome provision” is a positive commendation, while “intoxicants” is mentioned without the qualifier “wholesome.” The verse plants the seed of a moral distinction: from the same divine blessing, humans can choose to produce something that clouds the mind and leads to harm, or something that is pure and nourishing. The concluding statement, “Surely in this is a sign for those who understand,” appeals to the intellect. People of understanding will recognise that the existence of two such opposite outcomes from the same source is a test. The sign is not just in the fruit itself but in the human capacity to choose between the good and the harmful. This verse is often cited in discussions about the gradual prohibition of alcohol in Islam. It was revealed in Mecca, long before the clear prohibition came in Medina, laying the intellectual and spiritual groundwork. It encourages reflection on the nature of what we consume and the choices we make with Allah's gifts. The divine wisdom is that the same grape can be a source of benefit or a source of ruin, depending on human action. This principle extends to all of Allah’s provisions: money can be used for charity or for oppression, intelligence for guidance or for manipulation. For the contemporary believer, the verse is a call to use the intellect to distinguish between the wholesome and the intoxicating, not just in beverages but in all aspects of life. The modern world is full of “intoxicants”—addictive, mind-numbing substances and entertainments—and also abundant “wholesome provision.” The sign is for those who understand and choose wisely.
68 | And your Lord inspired the bees: “Make ˹your˺ homes in the mountains, the trees, and in what people construct, | The sūrah now reaches one of its most celebrated passages, the sign of the bee, from which the chapter takes its name. “And your Lord inspired the bees” uses the word awḥā, the same root used for divine revelation to prophets. This is a powerful indication that the bee's intricate behaviour is not a product of random evolution or mere instinct; it is a form of divine inspiration, a programmed guidance from the Creator. The bee is a tiny creature, yet it operates with astonishing precision and complexity. The address “your Lord” personalises the relationship; the same Lord who guides the Prophet and the believers also guides the bee. The content of this inspiration is first about habitation: “Make your homes in the mountains, the trees, and in what people construct.” The bee is given flexibility to build its hives in natural crevices in the mountains, in the hollows of trees, or in man-made hives that people construct for beekeeping. This tripartite division covers all possible habitats—wild natural formations, living trees, and human-engineered structures—demonstrating that the bee's adaptability is itself a divine gift. The fact that the verse mentions “what people construct” shows that Allah anticipated the domestication of bees and the practice of apiculture, which would become a human craft. The bee instinctively knows how to build its hexagonal wax cells with mathematical precision, a feat of engineering that humans have long admired. The inspiration given to the bee is a clear sign that Allah is the Lord of all creatures, great and small. The bee does not possess intellect or free will like humans, yet it flawlessly executes its divinely assigned tasks. This mirrors the earlier theme of all creation submitting to Allah. The polytheists are invited to reflect on this tiny insect: could any of their idols guide a bee to build its home and produce honey? The answer is obvious. For believers, this verse instills a sense of wonder at the natural world. The bee becomes a symbol of obedient service, working tirelessly, producing something sweet and beneficial. The modern study of bees, revealing their complex communication through dances and their highly organised social structure, only deepens the awe at the divine “inspiration” behind it all. The verse teaches that Allah's guidance is not limited to scripture but is woven into the very fabric of creation.
69 | and feed from ˹the flower of˺ any fruit ˹you please˺ and follow the ways your Lord has made easy for you.” From their bellies comes forth liquid of varying colours, in which there is healing for people. Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect. | This verse continues the divine instructions given to the bee. The inspiration continues: “and feed from the flower of any fruit you please.” The bee is given liberty to roam and gather nectar from a vast variety of blossoms, unrestricted except by the seasons and geography. The command “you please” indicates a divinely granted freedom within the bounds of its nature, and the bee does not overstep; it selects the best sources and returns to the hive. Then, “and follow the ways your Lord has made easy for you” describes the bee’s miraculous navigation. The Arabic word subul (ways) refers to the paths the bee travels, sometimes miles away from the hive, yet it finds its way back unerringly. Modern science has uncovered the bee's sophisticated navigational techniques, including the famous waggle dance that communicates the direction and distance of food sources to hive-mates. All of this is “made easy” by Allah, meaning He has facilitated this complex behaviour as a matter of course for the bee, without it needing to learn in a human sense. The result of this guided labour is then described: “From their bellies comes forth liquid of varying colours, in which there is healing for people.” The honey produced is of different colours depending on the nectar sources—from pale gold to dark amber. The phrase “in which there is healing for people” is a profound statement. Honey has well-documented medicinal properties, including antibacterial effects, wound-healing capabilities, and soothing for ailments of the throat and digestion. The verse presents this as a sign, not a medical textbook, but its truth has been confirmed by centuries of human experience and modern research. The healing is placed in the context of a divine gift emerging from a complex process that began with inspiration. The verse concludes, “Surely in this is a sign for those who reflect.” Reflection here goes beyond casual observation to deep contemplation of the entire chain: the Lord's inspiration, the bee's obedience, the varied nectar, the transformation in the belly, and the resulting healing liquid. It is a complete sign of the Creator's mercy, wisdom, and power. For believers, honey becomes a reminder of Allah's subtle care. The verse also encourages the pursuit of natural remedies in accordance with divine guidance. The bee's work ethic and the sweetness of its output serve as a metaphor for the believer whose words and deeds, guided by divine inspiration, should bring healing and sweetness to the world.
70 | Allah has created you, and then causes you to die. And some of you are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much. Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, Most Capable. | Following the magnificent sign of the bee, the sūrah returns to the human condition, reflecting on the lifecycle that manifests Allah's power. “Allah has created you, and then causes you to die.” This simple statement summarizes the entirety of human existence from the perspective of divine decree. Creation out of nothing, the gift of life, and the inevitability of death are all in Allah's hands. The verse then focuses on a particular stage within that lifecycle: “And some of you are left to reach the most feeble stage of life so that they may know nothing after having known much.” This refers to extreme old age, termed arḏal al-ʿumur, the most abject, weak period of life, where physical and mental faculties deteriorate. A person who was once sharp-minded, knowledgeable, and capable may regress to a state of forgetfulness and helplessness, becoming like a child again, not knowing what they once knew, and unable to care for themselves. This is a stark reminder of human frailty. The abilities we pride ourselves on—intelligence, memory, strength—are not intrinsic to us; they are loans from Allah that He can recall at any time. The verse punctures human arrogance by showing the full arc of life: from weakness to strength and back to weakness. The final part of the verse, “Indeed, Allah is All-Knowing, Most Capable,” connects this life stage to divine attributes. Allah's knowledge encompasses every phase of human life, and His power is such that He can easily reverse a person's condition. The decline of old age is not a defeat but a deliberate demonstration of the transient nature of worldly capabilities. Some commentators note that the one who reaches this feeble stage may have lost the worldly knowledge they accumulated, but their faith and the reward for their patience remain intact with Allah. For believers, this verse inspires humility regarding youth and health. It encourages treating the elderly with compassion and respect, recognising that their state could one day be ours. It also motivates taking advantage of the period of strength and knowledge to do righteous deeds before the inevitable decline. The verse is a memento mori, a reminder of the entire human trajectory under divine control, urging preparation for the life to come when our earthly faculties fade into insignificance.
71 | And Allah has favoured some of you over others in provision. But those who have been much favoured would not share their wealth with those ˹bondspeople˺ in their possession, making them their equals. Do they then deny Allah’s favours? | This verse shifts to the theme of economic provision and uses it as an argument against shirk. “And Allah has favoured some of you over others in provision” acknowledges a manifest reality: people differ in their wealth, sustenance, and resources. This disparity is part of Allah's divine wisdom and test. The verse then presents a logical analogy drawn from human behaviour: “But those who have been much favoured would not share their wealth with those bondspeople in their possession, making them their equals.” The wealthy masters do not consider their slaves or servants as equals in ownership of their wealth. A master would not give his slave an equal share in his property such that the slave becomes a partner with equal rights and decision-making power. If human beings are unwilling to make their own servants equal partners in their limited, temporary wealth, how can they dare to make created beings—who are ultimately Allah's servants—equal partners with Allah in His divine dominion and worship? The analogy is powerful and culturally immediate for the Meccan society where slavery was practiced. If a human master feels it absurd for a slave to be a co-owner, it is infinitely more absurd for a human to be set up as a partner to the Lord of all worlds. The verse concludes with the rhetorical question: “Do they then deny Allah’s favours?” The favours refer to the provision and the reasoning capacity they have been given. By committing shirk, they deny the very gifts that should have led them to exclusive worship. This verse uses rational argument to dismantle polytheism, showing that the polytheists' own social norms contradict their theological claims. It also carries a subtle message about human dignity and the arbitrariness of social hierarchies; the favour of provision is from Allah, not a measure of intrinsic worth. The verse challenges the believer to reflect on how they use their God-given provisions: do they recognise them as favours and act gratefully, or do they hoard them in denial of the Giver? The analogy also encourages fairness and humility, reminding that no matter how wealthy one becomes, they remain servants of Allah, never reaching the status of a partner with Him in any sense.
72 | And Allah has made for you spouses of your own kind, and given you through your spouses children and grandchildren. And He has granted you good, lawful provisions. Are they then faithful to falsehood and ungrateful for Allah’s favours? | Continuing the list of divine blessings, this verse highlights the blessing of family. “And Allah has made for you spouses of your own kind” refers to the creation of mates from the same human nature, so that they find comfort, love, and compatibility. The phrase “of your own kind” (min anfusikum) literally means “from yourselves,” indicating the deep psychological and emotional bond between husband and wife, who share the same essence. The spouse is not an alien being but a companion crafted from the same human mould to provide tranquillity. Then, “and given you through your spouses children and grandchildren” expands the blessing. The Arabic word ḥafadah, translated as grandchildren, can also include close relatives and descendants in general. Through marriage, Allah grants the joy of children and the further joy of seeing one's children have children, extending the family and providing support in old age. This is a profound mercy that ensures continuity, love, and care across generations. The verse then adds, “And He has granted you good, lawful provisions.” The term ṭayyibāt includes all pure, beneficial, and lawful things that sustain life and provide pleasure. The family is supported by these provisions, which are themselves a sign of Allah's care. The verse ends with a cutting question: “Are they then faithful to falsehood and ungrateful for Allah’s favours?” The falsehood (bāṭil) refers to the idols and all that they worship besides Allah. After receiving such immense and personal blessings—spouses, children, and sustenance—how can they have faith in false gods and be ungrateful to the true God? The pairing of “faithful to falsehood” with “ungrateful for Allah’s favours” shows that shirk is the ultimate ingratitude. They redirect their devotion and thanks to entities that provided them none of these blessings. This verse is a powerful reminder for believers to see their family as a divine gift. Every moment with a spouse, every child and grandchild, every meal shared, is a favour that should increase gratitude and strengthen faith, not lead to heedlessness. In a modern context, where the family unit is often undervalued or redefined, the verse reaffirms the sacredness of the family structure as a deliberate, merciful design of the Creator. The appropriate response to these favours is exclusive faith in Allah and a life filled with gratitude.
73 | Yet they worship besides Allah those ˹idols˺ who do not afford them any provision from the heavens and the earth, nor do they have the power to. | This verse directly contrasts the true Provider described in the preceding verses with the false objects of worship. “Yet they worship besides Allah” emphasizes the contradiction. After all the detailed blessings—spouses, children, cattle, rain, and the intricate provision described throughout the sūrah—some people turn away from the Giver to worship others. The phrase “those idols” is not in the original Arabic, which simply says “that which,” encompassing idols, false gods, and any being worshipped apart from Allah. The verse first states the empirical fact: these false gods “do not afford them any provision from the heavens and the earth.” Provision here includes rain from the sky, crops from the earth, and all the sustenance that has been meticulously listed. Not a single grain of wheat, not a drop of water, is provided by these idols. They have never given and can never give any form of sustenance. The verse then delivers the final blow: “nor do they have the power to.” Not only do they not actually provide, but they lack even the potential capability to do so. The Arabic word yastaṭīʿūn indicates complete, fundamental inability. They are not just passive non-providers; they are utterly incapable of providing, now or ever. This is in stark contrast to Allah, whose ability to provide is absolute and demonstrated constantly. The verse is a succinct, logical refutation of idol worship. Worship is the ultimate expression of gratitude and dependence, and it logically belongs solely to the One who provisions and has the power to do so. To worship something that cannot provide even for itself—since the idols are made of wood or stone and need human makers to exist—is the height of irrationality. The verse calls the worshipper to a simple accounting: who gives you your food, your family, your life? The answer is clear, and so the object of worship should be clear. In a modern context, the principle applies to anything people turn to as ultimate providers—the state, economic systems, science, or charismatic leaders. None of these have the power to create provision; they merely distribute or manipulate what Allah has already created. The verse reorients the heart to place its ultimate trust and worship only in the One who holds the keys to all provision.
74 | So do not set up equals to Allah, for Allah certainly knows and you do not know. | This verse serves as the direct imperative conclusion derived from the preceding arguments. “So do not set up equals to Allah” is the command to abandon shirk entirely. The word “equals” (andādan) refers to rivals, peers, or partners that are placed on par with Allah in worship, love, or obedience. The command is a prohibition born from the overwhelming evidence of Allah's exclusive power in creation, provision, and the intricate ordering of the universe. After demonstrating that idols provide nothing and possess no power, the conclusion is inescapable: do not make them equals. The second part of the verse, “for Allah certainly knows and you do not know,” grounds this prohibition in the vast disparity in knowledge. Allah knows the true reality of all things. He knows that these so-called gods are false, that they have no power, and that worshipping them is a grave injustice. He knows the hidden consequences of shirk in this life and the Hereafter. Human beings, on the other hand, are limited in their knowledge. They may ignorantly assume that their idols intercede or that their false beliefs are harmless, but they do not truly know. The polytheists had no certain knowledge that their idols were divine; they were merely following conjecture and tradition. This verse highlights that faith must be based on knowledge and certainty, not on guesswork or blind imitation. Allah, the All-Knowing, has informed us of the truth, and we must submit to His knowledge. The verse also implies that there may be hidden aspects of reality—the true nature of divinity, the consequences of actions, the details of the unseen—that are beyond human grasp, and therefore it is wise to follow the guidance of the One who knows everything. For contemporary believers, this verse is a call to evaluate all allegiances. Do we set up equals to Allah by obeying someone against His commands, by fearing someone as we should only fear Allah, or by loving someone with a devotion due only to Him? The command is not just about stone idols but about any rival that competes for the ultimate devotion of the heart. The humility to acknowledge “you do not know” and to trust Allah's infinite knowledge is the foundation of true submission.
75 | Allah sets forth a parable: a slave who lacks all means, compared to a ˹free˺ man to whom We granted a good provision, of which he donates ˹freely,˺ openly and secretly. Are they equal? Praise be to Allah. In fact, most of them do not know. | To further illustrate the absurdity of shirk, the sūrah now presents a parable. “Allah sets forth a parable” introduces an analogy that makes the abstract concept of tawḥīd tangible. “A slave who lacks all means” describes a person in bondage, owned by another, possessing absolutely nothing—no wealth, no authority, no ability to act independently. He is completely under the control of his master, incapable of providing anything. “Compared to a free man to whom We granted a good provision, of which he donates freely, openly and secretly.” This free man is not only independent but wealthy and generous. He has been given abundant provision by Allah, and he spends it freely, both in public and in private, benefiting others without constraint. The contrast between the two is stark: one is helpless, the other is empowered and beneficent. Then the question: “Are they equal?” The answer is obviously no. No rational person would equate a powerless slave with a generous, free provider. The parable then applies this obvious inequality to the comparison between Allah and idols. The idols are like the slave who lacks all means; they are owned, controlled, and utterly incapable of providing any good. Allah is like the free man, indeed infinitely above the analogy, possessing all wealth and power, and generously providing for His creation openly and secretly. “Praise be to Allah” is the natural response to recognising this truth; all gratitude and praise belong to the One who is the ultimate generous Provider. The verse ends with “In fact, most of them do not know.” This lament highlights that despite the clarity of the analogy, many people fail to grasp its implications due to heedlessness or stubbornness. The verse is a masterpiece of rhetorical persuasion, using a simple social reality to demolish the polytheists' complex theological error. For the believer, this parable deepens appreciation for Allah's generosity and reinforces the logical necessity of exclusive worship. It also encourages the quality of generosity: the free man who donates secretly and openly is a model for believers, who should emulate Allah's attribute of provision by being charitable with what they have been given.
76 | And Allah sets forth a parable of two men: one of them is dumb, incapable of anything. He is a burden on his master. Wherever he is sent, he brings no good. Can such a person be equal to the one who commands justice and is on the Straight Path? | This is a second parable, continuing the theme of contrasting powerless false gods with the true God. “And Allah sets forth a parable of two men” introduces another dual comparison. The first man is described with a series of severe deficiencies: “one of them is dumb, incapable of anything.” He is mute, unable to speak or communicate, and by extension unable to understand or respond. “Incapable of anything” translates lā yaqdiru ʿalā shay’, meaning he has absolutely no power to accomplish any task. He is completely impotent. Furthermore, “He is a burden on his master.” Not only does he not contribute, but he requires constant care and supervision. He is a drain, a dependent who offers nothing in return. “Wherever he is sent, he brings no good” means that even when given a task, he fails utterly and produces no positive outcome. This man is a picture of total uselessness and dependency. In contrast, the second man is described simply: “Can such a person be equal to the one who commands justice and is on the Straight Path?” This second man is articulate, able to command and enjoin justice, indicating sound intellect, moral uprightness, and the ability to guide others. He is himself on the Straight Path, meaning his own life is aligned with righteousness, and he leads others to it. The parable, when applied, exposes the folly of comparing Allah—the One who commands justice, guides to the Straight Path, and possesses all perfection—with idols that are like the dumb, incapable man. The idols cannot speak, cannot answer prayers, cannot provide, and cannot guide. They are a burden on their worshippers, and wherever the worshippers turn to them, they find no good. The parable also has a secondary application: the contrast between a believer and a disbeliever, or between a leader who promotes justice and one who is morally and spiritually mute. For the believer, the parable is a call to embody the qualities of the second man: to speak truth, command justice, and stay firm on the Straight Path. It also serves as a warning against being spiritually mute and incapable, a burden on society, contributing no good. The rhetorical question leaves the answer hanging in the air, forcing the listener to acknowledge the obvious inequality and, by extension, the irrationality of shirk.
77 | To Allah ˹alone˺ belongs ˹the knowledge of˺ the unseen in the heavens and the earth. Bringing about the Hour would only take the blink of an eye, or even less. Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything. | This verse shifts back to affirming Allah's exclusive knowledge and power, particularly regarding the unseen and the Last Hour. “To Allah alone belongs the knowledge of the unseen in the heavens and the earth” establishes that all hidden realities, whether in the celestial realms or the terrestrial world, are known only to Him. This includes the future, the past that is unrecorded, the secrets of hearts, and the exact timing of the Day of Judgment. The verse implicitly refutes any claim that the idols, the soothsayers, or the jinn have access to the unseen. The Quraysh would often consult diviners and believe their idols had some hidden knowledge, but here the absolute exclusivity of Allah's knowledge is declared. Then the verse addresses the power to bring about the Hour: “Bringing about the Hour would only take the blink of an eye, or even less.” The disbelievers thought the resurrection was impossible or would be a massive, difficult undertaking. The verse corrects this notion by stating that for Allah, the establishment of the Hour is as easy and instantaneous as the blink of an eye, or even quicker. He does not require preparation, tools, or time. His command “Be” suffices, as stated earlier in the sūrah. The phrase “or even less” emphasizes the absolute effortlessness; human language struggles to capture the instantaneous nature of divine action. The verse concludes, “Surely Allah is Most Capable of everything.” This is the logical seal. The combination of absolute knowledge of the unseen and absolute power over all things, including the instantaneous creation of the Hour, leaves no room for doubt about the truth of the resurrection. For the believers, this verse reinforces the importance of trusting in Allah's plan, even when the unseen is obscure. Matters that are hidden, such as the timing of relief or the outcome of events, are in the hands of the One who knows all. It also instills a sense of the imminence of the Hour, not in a chronological sense, but in the sense that its coming is so easy for Allah that it could occur at any moment. This awareness encourages constant preparedness. The verse also serves as a comfort: the same power that can bring the Hour in the blink of an eye can bring relief to the oppressed and justice to the wronged with similar swiftness.
78 | And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing, and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect so perhaps you would be thankful. | The sūrah returns to a most intimate sign, the creation of each human being. “And Allah brought you out of the wombs of your mothers while you knew nothing” describes the state of complete ignorance at birth. An infant emerges from the womb possessing no knowledge of the world, utterly dependent and unaware. This starting point of absolute zero knowledge is a humbling reminder of human origins. The phrase “brought you out” emphasizes the divine agency in the birth process, which is a miracle often overlooked due to its commonality. Then the verse mentions three specific faculties given by Allah: “and gave you hearing, sight, and intellect.” The order is significant and corresponds to developmental reality. Hearing is the first sense to develop and function, even in the womb, and is the primary channel for receiving revelation and guidance. Sight comes next, allowing the person to observe the signs in the world. Finally, intellect (af’idah, often translated as hearts or minds) refers to the faculty of understanding, reasoning, and spiritual perception, which integrates the data from the senses and enables comprehension of higher truths. These three faculties are the means by which a human being acquires knowledge and navigates life. They are gifts, not achievements. The purpose of these gifts is stated at the end: “so perhaps you would be thankful.” The entire process—from the emergence out of darkness and ignorance into the world equipped with these powerful tools—is designed to elicit gratitude. The gratitude should be directed to the Giver, not merely expressed as a vague thankfulness but manifested in using those faculties for the purpose they were given: to know Allah, to worship Him, and to do good. The verse is a profound call to self-reflection. Every moment of hearing, seeing, and understanding is a blessing that demands appreciation. The disbelievers use these very faculties to deny their Creator, which is the ultimate ingratitude. For the believer, this verse transforms the ordinary experience of sensory perception into a constant reminder of Allah's favours. It also places a moral responsibility on the use of these faculties: the ear should be kept from listening to falsehood, the eye from looking at what is forbidden, and the mind from entertaining corrupt thoughts. The verse connects the biological human development to the spiritual imperative of gratitude, forming a seamless link between the physical and the moral.
79 | Have they not seen the birds glide in the open sky? None holds them up except Allah. Surely in this are signs for those who believe. | This verse draws attention to a readily observable sign of divine power: the flight of birds. “Have they not seen the birds glide in the open sky?” The word “glide” (musakhkharāt) indicates that the birds are subjected and made subservient, flying effortlessly in the vast space of the sky. The phrase “in the open sky” (jaww as-samā’) refers to the expanse between heaven and earth, the atmosphere. A bird, heavier than air, suspended in mid-air without falling, is a daily miracle that people take for granted. The verse points out that this is not an autonomous natural phenomenon but a direct act of divine sustenance: “None holds them up except Allah.” The laws of aerodynamics that scientists later discovered are the mechanisms through which Allah's will operates. The bird does not hold itself up by its own power; it relies on the air, the design of its wings, its lightweight bones, and its instinctive flying skills, all of which are creations of Allah. The suspension of the bird is an ongoing act of divine power, moment by moment. This verse, like earlier ones, answers the materialist view that nature operates independently. It asserts that Allah is the immediate sustainer, holding the bird in the sky just as He holds the stars in their orbits. The verse concludes, “Surely in this are signs for those who believe.” The sign is specifically for believers because they have the faith that allows them to see the divine hand behind natural phenomena. The bird's flight becomes a symbol of the soul's dependence on Allah. Without His hold, it would plummet. Similarly, the believer's spiritual state is upheld only by Allah's mercy. The verse also evokes a sense of the beauty and freedom of birds in flight, which is a source of aesthetic pleasure, yet another layer of divine favour. In a modern context, where air travel has made humans jaded, this verse invites us to look at birds not as mere biological machines but as living signs, performing their flight by the command and hold of Allah. It encourages a contemplative look at nature, where every bird becomes a preacher calling to faith for those who have eyes to see and hearts to believe.
80 | And Allah has made your homes a place to rest, and has given you tents from the hide of animals, light to handle when you travel and when you camp. And out of their wool, fur, and hair He has given you furnishings and goods for a while. | This verse returns to the list of tangible blessings, focusing on dwellings and clothing derived from animals. “And Allah has made your homes a place to rest” begins with the most fundamental blessing after food and drink: shelter. The home is a place of sakīnah, tranquillity and rest, where a person retreats from the outside world, finds privacy, and enjoys comfort with family. This is a divine gift often underappreciated until one is homeless. The verse then expands to mobile dwellings: “and has given you tents from the hide of animals, light to handle when you travel and when you camp.” This was directly relevant to the nomadic Arab lifestyle. Tents made from animal skins provided portable shelter that could be easily carried and set up during journeys and at campsites. They were light, practical, and perfectly suited to the desert environment. The verse acknowledges Allah's provision in both settled and nomadic contexts. Then, “And out of their wool, fur, and hair He has given you furnishings and goods for a while.” The “their” refers back to the cattle (anʿām) mentioned throughout the sūrah. Wool from sheep, fur from camels, and hair from goats are all raw materials that Allah has provided for making carpets, blankets, cushions, clothing, and other household furnishings. The phrase “for a while” (ilā ḥīn) indicates that these goods are for a limited period, the duration of worldly life. They are temporary comforts, not eternal possessions. This verse ties together multiple threads: the blessing of animals, the blessing of shelter, and the temporary nature of worldly goods. It also implicitly addresses the nomadic Quraysh and other tribes, reminding them that their very shelters come from the animals created by Allah. The idols have no part in providing these necessities. For the modern believer, the verse encourages gratitude for the roof over one's head, the bed one sleeps in, and the clothing one wears, all of which trace back to the earth and its creatures that Allah has subjected. It fosters a minimalist spiritual attitude: these goods are for “a while,” so one should not become overly attached to them, but use them comfortably while focusing on the eternal home of the Hereafter. The verse beautifully combines the recognition of present blessings with the perspective of transience.
81 | And Allah has provided you shade out of what He created, and has given you shelter in the mountains. He has also provided you with clothes protecting you from the heat ˹and cold˺, and armour shielding you in battle. This is how He perfects His favour upon you, so perhaps you will ˹fully˺ submit ˹to Him˺. | This verse gathers several further blessings that demonstrate the completeness of Allah’s care, moving from homes and furnishings to shade, natural shelters, clothing, and armour. “And Allah has provided you shade out of what He created” points to the many things in creation that cast shade and protect from the harsh sun. In the Arabian desert, shade was not a luxury but a necessity for survival. Trees, clouds, mountains, and even the walls of houses are all among the things Allah created to provide relief from heat. The believer is invited to see a simple patch of shade not as a random physical phenomenon but as a deliberate gift from the Creator who knew His servants would need coolness and rest. The verse then says, “and has given you shelter in the mountains.” This refers to the caves and natural recesses in the mountains that served as refuges for travellers, hermits, and even prophets. Mountains are not just majestic stabilisers of the earth but also natural fortresses offering protection. The verse then mentions clothing: “He has also provided you with clothes protecting you from the heat and cold.” The Arabic mentions heat specifically, with cold understood as the opposite extreme. Clothing is a basic blessing that provides modesty and protection from the elements. The mention of heat as the primary threat resonates with the desert context, but the principle applies universally. Then the verse adds “and armour shielding you in battle.” This shifts from protection against nature to protection against human harm. Coats of mail, shields, and helmets are part of Allah’s provision, inspired to humanity through the same creative wisdom that taught the bee to build its hive. The verse ties these together: “This is how He perfects His favour upon you, so perhaps you will fully submit to Him.” The perfection of favour means that Allah has not left any essential need unaddressed. The purpose of this comprehensive care is to move human hearts to complete submission, Islam. When a person realises that their shade, their shelter, their clothing, and even their safety in war are all from Allah, the natural response is to surrender wholly to Him. The verse teaches that gratitude should lead to submission, not just a fleeting “thank you.” The believer who sees a tree’s shadow or puts on a coat should remember the Perfecter of favours and renew their commitment to live in obedience.
82 | But if they turn away, then your duty ˹O Prophet˺ is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly. | After listing the multitude of blessings that should logically lead to submission, the sūrah now addresses the reality of rejection. The verse begins with “But if they turn away,” referring to those who, despite all the signs and favours, still refuse to believe and submit. This turning away is not a passive neglect but an active aversion, a deliberate choice to reject the truth. The verse then comforts the Prophet and defines the boundaries of his responsibility: “then your duty O Prophet is only to deliver the message clearly.” The word “only” (innamā) restricts the Prophet’s obligation to clear conveyance. He is not responsible for forcing people to believe, for guiding their hearts, or for the ultimate outcome of their choices. The message has been delivered with clarity, supported by rational arguments, natural signs, and historical warnings. The eloquence and truth of the Qur’an are evident. If people still turn away, the Prophet is not to blame. This verse was crucial for the psychological well-being of the Prophet, who grieved deeply over the rejection of his people. It relieved him of a burden he was never meant to carry. This principle is reiterated in several places in the Qur’an, emphasizing that guidance is solely in Allah’s hands. The duty of the messenger is to convey, and he fulfilled that duty perfectly. For all callers to Islam, this verse provides a model: one must strive to deliver the message with wisdom and clarity, but one must not become despondent or feel personally responsible when people reject it. The clarity of the delivery is the standard; the response is between the individual and Allah. The verse also implies a warning to those who turn away: they cannot claim they were not warned or that the message was ambiguous. The clear delivery leaves them with no excuse. They turn away despite understanding, and thus their accountability is complete. The verse also teaches that the value of our work is not measured solely by the results in terms of conversions, but by the faithfulness and clarity with which we convey the truth. The Prophet’s mission was a resounding success not because everyone believed, but because he delivered the message with perfect clarity, leaving a lasting proof for humanity. This liberates the modern believer from the tyranny of results, focusing instead on the quality of effort and sincerity.
83 | They are aware of Allah’s favours, but still deny them. And most of them are ˹truly˺ ungrateful. | This verse diagnoses the spiritual sickness of those who turn away after the clear delivery of the message. “They are aware of Allah’s favours, but still deny them” points to a knowing rejection. The disbelievers of Mecca, and by extension all who reject faith after clear evidence, do not lack information. They recognize the blessings in their lives—the rain, the cattle, the home, the shade—and they know deep within that these come from Allah. The Arabic word yaʿrifūn indicates a deep, experiential awareness. They are not ignorant; they are in a state of wilful denial. This denial is not merely verbal but is expressed through their actions: they worship other beings, they fail to give thanks, and they use Allah’s gifts in disobedience. The verse then adds, “And most of them are truly ungrateful.” The word kāfirūn, translated as ungrateful, is the same word for disbelievers, revealing the intimate link between ingratitude and disbelief. To be ungrateful for Allah’s favours is a form of kufr, a covering up of the truth. The verse says “most of them,” indicating that there were some among the Quraysh who, despite the general atmosphere, were sincere seekers or who later embraced Islam. But the majority persisted in ingratitude. This verse is a profound reflection on human psychology. It is possible to live surrounded by blessings, to intellectually acknowledge their source, and yet to live as a practical atheist, giving devotion and ultimate thanks to other things—one’s own efforts, luck, nature, or false gods. This is the tragedy of the human condition: knowledge that does not translate into gratitude and worship is a deeper form of ignorance. The verse invites believers to examine their own lives: do we “know” that our health, family, and provision are from Allah, yet fail to demonstrate that knowledge in our daily worship, charity, and obedience? The verse warns against the danger of familiarity; the Quraysh were so accustomed to the favours that they took them for granted and denied their significance. The cure is the constant, conscious practice of shukr, gratitude, which keeps the heart alive to the reality of the Giver. The verse also serves as a warning that intellectual recognition without heartfelt gratitude and submission is a path to ruin.
84 | ˹Consider, O Prophet,˺ the Day We will call ˹a prophet as˺ a witness from every faith-community. Then the disbelievers will neither be allowed to plead nor appease ˹their Lord˺. | The sūrah now turns to scenes from the Day of Judgment to underscore the consequences of the ingratitude and denial just described. The verse begins with “Consider, O Prophet,” an interjection to draw attention to the gravity of the coming day. “The Day We will call a prophet as a witness from every faith-community” describes a universal courtroom scene. Every nation that received a messenger will have that messenger brought forward as a witness. The prophet will testify about his people: who believed, who disbelieved, who obeyed, and who rejected. He will confirm that he delivered the message clearly and will bear witness to their responses. This is both a vindication for the messengers and a devastating proof against the deniers. The prophets, who were mocked and persecuted in the world, will be honoured as the key witnesses, and their testimony will be decisive. The verse then states the immediate consequence: “Then the disbelievers will neither be allowed to plead nor appease their Lord.” On that Day, the disbelievers will be silenced. They will not be given permission to make excuses or argue their case, because the evidence is overwhelming and their own prophet’s testimony is against them. The word “appease” translates yuʿtabūn, which can mean to seek to please or make amends. They will not be given an opportunity to offer excuses or compensation to escape punishment. In the world, they might have talked their way out of trouble, but on that Day, mouths will be sealed and the facts will speak for themselves. This verse is a sobering reminder of the finality of divine judgment. The presence of the prophet as a witness underscores the mercy inherent in the sending of messengers; they are not just warners but also witnesses for or against their people. For the believers, this scene brings comfort: the truth for which they were ridiculed will be publicly vindicated, and the prophets they followed will stand as their advocates. For the deniers, it is a terrifying prospect: the very person they rejected will be the one whose testimony seals their fate. The verse encourages believers to live in a way that will make their prophet’s testimony a source of joy, not sorrow, on that Day.
85 | And when the wrongdoers face the punishment, it will not be lightened for them, nor will they be delayed ˹from it˺. | This verse continues the description of the disbelievers’ state on the Day of Judgment. “When the wrongdoers face the punishment” uses the word ẓalamū, those who wronged themselves through disbelief and sin. The moment they are confronted with the reality of the torment they used to deny, their ordeal begins. The verse immediately eliminates any hope of relief: “it will not be lightened for them.” The Arabic yukhaffaf indicates a reduction in intensity or duration. In this world, even the most severe suffering often has moments of respite, or one can hope for recovery or death to end the pain. In Hell, there is no such alleviation. The punishment is constant, unrelenting, and at full force. This is because their crimes were not temporary lapses but a persistent state of denial and ingratitude that defined their lives. The second part of the verse, “nor will they be delayed from it,” addresses a different hope: perhaps the punishment could be postponed, giving them a break or a chance to negotiate. On the Day of Judgment, once the judgment is passed, there is no delay. The punishment begins and continues without reprieve. This verse underscores the severity and finality of divine justice. It also highlights the mercy of Allah in the world, where punishments are often delayed and opportunities for repentance are abundant. The contrast is stark: in this life, a sinner can wake up the next morning, breathe fresh air, and turn back to Allah. That window of delay is a gift. Once the Hereafter begins, that window closes forever. For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivator to hasten to good deeds and repentance. It instills a healthy fear that prevents complacency. The wrongdoers in this context are those who rejected the clear message after being fully aware of Allah’s favours, as described in the previous verses. The verse also provides a perspective on the sufferings of this world: even the worst worldly pain is temporary and can be lightened by hope or death; the punishment of the next world offers no such escape. This realization should reorient one’s priorities, making the avoidance of that eternal punishment the central concern of life. The verse is a pure, unadorned warning that leaves no ambiguity about the fate of those who die in a state of persistent, unrepented wrongdoing.
86 | And when the polytheists see their associate-gods, they will say, “Our Lord! These are our associate-gods that we used to invoke besides You.” Their gods will throw a rebuttal at them, ˹saying,˺ “You are definitely liars.” | This verse zooms in on a specific, dramatic moment for the polytheists on the Day of Judgment. “When the polytheists see their associate-gods” indicates that the idols or beings they worshipped will be brought before them. Some commentators say that Allah will give life to the idols or that the false gods, whether devils, jinn, or even righteous beings who were worshipped, will be present. The polytheists, in a desperate attempt to shift blame or perhaps in a moment of confused hope, will cry out, “Our Lord! These are our associate-gods that we used to invoke besides You.” They point to those they worshipped, hoping perhaps that these beings will acknowledge them or intercede. This is a pitiful scene of abandonment. The response is immediate and shattering: “Their gods will throw a rebuttal at them, saying, ‘You are definitely liars.’” The word “throw” suggests a forceful rejection. The very objects of their worship will disown them and brand them as liars. This is the ultimate betrayal. The false gods, if they are devils or misguided leaders, will deny that they ever asked to be worshipped or that they had any divine authority. If they are idols, Allah will give them speech to refute their worshippers, declaring that the polytheists’ claims about them were lies. The polytheists used to claim that these idols were intermediaries or partners with Allah, but on that Day, the truth will be spoken by the very things they fashioned. This scene is deeply ironic and tragic. The beings they devoted their lives to, fought for, and sacrificed for, will turn against them. This verse exposes the utter bankruptcy of shirk. In the world, the polytheist invests so much hope and identity in false gods, but in the Hereafter, that investment yields only public humiliation and rejection. For the believer, this verse is a powerful reinforcement of tawḥīd. It also serves as a warning about the attachments of the heart: anything we cling to besides Allah—whether a human idol, a career, a political ideology—will ultimately fail us and testify against our misplaced devotion. The only loyalty that endures is loyalty to the truth. The verse encourages the believer to sever any hidden attachment to false hopes and to anchor the heart solely in Allah, who will never deny His sincere servants.
87 | They will offer ˹full˺ submission to Allah on that Day, and whatever ˹gods˺ they fabricated will fail them. | This verse continues the scene of the polytheists’ despair. After the shocking rebuttal from their associate-gods, “They will offer full submission to Allah on that Day.” The word “submission” translates aslamū, from the same root as Islam. On that Day, every being, willingly or unwillingly, will submit to the undeniable reality of Allah’s sovereignty. The polytheists who spent their lives in arrogant rebellion will finally bow in complete surrender. Their tongues, which used to mock the idea of submission, will now confess the truth. But this submission is not the voluntary, faith-based Islam that benefits a person in the world. It is the compelled submission of one who has no other choice, a recognition of reality when it is too late to change one’s destiny. The second part of the verse delivers the outcome: “and whatever gods they fabricated will fail them.” The word “fail” translates ḍalla, meaning to be lost, to vanish, to be of no avail. The gods they invented—whether carved from stone, imagined in myths, or elevated from among the jinn and humans—will disappear from their sight and their grasp. They will be utterly alone, with no intercessor, no protector, no helper. The investment of a lifetime of worship, offerings, and devotion will prove to be a mirage. This verse encapsulates the entire message of the sūrah regarding shirk. The false gods are not merely powerless; they actively abandon their worshippers at the critical moment. The submission that the polytheists finally offer is a tragic counterpart to the voluntary submission of the believers described earlier. The believers submitted in the world, unseen, based on faith, and will be rewarded with peace and Paradise. The polytheists submit when the unseen becomes seen, and they receive only loss. For the contemporary reader, this verse prompts a deep reflection on what one is ultimately serving. Many modern “fabricated gods”—such as money, status, or the self—will similarly vanish when death arrives. The only reality that remains is Allah. The verse calls for a proactive, voluntary Islam now, while the choice is meaningful and the submission can be born of love and hope rather than forced despair. It is a reminder that the time for choosing one’s ultimate allegiance is limited to this world, and the fabricated gods that seem so real today will be exposed as nothing on the Day when all masks fall.
88 | For those who disbelieve and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, We will add more punishment to their punishment for all the corruption they spread. | This verse pronounces a heightened punishment for a specific category of disbelievers: those who not only disbelieve themselves but actively prevent others from believing. “Those who disbelieve and hinder others from the Way of Allah” refers to the leaders of disbelief, the propagandists, the persecutors who blocked people from Islam. The Quraysh chieftains, like Abū Jahl and Abū Lahab, not only rejected the Prophet but stationed men to stop pilgrims from hearing the Qur’an, tortured converts to make them recant, and waged a relentless campaign of obstruction. The “Way of Allah” is the path of Islam, which leads to Him. Hindering from it is a grievous sin because it multiplies evil; the misguidance spreads, affecting generations. The verse declares the consequence: “We will add more punishment to their punishment.” This means that on top of the torment they deserve for their own disbelief, they will receive an extra dose of punishment for each person they misled or obstructed. The principle of double or multiple punishment for misleaders is also mentioned in other verses. The addition is described as being “for all the corruption they spread.” The word fasād, corruption, encompasses the moral, social, and spiritual chaos they caused. By suppressing the truth and promoting falsehood, they corrupted the natural order of society and the souls of individuals. The “addition” of punishment is a terrifying concept; the punishment of Hell is already unimaginable, and to have it multiplied is a fate beyond human comprehension. This verse serves as a severe deterrent against any form of obstructing the path to God, whether through mockery, coercion, producing misleading media, or misrepresenting Islam to turn people away. It also applies to those who, within the Muslim community, create obstacles for people seeking religious knowledge or practicing their faith. Conversely, it implies a great reward for those who facilitate the Way of Allah and make it easy for others to come to faith. The verse is a powerful reminder of social responsibility: our actions regarding faith are not private; they ripple outwards. The Quraysh leaders thought they were defending their heritage; they were actually multiplying their own torment. The believer who reads this is motivated to be a means of guidance, not hindrance, and to use whatever influence they have to remove obstacles from the path of those sincerely seeking Allah.
89 | ˹Consider, O Prophet,˺ the Day We will call against every faith-community a witness of their own. And We will call you to be a witness against these ˹people of yours˺. We have revealed to you the Book as an explanation of all things, a guide, a mercy, and good news for those who ˹fully˺ submit. | This verse resumes the theme of witnesses from verse 84, with a direct and weighty address to the Prophet Muḥammad. “Consider, O Prophet, the Day We will call against every faith-community a witness of their own.” Every ummah will have its own prophet brought forward to testify. This witness will be “of their own,” meaning from among them, speaking their language, having lived among them, so his testimony is intimate and undeniable. Then comes the specific and awe-inspiring declaration: “And We will call you to be a witness against these people of yours.” The Prophet Muḥammad will be the witness over his own community, the Quraysh and all who came after him until the Day of Judgment. His testimony will encompass who believed, who rejected, who obeyed, and who innovated. This is a tremendous honour for the Prophet and a heavy responsibility for his ummah. The verse then shifts to a description of the Qur’an that underpins this witness: “We have revealed to you the Book as an explanation of all things, a guide, a mercy, and good news for those who fully submit.” The Qur’an is described with four attributes. First, it is “an explanation of all things” (tibyānan li-kulli shay’), meaning it clarifies every fundamental matter of faith, ethics, and law, either in detail or through principles. Second, it is “a guide,” showing the straight path. Third, it is “a mercy,” because following it leads to eternal bliss and even worldly peace. Fourth, it is “good news” for the submitters, promising them Paradise and Allah’s pleasure. The phrase “those who fully submit” (muslimūn) identifies the intended beneficiaries. The verse ties the Prophet’s role as witness to the clarity and completeness of the revelation he brought. He delivered the Book that explains everything; therefore, no one can claim ignorance. The verse is both a comfort to the Prophet, reminding him of his high station on that Day, and a warning to the community to live according to the Book, so that their prophet’s testimony is favourable. For Muslims today, this verse instills a profound sense of accountability: we will be among those about whom the Prophet testifies. The Qur’an, which we have in our hands, is the very Book described as an explanation of all things, and our response to it will be witnessed.
90 | Indeed, Allah commands justice, grace, as well as courtesy to close relatives. He forbids indecency, wickedness, and aggression. He instructs you so perhaps you will be mindful. | This verse is one of the most comprehensive summaries of Islamic ethics in the Qur’an, often recited in the Friday sermon. “Indeed, Allah commands justice” begins with the foundational principle of ʿadl. Justice means giving everyone their due, whether in legal rulings, business dealings, or personal relationships. It requires fairness, impartiality, and upholding the rights of others, even against one’s own interests. Then the command elevates: “grace” (iḥsān), which is excellence and benevolence beyond what is strictly due. Iḥsān means to do good, to forgive, to be generous, and to worship Allah as if you see Him. It is the higher level of morality where one not only refrains from harm but actively seeks to benefit others. The third command is “courtesy to close relatives” (ītā’i dhī al-qurbā), which emphasizes maintaining family ties, supporting kin, and showing special kindness to relatives. This triad of commands—justice, excellence, and family care—forms the positive moral framework. The verse then moves to prohibitions: “He forbids indecency” (faḥshā’), which includes all shameful acts, particularly sexual immorality and obscenity. “Wickedness” (munkar) is any act universally condemned by sound reason and revelation, encompassing major sins. “Aggression” (baghy) is transgression against others, oppression, and the unjust use of power. The verse then states the purpose of this moral instruction: “He instructs you so perhaps you will be mindful.” The goal is mindfulness, taẕakkur, which is a continuous awareness of Allah’s commands and prohibitions, leading to righteous conduct. This verse is profound in its breadth and balance. It combines the minimum standard (justice), the ideal standard (excellence), and the specific social bond (family). The prohibitions cover personal morality, public decency, and social justice. It is a complete ethical charter. According to some early commentators, this verse is so encompassing that if there were no other verse in the Qur’an, it would suffice as a guide to righteousness. For contemporary life, this verse offers a clear moral compass in a world of ethical confusion. Every decision, whether in policy, business, or personal life, can be measured against these commands. The believer is called to be a person of justice, to go beyond it with grace, to cherish family ties, and to avoid the triple evils of indecency, wickedness, and aggression. The verse ends with the hope that this reminder will lead to a state of constant God-consciousness.
91 | Honour Allah’s covenant when you make a pledge, and do not break your oaths after confirming them, having made Allah your guarantor. Surely Allah knows all you do. | Following the comprehensive ethical commands, the sūrah now addresses the sanctity of oaths and covenants. “Honour Allah’s covenant when you make a pledge” uses the phrase “Allah’s covenant” (ʿahd Allāh) to elevate the seriousness of any solemn pledge, particularly those made in the name of Allah or with Allah as a witness. This includes the covenant of faith itself, the pledge of allegiance to the Prophet, treaties, and personal vows. The command to “honour” means to fulfill the pledge completely, without cheating or breaking it. The verse then adds, “and do not break your oaths after confirming them.” The emphasis is on the moment after a solemn oath has been affirmed; to break it afterward is a sign of weak character and disregard for the divine witness. The phrase “having made Allah your guarantor” heightens the responsibility. When a person swears by Allah or makes Allah a witness to their promise, they are invoking the highest authority. To then violate that oath is to show contempt for that authority. The final clause, “Surely Allah knows all you do,” is both a reassurance for the faithful who keep their word and a warning for those who break it. Nothing is hidden from Him, and every broken oath is recorded. This verse was revealed in a context where the pagan Arabs had a custom of making alliances and treaties, but often broke them when it was convenient. The Muslims are commanded to be a people of their word, whose promises are ironclad. The verse also has a deeper spiritual dimension: the covenant with Allah is the primordial testimony of tawḥīd that every soul has made, and breaking it through shirk is the ultimate betrayal of an oath. In daily life, the verse teaches that a Muslim’s word should be reliable. Business contracts, marriage vows, political treaties, and even casual promises are all encompassed. The modern world often treats promises as flexible, but this verse calls for a radical integrity. The phrase “having made Allah your guarantor” is a powerful psychological barrier against breaking promises; it reminds the believer that the One who hears the oath will also hold them accountable. The verse also encourages carefulness before making an oath; one should not swear lightly, but when one does, fulfilment is a sacred duty.
92 | Do not be like the woman who ˹foolishly˺ unravels her yarn after it is firmly spun, by taking your oaths as a means of deceiving one another in favour of a stronger group. Surely Allah tests you through this. And on the Day of Judgment He will certainly make your differences clear to you. | This verse uses a vivid metaphor to illustrate the folly of breaking oaths. “Do not be like the woman who foolishly unravels her yarn after it is firmly spun.” The image is of a woman who has painstakingly spun thread, twisting the fibres together until they form a strong, cohesive cord. Then, in a moment of folly, she unpicks her own work, reducing the strong yarn back to useless, tangled fibres. This metaphor is applied directly: “by taking your oaths as a means of deceiving one another in favour of a stronger group.” This refers to the pre-Islamic practice where a tribe would make a treaty with another, but if a stronger tribe appeared, they would break the first oath to ally with the more powerful group. The oath, which should have been a bond of trust, was treated as a tool for opportunistic deception. The unraveling of the yarn is the destruction of trust and the moral fabric of society. The verse then says, “Surely Allah tests you through this.” The test is in the area of integrity. Will people keep their word when it is disadvantageous, or will they break it for worldly gain? The differing circumstances—a weak ally versus a strong one—expose the true content of character. The verse concludes, “And on the Day of Judgment He will certainly make your differences clear to you.” The disputes and betrayals that occur over broken treaties and oaths will be fully adjudicated by Allah, who will reveal the truth and assign blame where it belongs. This verse is a powerful lesson in the ethics of commitment. The metaphor of the unraveled yarn is particularly striking because it shows that breaking an oath is not just a harm to the other party but a self-destructive act that undoes one’s own moral integrity. The effort of building trust is wasted. In contemporary contexts, this applies to all forms of contractual and personal dishonesty: breaking business agreements when a better deal comes along, betraying confidences for social gain, or reneging on promises when they become inconvenient. The verse teaches that such behaviour is foolish, not clever, because it unravels one’s character and invites divine scrutiny. The test is a constant reality, and the Day of Judgment will expose the true winners and losers in the economy of trust.
93 | Had Allah willed, He could have easily made you one community ˹of believers˺, but He leaves to stray whoever He wills and guides whoever He wills. And you will certainly be questioned about what you used to do. | This verse returns to the theme of divine will and human responsibility, connecting it to the preceding discussion of oaths and differences. “Had Allah willed, He could have easily made you one community of believers.” This asserts that Allah has the power to create all humanity as a single, uniformly believing community, without any differences in faith. He could have made humans like angels, programmed to obey without free choice. But He did not will that. Instead, He granted free will and revealed guidance. The verse continues, “but He leaves to stray whoever He wills and guides whoever He wills.” This statement must be understood within the Qur’anic framework: Allah’s will to guide or leave astray is based on His wisdom and justice. Those who sincerely seek truth are guided; those who persistently reject it are left to stray. Allah does not randomly misguide; the straying is a consequence of the individual’s own choice, which Allah then seals. The verse then makes it personal: “And you will certainly be questioned about what you used to do.” The address is plural, encompassing all humans. This shifts the focus from the divine decree to human accountability. Despite the overarching divine will, every person is responsible for their actions and choices, and will be questioned about them. The differences among people, including the breaking of oaths and the disputes mentioned previously, do not negate individual responsibility. This verse elegantly balances the doctrines of predestination and free will. It answers the fatalistic argument used by the polytheists who said, “Had Allah willed, we would not have worshipped other than Him.” The response is that Allah’s will to give free choice does not excuse wrong choices. You will be questioned about what you did. For the believer, this verse inspires a balanced mindset: complete trust in Allah’s overarching will, combined with a keen sense of personal responsibility. One should not use divine decree as an excuse for sin or passivity. The fact that Allah could have made us all guided but did not, means that our current state of belief is a test, and our deeds are the measure of our success. The verse also fosters humility: guidance is a gift from Allah, not a personal achievement, so one should constantly pray for steadfastness and not feel superior over those who are astray, since it is Allah who guides.
94 | And do not take your oaths as a means of deceiving one another or your feet will slip after they have been firm. Then you will taste the evil ˹consequences˺ of hindering ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and you will suffer a tremendous punishment. | The sūrah returns to the specific warning about oaths, reinforcing the earlier metaphor with a more direct threat. “And do not take your oaths as a means of deceiving one another” repeats the prohibition of using solemn pledges for fraud. The phrase “or your feet will slip after they have been firm” uses a powerful image. Feet that were firmly planted on the path of faith and integrity will slip and stumble. The breaking of oaths leads to a spiritual fall. A person who had been a believer, or at least appeared to be on the right path, can lose their footing entirely through consistent deceit. The “slip” is both in this world—loss of reputation, trust, and inner peace—and in the Hereafter—falling into Hell. The verse then specifies the consequence: “Then you will taste the evil consequences of hindering others from the Way of Allah.” The link between oath-breaking and hindering from Allah’s way is clarified. When people see a believer breaking oaths and acting treacherously, they are repelled from Islam. The bad example becomes an obstacle. Thus, the oath-breaker sins not only personally but causes others to turn away from faith, incurring the multiplied punishment mentioned earlier. The verse climaxes, “and you will suffer a tremendous punishment.” The adjective ʿaẓīm magnifies the torment beyond ordinary description. This severe warning shows the gravity of deceit and its ripple effects. In the Meccan context, the Muslims were a small, vulnerable community. Their integrity was one of their greatest strengths. If they resorted to the same treacherous tactics as the pagans, they would lose the moral high ground and their dawah would be crippled. This verse therefore protects the community’s ethical fabric. For contemporary Muslims, the lesson remains vital: personal dishonesty is not a private matter. When a Muslim cheats in business, lies in personal relationships, or breaks promises, they not only harm themselves but also damage the reputation of Islam and become a barrier to others accepting the faith. The “tremendous punishment” is proportionate to the damage caused. The verse calls for a consistent integrity that keeps the feet firm on the path, recognising that a single slip through deceit can lead to a cascade of negative consequences both personal and communal.
95 | And do not trade Allah’s covenant for a fleeting gain. What is with Allah is certainly far better for you, if only you knew. | This verse encapsulates the core motivation behind oath-breaking and offers the divine alternative. “And do not trade Allah’s covenant for a fleeting gain” uses commercial language. The covenant, which includes oaths, promises, and the broader commitment to faith, is a precious asset. The worldly gain—money, power, an alliance with a stronger tribe—is a “fleeting gain,” a small, temporary profit. The imagery is of a foolish trader who exchanges a priceless, eternal treasure for a pittance of perishable goods. The phrase “Allah’s covenant” elevates every faithful promise to the level of a sacred bond with the Divine. The verse then presents the superior merchandise: “What is with Allah is certainly far better for you, if only you knew.” The reward that Allah has stored for those who keep their covenants—Paradise, His pleasure, and honour in this life—is incomparably greater than any worldly benefit gained through betrayal. The problem is a knowledge deficit: “if only you knew” (law kuntum taʿlamūn). If people truly grasped the value of what is with Allah, they would never trade it for temporary gains. This lack of knowledge is a lack of certainty (yaqīn). The believer who possesses yaqīn sees the Hereafter as more real and more valuable than the material world, and so they easily sacrifice the latter for the former. This verse is a powerful antidote to the temptations of dishonesty. When faced with a situation where breaking one’s word could bring financial profit or social advancement, the believer is reminded that the gain is fleeting, while the loss of Allah’s pleasure is eternal. The verse trains the mind to perform a cost-benefit analysis with an eternal timescale. The phrase “what is with Allah” includes not just the rewards of Paradise but also the spiritual states of peace, dignity, and divine love that accompany integrity in this life. The verse is a call to elevate one’s ambitions beyond the immediate and the material. For the modern Muslim navigating a competitive, often unethical marketplace, this verse is a constant reference. The temptation to lie on a contract, to betray a partner, or to break a promise for a promotion is the same ancient trade. The answer is the same: what is with Allah is better, and the fleeting gain will soon evaporate, leaving only regret.
96 | Whatever you have will end, but whatever Allah has is everlasting. And We will certainly reward the steadfast according to the best of their deeds. | This verse provides the foundational principle behind the argument of the previous verse. “Whatever you have will end” is a statement of universal truth. All possessions, all relationships, all worldly achievements, and even life itself are temporary and will inevitably perish or be left behind. The word “end” (yanfadu) implies complete exhaustion. No matter how vast one’s wealth or power, it is finite and running out. In contrast, “whatever Allah has is everlasting.” The treasures of the Hereafter, the reward, the pleasure of Allah, and the life of Paradise are eternal, never diminishing, never ending. This contrast is absolute. The fleeting gain of the previous verse is part of “whatever you have,” which is doomed to end. The covenant’s reward is part of “whatever Allah has,” which is everlasting. The verse then moves to a specific promise: “And We will certainly reward the steadfast according to the best of their deeds.” The steadfast (ṣābirūn) are those who patiently persevere in keeping their covenants, resisting the temptation of fleeting gains, and remaining firm in faith. They will be rewarded “according to the best of their deeds,” which means Allah will look at the finest actions they performed and reward them at that highest level, overlooking the lesser or mixed deeds. This is an incredible act of divine grace. The verse provides a complete framework for decision-making. When a believer is torn between a worldly benefit and an ethical principle, this verse provides the clarity: the worldly benefit is part of the temporary, exhaustible category; the ethical principle connects to the everlasting. The patient person, who can delay gratification and endure the loss of the fleeting for the sake of the eternal, is promised a reward that is not just equivalent but based on the highest standard of their overall conduct. This verse encourages a lifestyle of renunciation (zuhd) not in the sense of rejecting the world entirely, but in the sense of not attaching the heart to what will inevitably end. The modern believer surrounded by consumer culture can use this verse as a daily meditation. Every time we are tempted to compromise integrity for a material gain, we can ask: is this gain part of “what will end” or “what is everlasting”? The answer reframes the choice and makes patience not a burden but a wise investment with guaranteed, eternal returns.
97 | Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer, We will surely bless them with a good life, and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds. | This verse is a beautiful, inclusive promise that expands on the reward mentioned previously. “Whoever does good, whether male or female, and is a believer” establishes that the divine promise of reward transcends gender. Both men and women are equally capable of righteous action and equally eligible for its reward. The phrase “does good” translates ʿamila ṣāliḥan, meaning righteous deeds performed sincerely for Allah. The essential condition is “and is a believer,” because without faith, deeds lack the foundation that makes them acceptable and eternally rewarded. The promise is twofold. First, “We will surely bless them with a good life.” This good life (ḥayātan ṭayyibah) is a pure, wholesome, and contented existence in this world, even if it involves outward hardship. It includes inner peace, satisfaction with lawful provision, the sweetness of faith, and the ability to endure trials with patience. The good life is not defined by material abundance but by the quality of the heart’s connection to Allah. The believer may have less wealth than a disbeliever, but their life is “good” because it is filled with purpose, tranquillity, and divine companionship. Second, “and We will certainly reward them according to the best of their deeds.” This echoes the previous verse’s promise about the Hereafter, affirming that the reward will be based on the highest standard of their righteous actions. This verse is a cornerstone of hope and motivation. It demolishes any notion that piety leads to a miserable, deprived life. On the contrary, it promises that true goodness and faith produce the best form of life here and now, as well as the best eternal reward. The contemporary Muslim, facing the pressure to compromise faith for worldly success, is reminded that the “good life” is a direct promise from Allah to the righteous believer. It is a life that many wealthy and powerful people lack, despite their outward affluence. The verse also encourages both men and women to actively compete in good deeds, knowing that the playing field is level and the reward is certain. The inclusion of gender explicitly counters any cultural bias that might undervalue female piety. The verse is a declaration that the path to the good life and the eternal reward is open to every human being who combines faith with righteous action.
98 | When you recite the Quran, seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed. | After the extensive ethical teachings and promises, the sūrah now provides a vital spiritual tool for engaging with the Qur’an itself. “When you recite the Quran” addresses the believers directly, instructing them on the proper etiquette and spiritual preparation for approaching the divine word. The command is “seek refuge with Allah from Satan, the accursed.” The Arabic phrase is istiʿādhah, a seeking of protection. This instruction acknowledges a spiritual reality: the Qur’an is the source of guidance, and Satan, the sworn enemy of humanity, will do everything in his power to prevent a person from benefiting from it. The accursed one (ar-rajīm) is the one who is expelled from divine mercy, and he attacks the human heart with distractions, doubts, and whispers, especially during the recitation of the Qur’an. He might cause the mind to wander, bring up worldly concerns, cast doubts about the meaning, or induce arrogance or self-admiration. By saying “Aʿūdhu billāhi min ash-shayṭān ir-rajīm” before reciting, the believer consciously disengages from the enemy’s influence and places themselves under Allah’s protection. This act is not merely a ritual formula but a genuine turning of the heart to Allah, acknowledging one’s vulnerability and seeking His shelter. The verse thus teaches that reciting the Qur’an is not just an intellectual activity but a spiritual encounter that requires divine aid. The command is general, applying to every occasion of recitation, whether in prayer, in personal study, or in teaching. This practice became a consistent Sunnah of the Prophet and all Muslims. The placement of this verse in the sūrah is significant. The preceding verses dealt with oaths, covenants, and the temptations of worldly gain—all areas where Satan is particularly active in leading people astray. Seeking refuge before reciting the Qur’an is like clearing the channel of reception so that the divine message can enter the heart without static. For the modern believer, this verse is a practical reminder that the struggle against Satan’s whispers is ongoing, and the Qur’an is the primary target. One should never approach the Qur’an in a state of heedlessness or overconfidence. The simple, conscious act of seeking refuge is the key that unlocks the door of understanding and spiritual benefit.
99 | He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord. | This verse provides the reassurance behind the command to seek refuge. “He” refers to Satan. “He certainly has no authority over those who believe and put their trust in their Lord.” The word “authority” (sulṭān) means power, control, or compelling influence. Satan cannot force a believer to sin or to disbelieve. He can only whisper, suggest, and beautify evil. For those who have true faith (īmān) and who actively place their trust in Allah (tawakkul), Satan’s whisperings are ultimately ineffective. Faith serves as a shield, and trust in Allah serves as an active force that repels the enemy. The believer who is attacked by a satanic thought immediately turns to Allah, seeks refuge, and the whisper dissipates. The verse does not say that believers are never tempted; it says Satan has no authority over them, meaning he cannot overcome them or lead them to ultimate ruin. They may stumble at times, but they return to Allah, and their essential allegiance is not to Satan. This is a profound comfort for the struggling soul. The feeling of being under attack by evil thoughts or desires does not mean Satan has control; it is the resistance and the turning to Allah that proves his lack of authority. The verse mentions two essential qualities: belief and trust. Belief is the intellectual and heart-felt acceptance of the truth. Trust is the practical reliance on Allah in all matters, including the battle against one’s own lower self and Satan. Together, they form an impenetrable fortress. For the contemporary believer facing the relentless onslaught of temptations in modern media and society, this verse is a source of immense strength. It assures that no matter how powerful the external or internal calls to sin may seem, they have no real power over the one who is fortified by faith and trust. The battle is won not by human willpower alone but by anchoring oneself in Allah. The verse also clarifies the limits of Satan’s power; he is not a rival god but a creature with a limited role, and his machinations are ultimately under Allah’s control. The believer’s task is to maintain the qualities that render Satan powerless.
100 | His authority is only over those who take him as a patron and who—under his influence—associate ˹others˺ with Allah ˹in worship˺. | This verse completes the picture by identifying those over whom Satan does have authority. “His authority is only over those who take him as a patron” describes a voluntary, ongoing alliance. The word “patron” (walī) implies a close, protective, and guiding relationship. These are people who choose Satan as their leader, following his suggestions and adopting his worldview. They reject divine guidance and instead embrace the satanic path, whether consciously or through consistent neglect of Allah’s commands. The verse then specifies the ultimate manifestation of this alliance: “and who—under his influence—associate others with Allah in worship.” Shirk is the chief goal of Satan. He leads his patrons step by step until they commit the gravest sin of associating partners with Allah. While Satan’s authority extends to all forms of sin, the verse highlights shirk as the hallmark of his dominion because it is the unforgivable sin if died upon without repentance. The polytheists of Mecca are the immediate referent: they took Satan as their patron by following the traditions of their forefathers, and they ended up worshipping idols alongside Allah. However, the principle applies universally. Anyone who consistently obeys Satan’s whisperings, making him their guide in life, falls under his authority. The verse does not mean that Satan physically controls them, but that they have placed themselves within his sphere of influence by their choices, and Allah, in His justice, allows Satan to lead them further astray. This verse serves as a stark warning. To take Satan as a patron does not require a formal ritual; it happens whenever a person consistently prefers his desires over Allah’s commands, when they mock the truth, or when they feel more loyalty to the satanic systems of the world than to the community of faith. The good news, however, is that this authority is not inevitable or irreversible. The door of repentance is open. As soon as a person renounces this patronage, seeks refuge in Allah, and embraces faith and trust, they move from the camp of Satan’s authority to the protected fortress of Allah, as described in the previous verse. Together, verses 99 and 100 draw a clear line between the people of faith and the people of Satan, leaving no middle ground. The believer is called to examine their allegiances and ensure they are not, in any area of life, taking the accursed one as a patron.
101 | When We replace a verse with another—and Allah knows best what He reveals—they say, “You ˹Muḥammad˺ are just a fabricator.” In fact, most of them do not know. | This verse addresses the phenomenon of abrogation, naskh, which the disbelievers used as a pretext to attack the Prophet. "When We replace a verse with another" refers to the divine wisdom whereby certain rulings or recitations were superseded by later revelations. This replacement was not a correction of an error but a deliberate, purposeful progression in legislation and guidance, suited to the evolving circumstances of the nascent Muslim community. The parenthetical clause, "and Allah knows best what He reveals," affirms that every revelation, whether abrogating or abrogated, is rooted in perfect divine knowledge. Allah knows what is most beneficial for His servants at each stage, and His command is always wise. The disbelievers, however, seized upon this concept maliciously. They said, "You Muḥammad are just a fabricator." Their accusation was that the Prophet invented verses and then changed them when it suited him, a claim of forgery and inconsistency. This attack ignored the reality that abrogation occurs within a single divine source and serves a clear educational and legal purpose. The verse responds by stating, "In fact, most of them do not know." Their ignorance is twofold: they do not understand the nature of prophethood and progressive revelation, and they do not recognize that the Qur’an’s internal coherence is actually strengthened by such divinely guided development. A fabricator would be inconsistent and self-contradictory, whereas the Qur’an’s abrogations are part of a wise legislative plan. This verse also comforts the Prophet, assuring him that the accusation is born of ignorance, not of any genuine flaw in the revelation. For believers, it establishes that abrogation is a reality within the Qur’an, to be accepted with trust in Allah's wisdom, and that changes in rulings do not imply a change in divine knowledge but rather a change in the circumstances of those addressed. It calls for humility before divine legislation and warns against the arrogant dismissiveness of those who lack deep understanding.
102 | Say, “The holy spirit has brought it down from your Lord with the truth to reassure the believers, and as a guide and good news for those who submit ˹to Allah˺.” | This verse instructs the Prophet to respond to the accusations of fabrication with a clear declaration of the Qur’an’s divine origin. "Say, 'The holy spirit has brought it down from your Lord with the truth.'" The "holy spirit" refers to the Angel Gabriel, Jibrīl, who is described as al-rūḥ al-qudus, the spirit of sanctity and purity. He is the trusted emissary between Allah and His messengers. The phrase "from your Lord" emphasizes that the ultimate source is Allah, not any human. The revelation is brought down "with the truth," meaning its content is entirely true, containing no falsehood, and it brings the truth that establishes justice and guidance. The verse then outlines three purposes of this revelation. First, "to reassure the believers." The truth of the Qur’an provides certainty, stability, and peace to the hearts of those who accept it, especially in the face of accusations and doubts cast by the disbelievers. When the believers hear that the holy spirit brought the Qur’an, their faith is strengthened, and their hearts are settled. Second, it is "a guide," offering clear direction in all aspects of life, leading humanity from darkness to light. Third, it is "good news for those who submit to Allah," promising them eternal bliss and divine pleasure. The word muslimūn, those who submit, is used here as the comprehensive term for the followers of the truth. This verse reframes the entire debate: the Qur’an is not a human fabrication but a heavenly descent through the holiest of angels, designed to bring certainty, guidance, and glad tidings. For the believer, this verse enriches the appreciation of the Qur’an's spiritual provenance. The mention of the holy spirit highlights the immense sanctity of the revelation, carried by the purest of beings. The threefold function—reassurance, guidance, and good news—provides a complete spiritual program. In moments of doubt or when facing external attacks on the faith, the believer can recall that the Qur’an was sent specifically to reassure and guide, grounding them in a truth that transcends human criticism.
103 | And We surely know that they say, “No one is teaching him except a human.” But the man they refer to speaks a foreign tongue, whereas this ˹Quran˺ is ˹in˺ eloquent Arabic. | This verse addresses another specific accusation leveled by the Meccan polytheists to explain away the Qur’an’s miraculous nature. "And We surely know that they say, 'No one is teaching him except a human.'" The disbelievers, unable to accept that an unlettered man could produce such sublime scripture, claimed that the Prophet was being tutored by someone—a foreigner, perhaps a Christian or Jewish person, who fed him stories and information. The verse acknowledges that Allah is fully aware of their slander. It then refutes the accusation with a simple, devastating argument: "But the man they refer to speaks a foreign tongue, whereas this Quran is in eloquent Arabic." The alleged human source was a non-Arab, whose language was not the pure, highly stylized Arabic of the Qur’an. Some reports mention a Christian slave named Jabr or a blacksmith, both non-Arabs. The disbelievers’ claim that such a person could produce the Qur’an—a book of unmatched linguistic perfection, profound wisdom, and eloquence in the Arabic language—is absurd. The Qur’an’s Arabic is not merely grammatically correct; it is the pinnacle of eloquence, a miraculous standard that challenged the greatest Arab poets to produce even a single chapter like it, and they failed. A non-native speaker, however knowledgeable, could not possibly be the source of such linguistic genius. This refutation is based on rational observation and linguistic reality. The verse also highlights the divine wisdom in revealing the Qur’an in clear Arabic, the language of the Prophet’s people, so that they could understand it directly and witness its inimitable quality. For believers, this verse is a reminder of the Qur’an's unique literary and spiritual status. It also teaches a methodology for responding to baseless accusations: address them with simple, logical facts. The critics often rely on the ignorance of the masses, but a clear-headed analysis exposes the hollowness of their claims. The eloquence of the Qur’an remains a living miracle, and the historical inability of its detractors to find a credible human source testifies to its divine origin.
104 | Surely those who do not believe in Allah’s revelations will never be guided by Allah, and they will suffer a painful punishment. | This verse delivers a definitive verdict on those who persist in rejecting the clear signs and arguments presented throughout the sūrah. "Surely those who do not believe in Allah’s revelations" refers to the same people who called the Qur’an a fabrication and claimed it was taught by a human. They have been presented with overwhelming evidence: the perfect creation, the clear signs, the linguistic miracle, the historical lessons. Yet they still refuse to believe. The verse states, "will never be guided by Allah." This is a declaration of the divine law: when a person consistently and arrogantly rejects the truth after it has been made clear to them, Allah seals their heart, and they are deprived of the gift of guidance. It is not that Allah is unwilling to guide, but that their own persistent rejection makes them unworthy and unreceptive to guidance. The door of guidance is open to all who sincerely seek, but those who slam it shut with hostility and pride are left to their chosen path. The verse then adds, "and they will suffer a painful punishment." The consequence of their chosen misguidance is the torment of the Hereafter, described as "painful" to emphasize its severity. This verse serves as both a warning and a statement of justice. It warns those who toy with the truth, thinking they can always accept later, that a time may come when the heart becomes too hard to accept guidance at all. It also provides a perspective on the spiritual state of hardened rejectors, helping the believer understand why some people, despite seeing the same signs, remain unmoved. For the believer, this verse instills a profound sense of reliance on Allah for steadfastness. One should never feel secure in their own faith, knowing that guidance is a continuous divine gift that can be withdrawn if one begins to reject Allah’s revelations. It motivates constant repentance and humility, and a deep gratitude for the faith that one has been given, recognizing it as a mercy, not a personal achievement.
105 | No one fabricates lies except those who do not believe in Allah’s revelations, and it is they who are the ˹true˺ liars. | This verse flips the disbelievers’ accusation against the Prophet back onto them. They called the Prophet a fabricator, but the verse declares, "No one fabricates lies except those who do not believe in Allah’s revelations." The act of fabricating lies against Allah—attributing false statements or revelations to Him—is the hallmark of the disbeliever, not the prophet. The Prophet Muhammad, who was known for a lifetime of truthfulness, was accused of something that only those who reject Allah’s signs would do. The disbelievers themselves were the fabricators of falsehood, inventing lies about Allah by claiming He had partners, daughters, or that certain foods were divinely forbidden without command. Their entire system of shirk was built on falsehood. The verse then delivers a powerful moral verdict: "and it is they who are the true liars." The emphasis on "true" here indicates that while the Prophet was accused of lying, the reality is the opposite; the disbelievers are the ones who genuinely deserve the title of liars, both in their denial of the truth and in their invention of false beliefs. This verse is a profound lesson in the moral psychology of denial. Often, those who are most guilty of a sin are the first to accuse others of it. The Quraysh knew deep down that the Prophet was truthful; their accusation was a projection of their own inner state of dishonesty. For the believer, this verse offers a comfort: when people of falsehood call the truthful liars, it is a testimony against themselves. It also serves as a warning against the grievous sin of fabricating religious falsehood, whether by directly inventing lies about Allah, or by spreading misinterpretations and innovations that have no basis in divine revelation. The true liars are those whose hearts are empty of faith, and their falsehood will be exposed in both this world and the next. The verse reinforces the integrity of the prophetic message and places the burden of proof squarely on the accusers, who are revealed to be the actual fabricators.
106 | Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief—not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith, but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly—they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment. | This verse addresses the grave issue of apostasy, but with a crucial exception that reveals the depth of Islamic mercy. "Whoever disbelieves in Allah after their belief" refers to those who abandon faith after having accepted it. This was a reality in Mecca, where some weak believers were tortured until they verbally renounced Islam, and others, like the hypocrites, willingly turned back to disbelief. The verse immediately clarifies an all-important exception: "not those who are forced while their hearts are firm in faith." This refers to individuals like ʿAmmār ibn Yāsir, who was tortured by the Quraysh until he uttered words of disbelief, but his heart remained steadfast in faith. Allah, the All-Knowing, distinguishes between the outward utterance under duress and the inner conviction. Such people are not guilty of apostasy; their faith is intact, and they are forgiven. This is a foundational principle in Islamic law: compulsion negates culpability in matters of speech. The verse then describes the other category: "but those who embrace disbelief wholeheartedly." These are the ones who willingly, without any external force, open their hearts to disbelief. They have made a conscious, internal decision to reject faith. For them, the verdict is severe: "they will be condemned by Allah and suffer a tremendous punishment." The condemnation is divine anger, and the punishment is the great torment of Hell. This verse balances justice and mercy perfectly. It protects the coerced believer from guilt and offers them a way to preserve their life while maintaining internal faith, a concept known as taqiyyah in its proper, limited sense. At the same time, it warns those who treat faith lightly, showing that willing apostasy is a matter of grave consequence. For the believer, this verse inspires deep gratitude for Allah's mercy regarding human weakness under torture, and it also strengthens the resolve to protect one's faith internally even in the worst circumstances. It teaches that the heart's firmness is the true locus of faith, and that Allah's judgement is based on inner sincerity, not merely external words.
107 | This is because they prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter. Surely Allah never guides those who ˹choose to˺ disbelieve. | This verse explains the underlying motivation of those who willingly apostate, as described in the previous verse. "This is because they prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter." Their choice to abandon faith is not based on intellectual conviction but on a value judgment: they find the immediate pleasures, security, and status of this worldly life more attractive than the promise of the Hereafter. In Mecca, some apostates were lured by the prospect of regaining their social standing, avoiding persecution, or enjoying material benefits that Islam might restrict. The preference for dunyā over ākhirah is the root of many spiritual failures. The verse then states the divine law: "Surely Allah never guides those who choose to disbelieve." The phrase "choose to" is crucial; it indicates a deliberate, conscious preference for disbelief. Because they have made this fundamental choice against the truth, Allah removes His guiding grace from them. This is not an arbitrary deprivation but a just response to their own free decision. The verse highlights the principle that guidance is a light that Allah bestows, but it is withdrawn when a person repeatedly rejects it in favor of worldly gains. This verse serves as a powerful diagnostic tool for the believer. It prompts reflection on one’s own choices: when we sin, is it because we are preferring a fleeting worldly pleasure over the eternal reward with Allah? The cure for this disease is to strengthen yaqīn, certainty in the Hereafter, so that its reality outweighs the pull of the present world. It also serves as a warning to those who think they can return to disbelief for a worldly advantage and then easily come back to faith; the act of preferring this world can so darken the heart that the door to guidance closes. The verse emphasizes the immense value of the Hereafter, portraying the apostate's trade as the most foolish of all bargains: selling the infinite for the finite.
108 | They are the ones whose hearts, ears, and eyes are sealed by Allah, and it is they who are ˹truly˺ heedless. | This verse describes the spiritual state that results from the willing preference of this world over the Hereafter. "They are the ones whose hearts, ears, and eyes are sealed by Allah." The sealing of the heart means it becomes impervious to understanding spiritual truth and feeling the love of Allah. The sealing of the ears prevents them from hearing the message in a way that benefits them; they hear the words but not the meaning. The sealing of the eyes means they see the signs in creation and in revelation, but they fail to perceive them as evidence of the Creator. This triple sealing represents a complete spiritual shutdown, a self-inflicted sensory and cognitive blindness that Allah, in His justice, makes permanent. It is the consequence of their persistent rejection and preference for disbelief. The verse then identifies them: "and it is they who are truly heedless." Heedlessness is the state of being unaware, distracted, and unconcerned with the ultimate reality. While many people may be temporarily heedless and then awaken, these individuals are described as "truly" heedless because their heedlessness is chronic and fatal, sealed by divine decree. This verse is a terrifying depiction of the end-stage of rebellion against God. It teaches that the faculties of perception are trusts from Allah, and their misuse leads to their loss. The believer should pray for a heart that is soft, ears that are open to truth, and eyes that see the signs of Allah. It also provides an explanation for the baffling inability of some people to respond to the clearest arguments; their internal receptors have been sealed by their own prior choices. The verse serves as a warning to guard one's heart against the love of this world and to constantly seek Allah's guidance to remain among those whose senses are alive to the truth.
109 | Without a doubt, they will be the losers in the Hereafter. | This short, emphatic verse delivers the final verdict on the sealed, heedless ones described previously. "Without a doubt" translates the Arabic lā jarama, an expression of absolute certainty, leaving no room for speculation or hope of a different outcome. "They will be the losers in the Hereafter." The concept of loss, khusrān, in the Qur’an is the ultimate tragedy. It is not a financial loss that can be recovered but an eternal bankruptcy where one loses the opportunity for Paradise, the pleasure of Allah, and any possible good. Their entire life, with all its opportunities for faith and good deeds, will have been wasted. They traded eternal bliss for fleeting worldly gains and are left with nothing but punishment. The verse echoes the Qur’anic theme that true loss is the loss of one’s self and one’s potential for eternal happiness. This verse serves as a punchline to the preceding warnings, summarizing the end result of preferring this world, having one’s faculties sealed, and choosing disbelief. It instills a profound fear and urgency in the believer. In worldly terms, a person who loses wealth or health is considered unfortunate, but the Qur’an redefines loss as the state of a person who meets Allah with a heart sealed in disbelief. The verse encourages a constant evaluation of one’s spiritual ledger. Are we, in our daily choices, moving towards eternal profit or eternal loss? The emphatic "without a doubt" is a wake-up call, cutting through any complacency or false hope. It reassures the believers that their temporary worldly losses in the cause of Allah are not true losses; the real losers are those who seem to win in this world but lose everything in the next. This verse is a powerful tool for self-reflection and a motivator to ensure that one’s ultimate account is one of profit, not loss.
110 | As for those who emigrated after being compelled ˹to renounce Islam˺, then struggled ˹in Allah’s cause˺, and persevered, your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful after all. | This verse returns to the theme of those who were coerced into renouncing Islam, providing a beautiful promise of redemption. It refers to the group of persecuted Muslims in Mecca who could not bear the torture and, under severe duress, uttered words of disbelief, and perhaps even remained among the disbelievers for a time. Afterward, they managed to emigrate, leaving Mecca for Medina or Abyssinia, and then "struggled in Allah’s cause," meaning they engaged in jihad both spiritual and physical to uphold the faith, and "persevered," meaning they remained steadfast in their subsequent faith and efforts. For such people, the verse delivers a powerful reassurance: "your Lord O Prophet is truly All-Forgiving, Most Merciful after all." The phrase "after all" translates min baʿdi mā, emphasizing that even after the immense trial of being forced to renounce faith, followed by a period of weakness or even compliance, Allah’s forgiveness and mercy are vast and encompassing. Their emigration and subsequent struggle and patience are signs of the genuine faith that was in their hearts all along, and Allah, the All-Forgiving, wipes away the previous compulsion. This verse demonstrates that no matter how severe the fall or how desperate the situation, the door of repentance and divine mercy remains open. It is a message of hope for all who have faltered under pressure but whose hearts are still attached to Allah. The combination of emigration, struggle, and perseverance shows that true repentance is not just a word but a transformative action. For the believer, this verse is a source of immense comfort, teaching that the past does not define the future if one turns back to Allah with sincere effort. It also highlights the merciful character of Allah, who looks at the heart’s true state and the subsequent efforts to rectify one’s path, forgiving even the gravest of outward lapses when they were not a true reflection of the inner self.
111 | ˹Consider˺ the Day ˹when˺ every soul will come pleading for itself, and each will be paid in full for what it did, and none will be wronged. | The sūrah now redirects attention to the Day of Judgment, providing a snapshot of its reality. "Consider the Day when every soul will come pleading for itself." The word "pleading" translates tujādilu, indicating that each soul will be intensely arguing on its own behalf, desperately trying to defend itself. In this world, people have advocates, lawyers, and intercessors to speak for them, but on that Day, the soul stands utterly alone, its attention consumed entirely by its own case. The relationships and alliances of the world are severed, and the only concern is personal salvation. The verse continues, "and each will be paid in full for what it did." This means that every deed, good or bad, large or small, will be precisely recompensed. Nothing will be lost, forgotten, or undervalued. The scales of divine justice will be exact. Then the verse adds, "and none will be wronged." Even the smallest particle of injustice will be absent. No one will be punished for a sin they did not commit, nor will any good deed be unrewarded. This comprehensive statement of justice provides the ultimate context for all the moral commands and warnings in the sūrah. It tells both the oppressor and the oppressed that their final meeting with justice is inevitable. The image of each soul pleading for itself is particularly striking, emphasizing the utter personal responsibility of every individual. No parent, child, or friend can help; no wealth can buy a defense. This verse encourages the believer to live with a strong sense of individual accountability, preparing the case one will plead before Allah by filling the record with righteous deeds. It also offers comfort to those who have been wronged, assuring them that a Day is coming when they will receive full justice and the scales will be perfectly balanced by the Most Just.
112 | And Allah sets forth the example of a society which was safe and at ease, receiving its provision in abundance from all directions. But its people met Allah’s favours with ingratitude, so Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear for their misdeeds. | This verse presents a parable, the example of a community, often understood to be a reference to Mecca, though the principle is universal. "And Allah sets forth the example of a society which was safe and at ease, receiving its provision in abundance from all directions." This describes a town enjoying perfect security; no enemy threatens it, its inhabitants live in comfort and peace, and their sustenance flows in generously from every source. This was precisely the state of Mecca before the Prophet's mission; it was a sanctuary where even the powerful neighbors respected its sanctity, and its trade caravans brought prosperity from Yemen and Syria. The verse then describes the tragic fall: "But its people met Allah’s favours with ingratitude, so Allah made them taste the clutches of hunger and fear for their misdeeds." Instead of being grateful, they worshipped idols, oppressed the weak, mocked the messenger, and denied the one God who provided all their blessings. Their ingratitude was comprehensive, a practical denial of the Giver. The punishment was tailored to their crime: the security they took for granted was replaced with fear, and their abundant provision was replaced with the clutches of hunger. Commentators mention the severe famine that struck Mecca years after the Prophet’s migration, as well as the constant fear they lived in under the threat of the growing Muslim state, until the conquest of Mecca. This parable illustrates the sunnah of Allah: nations that respond to blessings with ingratitude and rebellion are eventually stripped of those blessings and made to suffer the opposite. For the believer, this serves as a stark warning to never take peace and provision for granted. The blessings are a test, and gratitude is the means to preserve them. The verse also provides a lens to understand history and current events, recognizing that societal collapse often follows widespread ingratitude and moral corruption.
113 | A messenger of their own actually did come to them, but they denied him. So the torment overtook them while they persisted in wrongdoing. | This verse clarifies the specific form that the ingratitude of the society took: the rejection of the messenger sent to them. "A messenger of their own actually did come to them." This emphasizes the mercy and the justification of the subsequent punishment. The messenger was not a stranger; he was one of them, from their own lineage, speaking their language, known for his honesty before his mission. He came with a clear message, calling them to gratitude and monotheism. "But they denied him," meaning they rejected his claim, called him a liar, and opposed his mission. Their denial was not out of confusion but out of arrogance and attachment to their forefathers’ ways. The verse then delivers the consequence: "So the torment overtook them while they persisted in wrongdoing." The punishment, which in Mecca’s case included the defeat at Badr, the prolonged fear, and the eventual conquest, caught them in the midst of their continuous wrongdoing. The phrase "while they persisted in wrongdoing" indicates that they were given time, the message was repeated, and they had every opportunity to repent, but they stubbornly continued. The punishment did not come out of the blue; it was the climax of a prolonged period of rejection. This verse reinforces the Qur'anic principle that divine punishment is never sent until a messenger has clearly delivered the warning. It also serves as a warning to all who reject the messengers sent to them: the historical pattern is that the deniers are eventually overtaken. For the believer, the verse highlights the value of accepting the messenger and his message while there is time, and it explains the afflictions of the world as potential consequences of collective denial. It calls individuals and communities to examine their response to the message of Islam, lest they fall into the same pattern as the destroyed towns.
114 | So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you, and be grateful for Allah’s favours, if you ˹truly˺ worship Him ˹alone˺. | After the parable of the ungrateful society, the sūrah turns to the believers with a direct command. "So eat from the good, lawful things which Allah has provided for you." The conjunction "so" links the command to the preceding warning: in contrast to the ungrateful who misused Allah's provisions, the believers are instructed to enjoy the good and lawful (ḥalāl ṭayyib) sustenance. The word "good" includes both the quality of being wholesome and the purity of being lawful. The believers are not to abstain from the good things of life; rather, they are to partake of them with a clear conscience, within the boundaries set by Allah. The second command is "and be grateful for Allah’s favours." The act of eating is to be coupled with shukr, a deep, active gratitude that acknowledges the Provider and uses the strength derived from the food in obedience to Him. Gratitude is the safeguard against the fate of the ungrateful society. The verse ends with the conditional phrase, "if you truly worship Him alone." This connects the act of eating and thanking directly to tawḥīd. True worship of Allah alone includes obeying His commands regarding food, recognizing Him as the sole Provider, and directing all thanks exclusively to Him. Eating from the lawful and being grateful are thus presented as essential components of monotheistic worship. This verse provides a holistic Islamic view of consumption. Eating is not a mundane, purely physical act; it is a spiritual practice when done with the right intention and awareness. In the modern context, where food is often consumed mindlessly, this verse calls believers to elevate their daily meals into acts of worship by ensuring the food is lawful, remembering Allah’s name over it, and cultivating a heart of gratitude. It also counters the extremist tendency to view all physical pleasures with suspicion; the Qur’an encourages enjoying the good things of life in a manner that increases one's connection to Allah. The verse thus defines the middle path of the grateful worshipper.
115 | He has only forbidden you ˹to eat˺ carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah. But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | This verse specifies the prohibited foods, giving a concise list of major dietary restrictions in Islam. "He has only forbidden you to eat carrion, blood, swine, and what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah." The word "only" indicates that the list of absolutely forbidden foods is short. Carrion is the flesh of an animal that dies naturally or is not properly slaughtered. Blood refers to flowing blood, which is forbidden to consume. Swine is the pig, considered impure. The fourth category, "what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah," includes any animal dedicated to idols or upon which a name other than Allah’s is invoked at the time of slaughter. This prohibition is directly connected to tawḥīd, ensuring that even the act of taking a life for food is consecrated solely to the Creator. The verse then provides the exception of necessity: "But if someone is compelled by necessity—neither driven by desire nor exceeding immediate need—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful." If a person is in a life-threatening situation where no lawful food is available, they may eat just enough of the forbidden food to survive, without taking pleasure in it or transgressing the minimal amount needed. This exception reveals the flexibility and mercy within Islamic law; the preservation of life takes precedence over the dietary prohibition. The verse closes with the divine names All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, indicating that Allah overlooks such unavoidable consumption out of His compassion. This verse establishes the foundational dietary laws and demonstrates a principle of Islamic jurisprudence: necessity makes the prohibited permissible, to a limited extent. For the believer, it provides clear guidance on what to avoid, and it reinforces the importance of intentionality in eating. The prohibition against what is slaughtered in other than Allah’s name ties dietary law directly to the core of faith, making every meal an affirmation of monotheism. The mercy shown in the exception encourages believers to think well of Allah and to know that His law is designed for ease, not hardship.
116 | Do not falsely declare with your tongues, “This is lawful, and that is unlawful,” ˹only˺ fabricating lies against Allah. Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed. | After specifying the lawful and prohibited foods, this verse warns against the sin of inventing religious rulings. "Do not falsely declare with your tongues, 'This is lawful, and that is unlawful,' only fabricating lies against Allah." The pre-Islamic Arabs had a habit of arbitrarily declaring certain foods, animals, or practices as forbidden based on their own whims or tribal traditions, and then attributing these prohibitions to Allah. This was a grievous sin because it involved speaking on behalf of God without any authority, effectively legislating in His name. The verse forbids the believers from falling into this trap. The authority to declare things lawful or unlawful belongs exclusively to Allah and His Messenger. Human beings, no matter how pious or learned, cannot invent religious rulings based on personal opinion. The verse then states the outcome: "Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed." Their fabricated rules may give them social power or a sense of piety, but in the ultimate sense, they will not achieve any real success; instead, they are destined for failure and punishment. This verse is foundational in Islamic legal theory: the default in matters of worldly benefit is permissibility unless proven otherwise by revelation, and the prohibition of something requires clear evidence. It also condemns the attitude of making religion overly restrictive or overly permissive based on human desires. For the contemporary Muslim, this verse is a call to strict adherence to the sources of Shariah—the Qur'an and Sunnah—and a warning against cultural innovations that are falsely given religious sanctity. It teaches the importance of saying "I do not know" and referring matters to the scholars, rather than pronouncing fatwas based on ignorance or bias. The fabrication of lies against Allah is one of the greatest sins, and this verse instills a healthy fear of ever misrepresenting the divine will.
117 | ˹It is only˺ a brief enjoyment, then they will suffer a painful punishment. | This short verse comments on the temporary worldly benefit that the fabricators of lies against Allah might enjoy. "It is only a brief enjoyment" refers to whatever worldly gains they acquire through their false religious pronouncements—perhaps social status, followers, or economic advantage from rituals and prohibitions they invented. The word "brief" underscores the fleeting nature of such gains; measured against eternity, even a long worldly life is just a moment. The verse then delivers the stark contrast: "then they will suffer a painful punishment." The enjoyment is fleeting, but the punishment is severe and lasting. This juxtaposition highlights the foolishness of trading eternal well-being for a momentary pleasure. The fabricators may think they have succeeded because they see the immediate results of their lies, but the verse exposes the reality that the enjoyment is a deceptive prelude to torment. This principle applies universally: any sin that brings short-term pleasure but leads to long-term suffering is a terrible bargain. For the believer, this verse is a powerful deterrent against compromising religious integrity for worldly gain. Whether it is a preacher altering the message to please an audience, or an individual justifying a sin with a self-made ruling, the brief enjoyment is not worth the painful punishment. The verse also comforts those who adhere to the truth despite lacking worldly benefits, reminding them that the fabricators' apparent prosperity is nothing but a brief illusion, soon to be replaced by unimaginable pain. It sharpens the believer’s perspective, training the heart to value the eternal over the temporal.
118 | To the Jews, We have forbidden what We related to you before. We did not wrong them, but it was they who wronged themselves. | This verse draws a parallel between the dietary prohibitions given to the Jews and the preceding discussion. "To the Jews, We have forbidden what We related to you before." This refers to a previous revelation, specifically in Sūrat Al-Anʿām (6:146), where Allah mentioned that due to the transgressions of the Jews, certain foods that were originally lawful were made unlawful for them as a punishment, such as animals with undivided hoofs and the fat of cattle and sheep. The verse clarifies that the strict dietary laws imposed on the Jews were not part of the original, universal law but were specific restrictions resulting from their rebellion. This context refutes the claim by some that the Islamic dietary laws were an innovation; rather, the laws given to the Prophet Muhammad were a return to the pure, original way, lifting the burdens that had been imposed as punishment on previous nations. The verse then adds, "We did not wrong them, but it was they who wronged themselves." The restrictions were a consequence of their own wrongdoing, not an arbitrary act of divine oppression. This reinforces the Qur'anic principle that divine punishment, in whatever form, is always a result of human sin and injustice. For the Muslims, this verse serves as a reminder of the mercy in the Islamic Shariah, which has lifted undue burdens and provided a balanced, moderate law. It also warns against repeating the mistakes of previous nations, whose transgressions led to the tightening of restrictions. Gratitude for the ease of Islamic law should be expressed through obedience, not rebellion. The verse also highlights that Allah's justice is perfect; He does not wrong anyone, and whatever hardship occurs is a reflection of human actions.
119 | As for those who commit evil ignorantly ˹or recklessly˺, then repent afterwards and mend their ways, then your Lord is surely All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | This verse opens a door of hope after the preceding discussions of divine punishment and the fate of fabricators. "As for those who commit evil ignorantly or recklessly" refers to people who sin due to ignorance of the law, or out of foolishness, impulse, and weakness, without the deep-seated arrogance and persistent rejection that characterize the hardened disbelievers. The verse does not excuse the sin—it is still evil—but it acknowledges a state of human frailty. The crucial condition is what follows: "then repent afterwards and mend their ways." Repentance (tawbah) involves sincere regret, immediate cessation of the sin, and firm resolve not to return to it. "Mend their ways" (aṣlaḥū) means they actively rectify the damage caused by their sin, restoring rights where possible and increasing in good deeds to compensate for their past. The verse then gives the divine promise: "then your Lord is surely All-Forgiving, Most Merciful." The combination of the two names, Al-Ghafūr (the Oft-Forgiving) and Ar-Raḥīm (the Most Merciful), indicates that forgiveness covers the sin completely, and mercy encompasses the repentant sinner with subsequent blessings. This verse is a beacon of hope for every sinner. No matter how great the evil, if it was committed out of ignorance or human weakness rather than outright defiance, and it is followed by genuine repentance and amendment, Allah's forgiveness and mercy are assured. It negates despair and encourages immediate turning back to Allah. The verse also establishes the complete formula for repentance: not merely a verbal apology, but a change of state and corrective action. For the modern believer struggling with recurring sins, this verse provides the motivation to keep repenting and reforming, trusting in the vastness of divine mercy. It shows that the goal is not a sinless life—which is humanly impossible—but a life of continuous turning back to Allah.
120 | Indeed, Abraham was a model of excellence: devoted to Allah, ˹perfectly˺ upright—not a polytheist— | As the sūrah nears its conclusion, it presents the example of Abraham, the patriarch of monotheism, whose legacy the Quraysh claimed to follow. "Indeed, Abraham was a model of excellence" translates the word ummah, which here means an exemplary leader embodying all goodness, a complete community of virtue in one person. Abraham is presented as the ideal for believers to emulate. The verse then describes his qualities: "devoted to Allah" (qānitan) means he was constantly obedient, humble, and devoted in his worship, never wavering. "Perfectly upright" (ḥanīfan) refers to his pure monotheism, his turning away from all falsehood and inclining solely towards the truth. He was not merely a believer but a paradigm of the original, uncorrupted religion of tawḥīd, the spiritual ancestor of all true faith. The verse concludes with the critical affirmation: "not a polytheist." This negates any claim that Abraham was ever an idolater, which is important because the Quraysh, while venerating Abraham, practiced shirk and claimed his legacy. The verse reclaims Abraham for pure monotheism, asserting that his way was the complete opposite of the polytheism practiced at the Kaʿbah. This depiction of Abraham serves as a bridge between the earlier prophetic missions and the message of Muhammad, establishing that Islam is not a new religion but the restoration of Abraham's original faith. For believers, Abraham is a role model of individual excellence, unwavering devotion, and the courage to stand for truth against a polytheistic society. The verse calls every Muslim to aspire to be an ummah of goodness in their own right, embodying devotion and uprightness, and to ensure their faith remains untainted by any form of shirk. It sets the stage for the subsequent verses that will elaborate on Abraham's qualities and his relationship with Allah.
121 | ˹utterly˺ grateful for Allah’s favours. ˹So˺ He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path. | This verse continues the description of Abraham begun in the previous verse, adding a quality that was the hallmark of his character and the direct cause of his elevated status. The phrase “utterly grateful for Allah’s favours” translates shākiran li-anʿumihi, indicating that Abraham was not merely occasionally thankful but lived in a constant state of profound gratitude. The Arabic intensive form suggests that gratitude was woven into the very fabric of his being. Every blessing he received, from his creation to his prophethood to his deliverance from the fire, was met with immediate and complete acknowledgment of the Giver. This gratitude was not passive; it manifested in his actions, his worship, and his unwavering commitment to spread the message of tawḥīd. He recognized that every good he possessed—his family, his wealth, his knowledge—was a favour from Allah, and he used each one in obedient service. The verse then states the divine response to this gratitude: “So He chose him and guided him to the Straight Path.” The word “chose” (ijtabāhu) implies a special divine selection, a pulling of the servant close, an election to intimate friendship. Abraham is known as Khalīl Allāh, the intimate friend of Allah, a title that reflects this divine choosing. The guidance to the Straight Path is both the initial guidance to monotheism in the midst of an idolatrous society, and the continuous, deepening guidance throughout his life that kept him firm and elevated his station. This causal link between gratitude and divine selection is profoundly instructive. It teaches that gratitude is not merely a virtue but a magnet for divine favour and increased guidance. The more a person recognizes and appreciates Allah’s favours, the more Allah draws them near and shows them the way. Abraham’s life exemplifies this dynamic: his rejection of idols, his debate with Nimrod, his willingness to sacrifice his son, his building of the Kaʿbah—all were expressions of his gratitude that led to his chosen status. For the believer, this verse provides a practical model for spiritual growth. The cultivation of deep, active gratitude for every blessing, both material and spiritual, is the path to being chosen by Allah and remaining firmly on the Straight Path.
122 | We blessed him with all goodness in this world, and in the Hereafter he will certainly be among the righteous. | This verse details the comprehensive reward Abraham received as a result of his devotion and gratitude. “We blessed him with all goodness in this world” translates ātaynāhu fī al-dunyā ḥasanah. The word “goodness” (ḥasanah) is wonderfully comprehensive. In Abraham’s case, this included a long life of faith and obedience, a righteous wife, illustrious children including Isaac and Ishmael who were prophets, immense wealth and hospitality, and a lasting legacy of praise and honour among all nations. He was blessed with the friendship of Allah, the station of prophethood, and the distinction of being the father of many prophets. His name is revered by Jews, Christians, and Muslims alike. The goodness also includes the physical blessing of the land, the rebuilding of the Kaʿbah, and the establishment of the pilgrimage that continues to this day. His supplication for a good name and a righteous progeny was answered in full. This worldly goodness was not without trials; Abraham faced exile, the fire of Nimrod, and the command to sacrifice his son. But the trials themselves were part of the goodness because they elevated his rank and were succeeded by immense blessings. The verse then states, “and in the Hereafter he will certainly be among the righteous.” The word “righteous” (ṣāliḥīn) indicates that in the next life, Abraham’s station will be among the highest ranks of the pious, in the company of the prophets and the truthful. The certainty of this is emphasized by the “certainly.” This dual reward—goodness in this world and righteousness in the Hereafter—is the pattern for all who follow Abraham’s example. The verse shows that true faith does not demand a rejection of all worldly good; rather, it invites a holistic blessing that encompasses both realms, provided the heart remains attached to the Hereafter as the ultimate goal. For the believer, this verse is a source of hope and aspiration. It teaches that following the Abrahamic path leads to a good life in this world, full of meaning, honour, and provision, and a secure place among the righteous in the eternal life. The comprehensive goodness promised to Abraham is a promise, in measure, to all who walk his path of devotion, gratitude, and uprightness.
123 | Then We revealed to you ˹O Prophet, saying˺: “Follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists.” | After presenting Abraham as the model of excellence, the sūrah now directly addresses the Prophet Muḥammad with a command that defines the foundation of his mission. “Then We revealed to you O Prophet, saying: ‘Follow the faith of Abraham, the upright, who was not one of the polytheists.’” This revelation establishes the essential continuity between the Abrahamic tradition and the final message of Islam. The Prophet Muhammad is not bringing a new or unfamiliar religion; he is commanded to follow the millah, the way and religion, of Abraham. The word millah refers to the foundational principles of faith and practice that Abraham embodied: pure monotheism, submission to Allah, rejection of idols, and a life of righteousness. The command to follow Abraham’s faith is particularly significant because the Quraysh claimed descent from Abraham through Ishmael and prided themselves on being his heirs. Yet they had corrupted his pure monotheism with idol worship. By commanding the Prophet to follow Abraham’s faith, the Qur’an reclaims Abraham for true Islam and exposes the Quraysh’s deviation. Abraham is described again as “the upright” (ḥanīf), the one who turned away from all falsehood and inclined solely to the truth. The explicit clarification that he was “not one of the polytheists” severs any link between Abraham and the pagan practices of Mecca. Abraham’s faith was the antithesis of shirk; he spent his life fighting against idolatry. For the Prophet and his followers, this command meant that their religion was not a break from the past but a restoration of the original, uncorrupted faith of their forefather Abraham. It gave them a powerful argument against the Quraysh and a deep sense of connection to a noble spiritual lineage. For Muslims today, this verse emphasizes that their faith is rooted in the Abrahamic heritage. The call to follow Abraham’s way is a call to uncompromising tawḥīd, to standing firm against the idolatry of one’s time—whether in the form of literal idols, materialism, or ideologies—and to embodying the uprightness that characterized the father of prophets. It is a reminder that Islam is the path of Abraham, the original and universal way of submission to the One God.
124 | ˹Honouring˺ the Sabbath was ordained only for those who disputed about Abraham. And surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their disputes. | This verse addresses a specific point of contention between the different Abrahamic communities, clarifying the status of the Sabbath. The phrase “Honouring the Sabbath was ordained only for those who disputed about Abraham” indicates that the strict observance of the Sabbath as a day of compulsory rest and ritual was a commandment given specifically to the Jews, not as an eternal, universal law, but as part of the legislation that followed the disputes and transgressions among the Children of Israel regarding Abraham’s legacy. The Jews and Christians argued about who truly followed Abraham; the Jews claimed him as exclusively theirs, while the Christians also claimed him. Their disputes led to divisions, and as part of the divine legislation for the Jewish people, the Sabbath was exalted as a sign. However, this command was not part of Abraham’s original faith, which was pure monotheism without the heavy burdens of later legislation. The verse therefore implies that the Prophet Muhammad and his followers are not bound by the Sabbath ordinance; their connection to Abraham is through the essence of his faith, not through the specific laws that were later imposed on other communities due to their particular circumstances and disputes. The verse then adds, “And surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their disputes.” All the disagreements about Abraham, about who correctly followed him, and about the laws, will be finally resolved by Allah on the Day of Judgment. He will make clear who was right and who was wrong. This statement serves to caution against excessive engagement in such theological disputes; the ultimate Judge is Allah, and the focus should be on following the clear revelation rather than getting lost in historical arguments. For Muslims, this verse is a reminder that the essence of faith is not in rituals that were prescribed for specific times and peoples, but in the core tawḥīd and submission that Abraham exemplified. It also teaches patience in the face of religious disputes, knowing that the final judgment rests with Allah. The emphasis on divine judgment encourages humility; no group should arrogantly claim exclusive possession of truth, as the final verdict belongs to the Lord of all.
125 | Invite ˹all˺ to the Way of your Lord with wisdom and kind advice, and only debate with them in the best manner. Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is ˹rightly˺ guided. | This verse provides a comprehensive methodology for dawah, calling others to Islam. It begins with the command: “Invite all to the Way of your Lord.” The address is primarily to the Prophet, but it extends to all believers who carry the message. The “Way of your Lord” is the path of Islam, the straight path that leads to Allah. The invitation must be carried out with three distinct approaches, depending on the audience and situation. The first is “with wisdom” (bil-ḥikmah). Wisdom involves sound knowledge, good judgement, choosing the right time and place, speaking to people according to their level of understanding, and prioritizing the most important matters. It also means using clear proofs and rational arguments that appeal to the intellect. The second is “kind advice” (al-mawʿiẓah al-ḥasanah). This involves gentle, heartfelt exhortation that appeals to the emotions and the innate human desire for goodness and truth. It includes reminding people of Allah’s favours, warning them of the consequences of disobedience with compassion, and encouraging them with stories of the righteous. The advice must be “kind,” avoiding harshness, condemnation, and public shaming. The third is “and only debate with them in the best manner.” When debate and intellectual discussion are necessary with those who oppose or question the faith, it must be conducted with the highest standards of etiquette: respect for the opponent, listening carefully, avoiding insults and personal attacks, using logical arguments, and being prepared to agree to disagree. The goal is not to win an argument but to convey the truth with dignity. The verse then provides the ultimate reassurance: “Surely your Lord alone knows best who has strayed from His Way and who is rightly guided.” The results of dawah are not the caller’s responsibility. After employing wisdom, kind advice, and best debate, the caller leaves the outcome to Allah, who alone knows the true state of hearts. This verse remains a timeless guide for Muslim outreach, education, and interfaith dialogue, emphasizing the combination of intellectual rigour, emotional warmth, and ethical conduct, all underpinned by trust in Allah’s knowledge and judgment.
126 | If you retaliate, then let it be equivalent to what you have suffered. But if you patiently endure, it is certainly best for those who are patient. | This verse provides a balanced ethical teaching on dealing with harm and injustice, transitioning from the methodology of dawah to the personal conduct expected when facing opposition. “If you retaliate, then let it be equivalent to what you have suffered” establishes the principle of just retribution. If a believer is wronged and chooses to respond, the response must be proportional, never exceeding the original harm. There is no room for excessive vengeance, escalation, or transgression. The measure of retaliation is exact equivalence, a principle that ensures justice and prevents cycles of increasing violence. This aligns with the broader Qur’anic concept of qisas, legal retribution, where the punishment must fit the crime. However, the verse immediately presents a superior moral option: “But if you patiently endure, it is certainly best for those who are patient.” Patience (ṣabr) here means forbearance, controlling the urge to retaliate even when one has the right to do so, and leaving the matter to Allah. The phrase “it is certainly best” emphasizes that this higher path leads to greater spiritual reward, inner peace, and often better practical outcomes. Patience is not weakness; it is strength of character and trust in divine justice. This verse was particularly significant in the Meccan context, where the believers were subjected to severe persecution and had limited means to retaliate. It guided them to channel their energy into patience and perseverance rather than rash actions that could lead to greater harm. For Muslims in every age, this verse presents a dual framework: the right to proportionate justice is affirmed, protecting against oppression, but the preference for patience and forgiveness is strongly encouraged as the path of true excellence. This teaching mirrors the divine attributes, as Allah is both the Just and the Forbearing. The verse calls the believer to rise above the natural impulse for vengeance and to aspire to the station of those who are patient, for whom the best reward is promised.
127 | Be patient ˹O Prophet˺, for your patience is only with Allah’s help. Do not grieve over those ˹who disbelieve˺, nor be distressed by their schemes. | This verse directly addresses the Prophet Muḥammad with a series of comforting commands that also serve as lessons for all believers. “Be patient O Prophet” is a singular command, placing the burden of patience squarely on the leader. The Prophet faced immense pressure: mockery, rejection, persecution of his followers, and elaborate plots against his life. The command to patience is both a recognition of the difficulty and a divine empowerment. The verse then clarifies the source of this patience: “for your patience is only with Allah’s help.” This is a crucial spiritual principle. Patience is not a purely human virtue generated by willpower; it is a gift from Allah bestowed upon those who turn to Him for assistance. The Prophet, with all his spiritual strength, needed Allah’s help to remain patient. This humility before the divine source of all virtues is a lesson for every believer who struggles to endure hardship. The verse then addresses the emotional toll of rejection: “Do not grieve over those who disbelieve.” The Prophet’s deep concern for his people’s guidance caused him grief, as mentioned elsewhere. This command does not negate his compassion but places a boundary on it. He is not to let their rejection consume him with sorrow, because his duty is to convey, and their choice is between them and Allah. The final part, “nor be distressed by their schemes,” addresses the anxiety caused by the constant plotting of the Quraysh. Their conspiracies were real and dangerous, but the Prophet is told not to let his heart be constricted by worry. Allah, who is aware of all their schemes, is the ultimate protector. This verse is a profound source of comfort for all who call to truth and face hostility. It teaches that patience is sustained by divine help, that grief over others’ choices should be tempered by trust in Allah’s wisdom, and that anxiety over the plots of opponents should be dissolved by reliance on Allah’s overarching power. The Prophet’s heart is to remain tranquil, focused on his mission, and anchored in the certainty that Allah is in control.
128 | Surely Allah is with those who shun evil and who do good ˹deeds˺. | The sūrah concludes with a concise, powerful assurance that encapsulates the entire message of the preceding verses. “Surely Allah is with those who shun evil and who do good deeds.” The word “surely” (inna) emphasizes the certainty of this divine companionship. “Those who shun evil” translates alladhīna ittaqaw, the people of taqwā, those who are constantly mindful of Allah and protect themselves from sin and harmful actions. Taqwā is the comprehensive quality of God-consciousness that has been a central theme throughout the sūrah. “Those who do good deeds” translates alladhīna hum muḥsinūn, the people of iḥsān, those who perform righteous actions with excellence, going beyond the minimum and striving for perfection in their worship and conduct. The combination of taqwā (avoiding evil) and iḥsān (doing good) represents the complete spiritual program: the negative and the positive, the fear and the love, the restraint and the action. The promise that Allah is “with” such people is the ultimate reward. Maʿiyyah, divine companionship, means Allah’s special protection, support, guidance, and love. It is the source of strength, peace, and ultimate success. This verse serves as a summary of the entire sūrah, which detailed Allah’s signs and favours, condemned ingratitude and arrogance, established ethical commands, and called to the way of Abraham. The ending leaves the believer with the heartwarming assurance that their efforts are not in vain and that they are not alone. The Lord of all creation, the Almighty, the All-Wise, is with them. This companionship is conditional, however: it is for those who maintain taqwā and iḥsān. The verse is both a promise and a challenge, an invitation to live in such a way that one becomes worthy of this divine nearness. For the Prophet, who was commanded to be patient and not grieve, this final verse is the seal of comfort. For every Muslim who reads the sūrah, it is the ultimate goal: to be among those with whom Allah is, in this world and in the Hereafter. Thus the sūrah ends on a note of profound hope, tying together all the lessons of gratitude, monotheism, and moral conduct into the single, beautiful promise of divine companionship.
