1 | Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies Allah. For He is the Almighty, All-Wise. | The surah opens with the same majestic declaration found at the beginning of Al-Hadid and Al-Hashr's neighboring surahs, linking it thematically to the grand cosmic order. "Whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth glorifies Allah" means that every single entity in existence, from the mightiest angel to the smallest particle, is engaged in a continuous state of proclaiming Allah's absolute perfection, His freedom from any defect, partner, or need. This glorification is not limited to verbal utterance; every motion, every obedience, every reflection of its created purpose is a form of tasbih, whether we perceive it or not. The verse then presents two names that explain why He alone deserves this universal praise: "For He is the Almighty, All-Wise." The Almighty indicates His irresistible power, the power that will soon be demonstrated in the expulsion of the Banu Nadir, a Jewish tribe that broke their treaty and plotted to assassinate the Prophet. The All-Wise indicates that every act of His power, including the humiliation of a once-proud tribe, is placed with perfect wisdom and serves a larger moral and educational purpose. By beginning a surah that deals heavily with military and political events with this verse of universal glorification, the Quran reminds the believers that their struggles, victories, and the spoils they might obtain are not merely tribal or national events; they are part of the cosmic rhythm of the divine will. The battles, the exiles, and the distribution of wealth are all occurrences within the theater of a universe that never ceases to declare its Maker's perfection. This elevates the perspective of the community: they are not just political actors but participants in a divine plan that all of creation is already acknowledging through its very existence.
2 | He is the One Who expelled the disbelievers of the People of the Book from their homes for their first banishment ever. You never thought they would go. And they thought their strongholds would put them out of Allah's reach. But the decree of Allah came upon them from where they never expected. And He cast horror into their hearts so they destroyed their houses with their own hands and the hands of the believers. So take a lesson from this, O people of insight! | This verse directly recounts the historical event that gives the surah its name, the expulsion of the Jewish tribe of Banu al-Nadir from Medina. "He is the One Who expelled the disbelievers of the People of the Book from their homes" immediately attributes the event not to the military might of the Muslims but directly to Allah, emphasizing that He is the true agent behind historical transformations. The Banu al-Nadir had a peace treaty with the Muslims but violated it by conspiring with the Meccan pagans and even attempting to assassinate the Prophet by dropping a stone on him. "For their first banishment ever" means this was the first time they were gathered for exile from the Arabian Peninsula, later followed by their complete expulsion from Khaybar. "You never thought they would go" reflects the psychological state of the Muslims, who viewed the Banu al-Nadir as a powerful, fortified tribe with seemingly unassailable positions. The verse then exposes the enemy's inner confidence: "And they thought their strongholds would put them out of Allah's reach." They had impressive fortresses and believed that their material defenses could protect them from any divine decree, a timeless delusion of those who rely solely on worldly power. "But the decree of Allah came upon them from where they never expected." The divine plan unfolded not through a direct assault but through a siege that lasted several days, psychological warfare, and the destruction of their morale. "And He cast horror into their hearts" describes the intangible weapon of fear that Allah placed within them, a fear far more effective than swords. This internal collapse was so complete that "they destroyed their houses with their own hands and the hands of the believers." As they prepared to leave, they demolished their own homes to prevent the Muslims from occupying them, tearing down beams and doors, while the believers demolished parts of the outer fortifications. The verse closes with a direct call: "So take a lesson from this, O people of insight!" This calls every person of understanding, across all times, to reflect on the fate of those who trust in material might and break their covenants. It teaches that no fortress, no treaty, no worldly power can shield those whom Allah's decree has targeted for humiliation.
3 | Had Allah not decreed exile for them, He would have certainly punished them in this world. And in the Hereafter they will suffer the punishment of the Fire. | This verse reveals a hidden mercy within the apparent calamity of exile. "Had Allah not decreed exile for them, He would have certainly punished them in this world." The meaning is that the penalty decreed for their treachery in this life was expulsion, and had that not been written as their fate, they would have faced a much more severe worldly punishment such as execution or captivity. Exile, while harsh, was a lighter sentence than what their crimes of attempted assassination and treaty violation could have legally warranted. The verse then transitions to the ultimate reality: "And in the Hereafter they will suffer the punishment of the Fire." The worldly punishment, whether exile or something more severe, does not expiate their disbelief and ultimate rejection of the Prophet after recognizing his truth. Their eternal fate is the Fire, which is far greater and more lasting than any earthly tribulation. This verse serves to correct the perspective of both the believers and the disbelievers. For the believers, it reminds them that the exile they witnessed was not the full extent of divine justice; the real reckoning is deferred to the Hereafter, so they should not feel that the enemy got off too lightly. For the Banu al-Nadir and all who reflect on their story, it is a chilling warning: to escape a temporary worldly punishment by being expelled with their families and some possessions is not a success; it is merely a delay before a far more terrifying and permanent punishment. The verse thus deepens the lesson from the previous sign, directing the "people of insight" to look beyond the immediate historical event to the eternal consequences that flow from defying Allah and His Messenger.
4 | This is because they defied Allah and His Messenger. And whoever defies Allah, then Allah is truly severe in punishment. | The surah now provides the concise cause of the punishment described. "This is because they defied Allah and His Messenger" is a simple, direct explanation. The word "defied" translates shāqqū, which literally means to split off, to oppose, to place oneself in a hostile camp against. The Banu al-Nadir did not merely disbelieve privately; they actively worked against the Prophet and his community, breaking their treaty and plotting murder. This is the root cause that triggered the divine intervention. The verse then generalizes the principle into a universal warning: "And whoever defies Allah, then Allah is truly severe in punishment." The severity mentioned is not cruelty; it is the perfect justice that meets rebellion with a proportionate and overwhelming response. The phrase "severe in punishment" is a divine attribute that the believer should hold in awe. It is not a punishment that can be softened by bribes, connections, or time; it is inescapable once decreed. For the listener, this verse destroys any sympathy that might arise for the exiled tribe. They are not innocent victims of aggression; they are reaping the consequence of their own defiance. The verse also functions as a deterrent for any person or group who might consider following their example. It teaches that the path of opposing the truth is not a path of victory or even honorable defeat; it is a path that leads straight to the severe grip of the Almighty, a grip that no one can loosen.
5 | Whatever palm trees you believers cut down or left standing intact, it was all by Allah's Will, so that He might disgrace the rebellious. | This verse addresses a specific controversy that arose during the siege of Banu al-Nadir. Some of the Muslims cut down certain palm trees of the enemy to facilitate the siege or to provoke them, while others left some trees standing. This created a disagreement: some felt that destroying the trees was a wasteful act, while others saw it as a legitimate war measure. The verse settles the matter by revealing that both actions were sanctioned by divine will. "Whatever palm trees you believers cut down or left standing intact, it was all by Allah's Will." The "you" is plural, addressing the community, and the reassurance is that neither action was sinful or wrong; both were within the bounds of divine permission. The purpose is then stated: "so that He might disgrace the rebellious." The cutting of the treasured palm trees, which were a source of pride and economic sustenance for the Banu al-Nadir, was a psychological blow designed to humiliate them and demonstrate the futility of their resistance. It showed that the very land they considered their own was now under the control of the believers by Allah's leave. The deeper lesson is that the means of warfare, including economic and psychological strategies, are permissible within the limits set by Allah, and that the ultimate aim is not destruction for its own sake but the establishment of truth and the disgrace of those who obstinately oppose it. The verse also teaches the companions, and all later Muslims, to avoid unnecessary internal disputes about operational matters in times of conflict, knowing that a larger divine wisdom governs the unfolding of events.
6 | As for the gains Allah has turned over to His Messenger from them—you did not even spur on any horse or camel for such gains. But Allah gives authority to His messengers over whoever He wills. For Allah is Most Capable of everything. | This verse introduces the concept of fay', the wealth that falls to the Muslims without actual combat. When Banu al-Nadir surrendered and agreed to exile, they left behind land, weapons, and property. The verse states that these gains are what "Allah has turned over to His Messenger from them," meaning they were obtained without a battle. "You did not even spur on any horse or camel for such gains" clarifies that unlike the spoils of war where fighters exert military effort, this wealth was acquired through siege and surrender, without a cavalry charge or a long expedition. Because no military effort was expended, the distribution of these gains was entirely at the discretion of the Prophet, as the following verses will explain. The phrase "But Allah gives authority to His messengers over whoever He wills" establishes the principle that the Prophet's authority over such gains is derived directly from Allah, not from the effort of the fighters. It is a divine privilege granted to the Messenger. The verse concludes with "For Allah is Most Capable of everything," a reminder that the transfer of wealth from a powerful enemy to a small Muslim community is no obstacle for the Almighty. This verse had profound practical implications: it meant that the lands of Banu al-Nadir were not divided among the warriors as other spoils were, but were placed under the Prophet's administration for the benefit of the wider community, especially the poor emigrants.
7 | As for gains granted by Allah to His Messenger from the people of other lands, they are for Allah and the Messenger, his close relatives, orphans, the poor, and needy travellers so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich. Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it. And whatever he forbids you from, leave it. And fear Allah. Surely Allah is severe in punishment. | This verse expands the ruling on fay' to include all future gains from non-Muslim communities acquired without battle. The categories of recipients are listed: "for Allah and the Messenger" indicates that the first portion is allocated for the general welfare of the religion and the state, under the Prophet's authority. "His close relatives" refers to the relatives of the Prophet from Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib, who were prohibited from receiving zakah but could receive from fay'. "Orphans, the poor, and needy travellers" are the classic categories of the vulnerable in society. The wisdom behind this distribution is explicitly stated: "so that wealth may not merely circulate among your rich." This is a foundational principle of Islamic economics. If wealth is only in the hands of the wealthy, society becomes stratified and unjust. By channeling fay' to the poor, the state ensures a wider distribution of resources and prevents the oligarchic concentration of capital. The verse then lays down a fundamental rule of Islamic authority: "Whatever the Messenger gives you, take it. And whatever he forbids you from, leave it." This establishes the Prophet's legislative authority beyond the Quran; his commands and prohibitions are binding in their own right. The verse closes with the dual command and warning: "And fear Allah. Surely Allah is severe in punishment." This reinforces that compliance with the Prophet's distribution and rulings is not optional but a matter of piety, and defiance carries a severe price.
8 | Some of the gains will be for poor emigrants who were driven out of their homes and wealth, seeking Allah's bounty and pleasure, and standing up for Allah and His Messenger. They are the ones true in faith. | The surah now begins to identify the primary recipients of the fay' from Banu al-Nadir. "Some of the gains will be for poor emigrants" specifically highlights the Muhajirun, the Meccan Muslims who left everything behind when they migrated to Medina. They were "driven out of their homes and wealth" by the persecution of the Quraysh, arriving in Medina with nothing. Their motivation is described with three phrases: "seeking Allah's bounty and pleasure, and standing up for Allah and His Messenger." Their migration was not for worldly gain but for spiritual reward and divine pleasure, and they proved their commitment by actively supporting the cause of Islam. The verse then bestows upon them a magnificent commendation: "They are the ones true in faith." The word "true" translates sādiqūn, meaning they are sincere and their claims of faith are verified by their sacrifices. This endorsement from Allah Himself removed any possible resentment from the Ansar or others who might have felt that the emigrants were receiving a disproportionate share. It also immortalized the emigrants' status as exemplars of genuine belief. The verse serves as a powerful reminder that true faith is proven through loss and displacement endured for the sake of Allah, and that divine provision in this world can come from unexpected sources to honor that sacrifice.
9 | As for those who had settled in the city and embraced the faith before the arrival of the emigrants, they love whoever immigrates to them, never having a desire in their hearts for whatever of the gains is given to the emigrants. They give the emigrants preference over themselves even though they may be in need. And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are truly successful. | This verse immortalizes the unmatched virtue of the Ansar, the Medinan helpers. "As for those who had settled in the city and embraced the faith before the arrival of the emigrants" defines them by their prior residence and their pre-existing faith, which meant that when the emigrants arrived, the Ansar were already established in Islam and could offer material and moral support. The description of their character is extraordinary. First, "they love whoever immigrates to them." This love is not a begrudging duty but a deep, welcoming affection. Second, they have "never having a desire in their hearts for whatever of the gains is given to the emigrants." When the lands of Banu al-Nadir were distributed primarily to the poor emigrants, the Ansar felt no envy or resentment. Their hearts were free from the grasping desire for material shares. Third, and most remarkably, "They give the emigrants preference over themselves even though they may be in need." This is the famous principle of īthār, altruistic preference. The Ansar would offer their own food, homes, and resources to the emigrants, even when they themselves were in poverty. A famous incident illustrates this: an Ansari offered one of his two wives to a newly arrived emigrant, and another offered half his property. The verse concludes with a universal principle: "And whoever is saved from the selfishness of their own souls, it is they who are truly successful." The Arabic word for selfishness, shuhh, is a deeper form of greed; it is the miserliness of the soul that holds back from giving. Being saved from this inner disease is the key to true success, both in this life and the next. The verse elevates the Ansar to the highest ranks of spiritual achievement and provides a permanent model of generosity and love for the sake of Allah.
10 | And those who come after them will pray, "Our Lord! Forgive us and our fellow believers who preceded us in faith, and do not allow bitterness into our hearts towards those who believe. Our Lord! Indeed, You are Ever Gracious, Most Merciful." | After mentioning the Emigrants and the Ansar, the surah now extends its view to the third generation: all believers who will come after them until the Day of Judgment. "And those who come after them" refers to later Muslims who did not have the privilege of seeing the Prophet or participating in those founding sacrifices. The verse describes their characteristic supplication: "Our Lord! Forgive us and our fellow believers who preceded us in faith." This is a prayer of humility and inclusion. The later believers recognize their own need for forgiveness and also actively pray for the earlier generations who established the faith. This creates an unbroken chain of love and du'a spanning the centuries. The second part of the prayer is profound: "and do not allow bitterness into our hearts towards those who believe." This is a plea to purify the heart from any resentment, envy, or ill-feeling towards fellow believers, especially those who came before and who might be perceived as receiving greater honor or reward. It is an acknowledgment that the spiritual diseases of the heart can creep in and destroy brotherhood, and that only Allah can protect the heart from such poison. The prayer closes with the invocation of two divine names: "Indeed, You are Ever Gracious, Most Merciful." This expresses trust that Allah's grace and mercy will cover their request. This verse has become a defining feature of Sunni Islam's ethos: love for the companions, respect for their hierarchy, and constant prayer for their forgiveness, while guarding one's heart from any bitterness that might arise from sectarian narratives.
11 | Have you O Prophet not seen the hypocrites who say to their fellow disbelievers from the People of the Book, "If you are expelled, we will certainly leave with you, and We will never obey anyone against you. And if you are fought against, we will surely help you."? But Allah bears witness that they are truly liars. | The surah now turns to expose the role of the hypocrites during the Banu al-Nadir crisis. Abdullah ibn Ubayy and his fellow hypocrites secretly sent messages to the Banu al-Nadir, promising them solidarity and military support. The verse quotes their exact promises: "If you are expelled, we will certainly leave with you," a promise of political solidarity and shared exile. "And we will never obey anyone against you," a guarantee that they would not be used as tools against their allies. "And if you are fought against, we will surely help you," a direct military commitment. These were powerful words meant to embolden the Banu al-Nadir to resist the Prophet. However, the divine commentary immediately exposes the hollowness of these vows: "But Allah bears witness that they are truly liars." Allah Himself testifies to their deceit. Their promises were never sincere; they were tactical lies designed to stir up conflict while the hypocrites themselves intended to stay safe. This verse teaches that Allah is fully aware of the secret alliances and whispered assurances of the enemies of truth, and He exposes them, sometimes through revelation and sometimes through the unfolding of events, to demonstrate that the plots of the hypocrites are built on nothing but falsehood.
12 | Indeed, if they are expelled, the hypocrites will never leave with them. And if they are fought against, the hypocrites will never help them. And even if the hypocrites did so, they would certainly flee, then the disbelievers would be left with no help. | The verse now unpacks the future reality that will prove the hypocrites' promises false. "Indeed, if they are expelled, the hypocrites will never leave with them." The exile they promised to share will be faced alone by the Banu al-Nadir; the hypocrites will find excuses to stay in their homes and property in Medina. "And if they are fought against, the hypocrites will never help them." Military assistance will never materialize. The verse then goes even further to expose the cowardice and unreliability of the hypocrites: "And even if the hypocrites did so, they would certainly flee." Even if, hypothetically, they were forced into the battlefield alongside the Banu al-Nadir, their lack of sincere conviction and their fear of death would cause them to break ranks and desert at the first sign of danger. The result would be catastrophic for the deceived party: "then the disbelievers would be left with no help." The Banu al-Nadir, having relied on false promises, would find themselves abandoned at the most critical moment, with no allies and no escape. This verse serves as a timeless warning: alliances with those who lack principle and courage, who operate on deceit and self-interest, are bonds that will snap under the slightest pressure. The people of truth must rely on Allah and on sincere, trustworthy allies, not on the hollow promises of hypocrites.
13 | Indeed, there is more fear in their hearts for you believers than for Allah. That is because they are a people who do not comprehend. | This verse diagnoses the deep psychological imbalance of the hypocrites. "Indeed, there is more fear in their hearts for you believers than for Allah." The hypocrites, despite their pretensions to faith, do not truly fear Allah's power and punishment. Their primary fear is worldly: they fear the believers, the loss of their social status, the exposure of their secrets, or even physical harm from the Muslim community. The proper hierarchy of fear has been inverted. A true believer fears Allah above all, and that fear liberates them from fearing creation. The hypocrites, lacking that divine fear, are consumed by fear of people. The verse then gives the root cause: "That is because they are a people who do not comprehend." They lack true understanding of Allah's majesty, His absolute power, and the reality of the Hereafter. If they truly comprehended that Allah alone controls life, death, and eternal destiny, their fear of any created being would shrink to insignificance. This verse teaches that courage comes from a correct understanding of Allah, and that the anxieties that plague people, especially the fear of others' opinions or harm, are ultimately symptoms of a spiritual comprehension deficit.
14 | Even united, they would not dare fight against you except from within fortified strongholds or from behind walls. Their malice for each other is intense: you think they are united, yet their hearts are divided. That is because they are a people with no real understanding. | The surah continues to analyze the weakness of the enemy. "Even united, they would not dare fight against you except from within fortified strongholds or from behind walls." This refers to the combined forces of the Banu al-Nadir and their hypocrite allies. Despite their numerical strength, they lack the courage for open confrontation. They can only wage war from behind walls, hiding in fortresses. This exposes their fundamental cowardice. The verse then reveals the internal reality behind the outward show of alliance: "Their malice for each other is intense." The word "malice" translates ba's, which indicates fierce hostility and aggression. The very groups that appear united against the Muslims actually harbor deep enmity and distrust towards one another. "You think they are united, yet their hearts are divided." This is a profound insight into the nature of coalitions built on shared hatred rather than shared principles. They may stand together on a superficial level, but their hearts are fragmented by mutual suspicion, jealousy, and conflicting interests. The cause is the same as before: "That is because they are a people with no real understanding." Their lack of genuine insight and wisdom prevents them from forming true unity, leaving them perpetually divided and vulnerable. For the believers, this was a reassurance that the enemy's alliance was far weaker than it appeared, and for all times, it explains why coalitions of falsehood inevitably crumble.
15 | They are both just like those who recently went down before them: they tasted the evil consequences of their doings. And they will suffer a painful punishment. | The verse draws a parallel with a recent historical example. "They are both just like those who recently went down before them" refers to the pagan Quraysh who were defeated at the Battle of Badr, which occurred a short time before the Banu al-Nadir incident. The "both" likely refers to the Banu al-Nadir and their hypocrite allies, or to the Banu al-Nadir and the previous disbelievers. The Quraysh at Badr had also faced a smaller Muslim force but were routed, and their leaders were killed. "They tasted the evil consequences of their doings" means that in this world, they received a bitter sample of the punishment their actions deserved. The verse then adds the eternal dimension: "And they will suffer a painful punishment." The worldly taste of defeat and death is only a precursor to the far greater suffering that awaits in the Hereafter. This comparison served to remind the Banu al-Nadir that the same fate that befell the Quraysh could befall them, and it urged the believers to remember Badr and take confidence that the same divine support that gave them victory before would not abandon them now.
16 | They are like Satan when he lures someone to disbelieve. Then after they have done so, he will say on Judgment Day, "I have absolutely nothing to do with you. I truly fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds." | The surah now illustrates the relationship between the hypocrites and the Banu al-Nadir with a powerful simile. "They are like Satan when he lures someone to disbelieve." The hypocrites are the devils in human form, enticing the Banu al-Nadir to resist and fight, just as Satan whispers to a person to commit disbelief. The seduction is persuasive, offering promises of support and solidarity. But the outcome is the same as Satan's ultimate betrayal of his followers: "Then after they have done so, he will say on Judgment Day, 'I have absolutely nothing to do with you. I truly fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds.'" On the Day of Judgment, when the disbeliever stands facing the consequence of his deeds and turns to the one who misled him, Satan will disown him completely. He will even claim to fear Allah, not out of genuine piety but as a hypocritical excuse to sever all ties and avoid any share of the blame. This is exactly what the hypocrites would do to the Banu al-Nadir on a smaller scale in this world: after egging them on with promises of support, they would abandon them to face their fate alone. The verse is a chilling warning about the nature of evil company and false leaders. They will use you, enjoy your support, and then discard you when the cost becomes too high. True loyalty exists only in the bond of sincere faith.
17 | So they will both end up in the Fire, staying there forever. That is the reward of the wrongdoers. | The verse delivers the final outcome for both the tempter and the tempted. "So they will both end up in the Fire" applies to both the hypocrite (the Satan-like figure) and the disbeliever from the People of the Book (the one lured). Despite their mutual betrayal and the breakdown of their worldly alliance, they will share the same ultimate destination. Neither can shift the blame onto the other; each is responsible for his own choice and will suffer the full consequence. "Staying there forever" indicates the eternal nature of this punishment for those who die upon disbelief and unrepentant hypocrisy. The verse then states the principle: "That is the reward of the wrongdoers." The word "wrongdoers" encompasses all who transgress the boundaries set by Allah, the greatest of which is shirk and disbelief. This verse serves as a sobering conclusion to the narrative of Banu al-Nadir and the hypocrites. It reminds the audience that temporary worldly alliances in sin will dissolve in the Hereafter, and the only thing that will remain is the individual's record of wrongdoing and its corresponding punishment.
18 | O believers! Be mindful of Allah and let every soul look to what deeds it has sent forth for tomorrow. And fear Allah, for certainly Allah is All-Aware of what you do. | After the long narrative of the Banu al-Nadir and the hypocrites, the surah returns to direct exhortation of the believers, drawing the moral lessons from the preceding events. "O believers! Be mindful of Allah" is the overarching command of taqwa, a God-consciousness that drives all action. The specific instruction that follows is immensely practical: "and let every soul look to what deeds it has sent forth for tomorrow." The "tomorrow" here is the Day of Judgment, which is as certain as the coming of the next day, yet often treated by people as a distant abstraction. The verse commands a daily, even moment-to-moment, self-audit. Just as a merchant reviews his accounts to see what profit he has made and what losses he has incurred, the believer should regularly examine his record of deeds, weighing his good actions against his bad, and preparing provisions for the journey into eternity. This introspection prevents the heedlessness that led the Banu al-Nadir and the hypocrites to their ruin; they failed to look ahead and see the consequences of their treachery. The command is then repeated with emphasis: "And fear Allah, for certainly Allah is All-Aware of what you do." The repetition of the command to fear Allah brackets the verse, and the divine name "All-Aware" assures that no deed, no matter how hidden, escapes His knowledge. This self-audit is thus conducted in the full consciousness that the Auditor is already perfectly aware of every entry in the ledger.
19 | And do not be like those who forgot Allah, so He made them forget themselves. It is they who are truly rebellious. | The surah now explicitly warns against the path that led the previous people to destruction. "And do not be like those who forgot Allah" refers to those who neglected the remembrance of their Lord, His commands, His rights, and their ultimate return to Him. The consequence is a profound and terrifying form of divine retribution: "so He made them forget themselves." When Allah forsakes a person because of their persistent neglect, He causes them to become oblivious to their own true nature, their purpose of existence, their spiritual needs, and the eternal interests of their soul. They become fully engrossed in their physical desires, worldly ambitions, and immediate gratifications, completely losing sight of what is truly good and harmful for them. This self-forgetfulness is the root of all sinful and destructive behavior. A person who remembers his own soul, its fragility, its responsibility, and its destiny, will be careful. The one who forgets it becomes reckless. The verse concludes: "It is they who are truly rebellious." The word "truly" emphasizes that these are the genuine deviators from the right path, not those who merely stumble and repent. This is a stark warning to the believers not to let their engagement with worldly affairs, even the legitimate spoils of war and the administration of the community, cause them to forget their primary purpose: the worship and remembrance of Allah.
20 | The residents of the Fire cannot be equal to the residents of Paradise. Only the residents of Paradise will be successful. | This verse draws the ultimate conclusion of the contrast between the two paths outlined in the surah. "The residents of the Fire cannot be equal to the residents of Paradise." It is a simple, self-evident, yet immensely powerful statement. In human affairs, outcomes can be ambiguous; a successful worldly life can be enjoyed by both the righteous and the wicked, and temporary suffering can befall both. This ambiguity can lead some to think that the two groups are essentially similar or that the distinctions between them are exaggerated. The verse cuts through this illusion by projecting the final, permanent state of each group. The residents of the Fire, whether they were the wealthy Banu al-Nadir, the cunning hypocrites, or anyone else who dies in a state of rebellion, and the residents of Paradise, whether they were the poor emigrants, the generous Ansar, or any sincere believer, are incommensurable. Their final conditions, one of eternal torment and the other of eternal bliss, have absolutely no point of comparison. The verse then isolates true success: "Only the residents of Paradise will be successful." All worldly successes—military victories, wealth, prestige—are merely means or illusions; true success is solely the attainment of Paradise and the escape from the Fire. This verse serves as the moral compass by which the believers evaluate every event, every gain, and every loss. It reframes the entire narrative of the surah: the Banu al-Nadir lost because they are not the residents of Paradise, while the believers, regardless of their temporary hardships, are the truly successful if they hold onto their faith.
21 | Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain, you would have certainly seen it humbled and torn apart in awe of Allah. We set forth such comparisons for people, so perhaps they may reflect. | This verse presents one of the most striking images in the Quran to illustrate the immense weight and majesty of divine revelation. Allah says, "Had We sent down this Quran upon a mountain," a scenario that imagines the Qur'an being revealed not to a human being but to the most solid, towering symbol of strength and stability in the natural world. The mountain, which stands firm against storms and time, would have been "humbled and torn apart in awe of Allah." The word "humbled" indicates a state of complete submission, where all pride and hardness dissolve, while "torn apart" describes a physical shattering from the intensity of the divine word. This is not mere destruction but a profound reaction to the overwhelming awareness of Allah's majesty contained in the revelation. The mountain, despite its immense size and strength, would be unable to bear the weight of the Quran's meanings and the responsibility of its trust. The verse then explains the purpose of such a vivid comparison: "We set forth such comparisons for people, so perhaps they may reflect." The mountain is an object lesson. The intended audience is not the mountain but human beings. The implication is piercing: the Quran was revealed to human hearts, which are softer than mountains in one sense but often prove harder and less responsive than rocks. A mountain would crumble in awe, yet a human being can listen to the same Quran and remain unmoved, unshaken, and unchanged. This is a profound indictment of human heedlessness and an urgent call to reflect. If the Quran can shatter a mountain, what should it do to a heart? It should humble it, bring it to tears, and transform it. The verse also rebuffs any suggestion that the Prophet invented the Quran; a speech so powerful that it would pulverize mountains cannot originate from a mere mortal. It also comforts the Prophet by showing that the hardness of his opponents is not due to any deficiency in the message but to the abnormal hardness of their hearts, hearts that proved harder than mountains. For the believer, this verse is an invitation to measure one's own response to the Quran. Does the recitation of the Quran produce a sense of awe, humility, and trembling in the heart, or has the heart become like a rock that no longer cracks? The verse teaches that the Quran is not ordinary speech; it is a divine force that should be approached with a receptive, humble spirit, constantly reflecting on its meanings so that it can have its intended transformative effect.
22 | He is Allah—there is no god worthy of worship except Him: Knower of the seen and unseen. He is the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful. | After the awe-inspiring image of the mountain crumbling before the Quran, the surah now concludes with a majestic crescendo of divine names that invites the heart to know the One who sent that mighty revelation. The verse begins with the fundamental declaration: "He is Allah—there is no god worthy of worship except Him." This is the core of tawhid, the absolute negation of any deity besides the one true God. It anchors the entire cosmic drama of the surah, from the glorification of the universe to the exile of the Banu al-Nadir, in the reality that all power, judgment, and mercy flow from this singular, unmatched Being. The first attribute mentioned is "Knower of the seen and unseen." This encompasses everything in existence. The seen is the visible world of matter, events, and actions; the unseen is the hidden realm of angels, the future, the secrets of hearts, and the decrees of destiny. The hypocrites and the Banu al-Nadir plotted in secret, thinking their conspiracies were hidden, but they were dealing with the Knower of the unseen, from whom nothing is concealed. This name reassures the believers that their secret sacrifices are known and warns the wrongdoers that their hidden schemes are fully exposed. Then the verse transitions to mercy: "He is the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful." These two names, al-Rahman and al-Rahim, are often the opening of every surah and represent the all-encompassing mercy that is Allah's dominant attribute. After the severe punishments described earlier in the surah, the mention of mercy here balances the picture. The expulsion of a treacherous tribe was an act of justice, but the door of mercy is wide open for those who repent. The universe began with mercy, and Paradise is the ultimate manifestation of that mercy. This verse connects the intellectual knowledge of Allah with the emotional comfort of His mercy, creating a complete portrait: He knows every detail, and He is overwhelmingly merciful. For the believer, this pairing means that one can trust the Knower of the unseen with all hidden anxieties, and can hope in the mercy that overcomes all sins. It invites the heart to both reverent awe and hopeful love.
23 | He is Allah—there is no god except Him: the King, the Most Holy, the All-Perfect, the Source of Serenity, the Watcher of all, the Almighty, the Supreme in Might, the Majestic. Glorified is Allah far above what they associate with Him in worship! | This verse piles up a series of divine names, each a brilliant facet of the divine majesty, creating a powerful cumulative effect. "He is Allah—there is no god except Him" repeats the fundamental creed, re-centering the listener. Then follows "the King," al-Malik. He is the true Sovereign, the Owner of all dominion. The political events of the surah, the transfer of lands and wealth from the Banu al-Nadir to the believers, are simply the operations of the true King redistributing His property. "The Most Holy," al-Quddus, means the One who is absolutely pure, free from any defect, imperfection, or resemblance to creation. He is so pure that even the angels who are near to Him can only veil themselves in awe. "The All-Perfect," al-Salam, is the Source of peace and perfection, the One from whom all safety and wholeness emanates. In a surah that details conflict and punishment, this name reminds us that the ultimate goal of His decrees is to establish true peace for the believing community. "The Source of Serenity," al-Mu'min, can also mean the Giver of faith or security. He grants faith to the hearts of the believers and will grant them ultimate security from fear on the Day of Judgment. "The Watcher of all," al-Muhaymin, is the One who observes, oversees, and protects. He watched over the conspiracies, the siege, and the distribution of spoils; nothing escapes His supervision. "The Almighty," al-Aziz, the One whose power cannot be frustrated, is paired with "the Supreme in Might," al-Jabbar, the One who enforces His will and mends what is broken, compelling everything to submit. Finally, "the Majestic," al-Mutakabbir, is the One whose greatness and pride are inherent and absolute, unlike the false pride of creatures which is a sin. The verse then breaks into glorification: "Glorified is Allah far above what they associate with Him in worship!" All these perfect attributes demonstrate how absurd and offensive it is to associate any partner with such a Being. The surah thus draws its audience away from any lingering attachment to idols, tribal alliances, or self-reliance, and places them before the overwhelming reality of this unique, majestic King.
24 | He is Allah: the Creator, the Inventor, the Shaper. He alone has the Most Beautiful Names. Whatever is in the heavens and the earth constantly glorifies Him. And He is the Almighty, All-Wise. | The final verse of the surah presents a triad of names related to creation, completing the magnificent description. "He is Allah: the Creator," al-Khaliq, is the One who brought everything into existence from nothing, planning and measuring its creation. "The Inventor," al-Bari', is the One who then brought forth the creation in actuality, differentiating the forms, separating the elements, and giving each thing its distinct nature. "The Shaper," al-Musawwir, is the One who formed and shaped each creation, giving it its specific appearance, features, and beauty. These three names represent the stages of the divine creative act: the decree and planning, the bringing into existence, and the detailed fashioning. This refutes any notion that the universe came into being randomly or through blind processes. Everything, from the galaxies to the human face, is the deliberate work of the Creator, Inventor, and Shaper. The verse then declares, "He alone has the Most Beautiful Names." All the names mentioned in this surah and throughout the Quran are His exclusive possession, describing His perfect attributes. They are "most beautiful" because they signify the highest possible qualities of majesty and beauty. The verse then circles back to the very first verse of the surah, creating a perfect frame: "Whatever is in the heavens and the earth constantly glorifies Him." The surah opened with this universal glorification, and now it closes with it, having demonstrated through the historical narrative, the legal rulings, and the descriptions of various groups how this glorification manifests and how its rejection leads to ruin. The final words are "And He is the Almighty, All-Wise," the same two names that ended the first verse. The Almighty ensures that His will is executed, as seen in the expulsion of the Banu al-Nadir, and the All-Wise ensures that every command, every trial, and every distribution is perfectly placed. The surah thus ends on a note of complete resolution: the entire story of the universe, with its conflicts, revelations, and communities, is enclosed within the knowledge, power, mercy, and wisdom of the One who is the Creator, Inventor, and Shaper, the Possessor of the Most Beautiful Names, continuously glorified by all that exists.
