1 | What are they asking one another about? | The surah opens with a powerful rhetorical question: “What are they asking one another about?” This is not a request for information but a device that arrests attention and draws the listener straight into the heart of the matter. The “they” here refers to the pagan Quraysh of Mecca, who, upon hearing the Prophet’s message about the resurrection and the Day of Judgement, began questioning one another in tones that mixed curiosity, scepticism and outright mockery. The verb “asking one another” shows that this was a widespread phenomenon—people were talking amongst themselves, exchanging doubts and half-mocking enquiries rather than seeking sincere answers. Some would say, “What is this news Muhammad is bringing? Is it sorcery, poetry or soothsaying?” Others would ask sarcastically, “Will we really be brought back to life after we have become dust and bones?” The question the surah poses mimics their own question back to them, but with a profound shift: Allah is now asking about their question. This immediately puts them in the spotlight and makes their casual chatter an object of divine scrutiny. The very act of asking “what” implies that something enormous is being discussed, something that has unsettled them, and the surah will soon name it. By framing the opening in this way, the Qur’an engages the reader or listener directly and creates a sense of anticipation. We are made to wonder: what is this news that has stirred such intense debate? The question also carries a subtle rebuke. It suggests that what they are casually asking about is far too serious to be reduced to idle talk or mutual wrangling. The opening verse, therefore, sets the stage for the entire surah. It introduces the theme of disputation surrounding the “momentous news” and hints at the arrogance of those who treat the ultimate realities of existence as matters for entertainment or partisan argument. The mention of “one another” underscores that the debate was not just private; it was a public, communal affair, and the entire society was caught up in it. This communal denial and confusion will be answered by a sequence of evidence and warning that leaves no room for doubt. So right from these first few words, the listener is positioned to take seriously what will follow, recognising that the question being bandied about in the streets of Mecca was, in truth, the most consequential question a human being can ever face.
2 | About the momentous news, | The answer to the opening question comes immediately: “About the momentous news.” The Arabic term translated as “momentous news” is an-naba’ al-‘azīm, a phrase packed with gravity. Naba’ means important news, the kind of report that changes everything once it is known. ‘Azīm intensifies it to mean something tremendously weighty, awesome and profound. The earliest commentators of the Qur’an, among them the Companion Ibn ‘Abbās, explained that this momentous news refers primarily to the resurrection and the Day of Judgement, while others said it is the Qur’an itself, for the Qur’an carries the news of that Day and many other unseen realities. Both interpretations are compatible because the Qur’an is the vehicle of this ultimate news. What the Meccans were debating was precisely the message that there is life after death, that every soul will be held accountable, and that a day of absolute justice and recompense is inevitable. To the pagan mind, this was astonishing, even unbelievable. They saw death as final and could not conceive of their decayed bodies being brought back to life. Yet the news was not a baseless claim; it came from the Lord of all worlds through a trustworthy messenger. The surah labels their topic of discussion “the momentous news” in order to jolt them out of their casual attitude. If they only grasped how immense this news truly is, they would not ask about it with smug condescension. The phrase also prepares the ground for the threats and promises that fill the rest of the surah. Calling it “news” reminds us that it is information about a future event, information that one can either accept or reject, but which, once it comes to pass, will no longer be a matter of debate. The word choice implies that the disbelievers themselves, by constantly talking about it, have testified to its importance. If it were trivial, they would have ignored it. Their intense disagreement itself proves that something monumental has been placed before them. The surah seizes on this reality and forces them to face the content of their own conversations, now described with the awe-inspiring epithet it truly deserves. This verse thus functions as a bridge: from the question about their talk, we move to the identity of that talk, and the remaining verses will first warn them that they will come to know its truth, then lay out the evidence in creation that makes the news entirely credible.
3 | over which they disagree. | Having identified the subject as the momentous news, the surah adds: “over which they disagree.” The Quraysh could not settle on a single coherent objection to the Prophet’s message. Some called it “magic,” others “poetry,” others “ancient fables,” and still others claimed it was a fabrication taught to him by someone. This disunity of criticism is itself a sign of the weakness of their position. When truth is clear, those who oppose it often resort to contradictory accusations because they cannot find one solid basis for rejection. The Qur’an highlights this elsewhere, noting that their hearts are alike in denial even though their words differ. The disagreement was not only about how to label the message, but also about the nature of the resurrection itself. Some believed in a form of afterlife but distorted it; others flatly denied any life after death, thinking the very idea absurd. The verse captures that chaotic state of mind in which people argue passionately about something they have not sincerely investigated. Deep down, their disagreement arose from a shared desire to evade accountability. If they admitted the news was true, they would have to change their entire way of life, abandon idolatry, and prepare for a judgement they did not wish to face. So they preferred to keep the matter in the realm of endless debate, hoping that as long as they disagreed among themselves and with the Prophet, they could postpone any personal reckoning. The surah’s response is to state that this disagreement does nothing to alter the fact of the news itself. The day will come when all the quarrelling will be silenced, and everyone will see with certainty what they once disputed. The placement of this verse right after the mention of the momentous news underscores a recurring Qur’anic pattern: humanity is given a clear message, yet instead of submitting, people often fall into contention. The believers are then comforted by the knowledge that this is not new; earlier nations also disputed with their prophets. The disagreement itself becomes a proof that the message is significant, for trivial matters do not provoke such sustained controversy. Finally, the verse ties back to the first verse: their mutual questioning was not a calm search for truth but a state of confusion and conflict, and this is precisely what the warning “they will come to know” will answer.
4 | But no! They will come to know. | This verse begins with the Arabic particle kallā, which is a strong, emphatic negation that interrupts the previous line of thought. Translated here as “But no!”, it sharply dismisses the disbelievers’ attitude of denial and mockery. It is as though Allah is saying: Their disagreement and scoffing are completely misplaced; reality is not defined by their opinions. Immediately after this forceful negation comes the threat: “They will come to know.” The future tense carries absolute certainty. The knowledge referred to is not intellectual discovery but experiential realisation—they will taste the consequence of their denial when the punishment arrives. Scholars of tafsir explain that this knowing may take place in stages. The polytheists of Mecca would soon experience the truth of the Prophet’s warning in this world through the defeat at Badr and other events, and ultimately every human being will know the truth with certainty at the moment of death or on the Day of Resurrection. Some commentators mention that the first “they will come to know” in this verse points to the punishment of the grave, while the repetition in the next verse refers to the terrors of the Day of Judgement, but these are interpretive possibilities, and the verse itself leaves the timing intentionally broad so that it covers every level of realisation. The threat is not an arbitrary show of force; it is the logical outcome of rejecting clear truth after being given every opportunity to accept it. By stating “they will come to know” rather than specifying the punishment in detail right away, the surah creates a powerful sense of dread and anticipation. The listener understands that something inescapable is heading towards those who persist in their arrogance. The verse also serves as a comfort to the Prophet and the believers, who were themselves mocked and harassed. It reassures them that the final word does not belong to the scoffers. Justice may be delayed, but it is never cancelled. The eloquence of the verse lies in its brevity and force; it is a thunderbolt in the middle of the dispute, cutting off all arguments and turning the tables on those who thought themselves safely entrenched in their denial. It prepares the heart for the detailed proofs of divine power that follow, because once the certainty of the outcome is established, the evidence for it can be calmly laid out.
5 | Again, no! They will come to know. | The repetition of the warning in identical words—“Again, no! They will come to know”—intensifies the effect dramatically. In Arabic rhetoric, repetition is a device used to emphasise the absolute certainty of a statement and to leave no room for ambiguity. The second kallā is like a double seal on the threat. It tells us that the matter is so serious that it deserves to be said twice, and that the coming knowledge is not a mere possibility but an iron-clad reality. If the first “they will come to know” breaks the disbelievers’ complacency, the second one shatters any lingering hope that the warning might be an exaggeration. Some scholars have noted that the repetition corresponds to two aspects of knowing: one in this life, when the signs of the Qur’an and the truth of the Prophet’s mission become undeniable, and the other in the Hereafter, when they face the full consequences. Others say it points to the repeated shocks of the Day of Judgement itself, where terrifying realisation will come in waves. Whatever the precise import, the overall message is that ignorance and denial have an expiry date. The surah now pivots from warning to proof. The rhetorical questions that follow in the next verses are connected to this warning by a profound logic: the One who created the heavens and the earth with such precision and care is surely able to bring about the Day He has appointed. Thus the threats in verses 4 and 5 act as a strong hinge. Before them, the surah identified the subject of dispute; after them, it will unfold the evidence that makes the news credible. This structure teaches a vital lesson about how the Qur’an addresses doubt: it first establishes the gravity of the message and the certainty of the outcome, and then it leads the listener gently through the observable signs of creation. The repetition also has an emotional effect on the reciter and the attentive listener. It instils a healthy fear of the Hereafter and a sense of urgency to reflect before that knowledge comes in a way that no longer benefits the soul. By ending the verse with the same phrase, the surah engraves the warning into the heart, making it impossible to read the subsequent signs with a detached or indifferent mind.
6 | Have We not smoothed out the earth ˹like a bed˺, | With this verse, the surah begins a magnificent series of rhetorical questions, each starting with “Have We not…?” The first of these draws attention to the earth itself: “Have We not smoothed out the earth like a bed?” The question expects an affirmative answer—indeed, every human being who walks upon the ground knows that the earth has been made spread out and stable. The word translated as “smoothed out” and “like a bed” comes from the Arabic root mahd, which refers to a cradle or a bed prepared for rest. The image is tender and domestic. Just as a caring mother prepares a comfortable bed for her child, so has the Creator prepared the earth to be a place of dwelling, ease and rest for humanity. The earth is not a hostile, jagged surface that makes life impossible; it has been levelled and softened so that we can build, farm, travel and sleep upon it. The metaphor of a bed also connects directly to what comes a few verses later, when sleep itself is described as a form of rest. There is a profound harmony between the earth as a bed and sleep as a state of repose; the outer bed of the earth and the inner state of sleep are two gifts that work together. Beyond the immediate blessing, the verse carries a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who smoothed out the earth from a chaotic, uneven state and made it a suitable home is fully able to transform it again on the Day of Judgement into the plain of gathering. The Meccans themselves enjoyed the benefits of the smoothed-out earth every day, yet they denied the power of the One who prepared it for them. By asking the question, the surah invites them to reflect on what they already take for granted. It reminds them that the earth did not make itself habitable; a wise and powerful Maker stands behind it. This gentle interrogation is far more effective than a blunt statement because it forces the mind to concede the truth before it moves on to the next sign. The earth as a bed is thus the first stone in the foundation of evidence that will leave the deniers without any excuse.
7 | and ˹made˺ the mountains as ˹its˺ pegs, | The second sign follows naturally: “and made the mountains as its pegs.” After spreading the earth like a bed, the Creator anchored it with mountains that function as pegs. The imagery is drawn from the Bedouin tent, which is held firm by pegs driven into the ground. Without the pegs, the tent would collapse or be blown away by the wind. Similarly, mountains have deep roots that extend into the earth’s crust and stabilise the land, preventing it from shaking excessively. The Qur’an makes this function explicit in other verses, such as “He has cast into the earth firmly set mountains, lest it shift with you” (16:15). The early Arabs, living in a landscape dominated by towering mountains, could immediately grasp the analogy. But the verse goes beyond physical function. The mountains are not only functional; they are also majestic signs of the Creator’s power. They stand as silent witnesses to a design that balances the planet. The transition from the earth as a bed to the mountains as its pegs creates a complete picture of a stable, secure world prepared for human life. The bed needs its pegs, and the pegs serve the purpose of the bed. There are no useless elements in Allah’s creation. For the deniers of the resurrection, the mountains posed a particular challenge to their thinking. They saw them as symbols of permanence and solidity, yet the surah will later declare that on the Day of Judgement these very mountains will be blown away and become like a mirage. So the description here of mountains as pegs is deliberate: it establishes that these colossal formations, which seem eternal, are in fact placed and controlled by Allah. He who pegged them down can also uproot them. The question “Have We not made…” again forces the listener to acknowledge the reality of the mountains’ stabilising role. No one can credibly claim that the mountains came into being by themselves or that their beneficial placement is the result of random chance. The verse humbles the human observer and points him towards the One who alone has the power to fashion the earth in this manner, and who therefore has the power to reshape it for the Day of Final Decision that the surah is building towards.
8 | and created you in pairs, | After the external signs on the earth, the surah turns to the human being himself: “and created you in pairs.” The term “pairs” translates the Arabic azwāj, which includes the meaning of male and female, spouses, and also more generally all complementary things. The immediate and most powerful implication is the creation of humanity as men and women, designed for companionship, love, procreation and mutual support. The pairing is a sign of Allah’s wisdom and mercy. Through it, life continues across generations, and the human heart finds comfort in the bond of marriage, as described elsewhere in the Qur’an: “And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them” (30:21). The verse in this surah, however, does not only point to emotional and social benefits. It serves as a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who originated human beings from a single soul and then spread from them countless men and women is certainly capable of bringing them back together after death. If He could produce the first pair and then multiply them through the reproductive process, why would a second creation be difficult for Him? The Meccan pagans, who acknowledged Allah as the Creator, were trapped by the very logic they refused to follow to its conclusion. By mentioning “pairs” right after the earth and the mountains, the surah weaves humanity into the fabric of creation. Humans are not observers standing outside the universe; they are part of the same divinely designed order. The pairing also subtly prepares for the later description of the rewards of Paradise, where the righteous will be joined with pure spouses. So the verse looks in two directions: back to the proofs in nature and forward to the fulfilment in the Hereafter. The question format continues: “Have We not created you in pairs?” It is a question that every human being, regardless of belief, can answer with a simple yes. In that admission lies the seed of recognition that the Creator who designed this pairing for our benefit is also the One who has the right to call us to account on a day that is as real as the earth we walk upon.
9 | and made your sleep for rest, | The signs now shift to the rhythms of daily human experience: “and made your sleep for rest.” The Arabic word subāt denotes a cutting off or a state of deep repose where all activity ceases. Sleep is a universal human phenomenon that is so ordinary it is often forgotten as a sign, yet the Qur’an elevates it to a miracle. Every night, the human being enters a state that closely resembles death: consciousness is suspended, the body lies still, and the soul is taken in a manner described elsewhere in the Qur’an as a temporary taking by Allah. Then, by His mercy, the person wakes up to a new day, as if given a new life. This daily cycle of “death” and “resurrection” is a powerful, tangible proof that Allah can bring the dead back to life on the grand scale of the Hereafter. The verse subtly reminds the deniers that they experience a miniature version of resurrection every single morning, yet they deny the larger resurrection to come. Beyond its evidential value, sleep is described here as a gift and a mercy. The surah has already called the earth a bed; now it names sleep as the rest that that bed was made for. The two are linked in a chain of divine care. The One who spread the earth for you is the One who gave you the capacity to close your eyes and find renewal. Sleep is not a defect in human design; it is an intentional feature that heals the body, consolidates memory and restores the spirit. The verse invites reflection on what life would be like without the blessing of sleep. The continuous exhaustion would make existence unbearable. By framing it as a question—“and made your sleep for rest?”—the surah prompts the listener to feel gratitude and to recognise the intentional kindness behind this provision. The deep rest of sleep also contrasts starkly with the torment of Hell described later in the surah, where there will be no coolness, no drink and no respite. Thus the verse functions on multiple levels: it is a sign, a mercy, a proof for the resurrection and a subtle warning that the comfort people now enjoy can be lost if they deny the Giver.
10 | and made the night as a cover, | Continuing the pattern of everyday blessings, the surah asks: “and made the night as a cover.” The word libās normally means garment or clothing. So the night is a garment that wraps the world in darkness. A garment covers a person’s body, concealing it, protecting it and giving it a sense of privacy and security. Night performs these very functions for the earth and its inhabitants. It conceals people and their movements from the eyes of potential enemies; it provides the darkness necessary for restful sleep, as visual stimuli are dimmed; and it covers the landscape with a serene stillness that allows the tired mind and body to recuperate. The metaphor of clothing is rich with connotations. Just as a person instinctively seeks clothing for comfort and protection, so humanity instinctively welcomes the night as a covering that brings relief after the glare and heat of the day. The verse also implies a moral and spiritual dimension: night is a cover for those who wish to pray in private, for lovers who seek lawful intimacy, and even for wrongdoers who try to hide their deeds. In all these cases, the night serves as a divinely appointed screen. Yet the very fact that some misuse this cover does not negate the blessing itself; it only shows that human choice operates within the framework of divine provision. The surah has already mentioned the earth as a bed and sleep as rest; the night as a cover completes the picture of a perfect sleeping environment. The earth is the resting place, sleep is the state of repose, and the night is the blanket that envelops the sleeper. All three work together seamlessly. The question “Have We not made the night as a cover?” expects the listener to acknowledge this obvious truth. It also prepares the ground for the immediate counterpart in the next verse—the day made for livelihood. The alternation of night and day is one of the most frequently cited signs in the Qur’an, and it serves here as a single coherent proof. The One who can spread the covering of night and then lift it to bring forth the day is the One who can bring the universe to an end and then recreate it for the Final Decision.
11 | and made the day for livelihood, | The counterpart to the covering of night is the day that has been “made for livelihood.” The Arabic ma‘āsh comes from a root meaning to live and to earn a living. The day is the time of activity, movement and work. When dawn breaks and light returns, people rise from their beds and go out to seek provision. Farmers tend their fields, traders open their markets, and all kinds of crafts and industries come to life. The bright light of day is not merely a physical phenomenon; it is a condition that enables human beings to see, to interact and to carry out the tasks that sustain their lives. The verse thus reminds the listener that the day is not an accident of cosmic mechanics but a deliberate gift from the Creator, tailored to human need. The mention of “livelihood” is significant. It acknowledges that earning a living is a central part of human existence. Islam does not regard material work as something low or separate from spirituality; rather, it is part of the divine plan that humans should disperse through the earth and seek Allah’s bounty. However, the verse also implies a test: the day is given for livelihood, but that livelihood is to be sought in ways that are lawful and mindful of the One who provided the day. The deniers of Mecca were busy in their trade and commerce, benefiting from the daylight, yet they attributed their success to their own efforts or to their idols, forgetting the One who made the day possible. The pairing of night and day in these two verses forms a powerful argument. The One who alternates the night and day with perfect consistency, never allowing one to encroach permanently on the other, is clearly in control of time and the universe. If He can bring the day back after the death-like stillness of the night, He can just as easily bring life back after the stillness of the grave. The question “and made the day for livelihood?” forces the listener to connect the daily rhythm he takes for granted with the greater rhythm of life, death and resurrection. It is a call to see the sign in the ordinary and to realise that the same Lord who gives the day for work has also appointed a Day for ultimate judgement and recompense.
12 | and built above you seven mighty ˹heavens˺, | After grounding the listener in the immediate blessings of earth, sleep, night and day, the surah lifts the gaze upwards: “and built above you seven mighty heavens.” The verb “built” (banaynā) evokes a strong, solid structure. The heavens are not mere empty space; they are a formidable construction, described as “mighty” (shidād), indicating their immense strength, vastness and perfection. The number seven is understood by the vast majority of scholars as literal, with each heaven being a distinct realm, as mentioned in the hadith of the Prophet’s night journey, where he passed through the seven heavens and met various prophets. The heavens are above us, yet they remain securely in place without visible pillars, as another verse states, “He raised the heavens without pillars that you see” (13:2). This is a sign of Allah’s absolute power that surpasses human engineering. The Quraysh, who looked at the sky every night, could appreciate the grandeur of the celestial dome. By calling the heavens “mighty,” the surah emphasises that they are not fragile; they are part of an unshakeable cosmic order. This sets up a dramatic contrast with the later verses that describe the sky being split open and becoming gates on the Day of Judgement. The very structure that seems so permanent will one day be torn apart. The rhetorical question “Have We not built?” again demands an affirmative acknowledgment. The seven heavens are a standing proof that the Creator is capable of anything. If He could build such a towering, complex edifice without any support, what difficulty would He have in raising the dead? The mention of seven heavens also links to the next verse, where within these heavens a shining lamp is placed. Thus the structure is not empty; it is populated with signs. For the human being standing on the smoothed earth, secured by the pegs of mountains, enveloped in the alternating garment of night and the brightness of day, and looking up at the seven mighty heavens, the entire universe becomes a temple of signs pointing to the One who created it all. The surah is methodically dismantling any excuse for denying the resurrection by placing the evidence in every direction.
13 | and placed ˹in them˺ a shining lamp, | Within those mighty heavens, the Creator “placed a shining lamp.” The “shining lamp” is the sun, described by the Arabic sirājan wahhājā, a lamp that is intensely glowing and radiant. The metaphor of a lamp is deeply instructive. A lamp is placed in a room to provide light, warmth and guidance. The sun does exactly that for the inhabitants of the earth. It illuminates the day, making livelihood possible, as mentioned earlier. It provides heat that drives the weather, nourishes plant life and sustains the entire ecosystem. Without this “lamp,” the earth would be a frozen, dark rock. The description wahhāj implies not just light but blazing brightness, indicating the sun’s tremendous energy. By calling it a lamp, the verse implicitly compares the sun to a smaller, familiar object that humans use in their homes, thereby making the vast and distant sun feel personal and directly beneficial. The placement of the sun “in them”—meaning within the framework of the heavens—shows that it is part of the ordered system, not an independent deity as some pagans believed. The Quraysh were surrounded by cultures that worshipped the sun and the moon, and the Qur’an consistently reframes celestial bodies as signs and servants of Allah, not objects of worship. This verse is a quiet but firm correction of any such tendency. The lamp is a servant, set in its place by the Builder of the heavens. It has no will of its own; it runs its course by the command of its Lord. The question “Have We not placed?” brings the listener back to the fundamental idea that all these blessings are given, not earned. The One who hung this brilliant lamp in the sky for human benefit will one day fold it up and bring the universe to an end. The sun that rises every morning with unfailing regularity is itself a daily reminder of the greater rising of the Day of Resurrection. Just as the lamp of the sun brings the dead earth to life with its light and warmth, so will the call of the Trumpet bring the dead out of their graves into the new dawn of eternity.
14 | and sent down from rainclouds pouring water, | From the heights of the shining heavens, the surah now brings the gaze back to the interaction between sky and earth: “and sent down from rainclouds pouring water.” The word mu‘ṣirāt can refer to clouds that are heavy with rain, ready to burst, or to winds that press and squeeze the clouds until they release their load. In either understanding, the imagery is one of abundance and readiness. The water is described as thajjāj, meaning pouring in torrents, copious and flowing forcefully. This is not a gentle drizzle but a generous, life-giving downpour that penetrates the earth, fills the rivers and replenishes the wells. The verse highlights the mechanism of rain as a deliberate act of sending down. Rain does not fall randomly; it is sent by the One who knows the needs of His creation. The pagan Arabs understood rain as a matter of life and death in their arid land. When the rains failed, famine followed. Yet many of them attributed rainfall to natural forces or to the movements of stars, failing to acknowledge it as a direct blessing from Allah. The question “Have We not sent down?” reclaims the phenomenon as a divine gift. The descending water also becomes a central proof for the resurrection. The Qur’an repeatedly draws the analogy between rain reviving the dead earth and Allah reviving the dead on the Day of Judgement. The dry, barren land, when touched by the pouring water, trembles, swells and puts forth every kind of beautiful growth. Human beings see this miracle annually or seasonally, yet they doubt that their own dry bones can be clothed with life again. The surah is building a cumulative case: the earth as bed, the mountains as pegs, the alternation of night and day, and now the descent of life-giving water—all point to a Creator who initiates life, sustains it and can restore it. The pouring water is the vital link between the static structures of the universe and the vibrant life that will be described in the next verses, where grain, plants and orchards burst forth.
15 | producing by it grain and ˹various˺ plants, | The purpose of the pouring rain is now made explicit: “producing by it grain and various plants.” The water that descends does not simply disappear into the ground; it is the direct cause of the vegetation that sustains human and animal life. The verse singles out “grain” (ḥabb) first. Grain refers to the edible seeds such as wheat, barley, corn, rice and all the staple crops that form the foundation of human food. Grain is the staff of life, and it is a marvel of design that a tiny, dry seed, when given water, germinates and multiplies into a plant bearing hundreds of new seeds. This cycle of death and rebirth is a miniature resurrection happening in every field and garden. After grain, the verse mentions “various plants” (nabāt), encompassing all kinds of herbs, grasses, vegetables and foliage that clothe the earth in green and provide food and medicine. The variety is a sign of Allah’s endless creativity. From the same water, the earth produces grapes and dates, olives and pomegranates, sweet and bitter, colourful and fragrant—a dazzling array that delights the senses and nourishes the body. The verse is a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who causes a lifeless seed to split open and grow into a living plant, using nothing more than water and soil, is more than able to cause a human body to be resurrected from its scattered remains. The analogy is precise: the earth dies in drought and lives again with rain; the human body dies and will live again by the command of Allah. The Meccans, who depended on grain and plants for their survival, were being asked to look at their own dinner plates and see the signature of the Creator who can bring forth the dead. The verse also teaches gratitude. Grain and plants are not automatic products of nature; they are gifts that flow from Allah’s mercy. Recognising this truth is the first step towards recognising the Giver and, subsequently, towards preparing for the day when all will be questioned about how they used these gifts.
16 | and dense orchards? | The sequence of botanical signs reaches a beautiful climax with “and dense orchards?” The word jannāt means gardens, and alfāf means interwoven, thick and tangled in a lush way. The image is of an orchard where the branches of fruit trees intertwine, creating a canopy of deep shade, abundant fruit and intoxicating fragrance. The density suggests a garden that is overflowing with life and fertility, far beyond a sparse, orderly row of trees. It is a place of delight, beauty and perfect provision. The choice of the word jannāt is deeply significant, because the same word is used throughout the Qur’an for the gardens of Paradise. The orchards of this world are thus a foretaste, a fleeting glimpse of the eternal gardens promised to the righteous. The surah later describes the reward of the believers in terms of gardens and vineyards, so this early mention of dense orchards creates a thematic thread that ties the signs in creation to the blessings of the Hereafter. The question mark at the end reminds us that the entire passage from verse 6 to verse 16 has been one sustained rhetorical question. All these signs have been strung together under the overarching “Have We not…?” that demands a single answer: Yes, indeed, the Lord has done all this. The cumulative weight of these signs is now almost overwhelming. The listener has been led from the vast expanse of the smoothed earth, through the anchoring mountains, the intimacy of human pairs, the rest of sleep, the cover of night, the brightness of the day for livelihood, the mighty seven heavens, the blazing lamp of the sun, the pouring rain, the sprouting grain and plants, and finally into the cool shade of the intertwined orchard. Each sign built on the one before it. The conclusion is inescapable: the Creator who designed this magnificent, interconnected system is perfectly capable of bringing about the Day of Final Decision. The dense orchards also serve as a bridge. They are the last of the blessings mentioned, and they carry a hint of Paradise that prepares the heart for the stark contrast with the horrors of Judgement Day and Hell that the surah is about to depict. The scene is set for the transition from signs to the appointed time.
17 | Indeed, the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision is an appointed time— | After the expansive list of signs, the surah returns with force to the main theme: “Indeed, the Day of Final Decision is an appointed time.” The word inna (“indeed”) underscores the absolute certainty of the statement. What follows is not a possibility or a myth; it is a fixed reality. The “Day of Final Decision” translates yawm al-faṣl. Faṣl means separation, distinguishing, and decisive judgement. On that Day, all the disputes of humanity will be settled. Truth will be separated from falsehood, the believer from the disbeliever, the obedient from the rebellious. The arguing that the Meccans engaged in regarding the momentous news will be brought to a definitive end. There will be no more room for disagreement. The second part of the verse says that this Day is “an appointed time.” The word mīqāt refers to a time that has been precisely set, like the appointed times of prayer or the fixed seasons of pilgrimage. It is not a vague future possibility; it is a specific moment in Allah’s knowledge, determined before the creation of the heavens and the earth. The use of mīqāt is comforting for the believers and deeply frightening for the deniers. For believers, it means that the injustice they suffer has a deadline; relief will come at the exact time Allah has decreed. For deniers, it means that the respite they enjoy is measured, and when the appointed time arrives, neither delay nor escape will be possible. The verse functions as a transitional hinge. The signs that were just presented prove that the One who created all this order in the universe is the One who has also appointed a fixed time for its conclusion and for the judgement of its inhabitants. The logic is watertight: a creation of such magnificent design cannot be left without a purpose and without a final accounting. The verse also picks up the warning from verses 4 and 5. There, they were told they would come to know; now the exact name and nature of that coming event are disclosed. The “Day of Final Decision” gives a name to the dread that the earlier verses evoked. It is a day that will sort out every confusion and resolve every dispute, including the one with which the surah began.
18 | ˹it is˺ the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will ˹all˺ come forth in crowds. | The appointed time is now described in vivid terms: “the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will all come forth in crowds.” The Trumpet, or ṣūr, is a mighty horn that the angel Isrāfīl will blow by Allah’s command. According to authentic hadith, there will be two main blasts: the first will stun all creation and cause death, and the second will bring about the resurrection. This verse focuses on the second blast, the one that summons the dead from their graves. The phrase “you will all come forth” addresses humanity directly and collectively. None will be left behind. The word “crowds” translates afwāj, meaning groups after groups, waves upon waves of people surging forward. The imagery is that of a vast, unstoppable human tide rising from the earth and moving towards the plain of gathering. Every human being who ever lived will be present, from Adam to the last child born before the Hour. The verse brings the abstract idea of the Day of Decision into sharp sensory focus. The blowing of the Trumpet is a sound that will penetrate every corner of the universe, and it is a sound that no one can ignore. The coming forth in crowds shows that the resurrection is not a private, individual event; it is a public, universal, overwhelming spectacle. The disbelievers who used to ask mockingly, “When will this promise come to pass?” will see it with their own eyes. The crowds will include them as well, but they will be coming forth in terror, not in honour. The verse also connects back to the earlier mention of pairs and the creation of human beings. The One who created people in pairs and scattered them across the earth is also the One who can gather them all in one place in a single moment. The apparent dispersion of humanity in life and in death is no obstacle to divine power. The scene is now set for the cosmic upheaval that will accompany this resurrection, which the next two verses will portray in startling language. The surah is moving quickly from the calm contemplation of creation’s signs to the thunderous reality of the end of the world.
19 | The sky will be ˹split˺ open, becoming ˹many˺ gates, | As the dead rise from their graves, the very fabric of the universe begins to dissolve: “The sky will be split open, becoming many gates.” The sky, which was earlier described as one of the seven mighty heavens built securely above, will now be rent asunder. The passive verb indicates that this opening is not a gentle unfastening but a violent tearing apart. The solid, protective canopy that humanity has always known will break into fragments. The image of the sky becoming “many gates” is both majestic and terrifying. Gates imply openings through which beings can pass. Many commentators explain that these gates will be the paths through which the angels descend, as mentioned later in verse 38 of the same surah, where the Spirit and the angels stand in ranks. The sky that once seemed like an impenetrable ceiling will become a series of portals, revealing the higher realms and making the presence of the angels unmistakably visible to all. The psychological impact on human beings who spent their lives oblivious to the unseen will be devastating. They will see what they denied and will have no place to hide. The verse also recalls other Qur’anic descriptions of the Day of Judgement, such as “When the sky is split asunder” (84:1) and “And the heaven will be opened, becoming gates” (78:19). The consistent theme is that the destruction of the physical universe is not an act of chaos but a transformation that serves the purpose of judgement. The sky is split to allow the descent of the divine command and the arrival of the angels who will carry out their Lord’s decree. For the deniers, the splitting of the sky is the first sign that their world, which they thought was eternal and self-sufficient, is unravelling before their eyes. The very sky they looked at while mocking the news of the Hereafter will become a scene of overwhelming power. The question from the beginning of the surah—what are they asking about?—finds its visual answer: they were asking about this Day when the sky itself will shatter into gates.
20 | and the mountains will be blown away, becoming ˹like˺ a mirage. | The transformation of the earth’s great anchors completes the scene of cosmic collapse: “and the mountains will be blown away, becoming like a mirage.” Earlier in the surah, the mountains were described as pegs that stabilise the earth. Now their apparent solidity is revealed to be entirely dependent on Allah’s command. The verb “blown away” (suyyirat) means they will be set in motion, uprooted and scattered. Other verses add that they will be crushed, ground to powder and then blown by the wind until no trace of them remains. After this complete disintegration, they will become “like a mirage.” A mirage, sarāb, is the optical illusion in the desert where distant sand appears as shimmering water. When a thirsty traveller approaches, he finds nothing. The mountains, which people trusted as the most permanent features of the landscape, will dissolve into nothingness in exactly that way. One moment they will be there, solid and immense; the next, they will be dust particles vanishing into the air, leaving behind a perfectly level plain. The image is devastating for those who placed their sense of security in the material world. The disbelievers of Mecca were surrounded by mountains that had stood for millennia; they could not imagine them ever disappearing. By stating that these mountains will become like a mirage, the verse shatters the illusion of permanence that attaches people to this world. It also brings the argument for the resurrection full circle. The One who anchored the mountains as pegs can pull those pegs out. The earth that was smoothed like a bed will be turned into a completely flat arena for judgement. There is a stark contrast with the dense orchards mentioned earlier. The orchards were a picture of lush, life-filled stability; the mirage is a picture of barren emptiness. The verse marks the end of the world as the Meccans knew it and prepares the way for the next section, which will describe the dreadful destination that awaits the transgressors. The surah’s architecture is precise: from the question about the news, to the warning, to the signs of creation, and now to the overwhelming reality of the Day that will make everything clear.
21 | Indeed, Hell is lying in ambush | After the sky splits into gates and the mountains dissolve into a mirage, the surah reveals what awaits the deniers beyond the destruction: “Indeed, Hell is lying in ambush.” The Arabic word mirṣād comes from the root raṣada, meaning to watch, to lie in wait, to be in a place of observation. Hell is not merely a passive pit; it is a sentinel, a vigilant observer stationed to seize its designated inhabitants. The image is deeply unsettling: the transgressors think they are free, yet a hunter is tracking them, and it will not miss its target. Some early commentators said mirṣād is a place where the angels appointed over Hell watch for its inmates, a bridge or a crossing point across which all must pass, with the sinners being caught there. The verse connects powerfully to the opening of the surah. The Quraysh were carelessly asking one another about the momentous news, never imagining that the thing they mocked was already prepared and waiting. The certainty of the statement—Hell “is” lying in ambush, in the present tense—emphasises that its existence is not dependent on their belief. It is already created, already standing ready for the Day it will be filled. The Prophet ﷺ said that after the believers are saved, Hell will be brought forth on the Day of Resurrection with seventy thousand reins, each held by seventy thousand angels. This hadith reinforces the idea of Hell being pulled into position as if it were a living, obedient creature awaiting its command. The lesson for every human being is clear: no one can outrun divine justice. In the hustle of daily life, people may forget the Hereafter, but the Hereafter does not forget them. Just as the earth was smoothed like a bed to provide comfort, Hell has been prepared to provide retribution, and it will not fail to deliver. The warning is not merely to frighten but to awaken the heart to the reality that deeds have destinations, and those destinations are closer than we think.
22 | as a home for the transgressors, | The ambush has a purpose: Hell is “a home for the transgressors.” The word translated as “home” is ma’āb, which literally means a place of return. Human beings are all on a journey back to their Lord, as the surah will later say. For the transgressors, their return is not a welcoming embrace but a terrifying incarceration. The term “transgressors” (ṭāghīn) refers to those who exceed all bounds, who rebel against Allah, who mock His signs and oppress others. The Quraysh leaders who persecuted the early Muslims were prime examples. This verse declares that the same people who enjoyed status, wealth and power in Mecca will find their final dwelling in the blazing Fire. The use of “home” is deeply ironic. A home is a place of rest, familiarity and safety—the earth was a bed and sleep was rest—but for the transgressors, their eternal home will contain none of that comfort. Instead of the cool shade of orchards, they will have scorching flames. Instead of the gentle night as a cover, they will have unending exposure to torment. The verse forms the first link in a chain of contrasts with the later description of Paradise. The righteous will have salvation as their home, gardens and vineyards, while the transgressors get Hell as their home. The idea of “return” also hints at the original covenant when souls were taken from Adam’s loins and testified that Allah is their Lord. Those who honoured that covenant will return to mercy; those who broke it will return to this ambush. The justice in this allocation is perfect. No one is sent to Hell arbitrarily; it is the natural consequence of a life spent transgressing the limits set by the One who gave that life. The verse invites deep self-reflection: what have we made our home in this world, and what will our home be in the next?
23 | where they will remain for ˹endless˺ ages. | The duration of this stay is now made explicit: “where they will remain for endless ages.” The Arabic term aḥqāb is the plural of ḥuqub, which generally means a long period of time, often translated as “ages.” The question of whether this implies an eventual end to the punishment of the disbelievers has been discussed by scholars. The overwhelming majority view of Ahl as-Sunnah is that Hell is eternal for those who die upon disbelief, based on numerous clear verses such as “they will abide therein forever” (4:169) and “nor will its torment be lightened for them” (2:162). The plural aḥqāb in this verse is understood to mean ages that follow one another endlessly, each age succeeded by another of the same kind, without termination. The Arabs would say “I will stay for ages” meaning forever. Alternatively, some early commentators held that aḥqāb refers to specific time periods after which the punishment may change in nature, but the stay itself remains everlasting for the disbelievers. The verse’s primary function is to crush any false hope that Hell is a temporary refining fire from which everyone will eventually be released. The Meccan pagans, who thought of the world in terms of transient seasons and years, are told that what awaits them is not a short, bearable sentence but an unceasing reality. The same power that set the sun as a shining lamp and appointed the alternation of night and day with precise measure has also appointed the measure of punishment. There is no escape, no parole, no death to end the suffering. This is the ultimate consequence of denying the Day of Final Decision. For the believer, reflecting on this verse produces a profound sense of urgency to seek Allah’s mercy and avoid the deeds that lead to such a destination. It also reinforces the seriousness with which the Qur’an treats the rejection of faith, serving as a sobering reminder that the human soul is eternal and that its choices in this fleeting life carry everlasting consequences.
24 | There they will not taste any coolness or drink, | The nature of the torment begins to unfold with stark deprivation: “There they will not taste any coolness or drink.” The word “coolness” (bard) refers to any form of cooling relief—a gentle breeze, a cold surface, a shade—and “drink” (sharāb) includes any kind of beverage. In the burning environment of Hell, the absence of these two things is a torture beyond imagination. The Arab of the desert knew that the difference between life and death often came down to a sip of water and a moment of shade. By denying them even a taste of these basic comforts, the verse depicts an existence of unrelenting, scorching agony. The double negation is absolute: they will not taste any coolness, nor any drink. There will be no exceptions, no moments of respite. This is the direct opposite of what the believers will receive, as later verses promise full cups of pure wine and delightful gardens. The punishment fits the crime with precise symmetry. The disbelievers rejected the coolness of faith and the spiritual drink of divine guidance while they were on earth; they turned away from the refreshing revelation that could have quenched their souls’ thirst. So in Hell, their physical thirst will mirror their spiritual bankruptcy. Moreover, this verse reminds us that the blessings we take for granted—a glass of water on a hot day, the shade of a tree—are not owed to us; they are gifts from the Most Compassionate. When those gifts are withdrawn as a form of punishment, the deprivation itself becomes a searing pain. The verse serves as both a warning to the heedless and an invitation to the thankful to appreciate and use Allah’s blessings in ways that lead to eternal coolness and the drink of Paradise.
25 | except boiling water and ˹oozing˺ pus— | The only things available to the inmates of Hell are described with horrifying precision: “except boiling water and oozing pus.” The boiling water, ḥamīm, is water heated to its extreme, so hot that it scalds faces, tears intestines apart and brings no relief. Hadith literature describes how the people of Hell will be made to drink this boiling water, and when they bring it near their faces, the skin will fall off. The oozing pus, ghassāq, is a foul, thick fluid that oozes from the bodies of the tortured, a mixture of sweat, tears, blood and wounds. Other interpretations say it is the discharge that flows from the skins of the disbelievers as they burn. It is nauseating and utterly repulsive. The verse uses the word “except” to indicate that these are the only alternatives to the coolness and drink that were denied. There is nothing else. Earlier in the surah, the signs of Allah’s mercy included pouring rain that brought forth grain, plants and dense orchards. The water sent down from the clouds was pure, life-giving and abundant. The disbelievers enjoyed that water, yet they refused to worship the One who sent it. Now the water they are given is transformed into an instrument of torture. The contrast is deliberate and devastating. The boiling water represents the extreme opposite of the cool, refreshing rain; the oozing pus represents the complete corruption of what was once pure nourishment. This is a fitting reward, as the next verse will state, because they chose filth in their beliefs and actions. The verse leaves no room for imagining that Hell might contain some hidden comfort; its only provisions are torment. This drives home the urgency of repentance and the incomparable value of even the smallest mercy we enjoy in this world.
26 | a fitting reward. | Three words in English, yet they carry the weight of perfect divine justice: “a fitting reward.” The Arabic phrase jazā’an wifāqā means a recompense that is exactly proportionate, perfectly matched to the deeds. There is no excess punishment, nor is there any shortcoming. What the disbelievers receive is precisely what they earned. Their crime was not a small one: they rejected the Lord of the heavens and the earth, mocked His messengers, denied the resurrection, and lived as if they would never be called to account. The punishment of boiling water and pus is the precise mirror of their spiritual state—a state that was boiling with arrogance and oozing with the corruption of disbelief. The verse simultaneously refutes two potential misconceptions: that Allah punishes out of mere wrath or arbitrary vengeance, and that the punishment might be unfair. On the contrary, it is exact justice. This principle is foundational in Islam. Every soul will be recompensed for what it did, and no one will be wronged even by the weight of a mustard seed. The earlier signs of creation all demonstrated Allah’s wisdom, power and orderly arrangement. That same wisdom dictates that the moral realm also operates on the principle of consequence. The One who placed the mountains as pegs and the sun as a lamp does not leave human actions without outcome. The phrase also serves as a comfort to the oppressed believers who suffered at the hands of the mockers. They can trust that the justice they were denied in this world will be fulfilled in the most exact manner. Nothing is forgotten, no insult is overlooked, and the fitting reward will be dispensed with absolute precision. This verse is thus a call to trust in Allah’s judgement and to recognise that every action, good or bad, has a weight that will be felt on the Day when the scales are set.
27 | For they never expected any reckoning, | The surah now explains the root cause that made them deserving of such a reward: “For they never expected any reckoning.” The word ḥisāb means taking account, a careful calculation of all deeds. These people lived their entire lives as if no such accounting would ever take place. Their disbelief in the resurrection was not a passive ignorance; it was an active, willed rejection of the possibility of being held answerable. Because they never expected a reckoning, they felt free to transgress without restraint. They indulged their desires, cheated in trade, oppressed the weak and mocked the prophets, all under the false assumption that death was the end and that no one would ever call them to explain what they had done. This verse strikes at the heart of moral psychology. Belief in the reckoning is the strongest deterrent against sin. When that belief is removed, the door to all kinds of corruption swings wide open. The disbelievers of Mecca were not irreligious in the sense of acknowledging no god; they believed in a creator, but they deliberately pushed away the thought of meeting Him and being judged by Him. The Qur’an repeatedly diagnoses their condition: they loved the fleeting world and ignored the heavy Day. The phrase “never expected” implies they did not even consider it a remote possibility; they planned their lives entirely for the present. The lesson for believers is profound. A Muslim is someone who lives with the constant awareness that every deed is being recorded and will one day be displayed. This expectation reshapes conduct, instils humility and fosters a sense of responsibility that transforms society. The verse also connects back to the opening of the surah: their casual questioning about the momentous news was evidence that they did not truly expect any reckoning. That lack of expectation sealed their fate.
28 | and totally rejected Our signs. | Not satisfied with mere heedlessness, the disbelievers took an active stance: “and totally rejected Our signs.” The emphasis on “totally” indicates that their rejection was thorough, vehement and persisted until the end. The “signs” encompass everything that pointed to the truth: the verses of the Qur’an recited by the Prophet, the moral teachings, the news of the unseen, and the natural signs mentioned earlier in the surah—the earth as a bed, the mountains as pegs, the seven heavens and the rain. They called the Qur’an magic, poetry or ancient fables, and they refused to reflect on the marvels of creation that surrounded them. This rejection was not based on intellectual difficulty; it was rooted in arrogance and the desire to follow the ways of their forefathers. By coupling this verse with the previous one, the surah shows that their ruin was built on two pillars: a lack of expectation of accountability and an aggressive denial of the evidence. Had they only been heedless, a wake-up call might have sufficed, but when heedlessness hardened into active rejection, the door of mercy was closed. The possessive “Our signs” reminds the reader that these signs belong to Allah; rejecting them is a personal affront to the One who spread the earth and raised the heavens. This explains why the punishment is so severe. It is not merely intellectual error but wilful rebellion against clear truth. The verse also carries a warning for anyone who hears the Qur’an: to treat Allah’s signs with contempt or to dismiss them without sincere reflection is to walk the path of those described here. The contrast with the believers is implicit: the believers accepted the signs, and that acceptance led them to salvation.
29 | And We have everything recorded precisely. | Lest anyone imagine that something might slip through the cracks, the surah adds: “And We have everything recorded precisely.” The word “everything” is absolute. Every deed, word, intention and omission is inscribed in a clear record. The Arabic kitāb refers to the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ) as well as the individual books of deeds that every person will receive on the Day of Judgement. The recording is done with absolute precision (ta‘dād), meaning it is counted, itemised and nothing is left out. The disbelievers used to mock the idea that their long-decayed bones could be resurrected and that some ancient record would hold their deeds. This verse insists that the record is not a metaphor; it is a reality as concrete as the earth they walked on. The recording angels, who accompany every human being, do not sleep nor forget. The One who set the sun and moon with precise calculations and caused grain and plants to grow from the earth is also the One who has perfectly accounted for human actions. This assurance of precise recording serves a dual purpose. For the wrongdoer, it is terrifying: every secret sin, every hidden mockery, every moment of hypocrisy is preserved and will be brought to light. For the believer, it is comforting: no good deed, no suppressed tear, no patient endurance of injustice is lost. The recording is a promise that justice will be complete and that no one’s story will be incomplete on that Day. The verse also ties back to the “fitting reward” mentioned earlier; the reward can only be fitting because the record is exact. The judge never errs, the evidence is never tampered with, and the verdict is always just. In a world where human records are often biased or incomplete, this verse anchors the heart in the certainty of divine precision.
30 | ˹So the deniers will be told,˺ “Taste ˹the punishment˺, for all you will get from Us is more torment.” | The scene reaches a terrifying climax as the deniers are addressed directly: “Taste the punishment, for all you will get from Us is more torment.” The command “Taste” is a bitter reversal of the blessings they once enjoyed. They tasted the cool water, the delicious fruits and the pleasures of life, but they failed to taste the sweetness of faith. Now they are ordered to taste what they prepared for themselves. The phrase “all you will get from Us is more torment” translates the Arabic fa-lan nazīdakum illā ‘adhābā, which means We will never increase you in anything except punishment. There is no hope of reduction, no possibility of a lightening, no eventual comfort. The punishment is not static; it increases and intensifies. Scholars explain this increase in various ways. Some say the gates of Hell are closed upon them, and their torment grows deeper with each passing moment as the flames are stoked. Others say that as they beg for relief, their only answer is an increase in suffering. The psychological dimension is especially cruel: the knowledge that things will never get better, that every plea is met with more pain, is itself a torment that compounds the physical agony. The verse echoes the earlier warning in verses 4 and 5: “But no! They will come to know. Again, no! They will come to know.” Now that knowledge has arrived, and it is a knowledge that brings no benefit, only remorse. The surah is drawing the first major section to a close. The momentous news that was disputed has now been unveiled in its horrifying reality. The next verses will turn to the other side of the news: the reward of the righteous. This transition from warning to glad tidings follows the Qur’anic pattern of pairing threat with promise, fear with hope, so that the human heart remains balanced between the two.
31 | Indeed, the righteous will have salvation— | With a single word, the surah pivots from the blazing descriptions of Hell to the serene promise of Paradise: “Indeed, the righteous will have salvation.” The Arabic mafāz is a place of success, victory and safety. It comes from the root fawz, which means to escape danger and attain what one desires. The righteous (muttaqīn) are those who shielded themselves with taqwā—constant God-consciousness, obedience and avoidance of sin. They lived in the world with the reckoning always in mind, and now their mindfulness has borne its fruit. The word “salvation” encompasses everything positive: they are saved from the Fire, they enter the Gardens, and they achieve the ultimate success of Allah’s pleasure. The contrast with the previous verses could not be starker. Where Hell was lying in ambush for the transgressors, salvation is waiting for the righteous. Where the disbelievers tasted boiling water, the righteous will taste pure wine. Where the disbelievers will remain for endless ages in torment, the righteous will remain forever in bliss. This verse also brings relief to the listener after the intense buildup of warning. The Qur’an always balances justice with mercy, fear with hope. The surah addressed the mockers and warners of the Prophet; now it addresses those who believed him despite the persecution. For them, the news of the momentous day was not a cause for scoffing but a source of comfort, because they knew that their suffering was not in vain and that a magnificent reward awaited them. The verse also defines the essential quality of those who attain salvation: taqwā. It is not lineage, wealth or worldly status that matters on that Day, but the fear of Allah and the righteousness that flows from it. This lays the groundwork for the detailed blessings that follow.
32 | Gardens, vineyards, | The first glimpse of the reward opens with a scene of lush abundance: “Gardens, vineyards.” The word ḥadā’iq refers to enclosed, cultivated gardens filled with trees and plants, a vision of perfect order and beauty. Unlike the dense orchards of the world mentioned in verse 16, which are subject to seasons, pests and decay, these gardens are eternal and flawless. The specific mention of “vineyards” (a‘nāb) highlights grapes, which were among the most cherished fruits in the Arabian peninsula, prized for their sweetness, their shade and the pure drink derived from them. The pairing of gardens with vineyards suggests a landscape of intertwined orchards, where every step reveals a new delight for the eyes and the palate. This verse also subtly corrects the pagan worldview. The Meccans loved their gardens and vineyards, and they attributed their prosperity to their idols or to their own efforts. Now they are told that the real, everlasting gardens belong to the righteous, and they are the gift of the Lord who created the heavens and the earth. The description is not merely material; it is deeply emotional. The gardens are a homecoming, a return to a state of perfect provision and peace that the human soul instinctively longs for. The righteous are not given a bare minimum reward; they are honoured with the finest, most delightful imagery the human mind can appreciate. Every element of Paradise is chosen to exceed expectations and to convey a sense of safety, joy and belonging. The verse thus begins to paint a picture that will be filled in with companions, drinks and perfect social harmony in the verses to come.
33 | and full-bosomed maidens of equal age, | Among the delights of Paradise are “full-bosomed maidens of equal age.” The Arabic kawā‘ib refers to maidens whose breasts are firm and fully developed, indicating youthful beauty and vitality. The word atrāb means they are of one age, likely in the prime of youth, and that they are perfectly matched to one another and to their spouses, creating an atmosphere of harmony and mutual delight. The houris are created by Allah specifically for Paradise; they are not human women who lived on earth, though believing women who enter Paradise will themselves be perfected in beauty and rank above the houris according to many scholars. The Qur’an describes the houris elsewhere as being pure, untouched by any man or jinn, with eyes of stunning beauty, like preserved pearls. The mention of such a reward has been the subject of much criticism from those who view it as a base, carnal incentive. This criticism misses the essence of the Qur’anic portrayal. Paradise is a realm where every permissible human longing, purified of all impurity, jealousy and harm, is fulfilled to the utmost. The companionship of beautiful, loving spouses is not presented as a crude physical pleasure but as an integral part of complete human contentment and joy. The believers who struggled to keep themselves chaste in a society filled with temptation are rewarded with companions whose beauty and character bring nothing but peace and delight. The emphasis on equal age and harmony underscores that this is a social bliss free from the tensions, ageing and disappointments of earthly relationships. The verse thus extends the picture of Paradise from the physical environment to the social and emotional environment, all of which combine to create the “salvation” that the righteous have won.
34 | and full cups ˹of pure wine˺, | The blessings flow on: “and full cups of pure wine.” The cups (ka’s) are filled to the brim, never empty, always ready to delight. The wine of Paradise is described as pure, meaning it is free from the negative qualities of earthly wine. It does not cause intoxication, headaches, or loss of reason. It does not lead to foul speech or quarrelling, as the next verse will explicitly state. The word dihāq indicates that the cups are filled to the top and perfectly prepared. In earthly banquets, wine might run out or lose its quality; in Paradise, the supply is infinite and the taste is ever fresh. The mention of wine is particularly significant in the context of a surah revealed in Mecca, where the prohibition of intoxicants had not yet been legislated but where the Arabs were well aware of the social ills caused by drinking. By describing a pure wine that brings only pleasure and no harm, the Qur’an presents an ideal that transcends the flawed pleasures of the world. The believers who abstain from intoxicants in this life, or who avoided the excesses of the pagan drinking culture, are promised a drink that is pure and blessed. The cups are part of a banquet scene, echoing the gardens and vineyards, now completed with the finest drink. The atmosphere is one of abundance, celebration and profound gratitude. Every sip will be a reminder of the Lord’s generosity, and the joy will not be dulled by any impurity. This verse also serves as a contrast to the boiling water and pus that the deniers are given to drink. The two drinks represent the two ultimate destinations: one is a reward of purity, the other a punishment of filth. The righteous taste the pure wine of nearness to their Lord; the wicked taste the scalding water of His justice.
35 | never to hear any idle talk or lying therein— | The social environment of Paradise is now perfected with a guarantee of pure speech: “never to hear any idle talk or lying therein.” The word laghw refers to vain, empty, offensive or nonsensical talk—the kind of chatter that fills worldly gatherings with backbiting, gossip, slander and pointless arguments. Kidhdhāb is intensive, meaning persistent lying, falsehood and denial. In Paradise, such speech simply does not exist. The ears of the righteous will be completely protected from anything that could disturb their peace. This is a profound blessing, especially when we recall the environment in which the early Muslims lived. In Mecca, the believers were constantly subjected to the mockery, lies and slander of the disbelievers. They were called madmen, sorcerers and liars. The verbal abuse was relentless. Here, Allah promises them an abode where no such word will ever reach their hearing. The only sounds will be words of peace, greeting and praise. The verse also points to the inner purity of the inhabitants of Paradise. In the world, even good people sometimes slip into idle or harmful speech, but those who enter Paradise will have been purified of such flaws. Their hearts will be clean, and their tongues will speak only what is beautiful and true. The absence of lying is especially significant, because the disbelievers were condemned for totally rejecting Allah’s signs and calling them lies. In Paradise, falsehood is banished. The entire atmosphere is one of truth, serenity and mutual love. This verse thus completes the picture of a perfect society where the environment, the companionship and the conversation all work together to create unending happiness.
36 | a ˹fitting˺ reward as a generous gift from your Lord, | The nature of the reward is now summed up beautifully: “a fitting reward as a generous gift from your Lord.” The Arabic word for “fitting reward” is jazā’, recompense that matches the deeds. The righteous earned this reward through their faith and good actions. But it is also described as ‘aṭā’an ḥisābā, a generous gift that is ample, sufficient and given out of pure grace. The pairing of “reward” and “gift” is profoundly important. No human being can possibly do enough good to earn Paradise on deeds alone. The Prophet ﷺ himself said that no one enters Paradise by their deeds but by Allah’s mercy. Yet Allah, in His infinite generosity, treats the deeds of the righteous as if they were sufficient and then multiplies the reward beyond all calculation. So the blessing is both something deserved by divine promise and something given freely by divine grace. The phrase “from your Lord” personalises the gift. It is not from a distant, impersonal force but from the Rabb, the Lord who nurtured them, guided them, and now welcomes them home. This direct address touches the heart of the Prophet and every believer who reads the verse. It is as if each one is being told individually: this is your Lord’s gift to you. The word ḥisābā also carries a nuance of being precisely calculated to satisfy every desire. Nothing is lacking; the gift is tailored perfectly to the recipient. The verse thus combines the ideas of justice, mercy and intimate care. It brings the description of the reward to a warm, personal conclusion before the surah turns to magnify the Giver Himself.
37 | the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, the Most Compassionate. No one will dare speak to Him | The surah now focuses on the majesty of the Giver: “the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, the Most Compassionate. No one will dare speak to Him.” The reward came from “your Lord,” and now that Lord is described in terms that fill the heart with awe and love. He is the Rabb of all existence, the sovereign owner of every realm that was mentioned in the earlier signs—the seven mighty heavens, the smoothed earth, the mountains, the sun, the clouds and all that lies between them. The same Lord who spread the earth like a bed and sent down pouring rain is the One who gives Paradise to the righteous. The name “the Most Compassionate” (ar-Raḥmān) is placed here with great effect. His mercy is the source of all the blessings just described. Yet immediately the verse balances this mercy with a statement of overwhelming authority: “No one will dare speak to Him.” This refers to the Day of Resurrection. On that Day, all creation will stand in complete silence before the Lord. The kings, the tyrants, the eloquent orators, the argumentative deniers—all will be struck dumb with awe. The permission to speak will not be granted except to those whom He wills. This silence is a direct reversal of the worldly condition where people spoke about Allah without knowledge, argued about the momentous news, and raised their voices in denial. On that Day, the courtroom of the Almighty will be utterly still. The verse prepares the listener for the next verse, which will describe the ranks of the angels and the strict conditions under which speech is allowed. It is a breathtaking pause that reorients the heart from the pleasures of Paradise to the majesty of the One who grants them.
38 | on the Day the ˹holy˺ spirit and the angels will stand in ranks. None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true. | The scene of silence is filled in with an image of perfect order: “on the Day the holy spirit and the angels will stand in ranks.” The “holy spirit” (ar-Rūḥ) according to the vast majority of commentators refers to Jibrīl, the archangel of revelation, who holds an exceptionally high rank. He and all the angels will be arrayed in rows, standing in disciplined, humble submission. The angels, who are beings of immense power and who never disobey Allah, will be utterly motionless and silent, awaiting the command of their Lord. If the greatest angels stand in such awe, how should human beings, weak and sinful, imagine their own standing on that Day? The verse then states the sole exception to the universal silence: “None will talk, except those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are true.” Speech will be a rare privilege, granted only to those whom Allah permits. The phrase “whose words are true” translates ṣawāb, meaning correct, right and truthful. On that Day, no falsehood will be uttered. The prophets will speak to intercede for their nations, the angels will speak to carry out commands, and the believers may be permitted to speak in their defence after permission is given. The hadith of intercession describes how the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ will be granted the station of praise and will be the first to speak and intercede after being given permission. This verse magnificently connects to the earlier themes of the surah: the people who once “disagreed” and “totally rejected Our signs” will find their voices silenced, while those who spoke truth in the world and were silenced by oppression will be given the honour of speaking truth before the Lord. The Compassionate’s permission is the gateway, and truth is the only currency. The scene underscores the absolute sovereignty of Allah and the complete accountability of every soul.
39 | That Day is the ˹ultimate˺ truth. So let whoever wills take the path leading back to their Lord. | After the cosmic courtroom scene, the surah declares: “That Day is the ultimate truth.” The Arabic al-ḥāqqah is an intensive form, meaning the sure reality, the inevitable event that will come to pass without doubt. The whole surah has been building up to this assertion. The momentous news that the Quraysh were disputing is this Day, and it is nothing less than the absolute truth. All the signs in creation, all the warnings and promises, point to it. With this foundation firmly laid, the verse issues a direct call to action: “So let whoever wills take the path leading back to their Lord.” The word “path” (sabīl) is a way that is clear and well-marked. The path leading back to the Lord is the path of Islam—faith in Allah, His messengers, His books, the Last Day, and righteous deeds. The phrase “whoever wills” indicates that while the truth has been made plain, the choice remains with the individual. Allah does not compel belief; He guides those who sincerely seek and opens the door for every repentant soul. The path is described as “leading back to their Lord” because the human being’s ultimate journey is one of return. We came from Allah, and to Him we shall return. The wise person is the one who takes the path of return willingly, in obedience and love, rather than being dragged back on the Day of Judgement in chains of regret. This verse is the heart of the surah’s message. It transforms the terrifying descriptions of the Day from mere information into a personal call. Every listener is invited to become one of the righteous who will have salvation, to take the path now, before the Trumpet is blown and the sky splits into gates. It is an invitation wrapped in a warning, full of hope and urgency.
40 | Indeed, We have warned you of an imminent punishment—the Day every person will see ˹the consequences of˺ what their hands have done, and the disbelievers will cry, “I wish I were dust.” | The surah concludes with a final, piercing warning: “Indeed, We have warned you of an imminent punishment.” The punishment is described as “imminent” (qarīb) because, relative to the eternal afterlife, the duration of this world is very short, or because the individual’s death, which marks the beginning of the reckoning, could occur at any moment. For the Quraysh, there was also the proximate warning of worldly defeat if they persisted in their hostility. The warning is followed by the scene of the Day when “every person will see the consequences of what their hands have done.” This is the ultimate moment of clarity. Every deed, good or bad, will be presented before its doer. The righteous will see their good deeds multiplied and will rejoice. The disbelievers will see their evil deeds laid bare and will be overcome with a regret so profound that they will cry, “I wish I were dust.” Dust is the ultimate symbol of insignificance, lifelessness and oblivion. The disbelievers will wish they had never been created as humans, that they had remained inert matter, never resurrected, never judged. They will envy the dust because dust is not held accountable. The animals, who will be turned to dust after justice is served between them, will have an end, but the disbelievers will face an unending future of torment. This final cry is the tragic echo of the casual talk with which the surah began. The people who once asked one another mockingly about the momentous news now wish they could dissolve into the earth they once walked upon so carelessly. The surah thus returns to its opening with a perfect, chilling symmetry. The news is no longer a matter of debate; it is an experienced, regretted reality. For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivation to fill one’s hands with deeds that will bring joy on that Day, so that one never has to utter the words of those who wished they were dust.
