1 | Indeed, ˹it is˺ We ˹Who˺ sent this ˹Quran˺ down on the Night of Glory. | The surah opens with a declaration of immense majesty and certainty: “Indeed, it is We Who sent this Quran down on the Night of Glory.” The use of the emphatic particle “indeed” leaves no room for doubt. The pronoun “We” is the majestic plural, reflecting the grandeur of Allah and the magnitude of the act. It is not a single angel or a limited force that brought the Quran; it is Allah Himself, in His supreme authority, who sent it down. The verb “sent down” translates the Arabic “anzalnā,” which implies a descent from a high, preserved realm to the lower world. This descent is not a simple transfer of words; it is a cosmic event that connected the eternal speech of Allah with the temporal earth. The object “this Quran” points to the very book that the Prophet was reciting, the living miracle that was changing hearts and shaking the foundations of ignorance. The phrase “on the Night of Glory” identifies the specific time when this momentous descent began. The Night of Glory, or Laylat al-Qadr, is the night when the first verses of Surah Al-‘Alaq were revealed in the cave of Hira, and it is also the night when, according to many scholars, the entire Quran was brought down from the Preserved Tablet to the lowest heaven in a single, complete descent, and then revealed gradually over twenty-three years. This double descent magnifies the night’s significance. The word “Qadr” carries multiple meanings: it denotes honour and glory, because the night itself is glorious due to the revelation; it denotes decree, because on this night the divine wisdom ordains the affairs of the coming year; and it denotes constriction, because the earth is constricted by the sheer number of angels descending upon it. The opening verse thus sets the stage for a surah that is entirely devoted to celebrating the night that changed human history. It connects directly to the previous surah, Al-‘Alaq, which began with the command “Read,” for this is the night that command was first issued. The believer reciting this verse is invited to reflect that the Quran in their hands is not a human composition but a divine sending, rooted in a night of unparalleled majesty, a night that began the journey of light from the heavens to the heart of the Prophet and from there to all humanity.
2 | And what will make you realize what the Night of Glory is? | The surah now employs the powerful Quranic device of magnification: “And what will make you realize what the Night of Glory is?” This rhetorical question is not a request for information; it is a declaration that the true nature of this night is beyond ordinary human comprehension. The formula “what will make you realize” is used in several places in the Quran when the subject is so immense, so profound, that mere words cannot fully capture its reality. The listener’s attention is arrested, and a sense of awe is created. The question implies that the human mind, with all its concepts of time and worth, cannot properly weigh the value of Laylat al-Qadr. Even the Prophet, to whom the Quran was revealed, is addressed through this question, teaching him and his followers that the night’s worth is a divine secret whose full glory will only be experienced by those who witness it with faith and worship. The question also serves as a transition to the answer that follows in the next verse, but the answer itself is a comparison that stretches the imagination. The very word “Qadr” is repeated, and the question invites the heart to ponder its depths. Is it the Night of Power because of the descent of the angels? Is it the Night of Decree because of the writing of destinies? Is it the Night of Honour because of the honour bestowed upon the Quran and upon the Ummah? All these meanings blend into a reality that is too great for a single definition. The question also stirs a longing in the believer. It says: you may think you know what this night is, but there is a dimension of its majesty that you have not yet grasped. This stirs a desire to seek the night, to stay awake in its hours, and to approach it not with casual familiarity but with a trembling hope to taste a fraction of its hidden glory. Thus, the verse is both a humbling acknowledgment of human limitation and a thrilling invitation to discover the immeasurable gift that the Night of Glory truly is.
3 | The Night of Glory is better than a thousand months. | With this verse, the surah unveils a measure that the human mind can begin to grasp: “The Night of Glory is better than a thousand months.” The number “a thousand months” represents an extraordinary span of time—over eighty-three years, a full human lifetime and more. Worship performed during this single night surpasses in value the worship of a person who lived for eighty-three continuous years in devotion, had the person not reached that night. This is an act of sheer divine generosity, a multiplication of reward that defies any economic logic of effort and recompense. The scholars explain that this gift was specifically granted to the Ummah of Muhammad. The Prophet was shown the lifespans of previous nations, some of whom lived for hundreds of years, accumulating great stores of righteous deeds. He felt a longing that his followers, whose average lifespans are much shorter, might not be able to compete. Allah, in His mercy, granted them Laylat al-Qadr, a night that compensates for the shortness of their lives and gives them the opportunity to surpass even those who lived for centuries. The verse also uses the comparative “better” rather than “equal to,” indicating that the night is not just equivalent to a thousand months of worship but actually superior to them in quality, in the intensity of spiritual experience, and in the transformative power of its peace and angelic presence. It is better not only in the quantity of reward but in the very nature of the connection it forges between the worshipper and the Lord. The verse specifies a number, but the number is meant to convey an idea of vastness, not a limit. Some scholars note that the indefinite “a thousand months” may imply a very great many months, and the actual value could be far more. For the believer, this verse is a wellspring of hope and motivation. It means that no matter how many years one has wasted in heedlessness, a single night of sincere worship can outpace decades of neglect. It also instills a sense of urgency: if a night of such value exists, missing it is a loss greater than missing a lifetime.
4 | That night the angels and the ˹holy˺ spirit descend, by the permission of their Lord, for every ˹decreed˺ matter. | The surah now paints the scene of what actually happens on that glorious night: “That night the angels and the holy spirit descend, by the permission of their Lord, for every decreed matter.” The descent of the angels is an event of cosmic scale. Angels are beings of light, created for worship and obedience, and they fill the heavens and the earth. On Laylat al-Qadr, their descent is particularly intense, a special expedition of mercy. They come down in multitudes so vast that the earth becomes constricted with their presence. The mention of “the holy spirit” refers specifically to Jibril, the archangel of revelation, who is given this title as an honour. His descent is singled out because of his unique role in bringing the Quran to the Prophet, and his presence on this night reaffirms the connection between the heavenly scripture and the earthly community. The phrase “by the permission of their Lord” emphasizes that even these mighty beings act solely under divine command; they do not descend of their own accord but are sent by Allah’s authority. This descent is purposeful: “for every decreed matter.” The scholars of tafsir explain that on this night, the divine decrees for the coming year are brought down from the Preserved Tablet to the scribes among the angels. Matters of life and death, provision and hardship, guidance and misguidance—all that will occur from this night until the next Laylat al-Qadr—are written and entrusted to the angels to execute. The word “decreed” connects directly to the name “al-Qadr,” unveiling one of its profound meanings. The angels carry these scrolls of destiny, and as they pass by the believers engaged in worship, they greet them with peace, register their presence, and seek forgiveness for them. The night is thus a nexus point where heaven touches earth, where the unseen order becomes almost palpable, and where the worshipper’s heart can sense the stirring of the angelic hosts. This verse transforms the abstract concept of a blessed night into a vivid, dynamic reality that fills the imagination and stirs the soul to seek its share.
5 | It is all peace until the break of dawn. | The surah closes with a description of the night’s atmosphere and its duration: “It is all peace until the break of dawn.” The word “peace” translates the Arabic “salām,” a term rich with meaning. It signifies safety from all harm, evil, and Satanic interference. Unlike ordinary nights where devils roam and whispers of sin trouble the hearts, Laylat al-Qadr is a night of absolute security and tranquility. The angels, who are beings of peace, fill the atmosphere, and their greetings of “peace” to the believers create an environment of spiritual serenity. Every moment of the night, from sunset to the first light of dawn, is encompassed by this peace. The word “all” indicates a completeness; there is no interruption, no moment of disturbance. It is also a night of peace because the divine decrees for the coming year are those of justice, wisdom, and mercy, and those who worship on this night are granted an inner peace that remains long after the dawn. The phrase “until the break of dawn” marks the endpoint of this blessed window. The night begins at sunset and ends when the first rays of dawn appear, and every second within that span is saturated with peace and potential reward. The believer is thus urged to fill the entire night with worship, not to let a single hour pass in heedlessness, because the peace is continuous and the opportunity once lost will not return for another year. The mention of dawn also symbolizes the end of darkness and the arrival of light, mirroring the spiritual transformation that a soul undergoes when it has truly experienced Laylat al-Qadr: it emerges from the night into a new dawn of faith and nearness. The surah thus concludes on a note of gentle, hopeful calm, leaving the believer with a taste of the night’s sweetness and a determination to seek it, cherish it, and immerse themselves in its all-encompassing peace.
