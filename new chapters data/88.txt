1 | Has the news of the Overwhelming Event reached you ˹O Prophet˺? | The sūrah opens with a rhetorical question that immediately seizes the attention of the listener and fills the heart with a sense of impending gravity: “Has the news of the Overwhelming Event reached you O Prophet?” The Arabic hal atāka ḥadīthu al-ghāshiyah uses the interrogative particle hal to introduce the question. The phrase “the Overwhelming Event” translates al-ghāshiyah, which is one of the many powerful names of the Day of Resurrection. The root ghashiya means to cover, to envelop, to overwhelm. The Day of Judgment is called al-Ghāshiyah because it will overwhelm all of creation with its terror, its suddenness, and its absolute comprehensiveness. It will cover and envelop humanity so completely that no one can escape its effects. The question “Has it reached you?” is addressed to the Prophet (ﷺ) as a way of drawing him and the listeners into deep contemplation. It is not a question seeking information, for the Prophet has certainly received the news through revelation. Rather, it is a rhetorical device to magnify the importance of the subject and to create a sense of solemn anticipation. It implies: “Do you fully grasp the enormity of this event? Has your heart been truly seized by its reality?” The word ḥadīth, meaning news, story, or narrative, adds the dimension that the Overwhelming Event is not a mere concept but a concrete reality with a narrative that will unfold. It is an event so momentous that it deserves to be told and retold. The Meccan pagans who heard this question were being forced to confront the very Day they so arrogantly denied. They walked through the streets of Mecca, engaged in their trade and their pleasures, heedless of the overwhelming calamity that awaited them. The question is a divine wake-up call, a merciful shake to the soul that slumbers in the false security of the world. For the believer, this opening verse is a reminder to constantly refresh one’s awareness of the Last Day. The news has indeed reached us through the Qur’ān, but has it truly penetrated our hearts? Do we live as those who expect to be overwhelmed by the Day, or have we become numb to its mention? The question invites the listener to pause and internalise the reality before the detailed descriptions begin. It sets the stage perfectly for the vivid contrasts that follow between the faces of the damned and the faces of the blessed. The Overwhelming Event is not a distant myth; it is the singular, all-encompassing reality towards which every living moment is carrying us. The Prophet is being told to take this news with the utmost seriousness and to deliver it to a world that would prefer to ignore it.
2 | On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be downcast, | The answer to the implied question begins immediately with a stark depiction of the state of humanity on that overwhelming Day: “On that Day some faces will be downcast.” The Arabic wujūhun yawma’idhin khāshi‘ah uses the word wujūh (faces) to represent the whole person, as the face is the window to the soul’s condition. The adjective khāshi‘ah comes from khushū‘, which typically means humility, submissiveness, and stillness before a greater power. In this context, however, it describes a humiliating, forced abasement born of terror and regret. These faces are not humbly submitting to Allah out of love and awe, like the faces of the believers in prayer. Rather, they are downcast in utter disgrace, their heads bowed low, their eyes cast down in shame and fear, unable to lift their gaze. This is the face of the disbeliever who strutted arrogantly in the world, who refused to bow to his Creator, and who now finds himself compelled into the lowest state of humiliation. The verse captures the moment of judgment when all the false pride, all the boasting, all the mockery of the believers is stripped away, and only naked, terrified humility remains. The phrase “on that Day” anchors this state to the specific moment of the Overwhelming Event, differentiating it from any worldly humility. The khushū‘ of the damned is a punishment, not a virtue. It is the external manifestation of the inner collapse of their arrogance. This description is particularly potent for the Meccan elite, whose entire identity was built on pride, honour, and face-saving. To be told that their faces will be downcast was a direct assault on their most cherished values. For the believers who were mocked and treated as lowly, this verse was a powerful reassurance: the roles will be completely reversed. The faces that were lifted high in worldly power will be downcast, and the faces that were downcast in humble prayer will be radiant, as the later verses will show. The word khāshi‘ah also implies a sense of exhaustion and brokenness. These are not calm, reflective faces but faces worn down by the weight of the terrible reality they are witnessing. The following verses will add further layers to this picture of misery, detailing the physical and psychological torment that accompanies this downcast state.
3 | ˹totally˺ overburdened, exhausted, | The description of the downcast faces is immediately intensified by two more adjectives in verse three: “totally overburdened, exhausted.” The Arabic ‘āmilatun nāṣibah uses two words that together paint a picture of complete physical and mental breakdown. ‘Āmilah comes from ‘amila, meaning to work, to labour, to toil. Here, it describes faces—and thus people—who are worn out by the immense labour and strain of that Day. They are overburdened with the weight of their sins, with the crushing reality of the judgment, with the chains and fetters that will be placed upon them. Nāṣibah comes from naṣaba, meaning to be exhausted, to be wearied to the point of collapse, to suffer from intense fatigue. The repetition of the meaning through two synonymous adjectives emphasises the absolute, unrelenting exhaustion. Some early commentators also interpreted ‘āmilah as referring to the deeds they performed in the world. They laboured and toiled in this life, but their deeds were not for Allah; they were for the dunyā, and on that Day, they will see those deeds turn to scattered dust while the exhaustion of their effort remains. Others said it refers to the exhausting punishments of Hell, like dragging chains and climbing scorching slopes. All these meanings converge on a state of profound suffering with no relief. The verse teaches a critical lesson: not all labour is rewarded. The disbeliever may work tirelessly in the world, building his empire, amassing his wealth, achieving his ambitions, but if that labour is disconnected from faith and done for the sake of the fleeting world, it results in nothing but eternal exhaustion. In contrast, the believer’s striving, as later verses will show, leads to a state of being “fully pleased” with that striving. The contrast is between fruitless exhaustion and rewarded effort. The word nāṣibah also evokes a sense of continuous, unrelieved hardship. In the world, even the hardest worker can rest at night. In Hell, there is no rest, no sleep, no respite. The face that is downcast is also the face that is utterly spent, drained of all vitality, and yet the suffering continues. This verse adds a visceral, physical dimension to the humiliation of the previous verse. The disbeliever is not just ashamed; he is crushed, depleted, and broken. The next verse will reveal the cause of this exhaustion: the scorching Fire.
4 | burning in a scorching Fire, | The destination of these downcast, exhausted faces is now explicitly named in verse four: “burning in a scorching Fire.” The Arabic taṣlā nāran ḥāmiyah uses the verb taṣlā, which means to enter, to be exposed to, to burn in. It is the same verb used in previous sūrahs for the experience of the damned. The Fire is described as ḥāmiyah, from ḥamiya, meaning to be intensely hot, to be blazing, to be at the peak of its heat. It is a Fire that is not merely warm or even just hot; it is scorching, searing, at a temperature beyond human endurance, a heat that penetrates the very core of the being. The adjective creates an immediate, sensory impression in the mind of the listener, who knows the pain of touching a hot surface. The verse connects the inner state of exhaustion and humiliation directly to the outer punishment of the Fire. The downcast face is also a face that is burning. The Fire is the source of the exhaustion; its heat saps all strength and replaces it with agony. The choice of ḥāmiyah also implies that the Fire is perpetually at this scorching temperature; it never cools, it never lessens. The Meccan pagans, who would sit around their campfires in the cool desert nights, are told that a different fire awaits them, one that will not warm but will burn, one that will not comfort but will torture. The verse also evokes the earlier sūrahs where the People of the Ditch burned the believers in a pit of fire. Their fire was temporary and its victims entered Paradise. This scorching Fire is eternal, and its victims are the tyrants themselves. The believer, reading this, should feel a profound sense of urgency to seek protection from this Fire. The Prophet (ﷺ) frequently prayed, “O Allah, protect me from the Fire.” This verse is a divine warning that the Fire is real, its heat is unimaginable, and its inhabitants will be those who turned away from the reminder. The juxtaposition of the exhausted face with the scorching Fire creates a powerful image: a person utterly drained, yet submerged in a blazing inferno, with no strength to endure and no escape to find. The following verse will add another layer to the torment: the drink they are given.
5 | left to drink from a scalding spring. | The torment of the scorching Fire is compounded by the specific description of the drink provided to its inhabitants in verse five: “left to drink from a scalding spring.” The Arabic tusqā min ‘aynin āniyah uses the verb tusqā, meaning “they will be given to drink,” but the context makes it a drink of punishment, not of refreshment. The source of the drink is an ‘ayn, a spring, but it is not the cool, sweet springs of Paradise. It is an ‘ayn āniyah, a spring whose water has reached the ultimate degree of heat, boiling and scalding beyond imagination. The word āniyah comes from anā, meaning to reach its limit, to be intensely hot. In the blazing desert of Arabia, where cool water was the most precious of commodities, the threat of a scalding spring was a precise and terrible inversion of the deepest human longing. The people of Hell, burning in the scorching Fire, will be consumed by an unimaginable thirst. They will seek water, but the only water provided will intensify their suffering. It will not quench their thirst; it will burn their insides as it goes down. This scalding spring is described elsewhere in the Qur’ān as a boiling fluid, like molten brass, that scalds the faces. The verse illustrates the complete absence of mercy in the abode of punishment. In this world, even the worst prisoners are given water to drink. In Hell, the basic elements of life—water and fire—are turned into instruments of torture. The fire burns from the outside, and the scalding water burns from the inside. This dual punishment is a manifestation of perfect justice. The disbelievers enjoyed the cool drinks of the world and used their God-given strength to commit sins. In Hell, their drink will be scalding and their environment a blaze. The verb “left to drink” implies that they are forced to drink it; there is no alternative. Some commentators mention that they will be driven to the spring like thirsty animals and made to drink. The verse also connects to the earlier surahs that mentioned the sealed wine of Paradise, a drink of pure delight. The contrast is absolute: the believers drink pure wine sealed with musk from a spring called Tasnīm; the disbelievers drink scalding water from a spring of Hell. This contrast is designed to make the listener yearn for the drink of Paradise and fear the drink of Hell. For the believer, every sip of cool water in this world becomes an opportunity to thank Allah and to seek His protection from the scalding spring. The verse is a powerful sensory warning that engages the listener's own experience of thirst to convey a reality beyond experience.
6 | They will have no food except a foul, thorny shrub, | The description of the provision for the people of Hell continues with the specification of their food in verse six: “They will have no food except a foul, thorny shrub.” The Arabic laysa lahum ṭa‘āmun illā min ḍarī‘ uses a strong negation: “there is no food for them at all except from ḍarī‘.” The word ḍarī‘ refers to a specific type of plant that the Arabs knew well. It is a thorny, poisonous, foul-smelling shrub that grows in the desert. Camels and other animals will eat it when there is absolutely no other pasture, but even they derive no real nourishment from it. It is a plant of last resort, a symbol of starvation and deprivation. By saying that the inhabitants of Hell will have no food except this, the verse indicates the extreme degradation of their state. They will be so desperate that they will eat this repulsive shrub, not because it fills them, but because the pangs of hunger will force them to consume anything. The description of ḍarī‘ is a direct challenge to the Meccan pagans who prided themselves on the rich variety of their diets, the dates, the meats, the fine breads. The hellfire will strip them of all those luxuries and reduce them to the level of eating the worst thing they could imagine. The use of a known desert plant makes the warning immediate and visceral. They knew exactly what ḍarī‘ was: something they would never touch except in dire famine. The verse also carries a profound irony. The disbelievers refused to “taste” the sweetness of faith and the spiritual nourishment of the Qur’ān. In return, they will be forced to taste the foulness of ḍarī‘. They denied the good provision of the Hereafter, so their provision will be the worst imaginable. The word “no food except” emphasizes the absence of any alternative. There is no variety, no respite, no hope of a better meal. This is their eternal menu. The verse connects to the earlier surahs that described the blissful food and drink of Paradise. The believers will have abundant fruit, choice meats, and rivers of milk and honey. The disbelievers will have only ḍarī‘. The contrast is designed to motivate the believer to work for the food of Paradise and to warn the denier of the food of Hell. The following verse will explain the specific torment associated with this food, clarifying that it does not even serve the basic function of food.
7 | neither nourishing nor satisfying hunger. | The final verdict on the food of Hell is delivered in verse seven: “neither nourishing nor satisfying hunger.” The Arabic lā yusminu wa lā yughnī min jū‘ uses two precise negations. The first, lā yusminu, means “it does not cause fattening,” “it does not provide any nutrition.” The root samina refers to becoming fat, having flesh, being well-nourished. The ḍarī‘ will not add a single cell of healthy tissue to the body; it will provide no vitamins, no energy, no sustenance. The second negation, lā yughnī min jū‘, means “it does not avail against hunger,” “it does not satisfy or remove hunger in any way.” The root ghanā means to suffice, to avail, to make independent. The pangs of hunger will remain as sharp after eating as they were before. The food of Hell mocks the very concept of food. In this world, even the poorest meal provides some relief from hunger and some minimal nutrition. In Hell, the food is a cruel illusion of eating that only compounds the suffering. The person will eat and eat but remain starving, his body emaciated, his hunger unabated. This is the ultimate punishment of deprivation. The verse highlights the comprehensive nature of divine justice. The disbelievers who ate and drank their fill in the world without thanking Allah, who used their strength for sin, will find that the very basic functions of food are withheld from them. Their bodies, which were instruments of rebellion, will be tormented by a hunger that can never be quelled. The phrase “neither nourishing nor satisfying hunger” also has a psychological dimension. The disbeliever will realise that his suffering is designed; it is not a random accident but a deliberate, tailor-made punishment. The food is there to increase his misery, not to alleviate it. For the believer, this verse is a profound reminder to appreciate the simple blessings of a meal that nourishes and satisfies. Every time we eat and feel the hunger go away, we should thank Allah, for this is a gift that the damned will eternally crave but never receive. The verse also connects to the earlier descriptions of the withered chaff and the reduction of the green pasture to useless debris. The ḍarī‘ is the hellish version of that withered chaff: a dead, useless thing that fills the belly but gives no life. The portrait of the people of Hell is now complete, a picture of downcast, exhausted, burning, scalded, starving humanity. The sūrah is about to pivot to the radiant opposite.
8 | On that Day ˹other˺ faces will be glowing with bliss, | The stark contrast is introduced with the same opening phrase as verse two, creating a powerful parallel structure: “On that Day other faces will be glowing with bliss.” The Arabic wujūhun yawma’idhin nā‘imah uses the word nā‘imah, from the root na‘ama, meaning softness, ease, pleasure, and bliss. These faces are the opposite of the downcast, exhausted, burning faces of the damned. They are radiant, soft, and shining with the light of eternal joy. The word nā‘imah implies a life of complete comfort and luxury, where every need is met and every sense is delighted. The face, being the mirror of the soul, reflects the inner state of the person. The believers’ faces glow because their hearts are filled with the bliss of Paradise. They have been honoured by their Lord, their records have been given in their right hands, and they are now enjoying the rewards of their faith and patience. The description is deliberately simple yet profoundly evocative. The face that was perhaps tired and dusty from the struggles of the world, the face that was mocked and spat upon by the disbelievers, is now glowing with a beauty and radiance that surpasses all worldly standards. This is the same glow of delight mentioned in Sūrah Al-Muṭaffifīn, the naḍrah that can be recognized on the faces of the righteous. The verse provides immense hope to the believers, especially those who suffered poverty, illness, or hardship. Their worldly faces may have been marked by difficulty, but their eternal faces will glow with bliss. The word nā‘imah also connects to the “bliss” (na‘īm) that is repeatedly promised to the virtuous. The faces are described directly with the root of bliss, indicating that they are not just looking at bliss; they are saturated in it, they have become embodiments of it. The contrast with the faces of the damned could not be more complete. One face is downcast and exhausted, the other is lifted and glowing. One face reflects inner torment, the other reflects inner peace. The sūrah is painting a diptych of the human destiny, two faces, two fates, separated by the choices made in the short span of worldly life. The following verses will elaborate on the state of these glowing faces, detailing their satisfaction and their environment.
9 | ˹fully˺ pleased with their striving, | The inner state that produces the glowing faces is revealed in verse nine: “fully pleased with their striving.” The Arabic li-sa‘yihā rāḍiyah uses the word sa‘y, meaning striving, effort, work, the total sum of one’s deeds in the world. The believer’s face is not only glowing with bliss but also reflects a deep, profound satisfaction. The word rāḍiyah means pleased, content, approving. The believers look back upon their earthly life, upon the prayers they performed, the fasts they endured, the charity they gave, the patience they exercised, the temptations they resisted, and they are fully pleased. The struggle is over, and the reward is so magnificent that every difficulty seems trivial in retrospect. They have no regrets, no “if only I had done more,” because Allah’s generosity has exceeded all expectations. The phrase also implies that Allah is pleased with them, and their own pleasure is a reflection of His. The contrast with the damned is striking. The faces of the damned were ‘āmilah nāṣibah, exhausted by striving that led nowhere. Their worldly labour, done for the dunyā and devoid of faith, brought them nothing but fatigue and punishment. The believers’ striving, done for the sake of Allah, brings them eternal satisfaction. The word sa‘y encompasses all acts of worship and good deeds, but it also includes the internal struggle (jihād al-nafs) against the ego and desires. The believer who fought his lower self day and night, who forced himself to wake up for Fajr, who held his tongue from gossip, who controlled his gaze from the forbidden—that believer will look at the reward and feel that every moment of striving was infinitely worth it. This verse is a powerful motivator for the believer in the midst of the struggle. When the path feels difficult, when obedience feels heavy, remember the face that will be fully pleased with its striving. The temporary discomfort of discipline is the price of eternal satisfaction. The verse also instills a healthy sense of self-accountability: am I striving in a way that will make my face pleased on that Day? The believer is not called to a life of ease but to a life of meaningful effort, with the sure promise that the effort will be appreciated and rewarded beyond measure. The focus now shifts to the external environment that surrounds these pleased faces, the abode where their satisfaction unfolds.
10 | in an elevated Garden, | The location of the believers’ bliss is named in verse ten: “in an elevated Garden.” The Arabic fī jannatin ‘āliyah uses the word ‘āliyah, from ‘alā, meaning high, elevated, sublime. The Garden of Paradise is not situated in some lowly, ordinary place; it is raised high, both literally in its physical location and figuratively in its status, honour, and quality. The elevation implies a position of honour and overview. The believers will be in a garden that is above, looking down upon the rest of creation, including, as other verses indicate, the people of Hell. This elevation is a symbol of their high rank with Allah. The word ‘āliyah also conveys a sense of safety and security. A high place is difficult for enemies to reach. The believers are elevated above any possibility of harm, above the reach of Satan, above the memories of worldly sorrow. The Garden is also elevated in its beauty and perfection; its trees, its rivers, its palaces are of a standard that is utterly sublime and beyond anything on earth. The use of “Garden” (jannah) evokes the lush, green, shaded paradise that was the ultimate dream of the desert Arab. While the people of Hell are burning in a scorching Fire and drinking scalding water, the believers are in an elevated Garden, enjoying cool breezes, shade, and flowing rivers. The contrast in environment is as stark as the contrast in facial expression. The elevation also ties back to the earlier surahs that mentioned ‘Illiyūn, the highest place where the record of the righteous is kept. The elevated Garden is the physical manifestation of that high spiritual station. The word ‘āliyah also carries a moral meaning; it is a Garden free from the lowly, vain, and idle talk that plagues worldly gatherings. The following verse will explicitly state this absence of idle talk, reinforcing the sublime nature of the environment. For the believer, this verse is an invitation to aim high. The path to the elevated Garden is through elevating one’s intentions, one’s character, and one’s deeds. The one who lowers himself in humility before Allah in this world will be raised to the elevated Garden in the Hereafter. The promise of the elevated Garden is a source of hope that helps the believer rise above the pettiness and filth of the worldly life, fixing the gaze on a higher, purer destination.
11 | where no idle talk will be heard. | One of the defining qualities of the elevated Garden is described in verse eleven: “where no idle talk will be heard.” The Arabic lā tasma‘u fīhā lāghiyah uses a strong negation. The verb tasma‘u means “you will hear,” and the object is lāghiyah, from laghā, which means to speak vain, idle, false, or offensive words. Laghw is speech that has no benefit, that is frivolous, that includes lies, backbiting, slander, and obscenity. The verse promises that in Paradise, the ears of the believers will be completely free from any such speech. They will not hear a single word that is unpleasant, useless, or sinful. This is a profound relief for the human soul, which in this world is constantly bombarded by harmful speech—the gossip of the workplace, the slander of the media, the harsh words of adversaries, the idle chatter that wastes time and pollutes the heart. The believer who strove to guard his tongue and to avoid laghw in the world will be rewarded with an environment where such speech simply does not exist. The absence of idle talk indicates the elevated character of the inhabitants of Paradise. They are purified souls who speak only what is good, what is true, what is beautiful. The Qur’ān elsewhere describes their speech as “salam, salam” (peace, peace) and their conversations as revolving around the praises of Allah and the joyful recollections of His mercy. There is no envy, no sarcasm, no lying, no arguing. The verse also implies that the believers will be spared from hearing any words of reproach, any reminders of past sins, any hurtful comments. The peace of Paradise is not just physical but auditory and social. The contrast with Hell is again stark. The people of Hell will hear the boiling of the Fire, the cries of the damned, the rebukes of the angels. The people of Paradise will hear only peace. The verse is a powerful motivation to purify one’s own speech in this life. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should speak good or remain silent. The one who cannot control his tongue in the dunyā risks losing the auditory purity of the ākhirah. The believer is invited to sample the peace of Paradise by creating circles of remembrance and good speech in this world. The verse also underscores the Qur’ānic theme that Paradise is not merely a place of physical pleasure; it is a place of moral and spiritual perfection, where every aspect of existence, including conversation, is elevated to the highest standard. The next verses will continue to describe the physical luxuries that accompany this spiritual peace, completing the picture of the elevated Garden.
12 | In it will be a running spring, | The physical delights of the elevated Garden begin to be enumerated in verse twelve: “In it will be a running spring.” The Arabic fīhā ‘aynun jāriyah uses the word ‘ayn for a spring of water, and jāriyah means flowing, running, gushing continuously. This is the exact opposite of the scalding spring of Hell mentioned earlier. The spring in Paradise is not stagnant; it is fresh, cool, and constantly flowing, a source of life, beauty, and refreshment. The image of a running spring was particularly powerful for the Meccan Arabs, who lived in a dry, arid valley where water was the most precious resource. A spring that never stops flowing represented the height of luxury and security. The believer who enters Paradise will find such springs everywhere, providing water that is not only cool and sweet but also a delight to the eyes and the soul. The running spring is the source of the rivers that flow beneath the gardens, as mentioned in numerous other verses. It symbolises the perpetual, self-renewing nature of Paradise’s blessings. Unlike the wells of this world that can dry up, the springs of Paradise never cease. The verse also connects to the earlier descriptions of the believers’ state: their faces are glowing, their hearts are pleased, and their environment is filled with the soothing sight and sound of flowing water. The running spring is also a symbol of spiritual life and purity. The believers’ souls are nourished and purified by the water of Paradise, just as they purified themselves in the world through repentance and good deeds. In contrast, the people of Hell are given the scalding spring that burns their insides. The juxtaposition could not be clearer: one spring brings life and joy, the other brings death-like agony. For the believer, this verse encourages a longing for the springs of Paradise, a longing that can be partially satisfied in this world by seeking the springs of knowledge, faith, and good deeds that flow from the heart connected to Allah. Every time the believer drinks cool water, he should remember the running springs of Paradise and ask Allah for them. The mention of the spring also sets the stage for the descriptions of the furniture and cups that accompany such an environment of luxury. The elevated Garden is now coming to life with sensory details.
13 | along with thrones raised high, | The furnishing of the elevated Garden continues with verse thirteen: “along with thrones raised high.” The Arabic fīhā sururun marfū‘ah uses the word surur, plural of sarīr, meaning thrones, couches, or elevated seats of honour. These are not ordinary chairs; they are thrones, indicating kingship, honour, and authority. The believers are not just guests in Paradise; they are royalty, each one seated on their own raised throne. The adjective marfū‘ah means raised high, elevated, lifted up. This adds to the sense of grandeur and majesty. The thrones are physically high, perhaps overlooking the beautiful gardens and rivers, and they are also high in status, reflecting the elevated rank of the believers. The throne is a symbol of complete rest, security, and dignity. In the world, the believers may have been humiliated and made to sit on the ground while the arrogant sat on high chairs. In Paradise, the believers are the ones seated on raised thrones, while the arrogant are face down in the Fire. The image of the raised throne also connects to the description of the believers in earlier sūrahs as being “on canopied couches” (al-arā’ik), gazing around in joy. The raised throne is the seat from which they will observe the delights of Paradise and, as some narrations indicate, the punishment of the disbelievers in Hell. This is not a vindictive act but a part of the divine justice that allows the believers to fully appreciate the favour of their Lord. The verse provides immense comfort to the oppressed faithful. The Prophet (ﷺ) said that the believer who is poor and humble in this world will be among the first to enter Paradise, and his seat will be of the highest thrones. The raised throne is the reward for the humility of the heart. The word marfū‘ah also implies that these thrones are prepared and waiting. They are not constructed on the spot; they are a pre-prepared honour from the Most High. The believer is encouraged to strive for a seat among those raised thrones by raising his own character and deeds. The mention of thrones naturally leads to the description of the cups and the cushions, completing the image of a regal, luxurious banquet where the believers are the honoured guests of the Most Merciful.
14 | and cups set at hand, | The scene of regal comfort continues with the mention of drinkware in verse fourteen: “and cups set at hand.” The Arabic wa akwābun mawḍū‘ah uses the word akwāb, plural of kūb, which are goblets, cups, or vessels for drinking. These cups are described as mawḍū‘ah, meaning placed, set down, arranged, and ready. They are not far away, requiring a servant to fetch them; they are placed right at hand, conveniently positioned for the believers to take and drink whenever they wish. This detail conveys an atmosphere of effortless luxury and attentive service. The believers do not need to ask or wait; the cups of pure, delightful drink are already prepared and within reach. The cups are without handles, round and smooth, perfectly designed for the enjoyment of the drink. The drink they contain, as described elsewhere, is a wine that is pure, non-intoxicating, and delicious, sealed with musk and mixed with water from Tasnīm. The “cups set at hand” indicate the absence of any formality or hierarchy that might cause discomfort. Everyone has their own cup, and the service is immediate. In the world, one might have to call a waiter, wait for a drink, or settle for something less. In Paradise, the desire and its fulfillment occur simultaneously, without any effort or delay. The verse is part of a beautiful list that creates a cumulative effect of indulgent peace. The raised thrones provide the seating, the cups provide the drink, and the following verses will provide the cushions and carpets, completing the picture of a perfectly appointed resting place. The detail of the cups also reminds the believer of the cups of the dunyā, which are often mixed with imperfection—a chipped rim, a bitter aftertaste, a hangover. The cups of Paradise are perfect, and the drink is flawless. The believer who gave up the cup of intoxicating wine in this world out of obedience to Allah will find the cups of Paradise far superior. The verse is a subtle invitation to leave the forbidden pleasures of the world for the superior, permissible pleasures of the Hereafter. The akwāb are waiting, and the righteous effort of abstinence will be rewarded with these cups set at hand, forever. The next verse describes the cushions, adding to the softness and comfort of the setting.
15 | and ˹fine˺ cushions lined up, | The comfort of the elevated Garden is further detailed with soft furnishings in verse fifteen: “and fine cushions lined up.” The Arabic wa namāriqu maṣfūfah uses the word namāriq, the plural of numruqah, which are small cushions, pillows, or bolsters designed for reclining and resting. They are described as maṣfūfah, meaning arranged in rows, lined up neatly, placed in perfect order. This meticulous arrangement indicates the care and attention to detail that has gone into preparing the believers’ resting place. The cushions are not scattered haphazardly; they are lined up beautifully, creating an atmosphere of serene order and aesthetic delight. The image is one of a prepared lounge or a magnificent majlis, a reception hall where honoured guests are seated on cushions arranged in perfect symmetry. The cushions invite the believers to recline, to rest their backs, to lean on their sides in complete relaxation. In the world, cushions are associated with leisure, comfort, and the cessation of hard work. The believers, whose faces were described as pleased with their striving, have now entered the phase of eternal rest. Their striving is over; the cushions are for resting. This verse is a beautiful promise to the early Muslims who endured the harsh ground of Mecca, who sat on bare earth and rough mats. In Paradise, they will recline on fine cushions lined up. The word namāriq specifically refers to cushions of high quality, elegant and comfortable. The lining up implies abundance and order. There is no crowding, no competition for the best seat; every believer has their perfectly placed cushion. The verse also connects to the earlier description of the canopied couches in other surahs. The entire environment of Paradise is designed for the ultimate comfort and honour of its inhabitants. The order and beauty of the cushions also reflect the order and beauty of the believers’ own souls, which have been purified and arranged perfectly by the trials of the world and the mercy of Allah. The sensory details are now almost complete, building a vivid picture in the mind of the listener. The final furnishing element is described in the next verse: the spread carpets. Together, they form the complete setting for the divine reception of the righteous. The believer is encouraged to work for a place among those cushions by making his own life orderly, pure, and devoted to Allah.
16 | and ˹splendid˺ carpets spread out. | The description of the elevated Garden’s furnishings reaches its final element in verse sixteen: “and splendid carpets spread out.” The Arabic wa zarābiyyu mabthūthah uses the word zarābī, plural of zirbiyyah or zarbiyyah, which refers to large, thick, luxurious carpets, often richly decorated and colourful. They are described as mabthūthah, meaning spread out, laid out, scattered in abundance. The carpets cover the floors of Paradise, providing softness, warmth, and beauty underfoot. The image is one of opulent, expansive comfort. The believer walks upon carpets that are splendid, not the rough stones, hot sand, or cold earth of the world. Every step is taken on a surface of elegance. The word zarābiyy carries connotations of intricate patterns and vibrant colours, a feast for the eyes. The root zariba can also mean to be green or to have lush vegetation, suggesting that these carpets might be not just woven textiles but also the lush, thick grass of Paradise that is like a natural carpet spread over the land. Both interpretations are possible, and both convey the same sense of abundant, widespread beauty. The verb mabthūthah means scattered and spread out, indicating that these carpets are everywhere, not limited to a few special spots. Wherever the believer turns, the ground is covered with soft, splendid carpets. This is the final touch of the divine hospitality. The raised thrones, the cups at hand, the fine cushions lined up, and the splendid carpets spread out together form a complete vision of a paradisiacal reception hall. The believer is being welcomed as the most honoured guest. The contrast with the people of Hell is now complete in every sensory dimension. The damned are downcast, exhausted, burning in fire, drinking scalding water, and eating foul thorns. The blessed are glowing, pleased, in an elevated garden, with a running spring, raised thrones, cups at hand, cushions lined up, and carpets spread out. The two portraits could not be more different, and the sūrah has masterfully painted them side by side. The extensive, detailed description of Paradise serves to anchor the believer’s hope and to make the reality of the Hereafter tangible. The listener can almost see the scene, can almost feel the soft cushion and the smooth cup. This sensory vividness is a hallmark of the Qur’ānic style, designed to move the heart to action. The sūrah now shifts from the description of the two destinies to the argument from the natural world, calling the disbelievers to reflect on the signs around them. The splendid carpets of Paradise are a promise; the wonders of creation are the evidence. The next verse begins the reflective section with a rhetorical question.
17 | Do they not ever reflect on camels—how they were ˹masterfully˺ created; | The sūrah transitions to a powerful series of rhetorical questions aimed at the disbelievers, beginning with verse seventeen: “Do they not ever reflect on camels—how they were masterfully created?” The Arabic a-fa-lā yanẓurūna ilā al-ibili kayfa khuliqat uses the interrogative particle a- and the emphatic fa- to express astonishment and to urge contemplation. The verb yanẓurūna means to look, to observe, to reflect with the eye of the mind. The first object of this commanded reflection is the camel, al-ibil. The camel was the most important animal in the life of the desert Arab. It was his means of transport across vast, waterless deserts, his source of milk, meat, wool, and leather, his wealth and his companion. It was known as the “ship of the desert.” The verse asks: do the disbelievers not look at this magnificent creature and consider how it was created? The camel is a masterclass in divine design. Its feet are broad and padded, perfectly suited for walking on soft, shifting sand without sinking. Its nostrils can close to keep out blowing sand. Its hump stores fat, serving as a food reserve and a source of metabolic water, enabling it to travel for days without eating or drinking. Its eyes are protected by long lashes and a transparent third eyelid that acts like a windshield wiper. It can drink vast amounts of water in a short time without suffering osmotic shock, and its body temperature can fluctuate significantly to conserve water. Every aspect of the camel is a miracle of adaptation, pointing directly to a wise, knowing Creator. The verse calls the pagan Arab to reflect on the very animal he takes for granted. The camel is a walking sign, a living proof of Allah’s power and wisdom. If the camel were the product of blind chance, as the atheist claims, such intricate, coordinated design would be impossible. The question “how it was created” opens the door to the scientific and spiritual contemplation of nature. For the modern reader, the verse remains equally potent. The more we learn about the biology of the camel, the more its perfect design becomes evident. The verse teaches the Islamic approach to the natural world: it is not to be worshipped or exploited thoughtlessly, but to be looked at with a reflective gaze that leads to the Creator. The camel, a humble beast of burden, becomes a majestic teacher of theology. The following verses will extend this reflective gaze to the sky, the mountains, and the earth, covering the major realms of the visible world.
18 | and the sky—how it was raised ˹high˺; | The second object of reflection is presented in verse eighteen: “and the sky—how it was raised high.” The Arabic wa ilā al-samā’i kayfa rufi‘at shifts the gaze from the creature on the earth to the vast canopy above. The sky is a daily, visible miracle that is often overlooked precisely because of its constant presence. The question asks: do they not look at the sky and ponder how it was raised? The word rufi‘at means it was lifted, elevated, raised high. The sky is an immense, solid-seeming dome that arches over the earth without any visible pillars or supports. The Qur’ān elsewhere says, “He raised the sky without any pillars that you can see.” The vastness of the sky, the blue colour during the day, the darkness and the stars at night, the clouds and the rain—all of these are signs. The sky is not a random collection of gases; it is a precisely structured atmosphere that protects life on earth from harmful radiation, burns up meteors, and provides the air we breathe. The raising of the sky points to the power of the One who is al-A‘lā, the Most High, mentioned at the beginning of the previous sūrah. The sky is raised, but it is not self-raised. It did not lift itself. The passive verb rufi‘at implies a Raiser, a supreme Power that placed it in its position. The Meccan Arab, who lived under a vast, clear desert sky, was intimately familiar with its beauty and its terrors. He navigated by its stars, feared its storms, and prayed for its rain. The verse calls him to move from this experiential relationship to a theological one: the sky is not just an environment; it is a creation, a sign pointing to the Creator. The phrase “how it was raised” invites a sense of wonder and humility. The human being, standing on the earth, is dwarfed by the immensity of the sky. This should remind him of his own smallness and the greatness of his Lord. The believer is encouraged to regularly look up at the sky, not with a casual glance, but with a reflective heart that remembers the One who raised it without pillars. The next verse will bring the gaze down from the sky to the mountains, another towering sign of divine stability and power.
19 | and the mountains—how they were firmly set up; | The reflective tour continues in verse nineteen, bringing the gaze to the earth’s mighty stabilisers: “and the mountains—how they were firmly set up.” The Arabic wa ilā al-jibāli kayfa nuṣibat uses the word jibāl, mountains, and the verb nuṣibat, meaning they were erected, set up, placed firmly in position. The mountains, with their immense size, their solidity, and their rootedness in the earth, are a powerful sign of the Creator’s power. The Qur’ān describes mountains as awtād, pegs or stakes, that stabilise the earth’s crust and prevent it from shaking. This imagery aligns with the modern geological understanding of isostasy, where mountains have deep roots that penetrate the Earth’s mantle, providing balance and stability to the tectonic plates. The verse asks the disbeliever to look at the mountains and consider how they were set up. They were not just randomly placed; they were erected with a precise function. The mountains also serve as reservoirs of fresh water, sources of minerals, and habitats for diverse life. Their towering peaks, their snow-capped majesty, their deep valleys—all are scenes of beauty and awe. The Meccans lived in a valley surrounded by mountains, the ranges that encircled the sacred city. They passed by mountains on their trade journeys to Syria and Yemen. They knew the mountains as landmarks and as formidable obstacles. The verse tells them that these mountains are not just geographical features; they are signs. The fact that they are “firmly set up” indicates stability, order, and purpose. They were placed by a deliberate act of a Wise Creator. The verb nuṣibat implies that the mountains are standing upright, erected like tents, as another verse describes. They are not the result of random geological chaos; they are the result of a divine command. For the believer, the mountains inspire a sense of awe and reliance on the Creator who holds them in place. The mountains that seem so permanent will one day be blown away, as earlier sūrahs described. Their present firmness is a temporary gift, a sign of the world’s order that will one day be dissolved. The reflection on the mountains teaches the believer to see permanence only in Allah, and to see the mountains as transient structures that serve a purpose before their appointed end. The next verse will complete the four-part reflection with the earth itself.
20 | and the earth—how it was levelled out? | The four-part reflection concludes with the most immediate and encompassing sign in verse twenty: “and the earth—how it was levelled out?” The Arabic wa ilā al-arḍi kayfa suṭiḥat uses the verb suṭiḥat, meaning it was spread out, flattened, levelled. The earth, the very ground upon which they stand, is the final object of contemplation. The verb suṭiḥat does not imply that the earth is a perfectly flat disc, as the earth’s spherical shape was known to some early scholars and is consistent with other Qur’ānic descriptions like “egg-shaped.” Rather, it refers to the local, observable spreading of the earth’s surface, the fact that the land is laid out like a carpet for human habitation. The mountains may tower, but the plains and valleys are spread out, allowing people to walk, build, farm, and travel with ease. The earth’s surface is not a jagged, impassable mess; it is a carefully prepared habitation. The verb suṭiḥat invites the listener to reflect on the mercy of Allah in making the earth a comfortable home. The earth is not too hard, not too soft, not too hot, not too cold. It has a gravity that holds us, an atmosphere that protects us, a soil that grows our food, water that quenches our thirst. All of this is the result of a levelling and spreading that was done by the Creator. The Meccan Arab, who traversed the deserts and the valleys, knew the difference between smooth, passable ground and rugged, impassable terrain. The fact that most of the earth’s surface is accessible is a blessing. The verse is a call to gratitude. The disbeliever who walks upon the earth every day and never reflects on its design is guilty of a profound heedlessness. The four signs—the camel, the sky, the mountains, and the earth—encompass the most immediate realities of the Arab’s life: his primary domestic animal, the canopy above him, the towering landmarks around him, and the ground beneath his feet. Each is a masterfully crafted sign. The sequence moves from the specific (the camel) to the universal (the earth), from the near to the far and back to the near. The cumulative effect is overwhelming: all of these signs point to a single, powerful, wise Creator. The questions are rhetorical; the answer is obvious. No one who looks with an honest eye can attribute these things to chance or to idols. The reflection leads inevitably to the conclusion that the Creator of all this is the One who will also bring about the Overwhelming Event and judge humanity. The stage is now set for the final command to the Prophet to continue reminding, regardless of the response. The entire supplied passage of verses 1–20 has now been completed.
21 | So, ˹continue to˺ remind ˹all, O Prophet˺, for your duty is only to remind. | After the compelling reflection on the signs of creation, the sūrah returns to the direct address of the Prophet (ﷺ) with a command that serves as both a comfort and a clarification of his mission: “So, continue to remind all, O Prophet, for your duty is only to remind.” The Arabic fa-dhakkir innamā anta mudhakkir uses the imperative dhakkir, from the root dhikr, meaning to remind, to admonish, to cause to remember. The particle fa- (so, therefore) links this command to the preceding arguments. Because the signs of Allah’s power in the camel, the sky, the mountains, and the earth are so manifest, and because the fate of the deniers and the reward of the believers have been so clearly detailed, the Prophet is to persist in his mission of reminding. He is not to be disheartened by the rejection he faces. The word innamā is a particle of restriction, meaning “only” or “solely.” It strictly delineates the boundaries of his responsibility. The Prophet’s duty, his primary function, is to be a mudhakkir, a reminder. He is not a controller, a compeller, a guardian over their faith, or a guarantee of their guidance. His task is to deliver the message with clarity, persistence, and compassion, and to leave the outcome to Allah. This is an immensely liberating instruction. The Prophet deeply desired the guidance of his people, and their persistent rejection weighed heavily on his soul. This verse lifts that weight by clarifying that his success is not measured by their acceptance, but by his faithful delivery. He is a reminder, and the reminder has intrinsic value even if it appears to fall on deaf ears. The word “continue to remind” in the present tense implies an ongoing, unceasing effort. The Prophet is to keep reminding, morning and evening, in public and in private, with hope and with warning. The reminder is the lifeline thrown to drowning humanity, and the lifeguard is not responsible if the drowning person refuses to grab it, provided he has thrown it with all his might. The verse also applies to every caller to truth. The believer who shares the message is not responsible for converting hearts. The moment a da‘i begins to feel frustration that borders on despair, he must recall this verse: my duty is only to remind. The reminder itself is a mercy, a light, and it will not be wasted. Even if it only benefits some, as earlier surahs stated, the effort is commanded. The focus shifts from the response of the audience to the sincerity of the effort, and that shift is the source of the Prophet’s peace of mind. The next verse will reinforce this restriction, preventing any misunderstanding that the Prophet might have the power to force belief.
22 | You are not ˹there˺ to compel them ˹to believe˺. | The clarification of the Prophet’s role is made even more explicit in verse twenty-two: “You are not there to compel them to believe.” The Arabic lasta ‘alayhim bi-muṣayṭir uses a strong negation with lasta, “you are not.” The key word is muṣayṭir, from the root sayṭara, which means to be a controller, a dominator, a keeper, one who exercises authority and compulsion over others. The verse firmly removes any such role from the Prophet. He is not sent as a muṣayṭir over the people. He cannot and must not force them to accept faith. This is a fundamental principle in Islam, articulated clearly in the Qur’ān and later codified in the maxim “There is no compulsion in religion.” Faith, by its very nature, is a matter of the heart and the free will. If a person is forced to utter the words of the shahādah while their heart remains unconvinced, that is not true belief but hypocrisy, and it holds no weight with Allah. The Prophet’s role is to convey, to argue in the best way, to be a beautiful example, and to warn—but not to compel. The verse was revealed in a context where the Prophet was being mocked, persecuted, and many of his followers were being tortured. A lesser person might have wished for the power to force the disbelievers into submission. The Qur’ān instructs him that such power is not part of his mission. The battle is not for bodies but for hearts, and hearts can only be won through truth and mercy, not coercion. This teaching stands in stark contrast to the methods of many worldly powers throughout history that have sought to impose religious uniformity through force. It establishes the ethical boundaries of da‘wah. The da‘i is a witness, a herald, a lamp—not a jailer. The verse also carries a profound theological insight: guidance is ultimately in the hands of Allah alone. Even the Prophet, the best of creation, is not able to place faith into a single heart without Allah’s will. Therefore, he should not burden himself with what is beyond his capacity. The word muṣayṭir also carries the nuance of a guard or a watchman who keeps people under surveillance and control. The Prophet is not even that. He is not sent as a keeper over their deeds, just as earlier sūrahs told the believers that the disbelievers were not sent as keepers over them. The mutual release from the role of policing each other’s souls fosters an environment where conviction is based on genuine, free acceptance. The believer who understands this verse learns to offer the truth with love and then release the outcome, refusing to slip into the arrogance of wanting to control the spiritual destinies of others. The next verse will describe the consequence for those who, despite this gentle, compelling-free invitation, persist in turning away.
23 | But whoever turns away, persisting in disbelief, | The sūrah now turns to the other side of the equation, describing those who reject the reminder despite its clarity and the gentle manner of its presentation: “But whoever turns away, persisting in disbelief.” The Arabic illā man tawallā wa kafar uses the verb tawallā, which means to turn one’s back, to turn away, to retreat from something in aversion. This is not a passive neglect; it is an active, deliberate turning of the face and the heart away from the truth. The second verb, kafar, means to disbelieve, to cover up the truth. The conjunction wa (and) links the external act of turning away with the internal state of disbelief. The person described here is not someone who is merely ignorant or who has not heard the message clearly. This is someone who has been reminded, who has seen the signs, who has had the opportunity to reflect on the camel, the sky, the mountains, and the earth, but has made a conscious decision to reject. The phrase “persisting in disbelief” captures the ongoing, stubborn nature of their state. They do not have moments of reflection followed by regret; they deepen in their rejection. The verse implicitly contrasts with the earlier verses about the people of Paradise, who were pleased with their striving and received the reward. The ones who turn away are choosing the path of the people of Hell, the downcast, exhausted faces. The construction “whoever turns away” is general, applying to anyone, from the Meccan leaders to any human being until the end of time, who responds to the divine reminder with aversion and disbelief. The verse serves as a warning and also as a final demarcation. The Prophet is told: your duty is only to remind. For those who accept, there is bliss. For those who turn away and persist in disbelief, there is a different outcome, which the next verse will describe. This segmentation of humanity based on their response to the reminder is a recurring Qur’ānic theme, and it places the ultimate responsibility on the individual. No one can claim on the Day of Judgment that they were forced to disbelieve by a compelling Prophet. They turned away by their own choice. The word tawallā paints a vivid image of a person physically turning their back on something, a gesture of contempt and rejection. This is the posture of the disbeliever towards the truth. The believer is asked to examine his own heart: is there any trace of this turning away in my response to the commands of Allah? The verse serves as a mirror, urging a continuous turning towards the truth, not away from it. The consequence for this turning away is specified in the next verse as a major punishment from Allah.
24 | then Allah will inflict upon them the major punishment. | The consequence for the one who turns away and persists in disbelief is delivered in stark terms in verse twenty-four: “then Allah will inflict upon them the major punishment.” The Arabic fa-yu‘adhdhibuhu Allāhu al-‘adhāba al-akbar uses the verb yu‘adhdhibuhu, meaning He will punish him severely. The subject is explicitly named: Allāh. This is not a punishment from an angel or a natural force; it is the direct punishment from the Lord of all worlds. The punishment is described as al-‘adhāb al-akbar, the greatest punishment, the major punishment. This superlative form indicates that the punishment for this deliberate turning away from the clear reminder is of the utmost severity and magnitude. It is the punishment of the Hereafter, the eternal Fire, as opposed to any lesser, temporary punishment that might occur in this world, such as famine, defeat, or the torment of the grave. Some scholars have said that the “major punishment” refers specifically to the punishment of Hell on the Day of Judgment, while the “minor punishment” (mentioned elsewhere) refers to the afflictions of this world. Others said the major punishment is that of the Hereafter in general, because there is no punishment greater than the eternal wrath of Allah. The verse makes clear that this punishment is not arbitrary; it is the direct result of their own turning away. The particle fa- (then) indicates a logical sequence: they turned away, so Allah will punish them. Divine justice is a response to human choice. The severity of the punishment matches the gravity of the crime. The disbeliever rejected the Most High, the Most Merciful, the One who provided all the signs in creation and sent the clear reminder. For such a rejection, only the major punishment is fitting. The verse also serves as a comfort to the Prophet and the believers. It assures them that the justice they long for will be executed, not by their hands, but by the Lord Himself. They do not need to seek revenge or despair at the apparent prosperity of the deniers. The major punishment is in the hands of Allah, and it is certain. The word akbar (greatest) also implies that there are lesser punishments, but the one reserved for the obstinate, persistent disbelievers is the greatest of them all. This should fill the believer with a healthy fear and a renewed commitment to never be among those who turn away. The verse is a sobering conclusion to the section on the deniers, and the sūrah will end by emphasizing the absolute sovereignty of Allah over the entire process of return and reckoning.
25 | Surely to Us is their return, | The sūrah now anchors the entire warning in a foundational theological truth in verse twenty-five: “Surely to Us is their return.” The Arabic inna ilaynā iyābahum uses the emphatic particle inna and the possessive pronoun nā (Us) in the majestic plural. The word iyāb is a noun from the root āba, which means to return, to come back. It is a return after a journey. The verse declares that the ultimate destination of all these people—the ones who turned away and the ones who believed—is back to Allah. This is the same truth that was stated in Sūrah ‘Abasa and Al-Infiṭār, but here it is given as the direct follow-up to the threat of punishment. The disbeliever may turn away in this world, but he cannot turn away from the return. He will inevitably be brought back to his Lord. The phrase “to Us” is intensely personal. They are not returning to an abstract principle or a distant judge; they are returning to the very Lord who created them, provided for them, and sent them the reminder. This return is the ultimate meeting, the moment when the fleeing soul is brought face to face with its Maker. The believer finds immense comfort in this: the return to the Most Merciful is the homecoming of the soul. The disbeliever finds only terror: the return is to the One whose signs he denied. The word iyāb also carries a connotation of a final, permanent return. The journey of worldly life is over, and the soul arrives at its eternal abode. There is no further travel, no more movement. The return is the end of the wandering. The verse serves as a powerful reminder of the transience of worldly status. The disbeliever who strutted in the marketplace of Mecca as if he owned the earth will one day be returned, powerless and alone, to the Lord of the earth. The believer who was oppressed and had no advocate in this world will be returned to the best of advocates, the Lord who witnessed everything. The verse also provides the logical foundation for the reckoning. If all must return to Allah, then there must be a reckoning. The return is not for nothing. A return to the King demands an accounting. The sūrah thus builds up to the final verse, which explicitly mentions the reckoning, completing the circle that began with the Overwhelming Event. The return is certain; the reckoning is the purpose of the return.
26 | then surely with Us is their reckoning. | The sūrah concludes with the second half of the divine assurance, making the purpose of the return explicit: “then surely with Us is their reckoning.” The Arabic thumma inna ‘alaynā ḥisābahum uses thumma to indicate a sequence that is also a necessary consequence. After the return to Allah comes the reckoning. The word ḥisāb comes from the root ḥasaba, meaning to count, to calculate, to take account. It is the precise, meticulous judgment of every deed, word, and intention. The phrase “‘alaynā” (upon Us) is a declaration of divine responsibility. Allah has taken it upon Himself, in His justice and wisdom, to settle the accounts of every soul. No one will be overlooked, no deed will be forgotten, no oppression will remain unaddressed. This reckoning is not delegated to angels alone; it is attributed directly to Allah, emphasizing its ultimate authority and infallibility. The verse is a final, powerful answer to the disbelievers who denied the judgment. They refused to believe that their deeds would ever be counted, that the scales of justice would ever be set up. The sūrah ends by telling them that the reckoning is not just a possibility; it is a certainty, and it is upon Allah Himself. The One who created the camel, raised the sky, set up the mountains, and levelled the earth—that same One will conduct the reckoning. The believer finds in this verse the ultimate reassurance. The seeming injustices of the world are not the final word. The scales that were tampered with in the market, the mockery that was endured in silence, the faith that was held onto in the face of persecution—all of it will be weighed and recompensed in the reckoning that is with Allah. The word ḥisāb also implies a swift and easy account for the believers, as described in earlier sūrahs, and a severe, drawn-out account for the disbelievers. The sūrah that began with the “Overwhelming Event” and painted the vivid pictures of the downcast, burned faces and the glowing, pleased faces ends with the ultimate reason for those fates: the return and the reckoning. The entire arc of the surah is complete. The listener is left with the profound awareness that life is a journey towards a meeting with the Lord, and that meeting will involve a full, just, and unavoidable accounting. This is the news that was meant to overwhelm the heart, and with this final note, the supplied passage of Sūrah Al-Ghāshiyah has been fully completed.
