1 | By those ˹winds˺ sent forth successively, | The surah opens with a series of powerful, evocative oaths. "By those winds sent forth successively." The Arabic "wal-mursalati 'urfa" is rich with imagery and layered meaning. The word "mursalat" comes from "arsala," meaning to send forth. It refers to things that are dispatched, released, and sent on a mission. The commentators differ on exactly what is meant, and the beauty of the Quranic style is that multiple interpretations can coexist and enrich the meaning. One dominant interpretation is that these are the winds, sent forth by Allah one after another, successively, gently, in a manner that is known and recognized ('urfa). The winds are sent as a mercy, driven by divine command, carrying clouds and rain. Another major interpretation, supported by the following verses, is that these are the angels, sent forth with the revelations and commands of Allah, descending in succession with their messages. The word "'urfa" can mean in succession, or it can mean with goodness and recognized purpose. The winds come in waves, each wave fulfilling its task. This oath draws the listener's attention to the unseen forces that Allah dispatches in the universe. The winds are invisible, yet their effects are profoundly felt. Similarly, the angels are unseen, yet their missions are real and impactful. By swearing by these sent ones, Allah is emphasizing the ordered, purposeful nature of creation. Nothing is random. The winds do not blow chaotically; they are "sent forth." The angels do not descend on a whim; they are on a mission. This sets the stage for the central theme of the surah: the Day of Judgment is also "sent forth," decreed, and on its way. Just as the winds arrive at their appointed time, so will the Judgment. The oath is a solemn affirmation of the message that follows. The believer who reflects on the winds feels the presence of the Divine command operating in the world. The surah will continue to build these oaths, each adding a dimension to the cosmic testimony that the Day of Decision is real and inevitable.
2 | and those blowing violently, | The second oath intensifies the imagery: "and those blowing violently." The Arabic "fal-'asifati 'asfa" refers to winds or forces that storm and rage with great power. The word "'asifat" comes from "'asafa," meaning to blow fiercely, to uproot, to cause destruction. These are the tempestuous winds, the hurricanes, the gales that toss ships and flatten structures. While the first oath spoke of gentle, successive winds of mercy, this oath speaks of the winds of punishment and power. This duality reflects the nature of Allah's actions in the world. He sends gentle breezes that bring rain and life, and He also sends violent storms that destroy the rebellious, as He did with the people of 'Ad. The contrast teaches that the same force that is a mercy can become a punishment when Allah wills. The angels also have this dual role: some descend with mercy and guidance, others descend with punishment and severity. The "blowing violently" also evokes the chaos and terror of the Day of Judgment, when the Trumpet is blown, and the universe is torn apart. The surah is building a crescendo. The gentle succession of the mursalat gives way to the violent storming of the 'asifat. This mirrors the transition from the world of order to the Day of chaos. For the deniers, the winds they once knew as gentle breezes will become the instruments of their destruction. The oath serves as a warning: do not take the gentle winds for granted; the same Lord who sends them can send the violent blast. The believer is reminded to fear the power of Allah, which can manifest in sudden, overwhelming ways. The oath also points to the angels who execute divine punishment, blowing with a force that cannot be resisted. The surah continues to the next oath, completing the picture of the cosmic forces under Allah's command.
3 | and those scattering ˹rainclouds˺ widely! | The third oath moves from the winds themselves to their specific function: "and those scattering rainclouds widely!" The Arabic "fan-nashirati nashra" refers to the winds or the angels that spread and scatter. The word "nashirat" comes from "nashara," meaning to spread out, to unfold, to disperse. One clear interpretation is that these are the winds that take the rainclouds and spread them across the sky, distributing them widely over the earth. The winds gather the clouds, then push them and stretch them out like a vast canopy, so that rain falls over a wide area. Another interpretation is that these are the angels who spread out the scrolls of revelation, or who spread their wings in flight, or who scatter the souls to their destinations. The primary imagery, however, is that of the clouds being dispersed. After the gentle sending and the violent blowing, we now see the purposeful scattering. The clouds are not stationary; they are actively spread out to deliver water where it is needed. This is a sign of Allah's meticulous provision. The water cycle, driven by the winds that scatter the clouds, is a marvel of divine engineering. The oath calls on this natural phenomenon as a witness. The One who can scatter the heavy, water-laden clouds across the sky can certainly scatter the particles of the dead and then gather them for resurrection. The spreading of the clouds is a daily proof of the power of dispersal and gathering. The deniers who see the clouds spread out before the rain do not reflect on the lesson. On the Day of Judgment, the deeds of every soul will be spread out, the books of records will be unfurled, and the truth will be laid bare. The "nashr" (spreading) is a theme that connects this world to the next. The winds that spread the clouds are a rehearsal for the angels who will spread the records. The oath builds anticipation for the central message. The surah has now sworn by the sent, the violent, and the scattering ones, creating a trio of cosmic witnesses. The next oaths will shift more explicitly to the angelic realm, preparing for the declaration of the Day's certainty.
4 | And ˹by˺ those ˹angels˺ fully distinguishing ˹truth from falsehood˺, | The fourth oath shifts more clearly to the angelic realm: "And by those angels fully distinguishing truth from falsehood." The Arabic "fal-fariqati farqa" refers to those who separate and divide with a decisive, complete separation. The "fariqat" are the angels who descend with the revelation to distinguish between truth and falsehood, between right and wrong, between the lawful and the unlawful. The Quran itself is called "Furqan" because it is the criterion that separates. The angels who bring down the Quran are thus the "fariqat." They bring a message that does not blur lines; it clearly demarcates the path of guidance from the path of misguidance. On a broader scale, this also refers to the angels who are given the task of separating souls at death, distinguishing the believers from the disbelievers, and separating the righteous from the wicked on the Day of Judgment. The word "farqa" emphasizes the finality and completeness of the distinction. There is no gray zone. Truth and falsehood are not two equally valid opinions; they are separated by a divine, angelic intervention. This oath is a direct challenge to the moral relativism of the Meccan pagans, who mixed truth with falsehood, worshipped Allah alongside idols, and blurred the lines of morality. The angels bring a message that cuts through confusion. For the believers, this oath reinforces the clarity of the Quranic guidance. The revelation they follow is not ambiguous; it is a decisive criterion brought by noble angels. On the Day of Judgment, the angels will physically separate the people of Paradise from the people of Hell, a final, visible "farq." The verse continues the sequence: from sending to storming to scattering to separating. The functions of the unseen agents are multi-layered. The oath builds toward the final function, which is the actual delivery of the reminder that leaves no excuse.
5 | and those delivering revelation, | The fifth and final oath in this series brings us to the heart of the matter: "and those delivering revelation." The Arabic "fal-mulqiyati dhikra" refers to the angels who cast, deliver, and convey the Reminder. The word "mulqiyat" comes from "alqa," meaning to throw, to deliver, to cast into the heart. The angels, specifically Jibril and his assistants, deliver the revelation—the "dhikr," the remembrance—to the prophets. They descend with the words of Allah and cast them into the hearts of the chosen messengers. This is the ultimate purpose of the previous four functions. The winds blow, the clouds scatter, the angels descend with the criterion, all for the sake of this central act: the delivery of the reminder. The word "dhikr" is profoundly significant. The Quran is called a "dhikr" because it reminds the human being of his Creator, his purpose, his origin, and his end. It is a reminder of the covenant taken before birth. The angels are the carriers of this reminder. The oath honors their role and, by extension, the message they carry. The Prophet, peace be upon him, is the recipient of this angelic delivery. The surah, revealed in Mecca, is part of that very "dhikr." The oaths are not swearing by abstract forces but by the very process that brought the surah into existence. The angels were sent, they came with power, they scattered the truth, they separated right from wrong, and they delivered this warning. The oath is self-referential and awe-inspiring. It tells the deniers that the words they are hearing are not the words of a man; they are the product of a cosmic operation involving the noble angels. The believer who recites these verses feels the weight of the revelation. The Quran is not an ordinary book; it was delivered by the "mulqiyat." The surah now reaches the content of the delivery, the message that the oaths are meant to emphasize.
6 | ending excuses and giving warnings. | The purpose of the delivered revelation is now made clear: "ending excuses and giving warnings." The Arabic "'udhran aw nudhra" encapsulates the dual function of the divine message. The word "'udhran" (ending excuses) means that the revelation serves to cut off all pretexts and justifications for disbelief. After the prophets have been sent and the books have been delivered, no human being can stand before Allah on the Day of Judgment and say, "I did not know," or "No warner came to us." The Quran establishes the proof. The excuses are nullified. Those who reject the message do so with full knowledge and bear full responsibility. The word "nudhra" (giving warnings) means the revelation is a warning, an alarm bell, a caution of the impending punishment. It is a mercy because it gives people a chance to repent before it is too late. The angels deliver a message that simultaneously removes the possibility of pleading ignorance and provides the motivation to change through fear of the consequences. This dual function is perfectly balanced. The verse is a powerful statement of divine justice. Allah does not punish without first sending a warning that ends all valid excuses. The Meccan deniers cannot claim they were left in the dark. The Prophet has come, the Quran is being recited, the warnings are clear. If they persist, their excuses will not be accepted. The phrase "aw nudhra" (or giving warnings) indicates that the revelation is entirely a warning for those who reject, or it can be read as combining both functions: it is both an end of excuses and a warning. The oath series is now complete. The winds, the storms, the scatterers, the separators, and the deliverers all work together to bring a message that leaves no excuse and serves as a clear warning. The stage is set for the central declaration of the surah. The next verse delivers the thunderous truth that all these oaths have been building toward.
7 | Surely, what you are promised will come to pass. | After the five grand oaths, the surah delivers the subject of the oath, the core message: "Surely, what you are promised will come to pass." The Arabic "innama tu'aduna lawaqi'" is a statement of absolute certainty. The "ma tu'adun" (what you are promised) refers to the Day of Judgment, the resurrection, the accounting, Paradise, and Hell—all the things that the prophets warned about and that the deniers rejected. The word "waqi'" means something that will inevitably occur, something that will fall upon them, an event that is unstoppable. The emphasis is tripled: the "inna" (surely), the structure of the sentence, and the word "waqi'" itself all scream certainty. The deniers asked mockingly, "When is this Day of Judgment?" The surah answers: it is not a matter of "if" but "when." It is as certain as the winds that blow, as the clouds that scatter, as the revelation that is being recited. The oaths serve as evidence. The One who sends the winds successively, who blows the violent storms, who scatters the clouds, who sends the angels to distinguish and deliver—is that same One incapable of bringing the Day? The consistency and power of these natural and angelic phenomena are proof that Allah's promises are always fulfilled. The verse is a direct assault on the skepticism of the pagans. It leaves no room for doubt. For the believers, it is a reassurance that their faith is not in vain. The promise of justice, the promise of Paradise for the patient, the promise of punishment for the oppressors—all of it is "waqi'," definitely happening. The word "waqi'" also carries the connotation of something that falls heavily, like a hammer blow. The Day of Judgment will fall upon creation with all its weight, and no one will be able to escape it. The surah now proceeds to describe the cosmic events that will signal the arrival of that promised Day.
8 | So when the stars are put out, | The surah now begins to paint the terrifying scene of the Day of Judgment, starting with the cosmic events. "So when the stars are put out." The Arabic "fa-idha an-nujumu tumisat" uses the conjunction "fa" (so) to connect the certainty of the promise to the events that will unfold. The verb "tumisat" means to be effaced, wiped out, obliterated, extinguished. The stars, which for millennia have been symbols of permanence, guidance, and beauty, will be blotted out. Their light will die. The countless luminous bodies that fill the night sky, which the Arabs used for navigation and which some of them even worshipped, will cease to exist. The extinguishing of the stars represents the collapse of the cosmic order. The lights that illuminated the darkness will vanish, leaving an unimaginable, absolute blackness. This is the prelude to the resurrection. The familiar universe must be dismantled before the new, eternal world is established. The verse evokes a sense of dread. The stars are so far away, so massive, so seemingly eternal. If they can be put out, what power do humans have? What certainty do they have in their own permanence? The extinguishing of the stars is the first sign that the world they knew is ending. It also symbolizes the extinguishing of false lights—the idols, the false gods, the human luminaries—all will be snuffed out, and only the Light of the Almighty will remain. The use of the passive voice "are put out" indicates that an external force will act upon them; they will not die of their own accord. The One who kindled them will extinguish them. The surah continues to list the cataclysmic events, building the scene step by step.
9 | and the sky is torn apart, | The second cataclysmic event is even more terrifying: "and the sky is torn apart." The Arabic "wa idha as-sama'u furijat" means the sky will be split open, cleaved asunder, rent with great fissures. The sky, which appears as a solid, seamless blue dome protecting the earth, will crack and break apart. The word "furijat" implies opening and splitting. The protective barrier will be removed. The sky will become a scene of chaos, with gaps and tears, no longer the smooth canopy it once was. This is described in many surahs: the sky will become like molten brass, it will be torn open, it will be rolled up like a scroll. The tearing of the sky signifies the complete breakdown of the natural order. The sky was a sign of Allah's power and a source of protection, but on that Day, it will become a source of terror. The angels will descend through these tears. The divine command will become manifest. The sky that the deniers looked at and felt secure beneath will betray them. The tearing also represents the removal of the barrier between the seen and the unseen. The unseen realm of angels and divine presence will become visible. There will be no more veil. The deeds that were hidden under the sky will be exposed to the sky's Creator. The verse adds another layer to the horror. After the lights are extinguished and the sky is torn, the earth itself will be struck, as the next verse shows. The sequence is relentless. The surah is forcing the listener to visualize the impossible becoming real. For the believer, this is a reminder of the fragility of the world. The solid sky is not so solid. Only Allah is Al-Qayyum, the Ever-Lasting. The surah continues to the next cosmic target: the mountains.
10 | and the mountains are blown away, | The third event in the cosmic collapse strikes at the very foundations of the earth: "and the mountains are blown away." The Arabic "wa idha al-jibalu nusifat" uses the verb "nusifat," which comes from "nasafa," meaning to scatter, to blow away, to pulverize and disperse like dust. The mountains, those immense, towering symbols of stability and permanence, will be uprooted and blown apart. They will become like particles of dust carried by the wind. In other surahs, they are described as being like carded wool or shifting sand dunes. Here, they are simply "blown away," reduced to nothingness. The mountains that people relied upon as anchors of the earth, that they built their homes upon, that they worshipped as sacred peaks, will vanish. This is a direct answer to the arrogance of those who think the earth and its massive features are permanent. Nothing is permanent except the Face of Allah. The blowing away of the mountains also levels the earth, preparing it for the new creation. The flat, open plain of the Hereafter will have no mountains, no valleys, no hiding places. Every soul will be visible. Every deed will be exposed. The mountains that once hid secrets in their caves will give up their dead and then themselves disappear. The verb "nusifat" conveys a sense of utter helplessness. The mountains cannot resist; they are simply blown away by the divine command. The verse continues the sequence of terror. The stars are extinguished, the sky is torn, the mountains are blown away. The physical universe is being dismantled piece by piece. The surah now reaches the climactic event that is the purpose of all this destruction: the gathering of the messengers for their testimony.
11 | and the messengers’ time ˹to testify˺ comes up— | Amidst the cosmic destruction, the focus suddenly shifts to a solemn, purposeful event: "and the messengers' time to testify comes up." The Arabic "wa idha ar-rusulu uqqitat" is profoundly significant. The verb "uqqitat" means to be appointed a fixed time, to be gathered at a specified moment. The messengers, all of them, from Adam to Muhammad, peace be upon them all, have been waiting for this moment. They are gathered to fulfill their role as witnesses over their nations. The surah places this event alongside the destruction of the stars, sky, and mountains to show its immense importance. The purpose of the cosmos is to serve as a stage for the human test. When the test is over, the stage is dismantled, and the witnesses are called forward. The messengers will testify that they delivered the message, that they warned their people, that they conveyed the "dhikr" mentioned in the oath. They will testify about who believed and who denied. This testimony will be the basis for judgment. The deniers, who mocked the messengers in the world, will now face them in the court of the Almighty, unable to deny what they said. The word "uqqitat" also implies a precise appointment. The timing is not random; it is perfectly ordained by Allah. The messengers have been waiting for this Day, and now it has arrived. The verse serves as a powerful comfort to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. He is told that he, along with all the messengers, has an appointment with destiny. The hardships he faces in delivering the message are temporary; the Day of testimony is coming. The verse also warns the deniers that the prophets they ridicule will be the very witnesses against them. The surah now asks a rhetorical question to highlight the magnitude of this appointed time.
12 | for which Day has all this been set? | After listing the terrifying events and the gathering of the messengers, the surah poses a rhetorical question that magnifies the importance of the appointed time. "For which Day has all this been set?" The Arabic "li-ayyi yawmin ujjilat" asks: for what tremendous Day have these cosmic signs and the messenger's testimony been deferred? The verb "ujjilat" means to be postponed, to be appointed for a later time. The destruction of the universe and the gathering of the prophets were not done earlier; they were set for this specific Day. The question implies that the Day must be of unimaginable importance for such grand events to be scheduled for it. It is not an ordinary day. The entire history of the universe, from its creation to its end, has been leading to this Day. The question is asked by Allah, who knows the answer, to make the listener reflect and feel the weight of the moment. It is a literary technique to create suspense and awe. The listener is forced to ask himself: indeed, what Day could be worth all this? The answer is coming, but the pause created by the question is crucial. It gives the heart a moment to tremble in anticipation. The deniers who used to ask, "When is this Day?" in mockery are now given the answer with a gravity that should make them regret their flippancy. The question also serves to connect the cosmic events directly to the concept of a scheduled appointment. The universe is not collapsing randomly; it is doing so right on time, as per a divine timetable. The surah now provides the answer, naming the Day with a title that encapsulates its essence.
13 | For the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision! | The answer to the rhetorical question is given with thunderous finality: "For the Day of Final Decision!" The Arabic "li-yawm al-fasl" names the Day. "Al-Fasl" means the decisive separation, the judgment that cuts and divides. It is the Day when truth is separated from falsehood, the believer from the disbeliever, the righteous from the wicked, the eternal bliss from the eternal punishment. Every matter that was disputed in the world will be decisively settled. There will be no appeals, no further arguments. The word "fasl" also implies a surgical precision. The judgment will be exact, cutting through all pretenses and excuses, laying bare the reality of every soul. This is the Day for which the messengers were waiting, the Day for which the cosmos was dismantled. It is the ultimate purpose of creation. The name "Day of Decision" answers the mockery of the deniers. They used to say, "When will this decision be?" Here it is, named and defined. The surah now adds another layer of emphasis by asking a second rhetorical question about the nature of this Day, drawing the listener deeper into contemplation.
14 | And what will make you realize what the Day of Decision is? | The surah pauses the narrative to ask a question that magnifies the Day even further: "And what will make you realize what the Day of Decision is?" The Arabic "wa ma adraka ma yawm al-fasl" is the familiar Quranic formula for expressing something that is beyond human comprehension. It is as if Allah is saying, "No human knowledge can truly grasp the full reality of this Day. Only divine revelation can give you a glimpse." The question is directed at the Prophet and through him to all humanity. Even though the name "Day of Decision" has been given, and the events have been described, the true horror and majesty of that Day are beyond words. The phrase "wa ma adraka" is used for the most immense realities of the unseen, like the Night of Decree, the crushing disaster, and Hell itself. It creates a sense of awe and humility. The listener is made to feel that whatever he imagines, the reality is far greater. The question also serves as a warning: you cannot afford to be unprepared for a Day whose reality you cannot even fully grasp. The deniers who speak lightly of the Judgment are speaking about something they do not understand. The believer, on the other hand, trembles at the thought of this incomprehensible Day and strives with all his might to be ready. The question is a bridge to the first occurrence of the surah's haunting refrain, which will punctuate the rest of the chapter like a drumbeat of doom.
15 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The surah now introduces its famous, chilling refrain that will be repeated ten times throughout the chapter: "Woe on that Day to the deniers!" The Arabic "waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibeen" is a devastating pronouncement. "Wayl" is a word of severe threat and condemnation. It is a valley in Hell, or a specific punishment, or a general expression of utter destruction and ruin. It is woe, calamity, and grief piled upon grief. The word "yawma'idhin" (on that Day) specifies the exact moment when this woe will descend. It is the Day of Decision. The "mukadhdhibeen" are the deniers, those who belied the messengers, rejected the revelations, and called the Day of Judgment a lie. They are the specific target of this woe. The refrain is perfectly placed. After the cosmic destruction, the gathering of the messengers, the naming of the Day, and the rhetorical question emphasizing its incomprehensibility, the verdict is announced. The deniers who mocked and scoffed will face "wayl." The verse is a dagger of divine justice. It pierces through the comfortable assumptions of the pagans. The repetition of this refrain throughout the surah serves to hammer the message home. Every time the deniers' crimes are mentioned, the woe is pronounced. It creates a rhythm of warning that is both terrifying and mesmerizing. For the believers, the refrain is a source of sober reflection and gratitude. They are saved from this "wayl" by their faith. For the deniers who hear it, it is a final alarm. The surah will now provide the evidence for the Day of Decision by pointing to the destruction of previous nations and the creation of the human being, with the refrain punctuating each argument.
16 | Did We not destroy earlier disbelievers? | The surah now begins to present the rational and historical evidence for the Day of Decision, starting with a question: "Did We not destroy earlier disbelievers?" The Arabic "a-lam nuhliki al-awwaleen" asks the Quraysh to reflect on the well-known history of the nations that came before them. The "awwaleen" are the earlier generations, the ancient peoples who rejected their messengers—the people of Noah, 'Ad, Thamud, Lot, and Pharaoh. Their ruins were visible to the Arabs. The trade caravans of the Quraysh passed by the remains of these civilizations, the empty dwellings of the Thamud carved into the mountains, the desolate cities of the people of Lot. The question is rhetorical. Yes, Allah did destroy them. The Quraysh knew these stories and often told them around their fires. The verse uses this knowledge as evidence. If Allah destroyed the earlier disbelievers for their denial, why do the present disbelievers think they are immune? The law of divine justice does not change. The destruction of the ancients is a preview of the Day of Decision. Their worldly punishment was a foretaste of the eternal punishment. The verse also implies that the power that destroyed those mighty nations can easily destroy the Quraysh. They are not stronger or more numerous than the people of 'Ad or Pharaoh. The question invites the listener to learn from history instead of repeating its mistakes. It is a call to reason. The surah will now state that the same fate awaits the later disbelievers, linking the past to the present and future. The refrain will then punctuate this argument.
17 | And We will make the later disbelievers follow them. | The historical warning is brought directly into the present and future: "And We will make the later disbelievers follow them." The Arabic "thumma nutbi'uhum al-akhareen" uses "thumma" (then) to indicate a sequence. After the destruction of the earlier generations, Allah will cause the later ones—the disbelievers of Mecca and all who follow their path—to join them in the same fate. The verb "nutbi'uhum" means to cause to follow, to append, to join. The later disbelievers will be appended to the earlier ones, like a chain of condemned souls. This could refer to their destruction in this world, as some were killed at Badr, or it could refer primarily to their joining in the punishment of the Hereafter. Both meanings are valid and reinforce each other. The verse is a direct threat to the Quraysh. They are the "akhareen," the later ones. The earlier nations they spoke of as legends will become their companions in Hell. The phrase also indicates a divine principle: disbelief is a single, continuous chain throughout history. The deniers of all eras are united in their crime and united in their punishment. The surah is telling the Prophet that the opposition he faces is not a new phenomenon; it is the latest link in a long chain of rebellion, and it will be dealt with in the same manner. The verse provides a grim certainty. Just as the earlier destruction was a fulfillment of a promise, the coming punishment is equally certain. The surah now states the general principle behind this historical pattern.
18 | This is how We deal with the wicked. | The surah articulates the divine law that governs the destruction of the disbelievers: "This is how We deal with the wicked." The Arabic "kadhalika naf'alu bil-mujrimeen" presents the punishment as a consistent, unchanging practice. "Kadhalika" (like that) refers to the destruction and the joining together in punishment. This is not an arbitrary act of anger; it is the established way of Allah in dealing with the "mujrimeen," the criminals, those who commit the crime of disbelief and oppression. The word "mujrimeen" labels the deniers for what they truly are. They are not merely "misguided" or "unconvinced"; they are criminals who have rejected the clear evidence and waged war against the truth. Their punishment is not disproportionate; it is exactly what they deserve. The verse asserts the justice of Allah. The divine law is not secret; it is announced and demonstrated through history. The Quraysh are given fair warning: We deal with the wicked in this way. If you are among the wicked, this is your fate. The verse also serves as a warning to all who hear the Quran. The law applies universally. Any individual or nation that follows the path of the mujrimeen will meet the same end. The surah now brings back the haunting refrain, sealing this historical argument with the pronouncement of woe.
19 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain returns, punctuating the argument from history: "Woe on that Day to the deniers!" After establishing that the destruction of the ancients is a pattern and that the later disbelievers will follow, the surah pronounces the woe again. This repetition sears the warning into the heart. The deniers who heard the stories of the ancients but did not take heed are now explicitly told that the "wayl" is for them. The Day that the earlier disbelievers faced in a preliminary way through worldly destruction will be faced in its full, eternal form by all deniers on the Day of Decision. The refrain acts as a powerful punctuation mark, closing the historical section. It reminds the listener that the lesson of history is not academic; it is a matter of personal, eternal consequence. The deniers are not just hearing about others; they are being told that they are next in line for the "wayl." The rhythm of the surah is built around this refrain. It creates a sense of impending doom. The believer who recites this verse feels the chill of the warning and renews his determination to be among those who accept the truth, not those who deny it. The surah now shifts from the historical evidence to the evidence from human creation, beginning another cycle of argument that will also be capped by the refrain.
20 | Did We not create you from a humble fluid, | The surah now turns to the second major proof of Allah's power and the reality of the resurrection: the creation of the human being itself. "Did We not create you from a humble fluid?" The Arabic "a-lam nakhluqkum min ma'in maheen" is a rhetorical question that strikes at the very core of human arrogance. The "ma'in maheen" is a lowly, despised fluid—the sperm-drop. The word "maheen" comes from "mahana," meaning to be weak, contemptible, and insignificant. The human being who walks the earth with such pride, who denies the Day of Judgment, who builds empires and oppresses others, began as this utterly humble substance. The question is a devastating humiliation for the deniers. How can a creature whose origin is so base be arrogant? How can he doubt the power of the One who transformed that despised drop into a complete, intelligent human? The verse echoes the arguments of Al-Qiyamah and Al-Insan, which also used the sperm-drop as evidence for the resurrection. The logic is irrefutable: if Allah could create you from a drop of fluid, He can certainly recreate you after death. The recreation is, by human reasoning, easier than the original creation. The word "maheen" also implies that the substance itself has no intrinsic value or power. It is not a glorious origin. The glory of the human being is not in his physical beginning but in the soul that Allah breathed into him and the guidance He offered. The deniers, by rejecting that guidance, have debased themselves even further than their humble origin. The verse calls them to reflect on their own bodies. Their very existence is a walking proof of the Creator's power. The surah will now describe the stages of that creation, leading to the secure place of the womb.
21 | placing it in a secure place | After reminding humanity of their humble origin from a despised fluid, the surah continues the stages of creation: “placing it in a secure place.” The Arabic “faja‘alnahu fee qararin makeen” refers to the womb of the mother. The word “qarar” means a place of settlement, a firm resting place, and “makeen” means firmly established, secure, and well-protected. The fertilized ovum, that tiny drop, is implanted in the lining of the uterus, a place that Allah has designed to be a fortress of safety. It is shielded by the pelvic bones, cushioned by layers of muscle and tissue, and sealed by the cervix. In that secure place, the developing human being is protected from shocks, from temperature changes, from infections, and from the outside world. It is provided with nourishment through the placenta and the umbilical cord, receiving oxygen and food while its waste is carried away. This verse is a profound sign of Allah’s care and wisdom. The lowly drop is not discarded; it is given a place of honor and security where it will develop into a complete human. The description refutes any notion that human creation is a random, chaotic process. It is a precisely planned sequence. The secure place is a manifestation of Allah’s mercy. The mother herself may not even be fully aware of the intricate protection system that Allah has designed. The verse also connects to the theme of resurrection. The womb is a dark, enclosed space, yet from it emerges a living being. Similarly, the grave is a dark, enclosed space, yet from it the human will emerge on the Day of Judgment. The One who created the first secure place and brought forth life from it can certainly do it again. The deniers who doubt the resurrection are being asked to look at their own beginning, which was in a secure place inside their mothers. If they accept that they came from that hidden, protected space into the open world, why do they reject coming from the hidden space of the grave into the open plain of Judgment? The verse invites reflection on the miracle of the womb, a miracle that every human being has personally experienced but easily forgets.
22 | until an appointed time? | The secure place of the womb is not an eternal dwelling; it has a fixed term. “Until an appointed time.” The Arabic “ila qadarin ma‘loom” means until a specific, known measure. The duration of pregnancy is not random. Allah has decreed a term for each fetus, usually around nine months, but varying according to His wisdom. Some are born earlier, some later, but every birth happens at the time that Allah has precisely ordained. The phrase “qadarin ma‘loom” indicates that the term is known to Allah before the child is conceived. It is part of the divine decree written by the angel at the time of the soul’s breathing. This appointed time is a proof of Allah’s meticulous planning. The development of the fetus follows a strict timeline: the nutfah stage, the ‘alaqah stage, the mudghah stage, the formation of bones, the clothing of bones with flesh, all within specific periods. The appointed time also includes the individual lifespan of each human being, which is fixed and cannot be advanced or delayed even by a moment. The verse refutes the materialist notion that birth is a mere biological accident. It is a divinely scheduled event. This also serves as a metaphor for the larger appointed time of the Day of Judgment. Just as the fetus must emerge from the womb when its term is complete, so must creation emerge from the world when its term is complete. The deniers demand to know when the Day will come, but it is a “qadarin ma‘loom,” a fixed measure known only to Allah. The verse links the microcosm of the womb to the macrocosm of the universe. Both have an appointed term. The One who set the term for the fetus will bring the term for the world. The certainty of birth after nine months is a daily witness to the certainty of resurrection after death. The verse ends with a question mark in the translation, reflecting the rhetorical nature: “until an appointed time?” as if to say, “Did We not do all this for a fixed, known duration?”
23 | We ˹perfectly˺ ordained ˹its development˺. How excellent are We in doing so! | The surah completes the creation argument with a declaration of divine power and perfection: “We perfectly ordained its development. How excellent are We in doing so!” The Arabic “faqadarna fani‘ma al-qadirun” is a powerful statement. The verb “qadarna” comes from “qadara,” meaning to measure, to determine, to proportion, to ordain with precision. Allah ordained the stages, the size, the form, the faculties, the gender, the temperament—all in perfect proportion. The development from a single cell to a complex organism with trillions of cells, each in its proper place, each organ functioning in harmony, is a marvel of engineering that defies human comprehension. The word “faqadarna” also links back to “qadar,” the divine decree. Everything was pre-measured. Then the verse praises the One who does this: “fani‘ma al-qadirun,” how excellent are We, the Ordainers, the Determiners! The word “al-qadirun” is the plural of “al-qadir,” the All-Powerful, but here it emphasizes the attribute of precise measuring and ordaining. The excellence refers both to the perfection of the creation and to the majesty of the Creator. The human body, with its intricate systems, is a testament to this excellence. The brain, the eye, the heart, the immune system, the DNA code—all are examples of supreme design. The verse is a celebration of divine craftsmanship. It answers the deniers’ doubt about resurrection by appealing to the observable miracle of their own bodies. If Allah is so excellent in ordaining the first creation, how could He be incapable of the second? The verse also invites the believer to a state of awe and gratitude. Every human being is a walking masterpiece. The correct response to this is to bow to the Maker in worship. The denier, however, sees this perfection and attributes it to blind evolution or to multiple gods, which is the height of ingratitude. The surah now brings back the refrain, marking the end of this creation argument.
24 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain thunders once more, this time capping the argument from human creation: “Woe on that Day to the deniers!” After the detailed description of the humble origin, the secure womb, the appointed term, and the perfect ordainment, the verdict falls again. The deniers, who have been shown the overwhelming evidence in their own bodies, still reject the resurrection and the Day of Decision. For them, the “wayl” is confirmed. The placement of the refrain here is deeply significant. It tells the listener that the evidence of creation is not merely for intellectual satisfaction; it is a matter of eternal life and death. To deny the Creator after such clear signs is a crime that merits the woe. The refrain acts as a divine boundary marker. Every time a major argument is completed, the woe is pronounced, dividing the believers, who accept the argument, from the deniers, who reject it. It personalizes the warning. The listener is made to ask himself: am I among those who reflect on the miracle of my own creation and believe, or am I among the deniers to whom the woe is addressed? The repetition is not monotonous; it is emotionally intensifying. Each occurrence lands with a heavier weight, because the evidence has been accumulating. The denier who hears this after the stages of fetal development has no excuse. The woe is the just consequence of a willful blindness to the signs within one’s own self. The surah now moves from the creation of the human being to the creation of the earth as a dwelling place, presenting yet another set of signs, which will be followed by the refrain once more.
25 | Have We not made the earth a lodging | The surah shifts from the microcosm of the human body to the macrocosm of the earth: “Have We not made the earth a lodging?” The Arabic “a-lam naj‘al al-arda kifata” uses the word “kifat,” which means a place that gathers, contains, and holds. The earth is a “lodging” in the sense that it collects the living on its surface and the dead in its interior. It is a container, a dwelling, a receptacle. This rhetorical question invites reflection on the earth’s function. The earth’s surface is a home for humanity. It provides a stable ground to walk on, a place to build houses, to plant crops, to raise families. Its gravity holds everything in place. Its atmosphere protects from harmful radiation. It is a “kifat” for the living. Then, when people die, the same earth becomes their grave. It covers them, contains their remains, and hides their decay. The earth is a lodging for both the living and the dead, as the next verse will elaborate. The word “kifat” implies a sense of gathering and holding. The earth pulls things toward it and keeps them. This is a sign of Allah’s providence. The deniers who walk on the earth every day take it for granted. The verse asks them to consider who made this planet a suitable lodging. Could it have been by chance? The earth’s perfect distance from the sun, its axial tilt, its magnetic field, its water cycle—all are precisely calibrated to make it a habitable lodging. The question also connects to the resurrection. The earth that holds the dead in its bosom will one day “expel” them, as the Quran describes elsewhere. The lodging is temporary for the dead, just as it is temporary for the living. The One who made the earth a gathering place for the first life will make it a gathering place for the resurrection. The surah continues to explain the dual function of this lodging.
26 | for the living and the dead, | The dual function of the earth as a lodging is now explicitly stated: “for the living and the dead.” The Arabic “ahya’an wa amwata” indicates that the earth serves both categories of human existence. For the living, the earth provides a surface on which they dwell, eat, drink, work, and worship. Its soil grows their food, its springs quench their thirst, its minerals supply their needs. It is a carpet spread out, a resting place. For the dead, the earth provides a grave. It receives their bodies, covers them, and holds them in trust until the Day of Resurrection. The earth is a silent custodian of the deceased, keeping their remains until the trumpet is blown. This dual capacity is one of the great signs of Allah’s wisdom. The same ground that bursts with life in the spring receives the dead in every season. The living walk over the dead, often unaware that beneath their feet lie the bodies of countless generations. The earth does not complain; it fulfills its function. The verse inspires a profound reflection on mortality. Every human being is either among the “living” walking on the earth or among the “dead” buried within it. This should humble the arrogant. The deniers who strut on the earth’s surface, denying the resurrection, will soon be part of the “dead” held by that same earth. Then, when the Day comes, the earth will give them up. The dual function also points to the cycle of life, death, and rebirth. The earth that is a lodging for the dead is not a permanent prison; it is a temporary holding place. The resurrection is the moment when the lodging of the dead will be evacuated. The verse is a memento mori, a reminder of death, that softens the heart and makes the Hereafter a pressing reality. The surah now adds another feature of the earth that makes it a suitable lodging: the towering mountains.
27 | and placed upon it towering, firm mountains, and given you fresh water to drink? | The description of the earth’s design continues with two more blessings: “and placed upon it towering, firm mountains, and given you fresh water to drink?” The Arabic “wa ja‘alna feeha rawasiya shamikhatin wa asqaynakum ma’an furata” uses the word “rawasiya” for mountains that are firmly rooted, acting like pegs to stabilize the earth’s crust. “Shamikhat” means lofty, towering, majestic. The mountains are not just piles of rock; they are awe-inspiring, snow-capped peaks that inspire wonder and humility. They also serve a practical purpose: they stabilize the earth, preventing it from shaking excessively, as modern geology has confirmed with the concept of isostasy. They are reservoirs of fresh water, storing snow and ice that melt to feed rivers. They are sources of minerals and beautiful landscapes. The phrase “and given you fresh water to drink” uses “ma’an furata” for sweet, palatable, intensely fresh water. Water is the essence of life. The earth’s water cycle, driven by the sun, wind, and mountains, provides this fresh water. The mountains force moist air to rise, cool, and condense into rain and snow. The rainwaters flow down the mountains, forming streams and rivers, and are stored in underground aquifers. The “fresh water” is a direct gift from Allah, not a human product. The deniers drink this water every day without thinking. The rhetorical question “Have We not...?” is meant to awaken their consciousness to these favors. The mountains and the water are signs pointing to the Generous Creator. This verse, like the previous ones, is an argument for gratitude and belief. The One who provided these essential supports for life on earth will surely hold the human accountable for how he used them. The description ends here, and the inevitable refrain returns.
28 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain once again punctuates the argument from the earth’s design: “Woe on that Day to the deniers!” After the listing of the earth as a lodging for the living and the dead, the towering mountains, and the fresh water, the deniers are given this thunderous warning. The blessings are not an end in themselves; they are signs that should lead to faith and gratitude. The deniers, however, enjoy these blessings while denying the One who bestowed them and rejecting the Day of accountability. Therefore, the “wayl” is their due. The placement of the refrain here links the denial of the Creator’s favors to the denial of the Day of Decision. The two denials are intertwined. If a person truly appreciated the gift of fresh water, he would be grateful to the Giver and would fear the Day when he will be asked about that water. The refrain acts as a moral hammer. It forces the listener to see the connection between the earth’s beauty and the Hereafter’s terror. The surah has now presented three major arguments—historical destruction, human creation, and the earth’s design—each followed by the refrain. The effect is cumulative. The deniers are boxed in by evidence from history, from their own bodies, and from the world around them. There is no escape from the conclusion, and therefore no escape from the woe. The surah now shifts from the arguments and the refrain to a direct, dramatic address to the disbelievers, depicting the scene of their entry into Hell.
29 | ˹The disbelievers will be told,˺ “Proceed into that ˹Fire˺ which you used to deny! | The scene dramatically shifts to the Day of Decision, and the surah quotes the words that the disbelievers will hear as they are driven toward Hell: “Proceed into that Fire which you used to deny!” The Arabic “intaliqoo ila ma kuntum bihi tukadhdhiboon” is a command of humiliation. “Intaliqoo” means go, proceed, march. They are herded into the very punishment they mocked. The phrase “that which you used to deny” points directly to the Fire, the very Hell they said did not exist. Their denial is thrown back in their faces. The command carries a tone of dismissal and contempt. In the world, they walked freely and arrogantly, denying the truth. Now they are ordered to walk into the Fire they scoffed at. This is the ultimate irony and justice. The word “tukadhdhiboon” is the same verb from which “mukadhdhibeen” (deniers) is derived. The crime is revisited upon the criminals. They are not given a choice; the angels will drive them. The verse captures the exact moment of transfer from the plain of Judgment to the gates of Hell. The deniers, who used to ask mockingly, “When is this Day?” and who called the warnings “magic from the ancients,” now hear the command to enter the reality they denied. There is no argument, no appeal. The truth is now inescapable. The verse serves as a terrifying warning to those who hear it while still in the world. The invitation to faith is still open, but once the command “Proceed” is given, the time for belief is over. The surah will now describe the specific features of that Fire, beginning with its shade.
30 | Proceed into the shade ˹of smoke˺ which rises in three columns, | The description of the Fire continues with a horrifying detail: “Proceed into the shade of smoke which rises in three columns.” The Arabic “intaliqoo ila dhillin dhee thalathi shu‘ab” paints a picture that shatters any hope of relief. In the scorching desert of Arabia, shade is a precious blessing, a refuge from the blazing sun. Here, the disbelievers are mockingly told to seek the “shade” of Hell. But this shade is not a cool, pleasant shelter. It is a “dhill,” a shadow, but one made of thick, black smoke rising from the flames. The smoke separates into “three columns” or “three branches.” Some commentators say this means the smoke rises from Hell in three massive plumes, dividing and spreading out. Others say the three columns refer to the three components of the punishment: the fire, the smoke, and the sparks. Another interpretation is that the three columns represent the three types of sinners who are its main inhabitants. Regardless, the image is one of an oppressive, suffocating darkness. The word “dhill” is used ironically. They sought shade from the worldly heat, but now they find a shade that offers no relief. The smoke is dense, choking, and dark. The number three emphasizes the immensity and the multiple layers of torment. The verse is a direct response to the deniers’ love of ease and comfort in this world. They enjoyed the pleasant shades of their gardens and palaces, neglecting the Hereafter. Now they are given the shade of Hell. The command “Proceed into the shade” is a bitter sarcasm, highlighting their utter helplessness. The surah now clarifies why this shade is a punishment rather than a relief.
31 | providing neither coolness nor shelter from the flames. | The nature of the hellish shade is further clarified: “providing neither coolness nor shelter from the flames.” The Arabic “la dhaleelin wa la yughnee min al-lahab” means it is not cool and refreshing (dhaleel), nor does it avail or protect (yughnee) against the leaping flames (lahab). In the world, shade provides two things: a drop in temperature and a barrier from the direct rays of the sun. The shade of Hell provides neither. It is a shade that is itself scorching hot, composed of choking, fiery smoke. It does not cool the skin; it burns it. It does not shield from the flames; it is part of the flames. The disbelievers are trapped between the fire below and the suffocating smoke above. There is no escape. This triple negation—no coolness, no protection—is a perfect description of absolute misery. The verse uses the physical experience of the Arabs, who valued shade as a life-saving necessity, to describe a torment beyond their worst nightmares. The deniers who used to relax in the cool shade of their date palms, who mocked the idea of Hell, will now vainly search for a cool spot in a shade that is itself an instrument of torture. The word “lahab” specifically refers to the leaping, dancing flames that consume everything. The shade does not block these flames; it is permeated by them. The verse is a devastating portrayal of hopelessness. The surah now continues to describe the sparks that fly from that fire, using vivid imagery from the Arab environment.
32 | Indeed, it hurls sparks ˹as big˺ as huge castles, | The horror of Hell is magnified by the description of its sparks: “Indeed, it hurls sparks as big as huge castles.” The Arabic “innaha tarmee bi-shararin kal-qasr” uses the word “sharar” for sparks, the glowing particles that fly from a fire. But these are not the tiny sparks of a worldly campfire. Each spark is as large as a “qasr,” a huge castle or fortress. This is an unimaginable scale. The fire of Hell is so vast and furious that its flying embers are the size of towering buildings. If a single spark is as large as a castle, how massive must the Fire itself be? The comparison to a castle also evokes the idea of a fortified, solid structure. The sparks are not fleeting; they are like enormous, burning projectiles hurled by the Fire. This image would strike fear into the hearts of the Meccans, who knew the solid, imposing castles of the region, such as those built by the Ghassanids or the ancient structures they passed on their journeys. The verse is a powerful escalation of the terror. The fire does not simply burn; it actively “hurls” these colossal sparks at the inmates. The verb “tarmee” (it hurls) indicates an aggressive, violent action. Hell is not a passive furnace; it is an active, wrathful punisher. The description of the sparks also connects to the earlier mention of the flames (lahab). The fire is so intense that it ejects these castle-sized embers. The deniers who built grand houses and prided themselves on their structures will find that the sparks of Hell rival their proudest constructions. The surah now adds another dimension to the description of the sparks, using a second simile.
33 | and ˹as dark˺ as black camels. | The sparks of Hell are described with a second, equally vivid simile: “and as dark as black camels.” The Arabic “ka-annahu jimalatun sufr” completes the picture. Some recitations have “jimalatun sufr” (yellow camels), and others have “jimalatun sufr” (dark, black camels), both of which are authentic and convey complementary meanings. The sparks are not only as huge as castles, but their color resembles that of a herd of majestic, dark or yellowish-black camels. The camel was the most prized possession of the Arab, a symbol of wealth, strength, and beauty. A herd of camels moving across the desert was a magnificent sight. Here, the sparks of Hell are compared to that familiar image, but twisted into a source of terror. The sparks, massive and dark, leap and fly like a stampeding herd, but instead of being a sign of life and prosperity, they are the harbingers of death and punishment. The comparison is shocking precisely because it uses something the Arabs admired to depict something utterly horrifying. It forces the mind to grasp the unimaginable by linking it to the familiar. Some commentators have suggested that the sparks resemble the camels’ dark silhouettes against the fire, or that they are the size of camels and move with a similar undulating motion. The word “sufr” can also mean yellow, which in the context of fire would be the bright, blazing color. Others say it means dark or black, indicating the thick, sooty smoke that gathers around the flame. Both images amplify the terror. The surah now brings back the refrain, sealing this terrifying description with the pronouncement of woe.
34 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain returns after the vivid description of Hell’s shade and sparks: “Woe on that Day to the deniers!” This is the fifth occurrence of the refrain in the surah, and it comes after a passage that makes the reality of Hell terrifyingly tangible. The deniers are the ones who, when they hear these descriptions, still refuse to believe. They are the ones who mocked the concept of Hell and called it a fable. Now they are told that this very Hell, with its three-columned smoke, its non-cooling shade, its castle-sized sparks, and its black-camel-like embers, is their destination. The “wayl” is not an abstract curse; it is the encapsulation of all the horrors just described. The refrain acts as a summary and a verdict. Every detail of the punishment is a component of the woe. The rhythm of the surah is designed so that the listener feels the weight of this refrain after each block of description. The repetition etches the warning deeper into the soul. For the believer, it intensifies the gratitude for being saved from such a fate and the fear of ever straying into the path of the deniers. For the skeptic, it is a relentless battering of his defenses. The surah now moves from the description of the Fire to the state of the disbelievers on the Day, highlighting their utter helplessness and the finality of the judgment.
35 | On that Day they will not ˹be in a position to˺ speak, | The scene shifts to the courtroom of the Hereafter: “On that Day they will not be in a position to speak.” The Arabic “hadha yawmu la yantiqoon” means this is a Day when they will not speak. However, other Quranic verses mention that the disbelievers will speak, confess, and argue on the Day of Judgment. The reconciliation lies in the understanding that the Day has many stages, or that they will not be able to speak in their own defense. Their tongues will be sealed, and their hands and feet will testify. They will try to speak, but they will not be given permission to utter the excuses they want to make. Their speech will be incoherent, or they will be struck dumb by the sheer terror. The phrase “la yantiqoon” denies them the ability to speak effectively. In the world, they were eloquent in their mockery and their arguments against the truth. They used their tongues to ridicule the Prophet, to spread lies, to defend their idols. On that Day, those same tongues will be useless. The power of speech, which they misused, will be taken from them. This is a profound humiliation. The courtroom of Allah is not a place for the eloquence of lawyers and the sophistry of the debater. It is a place where the truth is manifest and excuses are silenced. The verse strikes at the arrogance of the orators and chieftains of Quraysh, who prided themselves on their speech. On the Day of Decision, their golden tongues will be paralyzed. The surah adds another layer to this silencing: they will not even be permitted to offer any justification.
36 | nor will they be permitted to offer excuses. | The silencing of the deniers is complete: “nor will they be permitted to offer excuses.” The Arabic “wa la yu’dhanu lahum fa-ya‘tadhiroon” means they will not be granted permission to present their excuses. Even if they could speak, they would not be allowed to make their case. The “i‘tidhar” is the act of offering an apology or a justification. In the world, when a person is accused, he has the right to defend himself. But on the Day of Decision, the evidence is so overwhelming, the record is so complete, and the witnesses are so numerous—the prophets, the angels, the body parts—that any excuse would be a futile lie. The court of Allah has no need for a defense. The deniers are not being tried to discover the truth; the truth is already known and presented. The refusal of permission to excuse themselves is part of the punishment. It is the ultimate humiliation. They are not even given the dignity of a hearing. The word “yu’dhanu” (permitted) emphasizes that all authority belongs to Allah. No one can speak unless He allows it. The deniers, who lived their whole lives rejecting Allah’s authority, now find themselves utterly under His judgment, with no power to negotiate or plead. This verse is a chilling reminder that the time for excuses is now, in this world, through repentance. Once the Day arrives, the door of excuse is slammed shut. The surah now pronounces the refrain, as if to seal this state of helplessness with the final curse.
37 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain falls again, marking the end of the description of the deniers’ helplessness: “Woe on that Day to the deniers!” The “wayl” is pronounced after the stripping away of their speech and their right to excuse themselves. They are now mute, condemned, and awaiting the Fire. The repetition of the refrain here intensifies the sense of impending doom. The deniers who were so vocal in their mockery are now silent. The woe is the only appropriate response to their state. The surah has now repeatedly hammered this refrain, each time after exposing another aspect of their crime or punishment. The effect is hypnotic and terrifying. The listener is forced to feel the inevitability of the curse. The surah now moves to the direct address from Allah to the gathered disbelievers, a speech of absolute justice and terrifying challenge.
38 | ˹They will be told by Allah,˺ “This is the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision: We have gathered you along with earlier disbelievers ˹for punishment˺. | On the Day of Judgment, Allah Himself will address the deniers, reminding them of what they were told: “This is the Day of Final Decision: We have gathered you along with earlier disbelievers for punishment.” The Arabic “hadha yawm al-fasl jama‘nakum wal-awwaleen” directly names the Day, echoing the earlier verse. “Yawm al-Fasl” is the Day of Sorting, the Day of Decisive Judgment. The disbelievers are now witnessing it. Allah informs them that they have been gathered—them and the “awwaleen,” the earlier generations of disbelievers who came before them. The gathering is for the purpose of punishment, as the context makes clear. This is the fulfillment of the earlier verse: “And We will make the later disbelievers follow them.” Now, the followers and the followed are all present, united in their doom. The phrase “We have gathered you” emphasizes the divine power to assemble all of humanity, from the first to the last, in a single plain. No one is missed. The generations of deniers, who lived centuries apart, are now standing side by side. The leaders of misguidance and their blind followers are together, sharing the punishment. There is no distinction of era or status. The address is humiliating; it is as if Allah is saying, “Here is the Day you denied. Look at it. Here are your fellow deniers from all ages. Enjoy their company in the Fire.” The verse connects back to the rhetorical questions about the destruction of the earlier disbelievers. That was the worldly punishment; now comes the eternal one. The gathering is a proof that the Day is indeed a “fasl,” a final separation. The surah now presents a chilling challenge to the gathered deniers.
39 | So if you have a scheme ˹to save yourselves˺, then use it against Me.” | The address from Allah to the deniers reaches its terrifying climax: “So if you have a scheme to save yourselves, then use it against Me.” The Arabic “fa-in kana lakum kaydun fa-keedoon” is a direct, sarcastic challenge. The word “kayd” means a plot, a scheme, a stratagem. In the world, the disbelievers were constantly plotting against the Prophet and the message. They held secret councils, they planned assassinations, they used propaganda, they tried every trick to defeat the truth. They thought they were clever. Now, on the Day of Decision, Allah challenges them: bring your schemes, your plans, your tricks. Use them against Me now. Can your plotting save you from this Fire? Can your idols, your wealth, your tribal alliances devise a way out? The challenge is, of course, impossible. They are utterly powerless. The “kayd” that they once wielded with arrogance has evaporated. The verse exposes the absurdity of their former confidence. It is the ultimate put-down. The deniers who thought they could outsmart the divine plan are now being mocked by the Almighty. The word “fa-keedoon” (then plot) is a command of derision. It is as if Allah is telling them to go ahead and try their best, and see what happens. This verse is one of the most striking examples of divine irony in the Quran. It shows the complete reversal of power. The schemers have become the schemed-against. The verse also serves as a warning to all who rely on their own cunning to circumvent the laws of Allah. On that Day, all human plots will be crushed. The surah now pronounces the refrain once more, closing this section with the final woe.
40 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain thunders for the seventh time, capping the divine challenge: “Woe on that Day to the deniers!” After the terrifying address, the mockery, the challenge, and the exposure of their powerlessness, the woe is pronounced again. The deniers who were told to bring their schemes are now met with the curse. They have no scheme. They stand silent, humiliated, and the “wayl” descends upon them. The repetition of the refrain at this point emphasizes the finality of the judgment. There is no escape, no clever twist, no eleventh-hour rescue. The woe is the last word. The surah has now completed the section that specifically addresses the deniers and their punishment. The next verses will turn to the contrasting fate of the righteous, offering a breathtaking shift from the darkness of the Fire to the light and bliss of Paradise. The refrain will continue to punctuate the surah, but for now, the deniers have been thoroughly warned and their doom sealed. The listener is left with the echo of “wayl” ringing in the ears, a powerful motivator to flee from the path of denial and run toward the mercy of the Lord. This batch ends here, with the woe hanging over the deniers, ready for the transition to the hope-filled description that follows in the next verses.
41 | Indeed, the righteous will be amid ˹cool˺ shade and springs | After the relentless repetition of woe upon the deniers and the horrifying descriptions of Hell, the surah now offers a breathtaking contrast. "Indeed, the righteous will be amid cool shade and springs." The Arabic "inna al-muttaqeena fee dhilalin wa 'uyoon" opens the door to Paradise for the God-fearing. The word "muttaqeen" refers to those who have taqwa, who lived in constant awareness of Allah, fearing His punishment and seeking His mercy. They are the ones who believed, prayed, gave charity, and avoided what Allah forbade. Their reward is described in terms that are the direct opposite of the deniers' punishment. The deniers were told to proceed into the "shade of smoke" that provided no coolness. The righteous, in contrast, are in "dhilal," pleasant, cool, refreshing shades. This shade is not made of choking smoke but of lush trees, canopies, and eternal comfort. It is the shade of the Gardens, where the sun's heat does not penetrate, and the climate is perfect. The "uyoon" are springs—flowing sources of pure, delightful water. While the deniers were given the boiling, putrid fluids of Hell, the righteous are given crystalline springs that gush forth at their will. The pairing of shade and water is the essence of refreshment, especially for a desert-dwelling people. It signifies peace, satisfaction, and the quenching of every thirst. The verse uses the word "fee" (amid) to show they are enveloped, surrounded by these blessings. They are not just looking at the shade and springs from a distance; they are immersed in them. This is the immediate, tangible reality of their reward. The transition from the previous verses is stark and intentional. The same surah that hammered the deniers with "woe" now gently cradles the believers with "indeed." It is a literary and spiritual shift from terror to tranquility, demonstrating the dual nature of the divine message: a warning and a glad tiding. The believer hears this verse and feels his heart settle into hope after being shaken by fear. The taqwa that seemed burdensome in the world has blossomed into eternal comfort. The surah will now elaborate on the fruits that accompany these springs, completing the picture of their hospitality.
42 | and any fruit they desire. | The blessings of Paradise continue to be described in terms of complete fulfillment. "And any fruit they desire." The Arabic "wa fawakiha mimma yashtahoon" means they will have fruits, all kinds of fruits, whatever they crave. The word "yashtahoon" comes from "shahwa," meaning desire, appetite, longing. In Paradise, every desire for any fruit is immediately satisfied. There is no season, no scarcity, no waiting. The fruit is always ripe, always perfect, and always within reach. The verse highlights the personalized nature of the reward. Each believer will have exactly the fruits he or she loves most. This is a profound mercy. In the world, even the richest person might crave a fruit that is out of season or unavailable. In Paradise, the mere thought of a fruit brings it into existence. The verse also implies a removal of all dietary restrictions. The believers can eat whatever they desire without any harmful consequences, no illness, no fullness, only pure pleasure. The mention of fruit, rather than other foods, is significant because fruit is a symbol of pure, natural delight, a gift that combines beauty, fragrance, and taste. It is the food of leisure and luxury. The deniers in Hell are given choking food and boiling water; the righteous are given fruits of their choice. The contrast is absolute. The verse also serves as a motivation for the believer to control his desires in this world. The one who restrains his "shahawat" from the forbidden for the sake of Allah will find his lawful desires fulfilled beyond imagination in the Hereafter. The word "yashtahoon" indicates that the appetite itself is a source of pleasure, and its immediate satisfaction is a double pleasure. The believer is not a passive recipient; his desires actively shape his experience. The surah now adds the welcoming words that accompany these gifts, words that bring the deepest joy to the soul.
43 | ˹They will be told,˺ “Eat and drink happily for what you used to do.” | The divine hospitality reaches its emotional peak with the words that the believers will hear in Paradise. "They will be told, 'Eat and drink happily for what you used to do.'" The Arabic "kulu washrabu hanee'an bima kuntum ta'maloon" is an invitation and a commendation. The command "kulu washrabu" (eat and drink) is the warm welcome of a generous host. The word "hanee'an" means wholesomely, with complete pleasure, without any fear of harm, without any consequence. It is a state of pure, unadulterated enjoyment. But the most touching part is the reason given: "bima kuntum ta'maloon" (for what you used to do). The believers are not receiving this as a random handout; it is the direct result of their own deeds in the world. Every prayer they prayed, every fast they endured, every charity they gave, every temptation they resisted, every tear they shed in repentance—all of it has been transformed into this food and drink, this shade and these springs. The phrase "what you used to do" is a beautiful acknowledgment of their striving. It tells them that their efforts were not in vain, that every small act of obedience was recorded and is now being repaid with exquisite interest. The word "hanee'an" also implies that the food and drink are not just physically pleasing but are accompanied by a profound spiritual satisfaction. They eat and drink with the full approval of their Lord, with no guilt, no anxiety, no fear that the pleasure will end. This verse echoes the similar commendation in Al-Insan: "Your striving has been appreciated." The believers are honoured by having their own deeds recognized. The verse teaches the fundamental principle of the Hereafter: it is a world of reaping what you sowed. The deniers are told to "taste" their punishment for what they used to do; the righteous are told to "eat and drink" for what they used to do. The same law of cause and effect governs both outcomes. For the believer, this verse is a profound source of motivation. It transforms the daily acts of worship from dry obligations into seeds of future bliss. Every time a believer restrains himself from a halal pleasure out of fear of Allah, or engages in an act of obedience, he is storing up a "hanee'an" for the Day when he will hear these words. The surah now states the general principle behind this reward, linking back to the recurring refrain.
44 | Surely this is how We reward the good-doers. | The surah articulates the divine principle that governs the reward of the righteous: "Surely this is how We reward the good-doers." The Arabic "inna kadhalika najzee al-muhsineen" is a statement of consistent divine practice. "Kadhalika" (thus, in this manner) refers to the lavish rewards just described—the shade, springs, fruits, and the honoured invitation to eat and drink. This is not a special favor reserved for a few; it is the standard treatment that Allah accords to "al-muhsineen." The word "muhsineen" comes from "ihsan," which is the highest level of faith: to worship Allah as if you see Him. The muhsin is the one who excels in his deeds, performing them with excellence, sincerity, and awareness of Allah's presence. He goes beyond the minimum obligations into the realm of loving devotion. The verse uses "muhsineen" instead of "muslimeen" or "mu'mineen" to highlight that this level of reward is for those who perfected their faith and adorned it with beautiful deeds. However, by Allah's mercy, even those with lesser degrees of faith will eventually enter Paradise and receive their share of rewards, but the highest degrees of the described bliss are particularly for the muhsineen. The statement "this is how We reward" establishes a law. It tells the believers that Allah's generosity is not arbitrary; it is His way. They can trust in this promise. It also encourages them to strive for ihsan. The verse provides a moment of reflection after the emotional high of the divine invitation. It generalizes the specific, making the promise available to all who choose the path of excellence. The surah then brings back the refrain, contrasting the fate of the good-doers with the deniers, keeping the two groups in sharp opposition throughout the chapter. The refrain acts as a constant reminder that the beautiful rewards are not for everyone; they are for those who believed and did good, while the deniers are excluded.
45 | ˹But˺ woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain returns for the eighth time, this time immediately after the description of the reward of the good-doers. "But woe on that Day to the deniers!" The conjunction "but" is implied in the Arabic "waylun yawma'idhin lil-mukadhdhibeen," standing in stark contrast to the preceding verse. Allah rewards the good-doers in this generous manner, but the deniers are met with woe. The placement of the refrain here is a masterstroke. The reader or listener has just been bathing in the cool shade and springs of Paradise, tasting the delicious fruits and hearing the welcoming voice. Then, suddenly, the "wayl" crashes down like a hammer, reminding that this bliss is not universal. The deniers are excluded. The contrast heightens both the desirability of Paradise and the dreadfulness of Hell. The deniers are those who belied the "Day of Decision," who rejected the prophets, who mocked the warnings. They refused to be among the good-doers, so they cannot share in the reward. The woe is their portion. The verse also serves as a check on spiritual complacency. A believer might hear the descriptions of Paradise and feel a surge of hope, which is good, but the "woe" immediately reminds him that entry is conditional upon faith and deeds. It is a balanced spiritual psychology. The refrain continues to function as the drumbeat of the surah, marking the rhythm of justice. The surah now shifts back to the deniers, delivering a final, chilling admonition in the form of a sarcastic, temporary permission.
46 | “Eat and enjoy yourselves for a little while, ˹for˺ you are truly wicked.” | The surah now addresses the deniers directly with a statement dripping with irony and warning. "Eat and enjoy yourselves for a little while, for you are truly wicked." The Arabic "kulu wa tamatta'u qaleelan innakum mujrimoon" mirrors the words spoken to the righteous in Paradise, but with a terrifying difference. The righteous were told "Eat and drink happily" as a reward for their deeds. The wicked are told "Eat and enjoy yourselves" as a temporary respite before the punishment. The word "qaleelan" (for a little while) is the key. Their enjoyment in this world is brief, fleeting, and will soon be cut off by death. Then comes the condemnation: "innakum mujrimoon" (indeed, you are criminals). The word "mujrimoon" is stronger than mere disbelievers; it labels them as guilty criminals, those who have committed the heinous crime of denying their Creator. This verse is a profound example of the Quranic technique of "istidraj," the divine plan of giving the wicked respite and worldly pleasures so that they increase in sin and become completely deserving of the punishment. The command "eat and enjoy yourselves" is not a genuine permission; it is a statement of the reality of their current state, coupled with a threat. It is as if Allah is saying, "Go ahead, enjoy your few days in the world. Eat your fill, laugh, and be merry. But know that you are criminals, and your time is short." The verse serves as a wake-up call. The deniers are so absorbed in their worldly pleasures that they have forgotten their criminal state. The verse shatters their complacency. The "enjoyment" is a bait that leads to the trap. The surah now follows this with the inevitable refrain, as if to say: this brief enjoyment is the prelude to the eternal woe.
47 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain follows the ironic permission, marking the ninth occurrence: "Woe on that Day to the deniers!" The "wayl" is the reality behind the fleeting enjoyment. The deniers are told to eat and enjoy for a little while, but the end is woe. The verse creates a powerful juxtaposition: present pleasure versus future pain. The deniers, who choose the brief enjoyment of the world over the eternal bliss of Paradise, are making a disastrous trade. The woe is the price they will pay for their choice. The repetition of the refrain here emphasizes that the worldly respite is not a sign of Allah's approval but a prelude to His wrath. The deniers might misinterpret their worldly success as evidence that they are on the right path, as the Quraysh did with their wealth and trade. The Quran corrects this misunderstanding. The "wayl" is what truly awaits them, and it is near. The surah has now used the refrain nine times, building a crescendo that will reach its climax in the final verses. The listener is conditioned to expect the refrain after each block, and each time it lands with increased weight. The surah now gives one final reason for the woe: their refusal to bow to Allah.
48 | When it is said to them, “Bow down ˹before Allah,” they do not bow. | The surah identifies a specific, concrete act of disobedience that encapsulates the deniers' entire attitude. "When it is said to them, 'Bow down before Allah,' they do not bow." The Arabic "wa idha qeela lahumu irka'u la yarka'oon" refers to the command to perform ruku' (bowing) in prayer, which represents the entire act of salah and, by extension, submission to Allah. The deniers are called to worship, to humble themselves physically before the Creator, but they refuse. This verse could refer to the Quraysh's refusal to pray when the Prophet called them to Islam. Many of them said they would not bow down to a God they could not see. Some were too proud to put their foreheads on the ground. Their refusal to bow is a symbol of their arrogance and their rejection of servitude. The verse also has an eschatological dimension. On the Day of Judgment, when the great horror is revealed and Allah commands the people to prostrate, the believers will fall down in prostration, while the backs of the hypocrites and disbelievers will become stiff like boards, unable to bow. This is mentioned in Surah Al-Qalam. The refusal to bow is a damning indictment. It shows that their problem is not intellectual confusion; it is arrogance and disobedience. The simple act of bending the back is a line they refuse to cross because it would mean admitting they are not gods, they are not independent, they are servants of a Higher Power. This verse highlights the centrality of prayer in Islam. The refusal to pray is a defining characteristic of the deniers, as seen in Al-Muddaththir and Al-Qiyamah. The verse is a powerful call to the believer to cherish and guard the prayer, to never be among those who, when called to bow, refuse. The surah now pronounces the final refrain of woe, the tenth and last in the surah, as the ultimate verdict on this refusal.
49 | Woe on that Day to the deniers! | The refrain appears for the tenth and final time: "Woe on that Day to the deniers!" This is the last nail in the coffin. After exposing their specific sin—the refusal to bow—the woe is sealed. The number ten in Arabic rhetoric often signifies completeness and finality. The tenfold "wayl" is a complete, all-encompassing curse upon the deniers. The refrain has been the backbone of the surah, and its final occurrence here leaves the listener with a profound sense of the certainty and severity of the judgment. The deniers are defined by their denial, and their punishment is defined by woe. The repetition has hammered this association so deeply that the very word "deniers" now echoes with the sound of "wayl." The verse is a final warning. The door of repentance is still open in the world, but once the Day comes, the woe is irreversible. The surah has used the refrain to create a powerful emotional and spiritual impact, moving the listener through cycles of evidence, warning, and judgment. Now, with the tenth woe pronounced, the case against the deniers is complete. The surah ends with a verse that shifts from warning to a profound rhetorical question, leaving the listener in a state of urgent reflection. The question challenges the very foundation of the deniers' rejection and opens the door for the next revelation, the next opportunity to believe.
50 | So what message after this ˹Quran˺ would they believe in? | The surah concludes with a penetrating, unforgettable question: "So what message after this Quran would they believe in?" The Arabic "fa-bi-ayyi hadeethin ba'dahu yu'minoon" is the final challenge to the deniers. The particle "fa" (so) links the question to everything that has preceded: the oaths, the evidence from history, the signs in creation, the descriptions of the Day, the horrors of Hell, the bliss of Paradise, and the tenfold woe. After all of this, if they still do not believe in this "hadeeth" (message, discourse, revelation), then what else will they believe in? The word "hadeeth" here refers to the Quran, the greatest, most truthful, most eloquent, most convincing message ever delivered. If the Quran does not move them, no other message will. If they reject the word of Allah, what word of who will they accept? The question exposes the irrationality of their position. It is not that the Quran lacks evidence; it is that they have shut their hearts. The question also carries an implicit prophecy: they will not believe in any other message because the Quran is the pinnacle of divine revelation. There is no "hadeeth" after it that can guide if it does not. The question is both a lament and a condemnation. The surah that began with powerful oaths by the forces of nature and angels delivering the reminder ends with the declaration that this reminder is the final, ultimate word. No further arguments, no additional signs, no new prophets will come. If they reject this, they are hopeless. The verse is a call to urgency. The listener who has not yet believed is asked to look at the Quran, to recognize its truth, and to seize the opportunity before the Day when no speech will be permitted. The surah's ending leaves the matter hanging in the air, a challenge for every human being until the Day of Judgment. It is a fitting conclusion to a surah that has relentlessly pursued the theme of denial and the certainty of the Day of Decision.
