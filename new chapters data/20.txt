1 | Ṭâ-Hâ. | The surah opens with the disconnected letters Ṭâ-Hâ. These are among the mysterious openings of certain Quranic chapters, whose precise meanings are known fully only to Allah. Many scholars, however, have offered insights. One strong view is that these letters draw attention to the miraculous nature of the Quran, composed of the very same letters of the Arabic language yet inimitable. They serve as a wake-up call for the listener, breaking the silence and demanding reflection. Some companions and early interpreters considered Ṭâ-Hâ to be one of the names of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, or a form of address meaning "O man" or "O peace," derived from the word "tah" in certain dialects. In that understanding, it is a term of endearment and comfort. This interpretation ties beautifully to the very next verse which assures the Prophet that the Quran was not sent to cause him distress. After the initial period of revelation, there was a pause that made the Prophet anxious, longing for communion. When revelation resumed, the pagans claimed it was a source of hardship for him. Some reports mention that he used to stand in prayer all night until his blessed feet swelled, and his wife Aisha, may Allah be pleased with her, asked why he exhausted himself when his past and future sins were forgiven. He replied, "Should I not be a grateful servant?" Thus, the opening letters and the subsequent verses come as a soothing reassurance: this call is a mercy. The name Ṭâ-Hâ signals a gentle elevation of the Prophet’s status and a reminder that the revelation is a gift, not a burden. It sets the tone for a surah that will recount how Allah strengthened Moses with words of comfort and guidance in the face of a tyrant, just as He now strengthens the final Messenger. So the surah begins by capturing attention, consoling the heart, and preparing for profound stories and timeless truths.
2 | We have not revealed the Quran to you ˹O Prophet˺ to cause you distress, | This verse directly addresses a misconception and comforts the Messenger, peace be upon him. The revelation did not descend to make him miserable, physically exhausted, or psychologically overwhelmed by the weight of his mission. Rather, the Quran is a source of honour, light, and nearness to Allah. The background includes the Prophet’s intense devotion; he would pray until his feet swelled, and he worried deeply when revelation paused. The Quraysh would mock him, saying his Lord had abandoned him or that the Quran was causing him sorrow. Allah negates all of that. The word "distress" translates the Arabic "shaqā’," which implies wretchedness, toil, and unhappiness. The Quran was never meant to be a punishment or a cause of complaint. It is a book of guidance that uplifts the soul. This verse teaches that religious obligations, including prayer and recitation, are not meant to be a crushing weight. The Prophet himself said, "Religion is ease." If someone feels that practicing Islam is causing them unbearable hardship, they may be missing the spirit of the teachings. Allah intended the Quran to be a healing for what is in the hearts, a criterion between right and wrong, and a mercy for the believers. Thus, the opening declaration dismantles any false belief that divine guidance is a burden. It reminds every believer that the path of faith, though it requires effort, is ultimately a path of peace and liberation from the anxieties of ignorance. The Prophet is assured that his struggle will be eased, and his followers inherit the same assurance: the remembrance of Allah brings tranquility to hearts.
3 | but as a reminder to those in awe ˹of Allah˺. | The Quran’s true purpose is framed as a "reminder" or "tadhkirah." This is profound because it implies that the knowledge of God and moral truths are already engraved in the human soul, but they become clouded by worldly distractions. The reminder awakens that dormant inner awareness. The recipients are specifically "those in awe of Allah," meaning those who possess a conscious fear and reverence of their Lord, known as taqwā. Such people are sensitive to the truth, humble, and eager to receive guidance. They benefit from the Quran because their hearts are not sealed by arrogance or heedlessness. The verse thus clarifies that while the Quran is a universal message, its transformative power is unlocked by a particular attitude: a trembling awareness of standing before Allah. The Prophet is being told that his effort is not for those who stubbornly reject; he is not responsible for forcing them. The reminder will find its home in the God-conscious. This reassures him that his distress is unnecessary when the disbelievers turn away, because his primary audience are the ones whose hearts are alive with fear of their Lord. It also establishes a principle: the Quran is a mercy, but only those with receptive hearts will taste its sweetness. The relationship between the Prophet and the Quran is one of receiving and transmitting this reminder, and in doing so, he himself finds spiritual nourishment. So after negating distress, the verse gives a positive aim, connecting the consolation of the Prophet with the description of the true believers.
4 | ˹It is˺ a revelation from the One Who created the earth and the high heavens— | Here Allah identifies the source of the Quran. It is not the Prophet’s own thoughts, nor a poetic inspiration, but a "tanzīl" — a revelation sent down from on high. The One who sends it is described first by His act of creation, specifically creating the earth and the high heavens. This pairing is significant: the earth is our immediate home, low and spread out, while the heavens are elevated, vast, and magnificent. By mentioning both, Allah encompasses all of existence. The verse reminds the listener that the same Being who brought this immense universe into existence has also taken it upon Himself to provide moral and spiritual guidance. The act of creation is proof of His power, wisdom, and right to command. Why should the One who made the cosmos not also communicate with His creation? The mention of creation is a recurring Quranic argument for the authenticity of revelation. The One who created the eye and the ear, the stars and the mountains, surely knows what benefits His servants. Therefore, the Quran’s origin immediately inspires awe and trust. The Prophet is thus anchored in certainty: this message is not his own burden, it comes from the Creator who sustains him and all that exists. The verse forms a seamless link between the themes of comfort and the overwhelming majesty of Allah. By focusing on creation, it prepares the heart for what follows — a fuller description of the Most Compassionate’s attributes and dominion.
5 | the Most Compassionate, ˹Who is˺ established on the Throne. | The description continues by introducing the divine name "Ar-Raḥmān," the Most Compassionate, whose mercy encompasses all things. This immediately softens the grandeur of creation with an attribute of tenderness and care. The Quran’s revelation is thus an expression of immense mercy. Then, the verse mentions a foundational creedal truth: Allah is established on the Throne. This refers to His istiwā’, a manner of rising over the Throne that befits His majesty without resemblance to creation. The Throne is the greatest of created things, symbolising Allah’s supreme authority, kingship, and control over the entire universe. Affirming this doctrine is a crucial part of faith. The early generations accepted it without delving into the "how" and without negating the attribute. By combining "Most Compassionate" with the Throne, the Quran paints a picture of a Lord whose mercy and majesty are perfectly joined. He is not a distant, indifferent creator; He is actively governing and caring. For the Prophet, this is a profound reassurance: the One who rules the heavens and the earth with absolute power is also the Most Compassionate who has sent the Quran out of kindness, not to cause distress. This dual attribute should fill the heart with hope and reverence. The verse also counters the false beliefs of those who thought Allah had abandoned the Prophet; how could the Lord of the Throne, the Most Compassionate, forget His chosen servant? Thus, the description directly supports the earlier negation of distress and reinforces the source of the revelation.
6 | To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth and whatever is in between and whatever is underground. | This verse expands on Allah’s absolute dominion. Everything that exists — in the heavens, on earth, what lies between them like the air and clouds, and even what is beneath the soil — belongs to Him entirely. No creature possesses anything of its own. This comprehensive ownership means that Allah is free from any need, and all creation is dependent on Him. For the Prophet, this is a reminder that his Lord controls every force that might oppose him. Pharaoh’s tyranny and the pagans’ plots are all within a kingdom that belongs to Allah. He can dispose of them as He wills. The verse also instills humility: the Prophet and believers are themselves part of "whatever is on the earth," so they are His property, under His care and protection. This eliminates any sense of loneliness or helplessness. When Moses later goes to confront Pharaoh, he will rely on the same truth: the earth belongs to Allah, and He can command it to swallow the enemy or part the sea. The mention of "whatever is in between" highlights that no realm is outside His knowledge and authority, including spiritual and unseen realms. The phrase "whatever is underground" can refer to buried treasures, the dead, seeds, and even the hidden depths. Nothing escapes His grasp. This verse perfectly sets the stage for the story of Moses, demonstrating that the Lord who owns all will protect and guide His messenger.
7 | Whether you speak openly ˹or not˺, He certainly knows what is secret and what is even more hidden. | Allah’s perfect knowledge is then emphasised. He is aware of every uttered word, whether it is spoken aloud or kept inside the soul. But His knowledge goes deeper: He knows the "secret" — the concealed thoughts and feelings that a person may not express — and "what is even more hidden." This deeper level, referred to in Arabic as "akhfā," is the inner whisperings, the inclinations of the heart that have not yet formed into clear ideas, the subconscious layers that even a person might not fully discern. Such absolute knowledge is exclusive to Allah. For the Prophet, this is both comforting and awe-inspiring. He does not need to articulate every fear or pain; his Lord already knows them. When he faces rejection, ridicule, and danger, Allah sees and hears it all. In the coming story, Moses expresses his fears, and Allah reassures him, "I am with you, hearing and seeing." That same watchful care is for the Prophet Muhammad. The verse also carries a warning for those who conceal plots; nothing can be hidden from the Divine. It serves as a silent rebuke to the pagans who whispered against the message. Moreover, in personal devotion, this attribute encourages sincerity, for even the most hidden movements of the heart are laid bare before the Most Compassionate. This knowledge provides the foundation for the intimate relationship between the Lord and His servant, forming the heart of worship and trust.
8 | Allah—there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. He has the Most Beautiful Names. | The declaration of divine oneness, tawḥīd, is the core message of the Quran. The name "Allah" is the supreme name, encompassing all attributes of perfection. The phrase "there is no god worthy of worship except Him" negates any form of divinity from anything else and affirms it solely for the One True God. This is the ultimate purpose of revelation and the central call of all prophets. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse is both a creed and a sword against idolatry. The Meccans believed in Allah as a Creator but associated partners with Him, and this verse shatters that compromise. Then the verse adds a beautiful dimension: "He has the Most Beautiful Names" (al-asmā’ al-ḥusnā). These names describe His attributes — the Merciful, the Powerful, the Wise, the Loving, the Provider, and many more. They are all perfect, free from any defect. The Prophet is taught that he is connected to a Lord whose qualities are of absolute beauty and goodness. In moments of distress, he can call upon these names, like "the Most Compassionate" for comfort, "the All-Hearing" when he feels unheard, "the All-Powerful" when he faces enemies. The verse ends the opening section on a note of majestic simplicity. It also prepares for the narrative of Moses, who will be commanded to proclaim, "There is no god except Me," and who will invoke these beautiful names in his mission. This systematic presentation of tawḥīd, ownership, knowledge, and beautiful names acts as a spiritual anchor for the Prophet before the lengthy story of past trials and triumphs begins.
9 | Has the story of Moses reached you ˹O Prophet˺? | The surah now transitions from universal doctrines to a historical narrative, posing a rhetorical question to the Prophet. This question is not to seek information, for Allah knows best, but to capture attention, create anticipation, and console the Messenger. The story of Moses is the most frequently recounted in the Quran, and here it is presented in rich detail. By asking "Has the story reached you?", the Quran suggests that this is an important, well-known account, but what is coming will add fresh layers and a specific focus — the reassurance and patient perseverance of a prophet against a tyrant. The Prophet Muhammad was going through similar rejection in Mecca, with the Quraysh leaders acting like Pharaoh. The question implies, "Listen to this story, for it will strengthen your heart and remind you that the path of the prophets is one of trials followed by divine victory." It is as if Allah is gently turning His Prophet’s attention away from the current difficulties to see how His grace operated in the past. The question format also engages the listener’s mind, making them curious. The Prophet was unlettered and had no access to these historical details except through revelation, so the story itself is a sign of his prophethood. The mention of Moses right after the beautiful names of Allah links the coming narrative to the attributes just described: the Most Compassionate will show mercy, the Lord of the Throne will control events, the All-Knowing will guide Moses every step of the way. So the question serves as an elegant pivot, infusing the story with theological depth from the start.
10 | When he saw a fire, he said to his family, “Wait here, ˹for˺ I have spotted a fire. Perhaps I can bring you a torch from it, or find some guidance at the fire.” | The story begins with Moses traveling with his family in the cold, dark desert after leaving Madyan. He notices a fire burning on the side of Mount Sinai. His words to his family show his human care and leadership. He asks them to stay put, expressing hope that he might bring back a burning torch to provide them warmth and light, or at the very least find someone at the fire who could give them directions. This is a beautiful depiction of his human predicament — lost, in need, yet hopeful. The Arabic phrase "aw ajidu ‘alā an-nāri hudā" can mean guidance in terms of directions or information. Moses had no idea he was about to encounter the divine. This teaches that Allah’s plans unfold in the most ordinary circumstances. The fire that Moses saw as a source of physical comfort became the place of his prophethood. Allah often brings relief and immense blessings from where one least expects. For the Prophet Muhammad, there is a parallel: his retreat to the cave of Hira for contemplation became the venue for the first revelation. The story reminds believers not to underestimate small signs; what appears to be a simple fire might be the gateway to a life-transforming meeting with the Lord. The careful detail about Moses speaking to his family also underscores his responsible character, which made him a suitable vessel for the heavy trust of prophethood. The narrative is now set to unfold with sudden, awe-inspiring grandeur.
11 | But when he approached it, he was called, “O Moses! | As Moses draws near to the fire, the unexpected happens. Instead of finding an ordinary flame, he is addressed directly by a divine call. The verse uses the conjunction "but" to indicate a dramatic shift — he approached seeking a torch, but was met with something far greater. The call comes audibly: "O Moses!" This immediate address by name signals a personal, intimate connection. Allah knew him, chose him, and called him directly. This moment marks the beginning of Moses’s prophethood. The wording suggests that Moses did not see the Speaker; he heard the voice. According to many reports, the fire appeared on a green tree, burning without consuming it, a phenomenon that indicated the presence of the divine light. The call "O Moses" is full of mercy and honour. In the Prophet Muhammad’s life, the first word of revelation was "Read!" — also a direct address. Both moments share the pattern of a sudden divine intrusion into ordinary life, transforming everything. The verse teaches that when Allah intends to honour a servant, He calls them by name, pulling them out of their ordinary pursuits. For Moses, the fire that was meant to dispel physical darkness became the source of spiritual illumination. The narrative now pauses to let the weight of this call sink in, as the reader waits to hear what will be said next.
12 | It is truly I. I am your Lord! So take off your sandals, for you are in the sacred valley of Ṭuwa. | The voice declares its identity with emphasis: "It is truly I. I am your Lord!" The repetition "innī anā rabbuka" conveys absolute certainty and establishes the personal lordship. The immediate command to remove sandals is a mark of respect and a preparation for entering a holy presence. Taking off sandals was a sign of humility in the presence of kings, and here it signifies the sanctity of the ground. The valley is named Ṭuwa, a blessed place where Allah would speak directly to Moses. Some scholars say Ṭuwa means the valley that has been sanctified twice, or it is the valley traversed at night. By telling Moses to remove his footwear, Allah was teaching him adab — proper etiquette before the Divine. This outward gesture reflects an inner state of reverence and complete submission. For the Prophet Muhammad, this moment contains a subtle echo: during his Night Journey, he removed his sandals before entering the divine presence as well, according to some narrations, though others say he kept them on as a sign of his unique station. Nonetheless, the principle is clear: when approaching Allah, one must shed the attachments of the worldly journey. The instruction also comforted Moses, giving him a tangible action while his mind must have been overwhelmed. The revelation of "I am your Lord" is the greatest possible honour and also the granting of a direct relationship. The scene sets the stage for the heavy mission about to be entrusted to him.
13 | I have chosen you, so listen to what is revealed: | After establishing His Lordship and the sacredness of the place, Allah announces His selection of Moses. The word "ikhtartuka" — I have chosen you — is a declaration of divine election. This choice is purely out of Allah’s grace and wisdom, not based on any prior merit that would obligate such a choice, though Moses’s qualities are well known. The election is for prophethood, to bear the message to Pharaoh and the Children of Israel. The command "fa-stami‘ limā yūḥā" — so listen to what is revealed — requires Moses to be completely attentive, physically and spiritually. This is the moment of receiving the revelation, and it demands total absorption. The instruction to listen before speaking is a crucial lesson for all believers, especially those who carry the call to others. One must first receive fully from Allah before conveying to people. The verse is brief but filled with the weight of authority and love. For the Prophet Muhammad, this mirrors his own experience: he was chosen and commanded to listen to the revelation carefully, as later explicitly mentioned in the surah itself (verse 114). The voice from the fire now gives Moses a series of core commands that constitute the foundation of his mission. The text moves from the establishment of the special relationship to the content of the message, showing that the divine call always comes with a clear set of principles and responsibilities.
14 | ‘It is truly I. I am Allah! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me. So worship Me ˹alone˺, and establish prayer for My remembrance. | The revelation begins with the same emphatic self-identification as before, now using the supreme name "Allah." This repetition anchors Moses’s entire mission in tawḥīd. The phrase "There is no god except Me" is the absolute statement of monotheism, the dividing line between truth and falsehood. Immediately, the consequence is commanded: "So worship Me alone." All acts of devotion, fear, hope, and obedience must be directed to Allah alone, without any partner. Then the specific command to establish prayer is given, tying it directly to "My remembrance." The ultimate purpose of prayer is the remembrance of Allah; it is the most powerful means of keeping the divine reality alive in one’s heart. For Moses, who would face the tyrannical Pharaoh and his claimed divinity, anchoring his heart in prayer was essential. The prayer would be his strength, his refuge, and his constant connection. The verse also reveals a profound link: the establishment of prayer is not merely a ritual but a direct consequence of recognizing Allah’s sole divinity. It is the practical manifestation of tawḥīd. The phrase "li-dhikrī" can also mean "for My remembrance" or "so that I remember you," implying a mutual relationship. This first command to Moses contains the essence of the religion that all prophets conveyed. For the Prophet Muhammad, the same command was given, and he found coolness of his eyes in prayer. The verse thus lays down the spiritual constitution for the prophet who is being sent to confront the greatest tyrant.
15 | The Hour is sure to come. My Will is to keep it hidden, so that every soul may be rewarded according to their efforts. | After the command for worship, the next revealed truth is the certainty of the Day of Judgment. The Hour refers to the end of the world and the resurrection. It is "sure to come" without any doubt. The phrase "akādu ukhfīhā" has two famous interpretations: "I almost hide it" meaning it is so hidden that even its nearness is concealed, or "I am about to reveal it" which would mean it is imminent. The majority of commentators, including Ibn Abbas, take the meaning as Allah keeping it hidden from creation. The wisdom behind this concealment is then explained: so that every soul may be rewarded according to what it strives for. If people knew the exact time of judgment, they might delay repentance and good deeds, or their free will would be compromised. The hidden hour maintains a constant state of alertness and sincere effort. For Moses, this doctrine was necessary because he was being sent to a people who had been oppressed and might lose hope in divine justice. The Hour is the ultimate day of recompense, when the oppressed will be vindicated and the oppressors punished. It adds an urgent moral dimension to the mission: the Pharaoh’s worldly power is temporary, and the true lasting reality is the Hereafter. This revelation also connects beautifully with the earlier themes of Allah’s ownership and knowledge. The same Lord who knows the secret and what is more hidden has hidden the Hour in His perfect wisdom. The verse reminds every believer to live with a sense of accountability, focusing on the quality of their efforts.
16 | So do not let those who disbelieve in it and follow their desires distract you from it, or you will be doomed. | Having affirmed the Hour’s certainty, Allah warns Moses not to be diverted from belief and preparation for it. The warning is against two types of people: those who deny the Hour outright and those who merely follow their lowly desires, living as if there is no judgment. The word "fa-tardā" means "you will perish" or "you will be ruined." The warning is addressed to Moses directly, and by extension to all who carry the message. Even a prophet is not immune from needing this reminder; it emphasizes the gravity of being distracted from the Hereafter. The disbelievers and the hedonists will try to make the Hereafter seem irrelevant, but the believer must resist that current. This is incredibly relevant for the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, where the pagan leaders mocked the concept of resurrection. Allah is telling him through Moses’s story that the denial of the Hereafter is a key obstacle, and the messenger must guard his own heart against its influence. The verse connects desire with disbelief: following one’s whims without divine guidance leads inevitably to rejecting the truth. Moses needed this inner fortification before confronting Pharaoh, whose kingdom was built on the denial of any higher authority. The warning "or you will be doomed" is stark, showing that even the chosen ones are only safe through steadfastness. This instruction immediately precedes the giving of the physical signs, indicating that inner conviction in the unseen must be anchored before outward miracles are granted.
17 | ˹Allah added,˺ “And what is that in your right hand, O Moses?” | After the foundational creed and warning, the discourse takes a gentle, dialogical turn. Allah asks Moses a question to which He already knows the answer: "What is that in your right hand?" The question serves multiple purposes. It calms Moses by engaging him in a normal conversation after the awe-inspiring revelations. It also draws his attention to the ordinary object he held — his staff — preparing him for the miracle about to occur. By asking, Allah allows Moses to describe his staff and his reliance on it, thereby making the subsequent transformation more impactful. The phrase "in your right hand" specifies the hand, perhaps because the right hand is associated with strength, honour, and possession. This question is a teaching method; it makes the learner reflect on what he already knows before showing him something beyond his knowledge. The entire sequence mirrors how Allah often builds faith through personal engagement. For the Prophet, who himself would be asked "What is that in your right hand?" or similar queries in the context of his own miracles, this is a template of how Allah instructs His messengers. The question also subtly shows that Allah cares about the details of a prophet’s life, even the staff he uses. It is a beautiful, intimate moment where the Lord of the Throne speaks to Moses as a beloved interlocutor, calming his awe with a gentle query, all while setting the stage for the miraculous.
18 | He replied, “It is my staff! I lean on it, and with it I beat down ˹branches˺ for my sheep, and have other uses for it.” | Moses answers with a detailed description, much more than a simple "staff." He says, "It is my staff," asserting his ownership and familiarity. Then he lists its functions: he leans on it for support during long walks or while resting; he uses it to beat down leaves and branches to feed his sheep; and he has other various needs for it. This elaboration reveals Moses’s human nature and his shepherd background. Prophets were often shepherds before their mission, learning patience, care, and reliance on simple tools. By enumerating the staff’s practical benefits, Moses shows his attachment to it and how integral it was to his daily life. The staff was a symbol of his worldly occupation. The response is longer than the question required, perhaps stemming from a mix of nervousness and eagerness to speak in that holy presence. Allah’s question was open-ended, and Moses took the opportunity to share. The "other uses" might include defense, reaching things, or leading his flock. This human detail is preserved in the Quran to make the scene relatable. Then, the divine plan will take this utterly ordinary object and turn it into a mighty sign. The lesson is profound: when Allah enters a person’s life, He often uses what they already have, transforming their mundane skills and possessions into tools for His mission. The staff, which Moses leaned on, would soon be leaned on by the whole nation as it parted the sea. The moment is now set for the first miracle.
19 | Allah said, “Throw it down, O Moses!” | The command is simple and abrupt: "Throw it down." There is no explanation, no preparation. Moses is asked to cast away the object of his reliance, the staff that supported him physically. This is a test of obedience and trust. Often, Allah asks believers to let go of what they depend on in order to show them that true reliance is on Him alone. When Moses threw the staff, he had no idea what would happen, but he obeyed instantly. The command to throw represents the first step of letting go of worldly attachments in the journey of prophethood. The staff was not evil, but it symbolized Moses’s past identity as a shepherd. The new identity required total submission. The phrase "O Moses" is used again, maintaining the personal connection even in the command. This moment is replicated in some ways in the life of the Prophet Muhammad, when he was asked to leave his home and city, abandoning his familiar surroundings. The courage to obey without hesitation is a hallmark of prophets. The verse also illustrates that miracles occur at the intersection of divine command and human compliance. Had Moses hesitated or clung to his staff, the sign would not have manifested. The brevity of the verse adds to the dramatic tension. The reader is left in suspense, just as Moses must have been in those split seconds, before the next verse reveals the astonishing result.
20 | So he did, then—behold!—it became a serpent, slithering. | Moses obeyed and threw the staff. The Arabic uses "fa-idhā hiya ḥayyatun tas‘ā" — a particle of suddenness indicating the instantaneous transformation. The staff became a living serpent, rapidly moving as if with a purpose. The word "ḥayyah" refers to a snake, and some reports describe it as a large, swift serpent that even opened its mouth. The suddenness and vividness emphasize the divine power behind the transformation. A dead piece of wood turned into a living, moving creature in an instant, without any physical means. This miracle was one of the two primary signs given to Moses for Pharaoh. It demonstrated that the Creator can bring life out of inanimate matter, a reminder of the resurrection that Moses had just been told to believe in. For the Prophet Muhammad, this served as a confirmation that the same power would produce the miracles he was given, though his were primarily intellectual and spiritual, culminating in the Quran. The staff-snake also symbolized the swallowing of falsehood, as it would later swallow the magicians’ illusions. The movement "tas‘ā" conveys energy and purpose; it was not a static image but a dynamic, slightly fearsome sight. Moses’s initial reaction, as noted elsewhere, was fear, causing him to turn and flee until Allah called him back. This miracle was the first external proof of his prophethood, and it marked the beginning of his public confrontation with Pharaoh. The narrative has now moved from the intimate call in the sacred valley to the tangible demonstration of power, preparing Moses to return to Egypt with confidence.
21 | Allah said, “Take it, and have no fear. We will return it to its former state.” | After Moses witnessed his staff transform into a slithering serpent, a natural human reaction of fear arose. Allah immediately addressed this fear with two commands: “Take it” and “have no fear.” The order to seize the serpent was itself a test of trust. Moses was being asked to place his hand upon what had just become a terrifying creature, demonstrating that his reliance on Allah must now override his physical instincts. The reassurance “have no fear” acknowledges the human element—fear is not condemned, but it must be overcome by faith. Then comes the promise: “We will return it to its former state.” The Arabic uses a plural pronoun of majesty, “sa-nu‘īduhā,” emphasizing the divine power behind the transformation. Once Moses obeyed and grasped it, the serpent reverted back into his familiar staff. This immediate restoration showed that miracles operate under Allah’s complete control and serve a temporary purpose rather than permanently altering the natural order. The event teaches that what Allah commands us to take hold of, even if it appears frightening or difficult, is ultimately safe when He has guaranteed its outcome. The staff would now serve a dual purpose: a shepherd’s tool and a divine sign. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, the lesson is profound. He too would be asked to “take” the message with confidence, without fearing the serpents of opposition. The sequence—throw, fear, take, reassurance, restoration—becomes a pattern for every believer facing daunting tasks. The verse also reassures that whatever we sacrifice or abandon for Allah’s sake can be returned to us in a better and more blessed form.
22 | And put your hand under your armpit, it will come out ˹shining˺ white, unblemished, as another sign, | The second great sign given to Moses was the radiant hand. The instruction was to insert his hand into his upper garment near the armpit, and when he withdrew it, it would emerge shining white, yet without any disease or blemish. This whiteness was a brilliant, luminous light, not the whiteness of leprosy, which in some other verses is explicitly negated. It was a supernatural glow, a celestial light that testified to prophethood. The phrase “as another sign” indicates that this was distinct from the staff, a separate miracle to reinforce the message. The location “under your armpit” or “into your bosom” implies a place close to the heart, perhaps symbolizing that the light of guidance springs from a pure, inner source. For Moses, these two signs—one external, in the staff, and one internal, in his own body—equipped him with comprehensive proofs. The shining hand would later be shown to Pharaoh and his court, demonstrating that Allah’s messenger bore marks of divine favour that no magician could replicate. The miracle was instantaneous and reversible; when Moses returned his hand, it reverted to normal. This taught him that the signs were not his own power but a temporary manifestation of Allah’s will. For the Prophet Muhammad, the similar night journey and the splitting of the moon were signs tailored to his context, while here Moses’s signs were suited to a society steeped in illusions of magic. The whiteness of the hand also symbolized purity, truth, and clarity, contrasting with the darkness of idolatry and oppression that filled Egypt.
23 | so that We may show you some of Our greatest signs. | The purpose of granting these miracles is articulated clearly: to show Moses “some of Our greatest signs.” The Arabic “min āyātinā al-kubrā” uses the plural “greatest,” indicating that what Moses was given were selections from a vast repertoire of divine wonders. The staff and the shining hand were just the beginning; the plagues, the parting of the sea, and the manna and quails would follow. Each sign was an “āyah,” a pointer to a deeper truth—Allah’s oneness, power, and mercy. The phrase “so that We may show you” reveals that these signs served a dual purpose: they would convince Pharaoh and his people, but they also served to strengthen Moses’s own faith. Seeing the transformation firsthand grounded him in the certainty that the One Who spoke to him in the valley was truly the Lord of all creation. The greatest signs are not just spectacular displays; they are intimate revelations of the divine attributes. For the Prophet Muhammad, the Quran itself is described as containing the greatest signs, as its verses continuously unveil the wonders of creation and the realities of the unseen. The verse humbles the reader: even a prophet like Moses needed to witness signs to carry his heavy mission. Allah trains His servants gradually, showing them His power in manageable steps. The mention of “some” suggests that no matter how many signs a person sees, the totality of Allah’s greatness can never be fully comprehended. This prepares Moses for the immense task ahead, assuring him that he is equipped with more than enough evidence to confront the most arrogant tyranny.
24 | Go to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺. | With the signs firmly in hand and his heart fortified, Moses receives the direct command: “Go to Pharaoh.” The mission is now explicit, moving from the private revelation in the sacred valley to public confrontation. Pharaoh is singled out as the primary target because he represents the pinnacle of rebellion against Allah. The word “ṭaghā” means he exceeded all limits—oppressing the Children of Israel, slaughtering their sons, enslaving them, and most egregiously, claiming divinity for himself by declaring, “I am your lord, most high.” His transgression was not a minor sin but a monstrous tyranny rooted in arrogance and unbelief. By ordering Moses to go to this specific tyrant, Allah demonstrates that the message of tawḥīd must confront power structures that set themselves up as gods. No matter how entrenched evil appears, the prophet is not allowed to retreat into the comfort of the wilderness. The command is stark and direct, lacking any softening detail, to emphasize the urgency. For Moses, this order must have been overwhelming—he was a fugitive from Egypt, wanted for a killing, and now he was told to return and face the very ruler he had fled. Yet the command is paired with the earlier signs, providing the tools. The Prophet Muhammad’s own mission similarly involved confronting the Quraysh leadership, the Pharaohs of Arabia. The verse teaches that the message of Islam does not exist in a vacuum; it must challenge injustice and false authority. The phrase “he has truly transgressed” also serves as a moral verdict before any dialogue, establishing that this is not a negotiation between equals but a call from truth to falsehood.
25 | Moses prayed, “My Lord! Uplift my heart for me, | Hearing the command, Moses turned immediately to his Lord in supplication. He did not argue or refuse; he asked for the inner and outer resources needed to fulfill the mission. His first request was “Uplift my heart,” or in Arabic “ishraḥ lī ṣadrī.” This phrase means to expand, open, and fill the chest with calm, courage, and clarity. A constricted heart cannot bear heavy responsibilities or face aggressive enemies. Moses knew that the task required a vast inner space that could absorb fear, ridicule, and pressure without breaking. The request acknowledges that such an expansion is a divine gift, not a human achievement. The Prophet Muhammad was granted the same blessing, as mentioned in Surah Ash-Sharḥ: “Did We not expand for you your chest?” This expansion includes patience, wisdom, and the ability to see the bigger picture. Moses was about to speak truth to power, and he knew his own human limitations—the memory of having killed a man, his speech difficulty, and his long absence. He needed a heart that could contain the light of revelation and withstand the darkness of Pharaoh’s court. The prayer models the proper attitude for anyone taking on a daunting responsibility: begin by asking Allah to prepare the vessel of the heart. Without inner expansion, knowledge and miracles alone are insufficient. The verse beautifully demonstrates the intimacy between Moses and his Lord; immediately after a heavy command, he turned to prayer, showing that all strength comes from Allah.
26 | and make my task easy, | After the inward preparation, Moses asks for external facilitation: “Make my task easy.” The task was immense—confronting a tyrant, demanding the release of an enslaved nation, and conveying a message that would uproot an entire system of belief and governance. He did not ask for the task to be removed, only that it be made easy. This teaches that difficulty in a divine mission is guaranteed, but Allah’s facilitation can render the hardest burden light. The Arabic “yassir lī amrī” encompasses all aspects: ease in delivering the message, ease in being accepted, ease in facing opposition, and ease in achieving the objective. It is a comprehensive prayer for success. The word “amrī” (my affair, my task) shows that Moses recognized the mission as his own personal responsibility, not merely an abstract command. By asking for ease, he acknowledged that without Allah’s help, the mission would be unbearably heavy. The Prophet Muhammad often recited a similar supplication: “O Allah, I seek refuge in You from worry and grief, from incapacity and laziness…” The request for ease does not imply a life free from struggle, but a state where the heart remains serene and the obstacles are smoothed. The verse aligns with the Quranic principle, “Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship.” Moses’s prayer thus becomes a template for all believers facing seemingly impossible tasks: ask for the burden to be lifted, not by escaping the duty, but by the infusion of divine assistance that makes the difficult attainable.
27 | and remove the impediment from my tongue | Moses’s third request addresses his known speech difficulty. According to many reports, he had a lisp or a stammer that made his words sometimes unclear, especially when he was anxious. The impediment, translated from “‘uqdatam min lisānī,” literally “a knot from my tongue,” was not removed entirely as a physical defect but was alleviated so that his speech could be understood. The request for removal is specifically connected to the mission: he needed to communicate effectively with Pharaoh, the elite, and the masses. Speech is the primary tool of a messenger, and any barrier to clear communication could hinder the delivery of the message. Moses’s humility is evident; he did not pretend he had no weaknesses. He laid his vulnerability before Allah and asked for it to be healed. This demonstrates that Allah’s prophets, despite their high station, were fully human and reliant on divine grace for their abilities. The Prophet Muhammad, though extremely eloquent, also depended on Allah for the clarity of his message, and the Quran itself is the ultimate miracle of clear Arabic speech. The “knot” may also symbolize more than a physical impediment; it could refer to any psychological or emotional block that arises when facing a fierce enemy. By asking for its removal, Moses sought confidence and fluency. The response to this prayer is mentioned elsewhere: Allah granted that Aaron would assist him, and that Pharaoh’s own arrogance would ultimately be conquered. The lesson for believers is that one should never hesitate to ask Allah to correct personal weaknesses, especially those that affect the service of the faith.
28 | so people may understand my speech, | The purpose of removing the impediment is stated explicitly: “so people may understand my speech.” The goal is not personal comfort but effective conveyance of the divine message. Moses’s prayer is entirely mission-oriented. He wants to be understood so that the truth can penetrate hearts. This reveals the profound prophetic concern: the messenger’s first duty is clear communication. If the message is garbled or misunderstood due to a speech defect, the responsibility of the people might be diminished. Thus, Moses seeks to remove that barrier. The Arabic “yafqahū qawlī” implies deep comprehension, not merely hearing sounds. He wanted his words to penetrate and be grasped correctly. This shows the importance of eloquence and clarity in Islamic preaching. The Prophet Muhammad was given “jawāmi‘ al-kalim,” concise and comprehensive speech, enabling his message to spread. For Moses, the understanding of his speech was crucial because he would be addressing a proud court skilled in rhetoric and argument. The verse also underscores that while guidance is from Allah, the medium of clear language is a means that should be sought. The supplication forms part of the comprehensive prayer that includes inner expansion, external ease, and now effective communication. Everything is woven together for one end: fulfilling the mission. It highlights that good communication skills are a blessing to be prayed for, not taken for granted. The verse continues the sequence of Moses’s humble but strategic requests.
29 | and grant me a helper from my family, | Moses now asks for a human support. He does not merely rely on the divine signs; he recognizes the value of companionship in a difficult mission. He asks for a “helper” or “wazīr” (literally a minister or someone who shares the burden) from his own family. The choice of family is significant—a relative would share his background, loyalty, and concern. Prophets were often supported by their kin, like the Prophet Muhammad’s uncle Hamza and cousin Ali. The request acknowledges that even the greatest prophets need close allies. The task of confronting Pharaoh was not a solo endeavor; it required a partner who could stand beside him, reinforce his message, and provide emotional support. The word “ashuddu bihī azrī” in the next verse elaborates on how this helper would strengthen him. By specifying “from my family,” Moses also might have had Aaron in mind already, knowing his brother’s eloquence and righteousness. The prayer teaches that seeking human assistance while relying on Allah is not a sign of weak faith but of wisdom. The Prophet Muhammad’s frequent consultation with his companions and his reliance on Abu Bakr exemplify this principle. The helper would share the task, pray together, and face the enemy together. The request also implies that the mission is larger than one individual; it requires a team. The verse continues the intimate conversation, where Moses pours out his needs with complete candor, and his Lord listens.
30 | Aaron, my brother. | Moses finally names the specific person he desires as his helper: Aaron, his biological brother. Aaron is described elsewhere as eloquent, more articulate in speech than Moses, which would compensate for Moses’s speech impediment. The mention “my brother” emphasizes the natural bond of love and trust. Aaron was already a believer and a righteous man. This request was not a random selection; it was divinely inspired, and Allah had already planned to make Aaron a prophet alongside Moses. The request thus aligns perfectly with divine will. Moses’s prayer here is a model of asking for something that is already known to be good and that Allah has implicitly approved. By naming Aaron, Moses shows his deep longing for his brother to share in the honour of prophethood and in the burden of the mission. The relationship between Moses and Aaron is one of the most beautiful brotherhoods in the Quran. The Prophet Muhammad also cherished his bond with his companions, particularly Ali, to whom he said, “You are to me like Aaron was to Moses, except that there is no prophet after me.” The specificity of the request highlights that the person chosen to assist in a sacred cause should be someone of proven character, closeness, and ability. The verse segues naturally into the elaboration of how Aaron would help, which follows next, tying the family bond to the practical strengthening of the mission. The request for Aaron completes Moses’s five-part supplication: expanded heart, eased task, loosened tongue, a helper, and specifically his brother.
31 | Strengthen me through him, | Moses explains the purpose of having Aaron as his helper: “Strengthen me through him.” The Arabic “ushdud bihī azrī” literally means “reinforce my back by him.” The back is a symbol of strength and support; a burden becomes lighter when someone else helps carry it. Moses knew that standing before Pharaoh would require immense courage and resilience, and the presence of Aaron would double his strength. The spiritual and emotional support of a trusted brother can fortify a person against fear and despair. This request also implies a partnership where each complements the other. Aaron would not merely be a passive assistant but an active partner who would lend his own gifts, particularly his eloquence and perhaps his calmer temperament, to the mission. The verse highlights that even a direct recipient of divine revelation does not become self-sufficient; human interconnectedness remains a divine design. The Prophet Muhammad often prayed for his companions and drew strength from their presence, especially in times of persecution. The choice of the word “azrī” (my strength, my back) evokes the image of two pillars supporting a structure together. For believers today, this underscores the importance of righteous companionship in carrying out good work. Solitary struggles are far more vulnerable; a partner who shares the vision can make the heavy load bearable. Moses’s supplication thus becomes a comprehensive request for both divine and human resources, blending spiritual dependence with practical wisdom.
32 | and let him share my task, | The request extends from strength to actual participation: “let him share my task.” The Arabic “wa-ashrik-hu fī amrī” means to make him a partner in my affair, my mission. This is not merely about assistance but about co-ownership of the prophetic duty. Moses was asking that Aaron become a fellow messenger, which indeed was granted. Sharing the task implies shared authority, shared responsibility, and shared accountability. It lightens the burden mentally, for a partner understands the weight and can make decisions together. The mission of calling Pharaoh to monotheism and liberating the Israelites was too vast for one person alone. The verse reflects the Quranic principle of shura, consultation and collective effort. The Prophet Muhammad consulted his companions on major matters, showing that the principle of sharing tasks is divinely endorsed. The request also ensures continuity and support; if Moses faltered or faced direct threat, Aaron would carry forward the call. By asking for this sharing, Moses demonstrated a lack of ego; he did not want to be the sole hero. He recognized that the goal was the message, not personal glory. The request for a partner in the task is thus an act of supreme selflessness. It also teaches that when one is given a leadership role, delegating and sharing responsibilities with capable, trusted individuals is a prophetic practice. The verse rounds off Moses’s vision of a collaborative prophetic mission, firmly rooted in the love and trust of brotherhood.
33 | so that we may glorify You much | Moses explains the ultimate objective of this partnership: abundant glorification and remembrance of Allah. The partnership was not for worldly power or prestige but for intensified worship. By having a brother and co-messenger, they could together perform dhikr and tasbīḥ on a much greater scale. The two of them could establish prayer, lead the people in worship, and constantly proclaim Allah’s perfection. The word “nusabbiḥaka kathīrā” means to declare Allah free from any imperfection, frequently and repeatedly. This glorification would be both a personal spiritual practice and a public proclamation that counters Pharaoh’s false claims of divinity. The presence of a partner would enable them to sustain this worship even in the face of relentless hostility. When one grew weary, the other would continue. Together, they could organize worship for the Children of Israel. The request reveals that the ultimate goal of any prophetic mission is the establishment of Allah’s remembrance on earth. The Prophet Muhammad similarly said that he and his followers were sent to worship Allah alone. The verse teaches that seeking helpers and building teams should be driven by the desire to increase collective worship and service, not by ambition. The glorification of Allah is the pinnacle of all human activity, and Moses’s prayer places it at the center of his mission plan. This shifts the focus from the confrontation with Pharaoh to the positive spiritual aim.
34 | and remember You much, | In close connection with glorification, Moses asks for the ability to remember Allah much. While tasbīḥ (glorification) declares Allah’s transcendence, dhikr (remembrance) encompasses gratitude, praise, and constant awareness. The repetition “kathīrā” (much) in both requests emphasizes quantity and consistency. The mission required an unwavering connection with Allah, as the challenges would be relentless. Constant remembrance is the fuel that keeps faith alive. Moses and Aaron would need to remember Allah when fear gripped them, when Pharaoh threatened them, and when their own people complained. The phrase “and remember You much” also hints at the joy found in worship. Remembrance is not a burden but a source of strength. The Prophet Muhammad was commanded, “Remember your Lord much, and glorify Him in the evening and the morning.” Moses’s supplication aligns perfectly with that command. The pairing of Aaron with Moses would allow them to remind each other and keep the flame of dhikr burning. For believers, the lesson is profound: in any collective effort for good, the group should be bonded by shared remembrance of Allah. This ensures that the endeavor remains spiritually rooted and protected from ego and deviation. The request for abundant remembrance follows logically from the request for a helper, because righteous companionship is one of the greatest aids to consistent worship. The verse thus concludes the specific purposes of the partnership on the highest spiritual note.
35 | for truly You have ˹always˺ been overseeing us. | Moses ends his supplication by grounding it in the knowledge of Allah’s continuous oversight. The Arabic “innaka kunta binā baṣīrā” uses the past tense “kunta” to indicate an eternal attribute: “You have always been seeing us.” This is a declaration of faith and an expression of intimacy. Moses is saying that Allah knows their state, their needs, and their intentions; He sees their weakness and their sincerity. The word “baṣīr” means All-Seeing, implying not just visual sight but deep, comprehensive knowledge and care. Because Allah is overseeing them, He knows exactly what they require for the mission. The prayer is thus offered with full confidence that it will be heard and answered, not because of Moses’s merit, but because the All-Seeing Lord who watched over him from infancy will continue to do so. The verse echoes the earlier verses of the surah about Allah’s knowledge of the secret and hidden. Moses is essentially saying, “You see the enormity of the task and my inner fears; therefore, grant these requests.” The phrase also expresses gratitude for past care. Allah had been watching over Moses when he was a baby in the river, and that same watchful Eye would accompany him into Pharaoh’s court. For the Prophet Muhammad, this is a reminder that the Lord who sees everything will not abandon him. The verse concludes the supplication on a note of tender reliance, perfectly setting the stage for the affirmative divine response that follows immediately.
36 | Allah responded, “All that you requested has been granted, O Moses! | The response is immediate, comprehensive, and emphatic. Allah says, “Qad ūtīta su’laka yā Mūsā” — “You have been given your request, O Moses.” The word “qad” indicates certainty and completion; it is done. Every item Moses asked for is included: the expansion of his chest, the easing of his task, the loosening of his tongue’s knot, and the appointment of Aaron as a partner. Allah answered the prayer in its entirety. This demonstrates the immense mercy of Allah and His responsiveness to His chosen servants. Moses did not have to wait or repeat the request; the answer came instantly in that same sacred encounter. The address “O Moses” is repeated, emphasizing the personal, loving nature of the response. This immediate granting of a comprehensive prayer is a source of immense hope for believers. It teaches that when one prays with sincerity and for goals aligned with Allah’s will, the answer can be swift and complete. The fact that Aaron was made a prophet simultaneously shows that Allah not only granted the helper but elevated him to the highest rank. The response also indirectly comforts the Prophet Muhammad, who would later face his own difficulties: the same Lord who granted Moses’s requests would certainly grant His final Prophet all that he needed. The verse marks the transition from Moses’s private preparation to the public mission, now fully equipped with divine assurance. It serves as a powerful testament to the effectiveness of dua, especially when offered in moments of great need and with an expanded heart.
37 | “And surely We had shown You favour before, | With the immediate mission granted, Allah then reminds Moses of His past favours. This serves multiple purposes: it strengthens Moses’s confidence by recounting how Allah protected and guided him from birth, proving that the same Lord who cared for him then will care for him now. The phrase “laqad manannā ‘alayka marratan ukhrā” uses the word “manna,” which implies a great, undeserved blessing. The “other time” refers to the entire history of Moses’s life before prophethood. The reminder of past favours is a common Quranic technique to inspire gratitude and trust. For Moses, remembering the miraculous way he was saved from slaughter, raised in Pharaoh’s palace, and guided through exile would dissolve any lingering fear. If Allah engineered such complex events to protect him, surely He would not abandon him now. This verse introduces a flashback sequence that will detail several episodes. The past favours include the inspiration to his mother, the casting into the river, the reunion with his mother, the killing of the Egyptian, the sojourn in Midian, and his destined arrival at the sacred valley. Each event is a testament to Allah’s precise planning. For the Prophet Muhammad, hearing this would be a reminder of his own protected childhood and the favours of Allah upon him. The verse beautifully links the present moment of prophethood with a lifelong pattern of divine care, showing that the mission was always part of a larger design.
38 | when We inspired your mother with this: | The first specific favour recalled is the divine inspiration given to Moses’s mother. The word “awḥaynā” means We inspired, conveying that Allah placed a firm understanding or command into her heart. This was not prophethood in the formal sense, but a direct spiritual communication to a righteous woman in a moment of extreme crisis. Pharaoh had ordered the killing of all newborn Israelite boys, and Moses’s mother feared for her infant son’s life. The inspiration was the solution: to place him in a chest and cast it into the river. The content of the inspiration is given in the next verse. The reminder highlights that Allah’s care for Moses began even before his birth, reaching his mother and guiding her actions. This demonstrates that Allah’s planning encompasses the righteous servants around His prophets, providing them with the intuition and strength to play their roles. The mention of the mother also honours the often unseen sacrifices of women in the prophetic stories. For the Prophet Muhammad, whose mother Aminah and foster-mother Halimah played crucial roles, the parallel is evident. The verse teaches that in times of fear and oppression, Allah can open a way out through subtle inspiration. It also illustrates the Quranic theme that Allah’s help can come through the most unexpected means—a terrified mother, a fragile chest, and the currents of a river. The stage is thus set for the detailed account of that miraculous preservation.
39 | ‘Put him into a chest, then put it into the river. The river will wash it ashore, and he will be taken by ˹Pharaoh,˺ an enemy of Mine and his.’ And I blessed you with lovability from Me ˹O Moses˺ so that you would be brought up under My ˹watchful˺ Eye. | The inspiration given to Moses’s mother is spelled out. She was told to place her baby into a chest—likely an ark of papyrus or wood—and then cast it into the Nile. This command defied all natural maternal instinct, yet she obeyed. The promise was that the river would carry the chest to the bank, and that Pharaoh himself, the very enemy who sought to kill the male infants, would take him. The phrase “an enemy of Mine and his” is striking: the one destined to raise Moses in his palace was simultaneously the arch-enemy of Allah and of the child. Yet, in a profound irony, Allah used the enemy to protect and nurture the very person who would later confront him. This illustrates the absolute sovereignty of Allah over all forces, even those of evil. Then Allah adds, “And I blessed you with lovability from Me.” This means Allah cast a special affection and beauty upon Moses, so that whoever saw him would love him. When Pharaoh’s wife, Asiyah, saw the infant, her heart instantly softened, and she persuaded Pharaoh to spare him. This “lovability” was a divine gift that acted as a shield. The phrase “li-tuṣna‘a ‘alā ‘aynī” means “so that you would be brought up under My watchful Eye.” This idiom implies being raised with special care under direct divine supervision. Moses was trained in the finest palace, educated and prepared for leadership, all while Allah’s eye watched over him. For the Prophet Muhammad, the parallel of being orphaned yet protected and nurtured under Allah’s watchful eye is clear. The verse shows that no enemy can harm whom Allah chooses to protect, and that the most hopeless situations can be transformed into stages of honour.
40 | ˹Remember˺ when your sister came along and proposed, ‘Shall I direct you to someone who will nurse him?’ So We reunited you with your mother so that her heart would be put at ease, and she would not grieve. ˹Later˺ you killed a man ˹by mistake˺, but We saved you from sorrow, as well as other tests We put you through. Then you stayed for a number of years among the people of Midian. Then you came here as pre-destined, O Moses! | The recounting of favours continues with further details. After Pharaoh’s household took Moses, they sought a wet nurse for him, but he refused every woman. His sister, who had followed the chest, came forward and offered to guide them to a suitable family, carefully not revealing her relationship. This led to the reunion with their mother, who then nursed him openly in the palace while being paid a wage. Allah says the purpose was “so that her heart would be put at ease and she would not grieve.” The mother’s emotional wellbeing mattered to Allah; the plan was not only to save Moses but also to comfort his mother. Then the narrative shifts to Moses’s adulthood: he intervened in a fight and accidentally killed an Egyptian. That event caused him fear and sorrow, but Allah says, “We saved you from sorrow.” He was guided to leave Egypt before the authorities could execute him, and his repentance was accepted. This is mentioned as a favour, showing that even Moses’s mistakes were wrapped in divine protection and led to his departure to Midian. There, he spent years gaining life experience, marrying, and maturing. Finally, all those events aligned so that he arrived at Mount Sinai exactly as “pre-destined” (qadaran). The word “qadar” means a precise measure or decree. Every step, from the river to the killing to the sojourn in Midian, was part of a perfect divine timetable. The address “O Moses” at the end ties the entire life story together, affirming that his entire journey was never random but orchestrated for this sacred moment. For the Prophet Muhammad, this is a profound comfort: his own life’s struggles—orphanhood, poverty, persecution—were all a qadar leading to his meeting with Allah. The verse concludes the flashback with an overwhelming sense of divine planning and loving care, setting the stage for Moses to go forward without fear.
41 | And I have selected you for My service. | After recalling the lifelong chain of favours, Allah now declares the purpose behind all that meticulous care: “And I have selected you for My service.” The Arabic verb “iṣṭana‘tuka” comes from the root meaning to manufacture or fashion something with special attention, implying that Moses was personally crafted and chosen for a divine role. This selection is not merely an appointment; it is the culmination of a process in which every event of his life—the river, the palace, the exile—was a stage of preparation. Allah was forming him for prophethood and direct conversation. The phrase “li-nafsī” means “for Myself,” indicating that Moses was selected for a uniquely intimate relationship with his Lord, one that involved speaking directly without an intermediary. This was a tremendous honour, for which Moses is often called Kalīm Allāh, the one to whom Allah spoke. The verse reinforces that the favours just mentioned were not random acts of kindness but deliberate steps in a plan that led to this sacred election. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this is a powerful reminder that his own selection as the final messenger was similarly part of an eternal decree. His upbringing as an orphan, his honesty among his people, and his retreat to the cave were all facets of that divine preparation. The concept of being chosen “for My service” also teaches that the highest honour is not worldly power but being dedicated to Allah. Moses was not chosen to become a king, but to carry the burden of revelation and lead his people to monotheism. The verse transitions beautifully from the comforting recollections of the past into the active command that now follows, giving Moses the full weight of his calling while grounding him in the certainty that he was made for this very moment.
42 | “Go forth, you and your brother, with My signs and never falter in remembering Me. | With the selection confirmed, the command to move into action is issued. “Go forth, you and your brother” pairs them as a team, fulfilling Moses’s earlier request. They are not to go empty-handed but “with My signs”—the staff and the shining hand, and all the subsequent miracles that would manifest. These signs served as both proof and protection. The instruction “never falter in remembering Me” is a profound addition. The Arabic “wa lā taniyā fī dhikrī” means do not slacken, weaken, or tire in My remembrance. The mission ahead would be exhausting, terrifying, and full of setbacks, but the lifeline was constant dhikr. If they kept their hearts and tongues occupied with the remembrance of Allah, fear would dissipate and resolve would strengthen. The command implies that faltering in remembrance leads to spiritual weakness, which opens the door to failure. For Moses and Aaron, dhikr was the fuel that would sustain them before Pharaoh’s arrogance and the eventual stubbornness of their own people. The verse connects the outward mission with the inward necessity of a living connection with Allah. The signs were external tools, but remembrance was the internal engine. For the Prophet Muhammad, this instruction was echoed when he was told, “And remember your Lord when you forget.” The greatest challenge for anyone engaged in calling to Allah is not the opposition of enemies but the drying up of one’s own spiritual reservoir. The command to go is thus immediately balanced with the command to stay connected. The pairing of the two brothers in the command also demonstrates that the responsibility of maintaining remembrance is shared; they could remind each other when one grew weak. The verse elegantly merges the physical departure to Egypt with the spiritual journey of the heart.
43 | “Go, both of you, to Pharaoh, for he has truly transgressed ˹all bounds˺.” | The specific destination and target are restated with renewed force. The command “Go, both of you, to Pharaoh” singles out the tyrant himself. They were not to start with the oppressed Israelites or the general public, but to march directly into the centre of power. This underscores the prophetic method of addressing the root of corruption. The reason is given again: “for he has truly transgressed all bounds.” The repetition of Pharaoh’s transgression from verse 24 reinforces the gravity of his crimes. His tyranny was not merely political; it was a rebellion against Allah’s sovereignty, marked by the claim to divinity, the enslavement of a whole people, and the brutal killing of their infants. The word “ṭaghā” implies a flood-like overflow, someone who has burst all limits of decency and submission to God. By sending Moses and Aaron directly to such a person, Allah demonstrates that no power on earth is beyond the reach of His message. The prophets are not sent only to the receptive; they must confront the obstinate and give them a clear warning. The fact that they are told “both of you” reassures Moses that Aaron shares the burden fully. They are to stand together before the mightiest king of the age. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a source of strength as he faced the Quraysh leaders who, like Pharaoh, considered themselves lords. The instruction to go directly to the transgressor also teaches that the call to truth must not be diluted by fear of the powerful. Justice begins with addressing the oppressor. Thus, the command is a declaration of war against falsehood, but a war waged with the weapon of truth and the signs of Allah, not with swords. It sets the stage for the confrontation that follows.
44 | “Speak to him gently, so perhaps he may be mindful ˹of Me˺ or fearful ˹of My punishment˺.” | Despite Pharaoh’s monstrous transgression, Allah instructs Moses and Aaron to adopt a gentle approach. The Arabic “fa-qūlā lahu qawlan layyinan” means “speak to him a gentle word.” This is one of the most profound lessons in the Quran on the etiquette of da’wah. The softness of speech does not imply weakness or compromise on the message, but rather a wise and compassionate mode of delivery that respects the humanity of even the worst tyrant. The purpose is given: “so perhaps he may be mindful or fearful.” The word “yatadhakkar” means to receive admonition and remember the truth, while “yakhshā” means to develop a reverential fear of Allah. The goal of the gentle approach is not to flatter Pharaoh but to open his heart. Had they begun with harshness, his arrogance would have instantly erected a barrier. Gentle speech is a means to penetrate pride and awaken the dormant conscience. The verse demonstrates the mercy of Allah, Who desires guidance for all, even a man who said “I am your lord, most high.” The use of “perhaps” (la‘alla) on Allah’s part indicates hope and possibility, not uncertainty; it teaches believers to remain optimistic in their calling, leaving the outcome to Allah. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a guiding principle when dealing with the Meccan chiefs. He was patient, gentle, and often absorbed their insults with grace, hoping that one day they would believe. The lesson extends to all Muslims: when conveying the truth, the manner matters as much as the content. A gentle word can soften the hardest heart, while harshness can repel even those inclined to accept. The command to Moses and Aaron remains a timeless charter for kind and wise communication, even in the face of extreme evil.
45 | They both pleaded, “Our Lord! This is not a sign of weak faith but an honest acknowledgment of their vulnerability. They address Allah as “Our Lord,” and confess two specific fears: that Pharaoh might swiftly punish them before they can deliver the full message, or that he might “act tyrannically,” meaning he would escalate in arrogance and transgression, making the mission even more dangerous. The phrase “an yafruṭa ‘alaynā” can mean that he might hasten to harm them or that his transgression might increase beyond all limits. Their fear is natural; they are two unarmed men walking into the court of a god-king with absolute power of life and death. This plea reveals the full humanity of the prophets, who were not superhuman beings immune to fear. Their courage was not the absence of fear but the strength to proceed despite it. This admission also serves as a form of supplication, indirectly asking for protection and reinforcement. The verse teaches believers that it is permissible to voice fears to Allah and to seek His help in overcoming them. Suppressing fear can sometimes lead to anxiety, but expressing it in prayer while still obeying the command is the prophetic model. The Prophet Muhammad similarly expressed his fear at the beginning of his mission and was repeatedly comforted. The plea of Moses and Aaron sets the stage for the beautiful divine reassurance that follows immediately, showing that Allah does not rebuke His servants for their fears but answers them with calming promises of His presence.
46 | Allah reassured ˹them˺, “Have no fear! I am with you, hearing and seeing. | The response to their fear is immediate and all-encompassing. When a believer internalizes that the All-Hearing, All-Seeing Lord is with him, the threats of creation diminish. The assurance is given before they even step into Pharaoh’s court, so they can carry that certainty in their hearts. The verse also illustrates that after the servant expresses vulnerability, Allah responds with more than they asked for—He gives His own company.
47 | “So go to him and say, ‘Indeed we are both messengers from your Lord, so let the Children of Israel go with us, and do not oppress them. We have come to you with a sign from your Lord. The first demand is the liberation of the Children of Israel: “so let the Children of Israel go with us, and do not oppress them.” The mission was not just about theological debate; it was about ending systematic injustice. The message of tawḥīd is inseparable from social liberation. The phrase “do not oppress them” implies that continuing enslavement is an act of oppression that must cease. Then they are to present the proof: “We have come to you with a sign from your Lord.” This sign would be the staff and the shining hand, tangible evidence of their divine mandate. The word “salām” here carries meanings of peace and safety from punishment. The message is thus complete: acknowledgment of a higher Lord, the demand for freedom, the evidence of prophethood, and the promise of salvation for those who believe. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was the very structure of his call to the Quraysh: worship Allah alone, free people from the slavery of idols, present the Quran as the sign, and promise eternal success for the followers of guidance. The verse distills the entire prophetic mission into a clear, powerful statement.
48 | “It has indeed been revealed to us that the punishment will be upon whoever denies ˹the truth˺ and turns away.’” | The warning that completes the message is now delivered. The phrase “innā qad ūḥiya ilaynā” emphasizes that this is not their personal opinion but a revelation from Allah. The punishment is specified for those who “deny” (kadhdhaba) the truth and “turn away” (tawallā). Denial implies rejecting the message after recognizing it, and turning away means abandoning it in practice and attitude. The warning of punishment balances the earlier promise of salvation; the message is complete with both glad tidings and warnings. This was necessary because Pharaoh and his court were steeped in disbelief and oppression; they needed to know the consequences of persisting in their ways. The punishment mentioned could encompass both worldly destruction and the torment of the Hereafter. Moses and Aaron were instructed to be gentle in speech, but that gentleness did not mean concealing the hard truths. The warning was delivered clearly, without ambiguity, leaving Pharaoh with no excuse. The verse demonstrates the prophetic balance between hope and fear. Presenting only mercy without warning can make people complacent, while only warning without mercy can lead to despair. The message of Moses combined both. For the Prophet Muhammad, the same balance is evident throughout the Quran, where the mercy of Allah is emphasized alongside the severe punishment for those who reject faith. The instruction to Moses and Aaron also shows that warnings should be given before punishment descends, out of Allah’s justice and mercy. The verse concludes the initial speech that the two brothers were to deliver, setting the stage for Pharaoh’s response.
49 | Pharaoh asked, “Who then is the Lord of you two, O Moses?” | After hearing the message, Pharaoh responds not with immediate violence but with a question. His question, however, is not an honest search for truth; it is a mixture of sarcasm, dismissal, and a challenge. By asking “Who then is the Lord of you two, O Moses?” Pharaoh deliberately ignores Aaron, perhaps belittling the partnership or focusing on Moses as the primary speaker. His phrasing “the Lord of you two” insinuates that he does not recognize any such being; he wants Moses to define this deity so he can mock or refute. The question reveals Pharaoh’s arrogance; he considered himself the lord of Egypt, and now two men from the enslaved race speak of a Lord over him. He asks to know the identity of this alleged competitor. The question also attempts to trap Moses: if Moses described a physical or local god, Pharaoh could easily deride it; if he described an abstract deity, Pharaoh could dismiss it as irrelevant. This is a common tactic of tyrants when confronted with divine authority—to demand a definition that can be undermined. The verse shows that those who are invested in their own false lordship find the very concept of a supreme Lord threatening. The question is a pivotal moment in the narrative, setting up Moses’s magnificent reply, which does not fall into the trap but instead points to the universal signs of the Creator. The Prophet Muhammad faced similar questions from the Quraysh, who would ask, “What is your Lord like?” The Quran taught him to respond by pointing to the signs in creation. The inquiry of Pharaoh, though laced with mockery, became the opportunity for a profound declaration of truth.
50 | He answered, “Our Lord is the One Who has given everything its ˹distinctive˺ form, then guided ˹it˺.” | Moses’s answer is a masterpiece of concise theological clarity. Instead of getting drawn into abstract names or debates, he describes Allah by His creative act. “The One Who has given everything its distinctive form” translates “alladhī a‘ṭā kulla shay’in khalqahu.” This means that Allah created each thing with its specific nature, shape, characteristics, and purpose. Every creature, from the smallest particle to the largest celestial body, has been given its proper form in the grand design. Then, after giving form, “He guided it.” This guidance is the innate direction that every creation follows: the fish knows how to swim, the bird how to fly, the celestial bodies how to orbit, the plants how to grow, and the human intellect how to reason. This includes the instinctual guidance (hidāyat al-wujdān) and the guidance through revelation for human beings. The answer beautifully demonstrates that the Lord’s existence and attributes can be known by reflecting on the world. Moses did not argue from scripture but from the observable facts of creation. Pharaoh, who prided himself on his knowledge of the natural world and his magicians’ manipulation of it, was being told that behind every orderly system is a single Creator. The answer also implies that just as Allah gave everything its form and guidance, He has now given Pharaoh a message and guidance through Moses; rejecting it means violating one’s own created purpose. The Prophet Muhammad would later give similar answers to the pagans, drawing attention to the creation of the heavens and the earth. The verse stands as a timeless proof of Allah’s lordship, accessible to every person with an open mind.
51 | Pharaoh asked, “And what about previous peoples?” | Pharaoh, unable to counter the argument from creation, shifts to another line of questioning. He asks about “previous peoples” — the generations of old who did not worship this Lord that Moses proclaims. This question is a rhetorical trap: if Moses says they were misguided and perished, Pharaoh might mock their ancestors. If Moses says they were saved, Pharaoh could ask where their gods are now. The question also reflects a common skeptical argument: if your Lord is the only true God, what happened to all the nations that came before? Were they all wrong and damned? Pharaoh’s intent is to paint Moses’s message as a novelty, a break from tradition. He wants to portray himself as the guardian of the established order and ancestral ways. This was exactly the argument of the Meccan pagans against the Prophet Muhammad: “We found our fathers upon a religion.” Pharaoh is using the weight of history to dismiss the call to tawḥīd. The question also carries a veiled threat: those who challenged the established powers in the past met their doom, so Moses should be careful. The verse captures the psychological warfare of the tyrant, who tries to undermine the messenger’s confidence by making him appear ignorant of history or disrespectful of the ancestors. The stage is set for Moses’s response, which masterfully deflects the question away from futile speculation and anchors all knowledge in the divine record, while maintaining the focus on the present call.
52 | He replied, “That knowledge is with my Lord in a Record. My Lord neither falters nor forgets ˹anything˺.” | Moses refuses to be drawn into a detailed historical debate that would sidetrack the mission. He responds by assigning the knowledge of the previous peoples entirely to Allah. “That knowledge is with my Lord in a Record” — the Preserved Tablet or the Divine Book of Decrees — meaning Allah knows exactly what those nations did, what they believed, and what their fate was. Moses’s role is not to give a historical lecture but to convey the current message. He then adds a powerful description of Allah: “My Lord neither falters nor forgets.” The word “yaḍillu” means to err or go astray, and “yansā” means to forget. Unlike human rulers whose knowledge is imperfect and memory fallible, Allah’s knowledge is absolute and eternal. He does not make mistakes, and nothing escapes His record. This statement indirectly answers Pharaoh’s challenge: whatever those previous peoples did is perfectly recorded, and they have been or will be judged with absolute justice. No error or injustice can occur. The response also implies a warning: Pharaoh’s own deeds are being recorded, and he will be held accountable just as previous tyrants were. The elegance of Moses’s answer lies in its refusal to entertain irrelevant debate while reaffirming Allah’s supreme knowledge. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a model for dealing with those who asked about the fate of past nations or the details of the unseen; the proper reply is to refer knowledge to Allah and keep the focus on the message at hand. The verse teaches believers that not every question deserves a detailed answer; some are traps, and the best response is to entrust matters to Allah and redirect to His attributes.
53 | ˹He is the One˺ Who has laid out the earth for ˹all of˺ you, and set in it pathways for you, and sends down rain from the sky, causing various types of plants to grow, | Moses continues his description of the Lord, now returning to the tangible signs of Allah’s power and mercy. After stating that knowledge of the past rests with Allah, he brings the conversation back to the immediate evidence of creation. The verse describes three specific blessings: the earth laid out like a carpet for habitation, pathways carved through mountains and valleys for travel, and rain sent down from the sky that causes a variety of plants to spring forth. “Laid out the earth” points to its stability and suitability for human life. The “pathways” are not just physical roads but also the instinctual guidance by which creatures navigate. Rain is the source of life, and its mention emphasizes the dependency of all life on Allah’s mercy. The “various types of plants” — paired, diverse species — reflect the intricate design. This description serves multiple purposes: it answers the earlier question about the Lord by making Him known through His works, it demonstrates His benevolence and thus His right to be worshipped, and it silently rebukes Pharaoh, who despite controlling the land, cannot create a single seed or raindrop. The imagery shifts the scene from the court of an arrogant king to the vast, orderly universe, reminding everyone that the true Lord is not a tribal deity but the Sustainer of all. The Prophet Muhammad frequently drew similar attention to the natural world as a sign of Allah’s oneness. The verse integrates theology with ecology, teaching that every natural phenomenon is a sign pointing to the Creator, and that rejecting such clear signs is an act of blindness.
54 | ˹so˺ eat and graze your cattle. The verse draws a clear link between observing nature and attaining spiritual truth. It is a call to contemplation. For Pharaoh and his court, who prided themselves on wisdom and magic, the challenge is thrown: if you truly have sound judgment, recognize the Creator in these clear signs. The meal and the pasture are daily proofs of a compassionate Lord, yet they are often taken for granted. The verse thus elevates the ordinary act of eating into a sign that can lead to faith. It is a recurring Quranic theme that the cycle of rain, plant, and sustenance is among the most accessible and powerful proofs of divine mercy and power.
55 | From the earth We created you, and into it We will return you, and from it We will bring you back again. | This verse encapsulates the entire human story in relation to the earth. “From the earth We created you” refers to the creation of the first human, Adam, from clay, and the ongoing creation of all humans from the nutrients that ultimately derive from the earth. This is a reminder of mortality, something Pharaoh with his false claim to divinity desperately sought to deny. “And from it We will bring you back again” is a powerful affirmation of the resurrection. The same earth that receives the dead will one day, by Allah’s command, yield them forth anew for judgment. The three stages—creation, death, and resurrection—mirror the plant cycle described just before: a seed from the earth grows, dies, and is revived. The human being is no different. This verse is strategically placed in the dialogue with Pharaoh, who rejected the concept of the Hereafter. It asserts the inevitability of the Hour that Moses was earlier commanded to believe in. For the Prophet Muhammad, who faced Meccan pagans denying resurrection, this verse and its profound logic were a source of argument. The entire story of life is contained in this single verse, and it is presented as an undeniable reality. The One who originated you from dust can just as easily bring you out again. The verse leaves no room for the arrogance of those who think they can escape their origin and their end. It is both a theological declaration and an existential grounding for every human being.

Allah says, “We certainly showed Pharaoh all of Our signs.” This includes the staff, the shining hand, and the subsequent plagues—the flood, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—nine clear signs in total. Pharaoh saw the miracles with his own eyes, yet he chose to label them as magic and to reject their implications. This reveals that disbelief is not always due to lack of evidence but often due to arrogance, vested interests, and an unwillingness to surrender one’s power and desires. Pharaoh’s kingdom and god-status were threatened, so he hardened his heart. This verse contains a deep warning: even the clearest signs cannot guide a heart that is determined to reject. The Prophet Muhammad experienced the same pain when the Quraysh saw the miracle of the Quran and yet denied it. The verse also serves to highlight the justice of Allah; He gave Pharaoh every opportunity, sending Moses with multiple signs and gentle speech, but when the rejection was final, punishment became due. The summary prepares the listener for the unfolding of Pharaoh’s schemes and his ultimate doom.

He accuses Moses of trying to “drive us out of our land” — a fear-mongering appeal to nationalism and territorial integrity. By using “us,” he aligns the Egyptian elite and perhaps the common people with himself, portraying Moses as an outsider threatening the homeland. He labels the signs “magic,” thereby dismissing their divine origin and reducing them to a contest of sorcerers. This was a common tactic of tyrants when confronted with prophets: discredit the messenger, attribute his miracles to trickery, and stir up fear of social upheaval. Pharaoh knew that Moses and the Israelites were an enslaved minority; accusing them of a takeover plot would rally the Egyptian populace against them. The irony is that Pharaoh himself was a foreign Hyksos descendant by some accounts, but he now poses as the defender of the land. The verse captures the psychological manipulation employed by those in power to deflect from the truth. The Prophet Muhammad faced the exact same accusations: the Quraysh said he wanted to undermine their ancestral religion, divide their community, and seize power. The question “Have you come…” is dripping with sarcasm, intended to put Moses on the defensive. Yet Moses does not fall for the bait; he will respond calmly with a challenge. This verse teaches that falsehood often cloaks itself in the guise of patriotism and cultural preservation, and that the messenger must see through these smokescreens.

He claims that Egypt can produce magic equal to Moses’s signs. The phrase “similar magic” reveals his refusal to accept the miracles as anything beyond sorcery. He is confident in the skills of his own magicians, who were among the most advanced in the ancient world. The demand for an appointment “that neither of us will fail to keep” and “in a central place” shows his cunning. He wants a neutral, open ground where the maximum number of people can witness the confrontation, believing that a public defeat of Moses would crush the Israelite morale and reestablish his own divine aura. The “central place” would be easily accessible to both the court and the common people. Pharaoh’s strategy was to turn a religious and moral confrontation into a spectacle that he assumed he would win. This reveals his arrogance and his reliance on worldly power and skill. He does not for a moment consider the possibility that Moses’s signs are truly from a Lord greater than him. For Moses, this challenge was an opportunity; a public showdown would make the victory of truth unmistakable. The Prophet Muhammad was similarly challenged by the Quraysh to produce miracles, and while his primary miracle was the Quran, the pattern of opponents demanding displays of power recurs. The verse highlights the desperate attempt of falsehood to compete with truth on its own theatrical terms.
59 | Moses said, “Your appointment is on the Day of the Festival, and let the people be gathered mid-morning.” | Moses accepts the challenge with calm confidence and sets the terms for the encounter. He chooses the “Day of the Festival” (yawm az-zīnah), a major Egyptian holiday when people would be off work, dressed in their finest, and gathered in celebration. This was a strategic choice: the festival guaranteed a massive public attendance, ensuring that the showdown would be witnessed by the widest possible audience. The “mid-morning” timing (duḥan) was when the sun was bright and visibility perfect, eliminating any suspicion of trickery done under the cover of darkness or shadows. Moses’s choice demonstrates his complete faith in Allah’s victory; he did not seek a secluded or secret meeting but the most exposed and crowded time. The festival likely honoured Pharaoh or their deities, and Moses’s victory on that very day would symbolically dethrone the false gods in the presence of their own worshippers. The verse also shows Moses’s wisdom and prudence, ensuring that no excuse could later be made about a rigged or poorly attended event. He gave Pharaoh exactly what he wanted—a public spectacle—but on terms that would magnify the glory of the true God. The Prophet Muhammad’s open proclamation of the message, despite the dangers, mirrors this prophetic confidence. The verse teaches that when truth confronts falsehood, there is no need for secrecy or dark corners; the light of truth thrives in the full glare of day. The stage is now set for the epic confrontation between the staff of Moses and the sorcery of Egypt.
60 | Pharaoh then withdrew, orchestrated his scheme, then returned. | After the appointment was set, Pharaoh departed from the negotiation to put his plan into action. The verb “tawallā” means he turned away and withdrew. What followed was a period of intense preparation. The phrase “fa-jama‘a kaydahu” means he gathered his plot or his deceitful scheme. This involved summoning the most skilled magicians from all corners of Egypt, training them, coordinating their performance, and perhaps preparing props and illusions. The word “kayd” implies cunning, strategy, and deception. Pharaoh was not taking any chances; despite his arrogance, he mobilized the full resources of his kingdom to ensure Moses’s defeat. Then “thumma atā” — then he came, meaning he returned to the appointed place on the agreed day with his assembled magicians and the public. The verse is concise but paints a vivid picture of the tyrant busily engineering his counterattack. It shows the machinery of falsehood working hard to suppress the truth, while Moses simply relied on what Allah had given him. The contrast is stark: Pharaoh with his massive scheme, and Moses with his staff and his Lord. The verse also subtly critiques the futility of human plotting against divine will. All of Pharaoh’s careful orchestration would crumble in moments before the serpent of Moses. The Prophet Muhammad’s Meccan opponents similarly plotted and planned, holding meetings in Dar an-Nadwah to decide how to stop him, but their schemes were also shattered. The verse teaches that while the people of falsehood will always prepare extensively, the outcome is in Allah’s hands. The narrative pauses here, building suspense for the dramatic event that follows.

Moses warned that such a crime would provoke a divine punishment that could “wipe you out,” utterly destroy them. Falsehood, no matter how elaborately constructed, carries within itself the seeds of its own collapse. The magicians were masters of illusion, but Moses spoke with the authority of revelation. His warning was not a lack of confidence; rather, it was a final call to conscience before the undeniable sign would appear. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, similarly warned his opponents that their idols and fabrications would lead them to ruin. This moment also highlights the prophetic ethics: even when facing enemies who sought to humiliate him, Moses gave them a chance to reconsider, demonstrating that the mission is to save, not to destroy. The verse sets the moral stage for the imminent miracle, showing that the real battle is between truth and fabricated lies.
62 | So the magicians disputed the matter among themselves, conversing privately. | Moses’s warning had a visible effect on the magicians. Instead of immediately proceeding with confidence, they began to argue among themselves in hushed, private conversations. The Arabic “fa-tanāza‘ū amrahum baynahum” suggests a heated internal dispute. This indicates that they were not a monolithic block of defiant unbelievers; some may have been shaken by Moses’s tone, sensing that this was not ordinary sorcery. Others might have dismissed the warning and wanted to crush him. The fact that they conversed privately shows that Pharaoh’s side was not as united as it seemed. The warning had planted a seed of doubt. The magicians were the intellectual elite of Egypt, well-versed in the secrets of illusion and sleight of hand. They could recognize that Moses’s confidence did not stem from the typical arrogance of a rival magician. Their dispute likely revolved around whether Moses was really just a magician or something more. This internal division is a common pattern when falsehood confronts truth: the coalition of falsehood begins to crack. The Prophet Muhammad’s opponents also experienced internal conflicts, with some secretly acknowledging the Quran’s beauty while publicly opposing it. The verse also shows the mercy contained in a well-timed word of warning; Moses’s few words became the catalyst for introspection. Yet, despite the dispute, they did not immediately embrace the truth; they decided to press forward, perhaps out of fear of Pharaoh or hope of reward. The private conversation foreshadows their ultimate surrender; the truth was already working in their hearts before the miracle even occurred. The stage is now set for the public resolution of their inner conflict, which will erupt in a stunning proclamation of faith.

Their statement, likely addressed to each other to boost morale, reveals the political and cultural framing they adopted. The accusation that they “want to drive you out of your land” echoes Pharaoh’s own earlier propaganda. This is a typical reaction of those invested in a status quo when confronted with a message that challenges its foundations. They portray reform as destruction and prophets as foreign agents. The verse reveals that even the magicians, who were about to witness the truth, initially rationalized their resistance by framing the conflict in worldly terms. The same accusations were hurled at the Prophet Muhammad: that he was a poet or magician seeking to divide the community and erase ancestral customs. Yet, the accusation is hollow; Moses’s demand was not for the Egyptians to lose their land but to free the Israelites and worship the One God. The magicians’ conclusion was a final attempt to steel themselves against the miracle that would shatter their illusions and, ultimately, their own hearts would be the first to be captured by the very tradition they were defending.

The phrase “orchestrate your plan” translates “fa-ajmi‘ū kaydakum,” meaning gather all your cunning and stratagems. They were to use every trick, every secret technique, to produce the most impressive display of magic possible. The instruction to “come forward in perfect ranks” emphasizes unity and order, presenting a formidable, disciplined front. The spectacle was to be a show of force, designed to intimidate Moses and convince the crowd of their superiority. Their metric of success was entirely worldly—public acclaim, Pharaoh’s favour, and the preservation of their privileged status. They could not yet conceive of a success that lay beyond this world. This reflects the mindset of those who measure everything by immediate, tangible outcomes. Yet, the verse contains a profound irony: the success they spoke of would indeed be achieved, but not by them. True success would be the imān that the defeated magicians would gain moments later, a success that would outlast Pharaoh’s empire. The call to perfect ranks also shows that falsehood often relies on impressive organization and numbers, while truth may appear small and humble. The Prophet Muhammad’s small band of followers facing the massive Quraysh armies at Badr mirrors this scene. The verse thus captures the pre-battle tension, where the forces of illusion are most confident, just before the truth shatters their ranks.
65 | They said, “O Moses! Either you cast, or let us be the first to cast.” | With the crowds gathered and the ranks formed, the magicians addressed Moses directly, offering him the choice to begin the contest. Their words “O Moses! Either you cast, or let us be the first to cast” are couched in apparent fairness and professional courtesy. In ancient contests of magic, the order of performance could be a strategic advantage; going first might overwhelm the opponent, while going last could allow a counter-response. By giving Moses the choice, they displayed a certain confidence, perhaps even a touch of condescension, as if to say, “We are so certain of our superiority that we let you decide.” It was also a way to appear magnanimous before the massive festival crowd. Moses, however, would make a choice that surprised them. This moment also underscores the prophetic calm in the face of provocation. Moses did not feel pressured to seize any perceived tactical edge. The magicians, despite their earlier internal dispute, now projected unity and bravado. Yet, this politeness masked their fundamental misunderstanding of what they were dealing with. They saw the encounter as a duel between magicians, while Moses carried a sign from the Lord of the worlds. The Prophet Muhammad was similarly offered compromises and challenges by the Quraysh, who thought they were negotiating with a mere human claimant. The verse sets up the dramatic sequence: the magicians, with all their ropes and staffs, are about to unleash their greatest illusions, and Moses will respond not with equal trickery, but with divine truth. The choice they gave him became the very means by which their own falsehood would be exposed and swallowed whole.
66 | Moses responded, “No, you go first.” And suddenly their ropes and staffs appeared to him—by their magic—to be slithering. | Moses’s response was unexpected and strategically brilliant. By telling them to cast first, he allowed the full force of their illusion to be displayed before he presented his sign. This way, there could be no excuse that his miracle merely disrupted their concentration or that they had not had a fair chance. When they cast, a terrifying spectacle unfolded: their many ropes and staffs, through a powerful act of mass illusion, appeared to be living serpents, slithering rapidly across the ground. The phrase “appeared to him—by their magic—to be slithering” carefully notes that this was a perceptual trick. The objects did not actually transform into living creatures; the magicians used some technique, perhaps involving quicksilver or hidden mechanisms combined with spells, to create the overwhelming impression of a floor filled with snakes. The verse acknowledges that even Moses, the prophet of Allah, was affected by the illusion and perceived the serpents as real. This highlights the sheer mastery of the Egyptian magicians; their craft was so potent that it could momentarily deceive even a prophet’s senses, though not his heart. The detail underscores the human vulnerability of Moses and makes the upcoming divine reassurance all the more necessary. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, also faced the psychological warfare of his enemies, whose words and propaganda were like magic, capable of causing momentary doubt in weak hearts. The verse teaches that truth does not always appear triumphant at the first glance; falsehood can put on a dazzling show. Yet, the illusion, no matter how convincing, remains hollow. The slithering ropes were a powerful symbol of the deceptive nature of sin and unbelief, which appear lively and formidable but are ultimately lifeless. The scene is now set for the confrontation between the dead illusions and the living miracle.
67 | So Moses concealed fear within himself. | Despite being a prophet armed with divine signs, Moses was fully human, and the terrifying sight of the magicians’ illusion filled him with a natural fear. The Arabic “fa-awjasa fī nafsihī khīfatan Mūsā” means he felt a hidden, inner fear. This fear was not a doubt in Allah’s promise, but an instinctive human reaction to a sudden, overwhelming threat. Imagine standing before a sea of writhing serpents, knowing the crowd and the tyrant are watching. Even the strongest heart can tremble. The Quran records this with honesty, neither diminishing the miracle of his prophethood nor hiding his human vulnerability. This is a powerful lesson: having faith does not mean the absence of fear, but the ability to overcome it through trust in Allah. Moses concealed this fear; he did not let it show on his face or in his actions, preserving his composure before the enemy. That is the mark of true courage—not being fearless, but controlling the fear. The verse also sets the stage for the immediate divine reassurance. In the most critical moment, when the heart is about to be overwhelmed, Allah’s help arrives. The Prophet Muhammad experienced similar moments, such as when he and Abu Bakr hid in the cave and the pursuers were at the entrance; he reassured his companion, “Do not grieve, Allah is with us.” The inner fear of Moses was a test, a moment of human weakness that would be met with divine strength, teaching believers that when they feel fear in the path of truth, they are not abandoned. The concealment of fear also demonstrates leadership: showing panic can demoralize followers. Aaron stood beside him, and the Israelites watched from afar; Moses’s self-control maintained their hope until the word of Allah came.
68 | We reassured ˹him˺, “Do not fear! It is certainly you who will prevail.” | At that very moment of inner fear, the divine voice spoke directly to Moses’s heart, repeating the earlier reassurance but now with even greater specificity. The command “Do not fear” is an instant balm, coming from the All-Seeing Lord who perceived the concealed fear of verse 67. This illustrates the immediate responsiveness of Allah to the emotional state of His prophets. The promise “It is certainly you who will prevail” uses the emphatic “innaka” and “anta al-a‘lā,” meaning you will be the uppermost, the victorious. There is no condition, no hesitation. The victory was not dependent on Moses’s skill or his own emotional state; it was decreed by Allah. This assurance transformed Moses’s fear into certainty. The promise of victory before any outward sign of it is a recurring pattern in the stories of the prophets. The Prophet Muhammad was similarly assured of ultimate triumph while still a persecuted minority in Mecca. The word “a‘lā” also carries the connotation of supremacy in truth and dignity, not just a physical win. Moses would not merely defeat the magicians in a spectacle; he would establish the superiority of divine truth over human deception. The reassurance did not deny the reality of the threatening serpents but promised that they would be nullified. This teaches believers that the awareness of Allah’s promise should overpower the perception of danger. The fear was real, but the promise was truer. The verse marks the turning point, the inner shift from anxiety to empowered certainty, which would immediately manifest in his command to his staff. It shows that the source of a believer’s courage is not in themselves but in the living word of their Lord that descends upon the heart.
69 | “Cast what is in your right hand, and it will swallow up what they have made, for what they have made is no more than a magic trick. And magicians can never succeed wherever they go.” | The instruction is specific: throw the staff that was in his right hand, the very object that had already turned into a serpent at the sacred valley. The command “it will swallow up what they have made” is a powerful image. The Arabic “talqaf” means to snatch up and devour rapidly. The staff, once transformed, would not merely match their serpents but consume them entirely, leaving no trace of the illusion. The explanation follows: “what they have made is no more than a magic trick.” The phrase “ṣana‘ū” implies something fabricated, an artifact of deception. Their serpents were not real life; they were a product of skill and trickery, devoid of substance. This contrast between divine creation and human fabrication is central. Allah creates with the command “Be,” while magicians merely deceive the eyes. Then the verse pronounces a universal law: “magicians can never succeed wherever they go.” The word “magician” here represents all who use falsehood to manipulate and deceive for unjust ends. Their apparent victories are temporary; ultimately, they fail because their foundation is illusion. This statement is both a reassurance for Moses and a judgment on the whole enterprise of sorcery. The magicians themselves would soon testify to this truth. The Prophet Muhammad’s mission similarly confronted the “magic” of eloquence and propaganda with the substantial truth of the Quran, which swallows all false ideologies. The verse beautifully dissects the nature of falsehood: it may look impressive and cause momentary fear, but it has no real existence and cannot withstand the truth when truth arrives. The command to cast also signifies that the believer must act on divine command, and only then will the miracle manifest. Moses had to throw the staff, an act of obedience, and Allah would handle the rest.
70 | So the magicians fell down in prostration, declaring, “We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses.” | When Moses cast his staff, it transformed into a massive serpent that devoured all the illusions instantly. The effect on the magicians was immediate and overwhelming. They did not simply admit defeat; they fell down in prostration. This physical act of sujūd was the ultimate submission, the very posture of worship they had reserved for Pharaoh or idols. Their prostration was not a polite bow but a collapse in recognition of the unmistakable divine power. They had enough expertise to know that what Moses displayed was not magic; no magician could truly transform a staff into a living creature that devours other objects, nor could any illusion resist their collective craft so completely. Their declaration, “We believe in the Lord of Aaron and Moses,” is profoundly significant. They did not just believe in Moses as a superior magician; they recognized the Lord of the two prophets. The mention of Aaron alongside Moses emphasizes that both brothers were messengers of the same God. The magicians, who moments ago were the instruments of Pharaoh’s propaganda, now became the first public confessors of faith from the Egyptian elite. Their conversion is one of the most dramatic in the Quran. It shows that true experts, once they see the truth, can recognize it faster than the laypeople blinded by prejudice. The Prophet Muhammad’s staunchest opponents, like Umar ibn al-Khattab, also turned into the greatest champions of Islam when their hearts were opened. The public prostration was an open challenge to Pharaoh’s authority; it was a political as well as spiritual revolution. The verse marks the complete reversal: the plot of Pharaoh became the stage for the triumph of faith, and the magicians, his prized defenders, became the first witnesses to the truth of Moses.
71 | Pharaoh threatened, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission? He must be your master who taught you magic. I will certainly cut off your hands and feet on opposite sides, and crucify you on the trunks of palm trees. You will really see whose punishment is more severe and more lasting.” | Pharaoh’s reaction was one of furious arrogance. His first statement, “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission,” reveals his totalitarian mindset. He considered himself the absolute sovereign whose authorization was required even for the innermost act of faith. This exposed the emptiness of his claim to divinity: true God does not need to grant permission for belief, nor can He be defied by a creature’s faith. Then Pharaoh, unable to refute the miracle, reverted to conspiracy theory: “He must be your master who taught you magic.” He accused Moses of being the secret grandmaster of the magicians, implying the whole event was a collusion to overthrow him. This was a desperate attempt to discredit the miracle in the eyes of the crowd. The threat that followed was brutal: cutting off hands and feet on opposite sides (right hand with left foot) and crucifixion on palm trunks. This was an ancient method of torture and public display, designed to strike terror and suppress any dissent. Pharaoh’s question, “You will really see whose punishment is more severe and more lasting,” was a direct challenge to the magicians’ newfound faith, contrasting his temporal punishment with the warning of divine torment they had just accepted. The irony is profound: the punishment that is truly more severe and lasting is the Hellfire that awaits Pharaoh, not the palm trunks he threatened with. The Prophet Muhammad’s opponents similarly tortured early believers like Bilal and the family of Yasir, yet those believers remained steadfast. Pharaoh’s threat shows that when tyranny is exposed, it resorts to violence, because it has no rational argument left. The verse captures the escalating conflict between the kingdom of fear and the kingdom of faith.
72 | They responded, “By the One Who created us! We will never prefer you over the clear proofs that have come to us. So do whatever you want! Your authority only covers the ˹fleeting˺ life of this world.” | The magicians, now believers, responded with astonishing courage. Their oath “By the One Who created us” shows their immediate internalization of tawḥīd; they swore by the Creator, not by Pharaoh. They declared they would never prefer Pharaoh over the clear proofs that had come to them. The “clear proofs” were the miracle of the staff and the undeniable truth it demonstrated. Their choice was absolute: the truth was worth any torture. Then they challenged Pharaoh with a remarkable statement of spiritual liberation: “So do whatever you want!” This was not despair but a declaration of independence. They recognized that Pharaoh’s power was limited to the fleeting life of this world. The Arabic “hādhihi al-ḥayāti ad-dunyā” emphasizes the lowliness and temporariness of worldly existence compared to the eternal Hereafter. This is a profound shift in perspective; once the heart is attached to the eternal, the threats of a mortal tyrant lose their terror. The magicians, who hours earlier were seeking Pharaoh’s reward, now dismissed his entire kingdom as insignificant. This transformation is a testament to the power of true faith, which reorders all priorities. The Prophet Muhammad taught, “Be in this world as if you were a stranger or a traveler.” The magicians embodied this instantly. Their words also served as a public dismantling of Pharaoh’s claimed divinity; by restricting his authority to this world, they denied his power over the soul and the afterlife. The verse inspires believers that the worst a tyrant can do is end a worldly life, but the believer’s true life is with Allah. This fearless stance is a high point of the Quranic narrative of courage.
73 | “Indeed, we have believed in our Lord so He may forgive our sins and that magic you have forced us to practice. And Allah is far superior ˹in reward˺ and more lasting ˹in punishment˺.” | The magicians then articulated the content of their faith and its motivation. They stated, “Indeed, we have believed in our Lord” — emphasizing their personal commitment. The purpose of their belief was “so He may forgive our sins.” They acknowledged their past of sorcery and opposition to the truth as sins that needed divine pardon. Specifically, they mentioned “that magic you have forced us to practice,” indicating that Pharaoh had coerced them into using sorcery against Moses, though they had initially participated willingly. The word “forced” (akrahtanā) might reflect the pressure they felt under his rule, or it could mean he compelled them to attempt to defeat Moses. In either case, they sought forgiveness for their involvement. This confession shows that sincere repentance can wash away even a lifetime of falsehood. Then they concluded with a declaration that answered Pharaoh’s earlier challenge: “And Allah is far superior in reward and more lasting in punishment.” Pharaoh had asked whose punishment was more severe and lasting; the magicians now affirmed that Allah’s reward for believers is superior, and His punishment for disbelievers is truly eternal and inescapable. This was a direct theological rebuttal to Pharaoh’s threat. They publicly declared that they feared the punishment of Allah more than the crucifixion, and they desired the reward of Allah more than Pharaoh’s gold. The Prophet Muhammad’s companions similarly weighed the temporary persecutions against the eternal bliss of Paradise. The magicians’ statement is a masterpiece of newborn faith: they acknowledged their sins, sought forgiveness, recognized Allah’s supreme attributes, and defied the tyrant with a calm and reasoned conviction. It shows that true imān brings immediate clarity and fearlessness.
74 | Whoever comes to their Lord as an evildoer will certainly have Hell, where they can neither live nor die. | After reporting the magicians’ bold declaration, the Quran inserts a universal divine decree that frames the entire conflict. The verse speaks of the ultimate outcome for those who come to their Lord as “an evildoer” (mujriman). The word mujrim refers to one who commits crimes against their own soul through disbelief and rebellion against Allah, like Pharaoh. Such a person will have Hell as their certain destination. The description of Hell is chilling: “where they can neither live nor die.” This means the punishment is a perpetual state of agony, not a death that brings relief nor a life that holds any comfort. The condemned soul will wish for annihilation but will not be granted it. This is a profound warning that the suffering of the Hereafter is continuous and inescapable, unlike worldly torture which ends with death. The verse serves as a direct commentary on Pharaoh’s threat; his punishment of crucifixion ends in death, but the punishment of Allah for unrepentant evildoers has no such escape. The magicians, by contrast, had chosen to come to their Lord as believers, thus avoiding this horrific fate. The verse also explains the inner state of the evildoer: they come to their Lord carrying the weight of their crimes, with no intercessor and no repentance. It underscores the urgency of repentance before death. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this was a core message: the Quraysh were warned that their forefathers who died in idolatry faced a similar doom. The verse seamlessly connects the historical narrative with the eternal reality, reminding listeners that the day of judgment is not a myth but the ultimate context in which all human choices are measured.
75 | But whoever comes to Him as a believer, having done good, they will have the highest ranks: | In beautiful contrast, the verse now describes the reward of the righteous. The condition is two-fold: coming to Allah as a believer (mu’minan) and having done righteous deeds (‘amila ṣāliḥan). Faith and deeds are intertwined; both are necessary for salvation. The reward is described as “the highest ranks” (fa-ulā’ika lahumu ad-darajātu al-‘ulā). The word “darajāt” means steps, levels, or stations of elevation. Paradise is not a flat, uniform experience; it has degrees of honour, nearness to Allah, and bliss, corresponding to the levels of faith and good deeds one achieved in this life. The magicians, by their instant but profound faith and their courageous stand, would be among those attaining lofty ranks. The “highest ranks” are for the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous. The phrase promises an exalted status far beyond what Pharaoh could offer. In Pharaoh’s court, ranks were determined by proximity to the tyrant and skill in magic; in the divine system, ranks are determined by imān and righteous action. This redefinition of honour was revolutionary for a society built on class and oppression. The magicians understood this and willingly traded the highest worldly rank for the lowest place among the believers, yet they were promised the highest spiritual rank. The verse encapsulates the Quranic principle that true honour belongs to the God-conscious. The Prophet Muhammad often encouraged his followers with the promise of lofty stations in Paradise, a motivation that made the hardships of Mecca bearable. The verse stands as a summary of the two paths, immediately interpreting the magicians’ choice as a sublime success.
76 | the Gardens of Eternity, under which rivers flow, where they will stay forever. That is the reward of those who purify themselves. | The highest ranks are now described as “the Gardens of Eternity” (jannātu ‘adn). The term ‘adn implies permanent residence, stability, and endless duration. The image of gardens with rivers flowing beneath them is one of the Quran’s most vivid depictions of Paradise, symbolizing perpetual comfort, beauty, and abundance. The phrase “where they will stay forever” emphasizes the eternal nature of this bliss; no fear of eviction or death. Then the verse identifies the recipients as “those who purify themselves” (man tazakkā). This purification (tazkiyah) encompasses both spiritual cleansing from shirk and sins and the development of virtuous character. It includes repentance, like that of the magicians who purified themselves from their past sorcery, and the continuous effort to remain obedient to Allah. The magicians, in their moment of prostration and confession, had purified themselves in the most dramatic way, and they were rewarded with this eternal promise. The verse teaches that true success is not the fleeting victory of a contest but the eternal reward of a purified soul. The Prophet Muhammad was sent to purify his followers, and the Quran itself is a means of purification. The contrast with the previous two verses is complete: the evildoer faces a Hell from which there is no release, while the purified believer enjoys an everlasting Garden. This passage, inserted at the climax of the confrontation, elevates the event from a historical episode to a timeless lesson. It assures all believers that any loss suffered in this world for the sake of faith will be compensated with unimaginable, everlasting good. The magicians’ “trade” with Pharaoh—life for Paradise—was the most profitable transaction possible.
77 | And We surely inspired Moses, ˹saying,˺ “Leave with My servants ˹at night˺ and strike a dry passage for them across the sea. Have no fear of being overtaken, nor be concerned ˹of drowning˺.” | The narrative shifts to the next stage of the story: the Exodus. After years of remaining in Egypt and witnessing the plagues, the final command came. Allah says, “We surely inspired Moses,” indicating that this was a direct divine instruction. The command was to “Leave with My servants at night.” The timing at night was strategic, allowing the Israelites to depart under cover of darkness and gain a head start before Pharaoh’s forces realized. The phrase “My servants” is a term of endearment and honour for the oppressed Children of Israel. Then came the miraculous instruction: “strike a dry passage for them across the sea.” Moses was to strike the sea with his staff, and Allah would create a dry path. The word “ṭarīqan” means a path, and “yabasan” means dry, ensuring that they could walk without sinking or fear. Allah also provided double reassurance: “Have no fear of being overtaken” — Pharaoh’s army would not catch them, and “nor be concerned of drowning” — the sea would not swallow them. This addressed the two obvious fears: the enemy from behind and the water ahead. The command and reassurance mirror the earlier one when Moses was sent to Pharaoh, confirming that Allah’s protection and guidance remain constant. For the Prophet Muhammad, the Hijrah (migration) from Mecca to Medina was a similar moment: a night departure, the fear of pursuit, and the miraculous protection of Allah, including the spider’s web and the cave. The verse encapsulates the pattern of divine deliverance: when the believers have done their part, Allah opens a way where there seems to be none. The stage is set for one of the most spectacular miracles in human history.
78 | Then Pharaoh pursued them with his soldiers—but how overwhelming were the waters that submerged them! | The verse describes the pursuit and its devastating outcome. When Pharaoh learned that the Israelites had fled, he mobilized his army and chased them. The phrase “fa-atba‘ahum fir‘awnu bi-junūdihi” indicates he followed them with his soldiers, determined to annihilate them. The timing was such that Moses and his people had crossed the dry passage, and Pharaoh’s army entered the sea bed behind them. Then the divine command came, and the waters returned to their normal state. The exclamation “but how overwhelming were the waters that submerged them” translates the Arabic “fa-ghashiyahum mina al-yammi mā ghashiyahum.” The wording is deliberately vague and majestic; it does not give a mundane description but evokes the sheer terror and totality of the drowning. The “yamm” is the deep sea, and what covered them was something only Allah fully knows — a deluge of water that closed over them, leaving no escape. This event was the final seal on Pharaoh’s disbelief and the salvation of the Children of Israel. It demonstrates that the same sea that was a path of salvation for the believers became a grave for the disbelievers. Water, a source of life, became an instrument of destruction by Allah’s command. The verse echoes the earlier assurance: Moses had no need to fear being overtaken or drowning. The destruction of Pharaoh and his army was total; not a single soldier survived. This event is a perpetual sign of Allah’s justice and power. The Prophet Muhammad would later recall this and the fate of other destroyed nations to warn the Quraysh. The verse is brief yet extremely powerful, leaving the reader to contemplate the suddenness with which Allah’s punishment can come when it is finally decreed.
79 | And ˹so˺ Pharaoh led his people astray, and did not guide ˹them rightly˺. | This verse pronounces the final verdict on Pharaoh’s leadership. “Pharaoh led his people astray” (wa-aḍalla fir‘awnu qawmahu) — he misled them into disbelief, tyranny, and ultimately to their destruction in this world and the next. As the supreme ruler and the one who claimed divinity, he bore the primary responsibility for the spiritual and moral state of his nation. Instead of guiding them to the truth when Moses came with clear signs, he used propaganda, threats, and arrogance to keep them in falsehood. The phrase “and did not guide them rightly” emphasizes that he failed in the most fundamental duty of a leader. He did not just passively let them stray; he actively led them astray. This is a warning to all who hold positions of influence: they will be accountable not only for their own souls but for the misguidance they cause in others. The magicians, by contrast, were guided after recognizing the truth, but the masses who blindly followed Pharaoh perished with him. The verse also serves to absolve the followers of complete innocence; they were led, but they also chose to follow. In the Quranic worldview, leadership and followership are moral choices. The Prophet Muhammad was commanded to be a guide, and he warned that every shepherd is responsible for his flock. The verse concludes the Egyptian episode with a somber reflection: the mighty civilization that seemed invincible was brought low by the very leader they idolized. The contrast with Moses, who guided his people to safety and the worship of Allah, is stark and instructive.
80 | O Children of Israel! We saved you from your enemy, and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭûr, and sent down to you manna and quails, | The address shifts directly to the Children of Israel, the generation that witnessed the Exodus. Allah reminds them of three immense favours. First, “We saved you from your enemy,” referring to the drowning of Pharaoh and the liberation from slavery. This salvation was a physical, political, and spiritual deliverance. Second, “and made an appointment with you on the right side of Mount Ṭûr.” This refers to the appointment where Allah would speak to Moses and give the Torah, with seventy elders accompanying him. The “right side” of the mount, according to commentators, indicates the blessed, righteous direction or the specific location on the mountain. This appointment was a tremendous honour, a direct communion with the Divine that no other nation had received at that time. Third, “and sent down to you manna and quails.” In the barren desert of Sinai, Allah provided miraculous sustenance: manna, a sweet substance that would appear like dew, and quails, birds that would come in abundance for them to eat. This was provision without toil, a sign of His care. The verse begins with “O Children of Israel” as a direct call to remember these favours and to live up to the covenant that would follow. It is both a reminder of gratitude and a rebuke for their later complaints and deviations. The favours are listed to show that Allah’s grace upon them was comprehensive—physical rescue, spiritual guidance, and material provision. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, recounting this story was a warning to the Jews of Medina who rejected his message despite the clear signs in their own history. The verse sets the stage for the subsequent events at Mount Ṭûr, where the Torah was given and the incident of the golden calf occurred.
81 | ˹saying,˺ “Eat from the good things We have provided for you, but do not transgress in them, or My wrath will befall you. And whoever My wrath befalls is certainly doomed.” | After reminding the Children of Israel of the miraculous manna and quails, Allah now gives them a direct command about those provisions. The phrase “Eat from the good things We have provided for you” is an invitation to enjoy the lawful, pure sustenance that descends as a mercy. The “good things” (ṭayyibāt) are not only physically nourishing but also divinely blessed. However, this permission comes with a stern warning: “but do not transgress in them.” Transgression here can mean several things: greed, hoarding beyond immediate need, ingratitude, complaining about the monotony of the food as they later did, or using the provision in disobedience to Allah. Some commentators mention that they were commanded to take only what they needed for each day and not store it for the next, yet some violated this, leading to spoilage. The deeper transgression is spiritual: receiving Allah’s gifts but then turning away from His worship and commands. The consequence is severe: “or My wrath will befall you.” Divine wrath (ghaḍab) signifies a state of intense displeasure that results in punishment. The verse then emphatically declares, “And whoever My wrath befalls is certainly doomed.” The Arabic “fa-qad hawā” implies a complete fall into destruction, both in this world and the next. The doomed person loses spiritual elevation and plunges into ruin. This warning was proven true when some among the Israelites transgressed and faced earthly punishments, but the ultimate doom is the Hereafter. The verse teaches that blessings come with responsibility; ingratitude and disobedience transform a gift into a curse. For the Prophet Muhammad’s community, it is a reminder to enjoy the lawful provisions of Allah with thankfulness and within the boundaries set by Him, avoiding excess and waste. The wrath of Allah is not a light matter; it is the worst loss a soul can suffer. The warning is balanced by the next verse, which opens the door of mercy and repentance, demonstrating that Allah’s justice is always paired with His forgiveness for those who turn back.
82 | But I am truly Most Forgiving to whoever repents, believes, and does good, then persists on ˹true˺ guidance.” | Immediately after the severe warning, Allah declares His immense forgiveness, emphasizing it with “truly” (innī). This verse lays out a clear path for redemption. The first condition is “whoever repents” (tāba) — genuine turning back from sin, with remorse and a resolve not to return. The second is “believes” (āmana), meaning faith in Allah and the pillars of belief. Repentance without faith is incomplete. The third is “does good” (‘amila ṣāliḥan), because true faith manifests in righteous deeds. Finally, “then persists on true guidance” (thumma ihtadā) emphasizes steadfastness. It is not enough to have a moment of repentance; one must continue upon the path of guidance until death. The word “ihtadā” implies actively seeking and adhering to the straight way. This fourfold condition encapsulates the comprehensive nature of salvation. The verse is a direct response to the possibility that some of the Israelites might transgress with the manna and quails, or later with the golden calf; it assures that no matter how grave the sin, the door of forgiveness is open for those who meet these conditions. The story of the calf worshippers would soon follow, and indeed, those among them who repented were forgiven. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this verse served as a powerful message to the Meccans who had committed idolatry and oppression: if they repented, believed, did good, and remained steadfast, they would find Allah Most Forgiving. It also comforted the believers who might fear past sins. The structure of the verse is profound: it begins with Allah’s attribute of forgiveness, then lists human actions. This shows that divine mercy is the initiator, but it is accessed through sincere human effort. The phrase “Most Forgiving” (la-ghaffār) is in an intensive form, indicating that Allah forgives repeatedly and abundantly. It is a timeless guarantee of hope for every sinner.
83 | ˹Allah asked,˺ “Why have you come with such haste ahead of your people, O Moses?” | The scene shifts to Mount Ṭûr, where Moses had been summoned for an appointment of forty nights. In his intense longing to be near his Lord, Moses left his people at the foot of the mountain and ascended ahead of the seventy elders who were also to accompany him. Allah’s question, “Why have you come with such haste ahead of your people, O Moses?” is not a reprimand but a gentle, knowing inquiry. The question draws attention to Moses’s extraordinary eagerness and love for his Lord. The phrase “such haste” (a‘jalaka) implies that his desire overpowered his usual measured approach. It is as if Allah wanted Moses to articulate the passion that drove him, setting the stage for the answer that would reveal the beauty of his yearning. This also subtly prepares Moses for the news that in his absence, his people had fallen into the worship of the golden calf. The question itself is a form of intimate conversation, demonstrating that Allah engages with His prophets as close confidants. Some scholars note that the question might contain a mild lesson: a leader should not be so absorbed in personal spiritual ecstasy that he leaves his followers unprepared. Yet, the primary tone remains one of affectionate curiosity. For the Prophet Muhammad, this reminded him of his own deep longing for revelation and prayer, while also teaching the balance between personal devotion and communal responsibility. The verse captures that Allah is fully aware of His servant’s innermost motivations, and He sometimes asks questions not to seek information but to allow the servant to express himself, to hear his own voice, and to build a deeper relationship.
84 | He replied, “They are close on my tracks. And I have hastened to You, my Lord, so You will be pleased.” | Moses’s answer is full of respectful love and concern. He begins by stating, “They are close on my tracks,” meaning his people and the elders are just behind him, approaching the mountain. This reassures Allah, as if to say, “I did not abandon them; they are right behind me.” It shows Moses’s awareness of his leadership responsibility; even in his haste, he ensured they were following. Then he reveals the true motivation: “And I have hastened to You, my Lord, so You will be pleased.” The Arabic “ḥastu ilayka” conveys a sense of hurried, eager movement towards the beloved. The goal was not merely to fulfill a command but to attain Allah’s pleasure (riḍā). This statement reveals the heart of a true lover of Allah; the highest aspiration is not personal reward but the smile of the Lord. Moses could have waited for the elders, but his yearning pulled him forward, and he trusted that his hastening would be accepted as a mark of his devotion. The phrase “my Lord” (rabbi) adds a tone of intimacy. The reply sets an example for every believer: the best reason to race towards good deeds and worship is the hope of pleasing Allah. The Prophet Muhammad would pray, “I ask You for Your pleasure and for Paradise.” The verse also indirectly addresses the upcoming test; Moses’s hastening for Allah’s pleasure is contrasted with his people’s hastening towards idol worship in his absence. The sincerity of Moses’s answer is so beautiful that Allah does not criticize him but instead reveals the test that has occurred. This teaches that Allah appreciates the pure intentions of His servants, even when the outward consequences seem difficult. Moses’s desire to please his Lord becomes a model for the believers’ spiritual ambition.

The response is not a direct answer to the question but a disclosure of a painful event. “We have indeed tested your people in your absence” — the word “fatannā” means We tried or tested them. The test was the golden calf, a trial to see whether they would remain steadfast in monotheism without their prophet present. The absence of Moses was part of the divine plan to expose the weakness of some hearts. Tests often come during the absence of spiritual leadership, revealing who truly worships the unseen God and who is attached only to a visible guide. Then Allah identifies the instigator: “the Sâmiri has led them astray.” The Sâmiri (often understood as a man from the tribe of Sāmir, or a notable figure among them) was the one who took the people’s gold and fashioned the calf. His name indicates his origin, and he became the primary culprit. The use of “aḍallahum” means he caused them to go astray, though they themselves were responsible for following him. Allah informed Moses of this before Moses descended, so that he would understand the gravity of the situation and be prepared. The verse teaches that tests are from Allah, but the actual misguidance is carried out by human agents who choose evil. It also shows that no matter how powerful a prophet is, his followers can falter if they rely on him rather than on Allah. For the Prophet Muhammad, the warning was clear: after his death, the community would face trials, and only those truly connected to Allah would remain steadfast. The mention of the Sâmiri also introduces the upcoming interrogation and punishment, showing that the agents of misguidance will be held accountable.

His reaction shows that true prophetic jealousy is for the honour of Allah, not personal ego. He immediately confronted them with a series of rhetorical questions that cut to the heart. First, “Had your Lord not made you a good promise?” — referring to the promise of the Torah, guidance, and reward. Breaking faith with Allah after such a promise is an act of profound ingratitude. Second, “Has my absence been too long for you?” — a forty-day absence is not a justification for idolatry, and the question exposes their impatience and weak faith. Third, “Or have you wished for wrath from your Lord to befall you, so you broke your promise to me?” — this is a sharp rebuke, suggesting that their actions were so severe it was as if they were actively inviting divine punishment. The promise they broke was the covenant to worship Allah alone, which they had affirmed before Moses left. Moses’s triple question moves from reminding them of Allah’s goodness, to exposing the flimsiness of their excuse, to condemning their recklessness. The anger was not uncontrolled rage but a righteous indignation that sought to awaken their conscience. He then threw down the Tablets of the Torah, according to other verses, not out of disrespect but from the overwhelming shock. The lesson for the Prophet Muhammad was to remain patient with the stubbornness of his own community, for even Moses, who spoke to Allah directly, faced such defiance. The verse shows that spiritual leadership involves immense emotional pain when followers stray, but the leader must continue to call them back to the truth.

Some reports say they had borrowed it, and when they left, it became a heavy burden of trust and temptation. The phrase “ḥummilnā awzāran” can also imply they were laden with the weight of sin or the heavy load of that jewelry. They then said, “then we threw it into the fire, and so did the Sâmiri.” Their narrative was that the Sâmiri told them to cast the gold into a fire to melt it, and he would do the same. The implication is that the Sâmiri orchestrated the whole affair, and they were merely following his lead. This argument is an attempt to shift blame. While the Sâmiri was the instigator, the people willingly gave their gold and participated in the worship of the resulting calf. Their excuse, therefore, holds no real weight. It resembles the excuses of those who follow false leaders and later claim they were merely obeying orders. The Quran exposes the psychology of weak-willed followers: they recognize the gravity of their sin but try to minimize their own agency. For the Prophet Muhammad, this served as a warning about the excuses that would be made by those who blindly follow misguiding leaders. The verse also highlights the danger of wealth and ornaments when they become objects of temptation; the very gold that was a sign of Allah’s deliverance became the material for an idol when misused.
88 | Then he moulded for them an idol of a calf that made a lowing sound. The Sâmiri “moulded for them an idol of a calf,” meaning he crafted a lifeless statue from the melted gold. Yet, this statue produced “a lowing sound,” which made it seem alive. According to many commentators, the sound was caused by a clever mechanical design—perhaps hollow passages that caught the wind—or by some trickery, but it was sufficient to captivate the simple-minded. The calf was not a living animal; it was an inanimate object manipulated to produce an effect. The people, entranced, declared, “This is your god and the god of Moses.” They attributed divinity to a crafted object, and shockingly, they claimed that this calf was the same deity that Moses worshipped. Then they added, “but Moses forgot where it was,” meaning they alleged that Moses had gone to the mountain to seek a god, but had forgotten that the true god was right here among them in the form of the calf. This is a profound depth of misguidance: not only did they commit shirk, but they also slandered their prophet by suggesting he was forgetful or misguided. The incident shows how quickly the human mind can rationalize idolatry, even constructing a theology around a hand-made idol. It mirrors the psychology of many polytheistic traditions that craft statues and then claim they represent the divine. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a parallel to the Quraysh who worshipped idols and claimed they were intermediaries to Allah. The verse also illustrates the Sâmiri’s cunning: he used a sound effect to give the idol a semblance of life, exploiting the people’s awe. It is a timeless warning against any charismatic figure who uses spectacle to divert people from the unseen God.
89 | Did they not see that it did not respond to them, nor could it protect or benefit them? | The Quran immediately demolishes the idol’s pretension with simple, rational arguments. The rhetorical question “Did they not see” appeals to their basic powers of observation and reason. The calf, despite its lowing sound, “did not respond to them.” When they prayed to it, asked for guidance, or called upon it, it remained silent. It could not speak a word of comfort or command; it was utterly unresponsive. Furthermore, “nor could it protect or benefit them.” It could not ward off any harm—if someone attacked them, the calf could do nothing. It could not bring any benefit—no rain, no provision, no healing. In summary, it was completely impotent. These two criteria—response and power to harm or benefit—are the minimum requirements for any entity that is worshipped. Even the pagan Arabs admitted that their idols could not create or provide; they only claimed they were intercessors. The verse teaches a fundamental principle of tawḥīd: that which is worshipped must hear and have power, otherwise worship is irrational self-deception. The argument is so clear that even a child can understand it. The fact that the Israelites, who had witnessed the plagues of Egypt and the parting of the sea, could fall for such a powerless object shows that misguidance is not a problem of intelligence but of heedlessness and desire. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a powerful tool against the idolatry of Mecca. The logic is timeless: any false god—whether a statue, a human leader, money, or an ideology—will ultimately prove unresponsive and powerless in the face of the true needs of the soul. The verse thus trains the believers to always apply this test of reason to any object of devotion.
90 | Aaron had already warned them beforehand, “O my people! You are only being tested by this, for indeed your ˹one true˺ Lord is the Most Compassionate. So follow me and obey my orders.” | The Quran now reveals that Aaron was not silent; he had actively opposed the idolatry. His warning began with the affectionate address “O my people,” showing his love and concern. He correctly identified the calf as a test: “You are only being tested by this.” The calf was a trial from Allah to distinguish the sincere from the weak. Tests come in various forms, and a seemingly miraculous object can be a means of trial rather than guidance. Aaron then redirected their focus to the true God: “for indeed your one true Lord is the Most Compassionate.” He used the name “Ar-Raḥmān,” the Most Compassionate, to remind them of Allah’s mercy that should inspire love and trust, not fear of abandonment. He emphasized that this Most Compassionate Lord is the only one deserving worship. Finally, he commanded, “So follow me and obey my orders.” As the appointed deputy of Moses, Aaron had the authority to lead them in Moses’s absence, and he urged them to obey him in rejecting the calf. His warning was clear, theologically sound, and delivered with gentleness. However, the people rejected him. This shows that even the most eloquent and correct advice can be ignored by those whose hearts are bent on desire. Aaron found himself in a nearly impossible situation: if he fought them violently, it would cause division and bloodshed; if he remained passive, they would continue in sin. He chose to maintain unity while persistently warning them. His stance was later vindicated by Allah. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a lesson in prophetic patience: sometimes a leader must endure seeing his people err without being able to forcefully stop them, trusting that Allah’s wisdom will unfold. The verse also highlights the proper response to any trial: recognizing it as a test and holding fast to the Lord of mercy.
91 | They replied, “We will not cease to worship it until Moses returns to us.” | The people’s response to Aaron’s warning was stubborn and conditional. They declared they would not stop worshipping the calf “until Moses returns to us.” This answer reveals several layers of their corruption. First, they implicitly admitted that the calf was not inherently worthy of worship; they tied their devotion to Moses’s absence, suggesting that they saw the calf as a temporary focus or a substitute during the leader’s absence. Yet, this was a catastrophic error: true worship cannot be directed to a substitute, no matter the circumstances. Second, they placed a condition on their obedience to Aaron, the legitimate deputy. By saying “until Moses returns,” they refused to acknowledge Aaron’s authority and made Moses an idol of sorts—their faith was attached to his person rather than to Allah. They treated Moses’s presence as the criterion for truth, which is a form of shirk in reliance. Third, their statement implied that if Moses approved the calf upon his return, they would be justified; if not, they would then reconsider. This is the mentality of those who follow falsehood and delay repentance, waiting for a convenient moment or a clearer sign, not realizing that death may come first. The verse exposes the danger of condition-based faith: one must respond to truth immediately, regardless of who delivers it or who is absent. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a warning that some of his followers might waver after his departure, clinging to personalities rather than the Quran and Sunnah. The response of the Israelites also shows the power of groupthink; they collectively reinforced each other’s defiance. The lesson for believers is to obey the truth when it comes, without delay or arbitrary conditions.

This rebuke reflects Moses’s passionate zeal for monotheism and his high expectations of his brother and partner in prophethood. It also highlights the immense pressure on prophets: they feel personally responsible for every soul that strays. The scolding is not a sign of a broken brotherhood but of the intensity of the prophetic mission, where even the smallest perceived shortfall is addressed directly. However, Moses’s understanding was incomplete because he did not yet know the full extent of Aaron’s efforts or the near-violent situation Aaron had faced. The verse sets up Aaron’s heartfelt defense, which will clarify the wisdom behind his actions. For the Prophet Muhammad, who also had close companions and sometimes needed to correct them, this episode demonstrates that even the noblest of people can be questioned, and that fraternal correction must be met with honest explanation.

For the Prophet Muhammad, who faced similar strategic decisions—whether to confront the Quraysh militarily or bear with patience—this dialogue illustrated the value of listening to the explanation of those who are on the ground. So Aaron’s inaction was actually a deep act of responsibility and loyalty to Moses’s ultimate command: to maintain the people’s solidarity. The explanation reveals profound prophetic wisdom: sometimes patience in the face of widespread sin is necessary to prevent greater fragmentation. The Prophet Muhammad would later demonstrate similar restraint at Ḥudaybiyyah, making concessions to preserve peace. The verse is a lesson in resolving conflicts: listen to the other side’s reasoning with an open heart, because what appears to be disobedience may be a higher form of obedience.

The question gives the Sâmiri a chance to speak, reflecting the divine practice of allowing even the worst criminals to present their case before punishment. It also exposes the Sâmiri’s reasoning, which will reveal the depth of his corruption. The Sâmiri’s answer, recorded in the next verse, shows that his act was not a mistake but a deliberate choice driven by a twisted interpretation of a spiritual experience. The questioning demonstrates that leaders must investigate the causes of a community’s deviation and address the root, not just the symptoms. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a model for dealing with the hypocrites and instigators in Medina, who were questioned and exposed. The verse also teaches that every soul is answerable for its actions, and no one who leads others astray will escape divine or prophetic scrutiny.

The Sâmiri’s defense, though honest, is not remorseful; it is an explanation of his inner temptation. Thus, the Quran continuously juxtaposes the two ultimate ends, urging a choice before the Day when the burden becomes fixed forever.
102 | ˹Beware of˺ the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and We will gather the wicked on that Day blue-faced ˹from horror and thirst˺. | The surah now paints a vivid scene from the Day of Judgment. The phrase “the Day the Trumpet will be blown” refers to the ṣūr, the horn blown by the angel Isrāfīl. The blowing of the Trumpet is a momentous event that signals the end of the world and the commencement of the resurrection. The Quran mentions it in several places as a terrifying blast that will stun all creation. Here, the focus is on the gathering of the “wicked” (al-mujrimīn) — those who have committed crimes against their own souls through disbelief and persistent sin. The description “blue-faced” translates “zurqan,” which literally means blue or livid. This is a colour of extreme fear, suffocation, and thirst. On that Day, the disbelievers will be resurrected in a state of profound terror, their faces pale and bluish from the horror they are witnessing and the deprivation of water. Some scholars say the blueness is the color of extreme thirst, and indeed the wicked will be driven to the place of gathering in a state of severe dehydration, their eyes turning blue from the intensity. This physical manifestation reflects their inner spiritual state: just as their hearts were dark and devoid of the light of faith, their faces lose the color of life and appear ghastly. The verse serves as a powerful warning, especially to the Meccan pagans who denied the resurrection. The image is designed to shake the listener from heedlessness. The Prophet Muhammad often described the horrors of that Day to instill taqwā. The contrast with the believers is stark: elsewhere the Quran describes the faces of the believers on that Day as radiant, laughing, and rejoicing. The gathering of the wicked “blue-faced” is a scene of humiliation and dread. It also connects with the earlier story: just as Pharaoh and his army were drowned and faced a humiliating end, the deniers of the Quran will be resurrected in a state of disgrace. The verse is a call to reflect on the reality that life’s temporary pleasures will culminate in that terrifying assembly unless one repents and believes.
103 | They will whisper among themselves, “You stayed no more than ten days ˹on the earth˺.” | In the midst of that terrifying gathering, the wicked will converse in hushed tones, comparing their earthly lives. The word “yatakhāfatūna” means they will whisper, mutter, or speak in low voices to one another, indicating their fear and the overwhelming solemnity of the occasion. Their conversation reveals a shocking realization: the worldly life they were so attached to, and for which they rejected the truth, now seems impossibly short. They estimate that they “stayed no more than ten days.” This is their perception upon resurrection—the entire span of their earthly existence, which might have been decades, now feels like a brief period of ten days. The number “ten” here is not meant literally but conveys a sense of shortness. Some say they might be referring to the period between death and resurrection, or the entire worldly life. In either case, the duration seems trivial compared to the eternity they now face. This psychological experience is described in other verses as well, such as “On the Day they see it, it will be as if they had not remained except for an evening or its morning.” The discrepancy between their previous valuation of life and the stark reality produces deep regret. Their whispering also indicates a lack of certainty; they are unsure and debate among themselves, but the truth is undeniable. The Prophet Muhammad would emphasize the fleeting nature of this world, saying, “What is the world compared to the Hereafter but like what one of you gets when he dips his finger into the sea; let him see with what it returns.” The verse exposes the deception of time: a long life seems short when faced with eternal consequences. It calls the listener to prioritize the everlasting over the temporary, a central theme in the surah that contrasts the pleasures of this world with the enduring reward of Allah.
104 | We know best what they will say—the most reasonable of them will say, “You stayed no more than a day.” | Allah, in His absolute knowledge, confirms that He is fully aware of all their whisperings. The phrase “We know best what they will say” asserts divine omniscience over the unseen future, including the exact words and the variations in their estimates. Even among the wicked, there will be degrees of perception. The “most reasonable of them” (amthaluhum ṭarīqatan) refers to those with the soundest judgment or the most balanced mind among the disbelievers. They will conclude that the worldly life or the interval of death lasted “no more than a day.” This further shrinking of the perceived time—from ten days to a single day—shows that the more rationally aware they are, the more they recognize the insignificance of the worldly duration. A day is the shortest unit of significant time; they realize that all their years of rebellion were worth less than a single day. This also underscores that even the best estimates of the disbelievers are still miles away from the truth; they can only grasp a fraction of the reality that the world was truly a fleeting illusion. The Quran often uses the dialogue of the people on the Day of Judgment to teach lessons. Here, their own admission becomes a proof against them. The verse also comforts the Prophet: Allah knows exactly what the deniers say and think, and their disbelief does not harm Him. It is a subtle warning that no matter how much one rationalizes or debates the truth, on that Day, all false justifications will evaporate, and even the most reasonable among them will concede the brevity of their test. This should motivate believers to invest their short time in what pleases Allah, knowing that soon they will look back and see their life as a single day, and they will wish it had been filled with righteousness.
105 | And ˹if˺ they ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about the mountains, ˹then˺ say, “My Lord will wipe them out completely, | The surah turns to a specific question that the Meccans might pose, or it simply addresses a common object of human awe: the mountains. The polytheists often marveled at the mountains’ stability and might consider them permanent fixtures. The verse instructs the Prophet on how to respond if they inquire about the fate of these massive formations on the Day of Judgment. The answer is clear: “My Lord will wipe them out completely.” The Arabic “yansifuhā rabbī nasfan” uses the verb “nasafa,” which means to scatter, pulverize, and reduce to dust. The mountains, which seem indestructible, will be utterly demolished by the command of Allah. This is a recurring Quranic image: the mountains will be like carded wool, crushed to powder, and the earth will be leveled. The purpose is to illustrate that nothing, no matter how solid and immense, can withstand the power of Allah when He wills the end of the current order. For the Prophet, this answer serves multiple functions. It refutes any lingering belief that the mountains have any divine permanence; they are mere creatures. It also reinforces the theme of the Hour’s suddenness and totality. The phrase “My Lord” emphasizes that the Prophet speaks with knowledge received from his Lord, not from himself. This answer also connects with the earlier description of the earth in verse 53: it is Allah Who laid out the earth, and He can easily unmake its elevations. The mountains, often objects of idolatry or symbols of pride, will be reduced to nothingness. This teaches humility: the mightiest physical structures are ephemeral before Allah, so how can human power or wealth boast? The verse prepares for the next description of the earth’s state after the mountains are gone, setting the scene for the assembly of humanity on a perfectly flat plain.
106 | leaving the earth level and bare, | After the mountains are obliterated, the earth will be left in a state described as “level and bare.” The Arabic “ṣafṣafan” means a smooth, flat plain, while “lā tarā fīhā ‘iwajan wa lā amtan” is elaborated in the next verse, but here “level” indicates a perfectly even surface, free from any rises or dips. The word “bare” is not explicitly in this verse but is implied by the context of a leveled plain with no vegetation or structures. The earth will become a vast, empty arena for the gathering of all humanity. There will be no hills, no valleys, no buildings, no trees—nothing to hide behind, no familiar landmarks. This physical transformation symbolizes the complete stripping away of the worldly life. All the distinctions and barriers that humans used for shelter, status, or separation will be removed. It is a scene of absolute exposure before Allah. The leveling of the earth also reflects divine justice: on that Day, all people will stand on an equal footing, with no one able to claim superiority due to ancestry, wealth, or geography. The grand mountains that people admired are reduced to dust, leaving a clean slate for the ultimate judgment. The Prophet Muhammad described the Day of Resurrection as the gathering of all creatures on a white, flat ground like a polished disc. The levelness of the earth ensures that the call of the summoner can be heard by all and that no one can escape the sight of Allah. The image is both awe-inspiring and terrifying; it reminds believers that the structures of this world, no matter how permanent they seem, are destined for annihilation, and only the face of the Lord will remain. Thus, one’s attachment should be to the Eternal, not to the transient landscapes of this life.
107 | with neither depressions nor elevations to be seen.” | The description of the earth’s transformation is completed with this phrase. “Neither depressions nor elevations” translates “lā tarā fīhā ‘iwajan wa lā amtan.” The word “‘iwaj” refers to any crookedness, curve, or unevenness, while “amt” means a small hill or bump. Some commentators explain that “amt” includes even the slightest rise or dip, like an anthill. The earth will be so perfectly flat that the eye will detect no variation whatsoever. This absolute flatness has profound implications. In the worldly life, valleys and mountains served as places of refuge, concealment, and strategic advantage. On that Day, there will be no place to hide, no terrain to aid escape. The disbelievers will be completely exposed. The verse also highlights the flawless execution of Allah’s command; He Who created the intricate topography of the earth will reduce it to a uniform plane with a single will. This flat plain is the stage for the universal gathering, where the entire human race from Adam to the last person will stand. The absence of any elevation or depression also symbolizes the end of all worldly distinctions: no king’s palace, no fortress, no cave. All are equally bare. The scene evokes the plain of Arafat on a grand scale, but stripped of all features. The Meccans, who prided themselves on their mountain-ringed valley, are warned that those very mountains will vanish. The verse encourages detachment from the world’s physical anchors and a focus on the inner state of the heart. In a perfectly flat world, nothing external can distract from the judgment; each soul will be entirely concerned with its own record. The imagery reinforces the surah’s central theme: the fleeting nature of the world and the enduring reality of the Hereafter.
108 | On that Day all will follow the caller ˹for assembly˺, ˹and˺ none will dare to deviate. All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate. Only whispers will be heard. | The scene of the gathering unfolds with absolute divine authority. “All will follow the caller” refers to the summoner, generally understood as the angel Isrāfīl or another appointed angel, who will call humanity to the place of assembly. The phrase “none will dare to deviate” indicates that no one will have the power or audacity to turn aside, resist, or go in a different direction. The Arabic “lā ‘iwaja lahu” means there will be no crookedness, no ability to deflect from following. The compulsion is not of violence but of overwhelming reality; the truth is so manifest that resistance is impossible. Then the verse describes the atmosphere: “All voices will be hushed before the Most Compassionate.” The mighty attribute “Ar-Raḥmān” appears here, the Most Compassionate, whose mercy is highlighted even in the context of judgment. The hushed voices (khasha‘at al-aṣwāt) denote a state of deep reverence, fear, and submission. The chatter, arguments, and loud protests of the worldly life will cease; only a profound silence will reign. “Only whispers will be heard” translates “illā hamsan,” meaning a faint, subdued sound, perhaps the shuffling of feet or the whispering of hearts. This immense silence illustrates the majesty and awe of the divine presence. It reflects the inner dread and the recognition that the only speech permitted is that which Allah allows. The contrast with the noisy, arrogant gatherings of the disbelievers in this world is sharp. The Prophet Muhammad would frequently emphasize the seriousness of that Day, and this verse draws a vivid picture that stills the heart. The mention of “the Most Compassionate” in a scene of judgment also carries a promise: even in His wrath, Allah’s mercy is the overarching reality, and those who deserve it will find it. The overall effect is a scene of total surrender, where every soul is focused on its outcome.
109 | On that Day no intercession will be of any benefit, except by those granted permission by the Most Compassionate and whose words are agreeable to Him. | The topic of intercession (shafā‘ah) is addressed. The Meccan pagans believed that their idols and ancestors would intercede for them. The verse shatters that hope. “No intercession will be of any benefit” means that no one can intercede on behalf of another purely on their own. All intercession belongs to Allah, and it operates only under two strict conditions: permission from the Most Compassionate, and the intercessor’s words being agreeable to Him. The phrase “by those granted permission” indicates that even the greatest prophets and angels must first receive divine leave before speaking. The Prophet Muhammad’s grand intercession on the Day of Judgment is a prime example: he will prostrate and praise Allah with words taught to him, and then Allah will grant him permission to intercede. The phrase “whose words are agreeable to Him” means that the intercessor’s speech must be pleasing to Allah, which implies that they can only intercede for those with whom Allah is already pleased, or in accordance with His will. This completely nullifies any pagan notion of unregulated intercession. The verse also connects with the earlier story: Pharaoh claimed no such intercession could save him, and indeed, his magicians’ newfound faith did not permit them to intercede for him. The condition “by the Most Compassionate” emphasizes that even this permission flows from Allah’s mercy. The intercession will only benefit those who had faith, however weak. The idolaters, who worshipped other beings precisely to intercede for them, will find their hopes dashed. The verse offers a balanced doctrine: while intercession exists and is a source of hope for sinful believers, it is entirely under divine control and does not compromise tawḥīd. It also serves as a warning: do not rely on imagined mediators; rather, seek the pleasure of the One Who grants permission. The silence of the previous verse is broken only by the voices that Allah permits, and those voices will speak only what is true and just.
110 | He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them, but they cannot encompass Him in ˹their˺ knowledge. | This verse declares the encompassing knowledge of Allah and the total limitation of human knowledge regarding Him. “He fully knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them” means Allah knows their future and their past, all that is before them of the Hereafter and all that they left behind in the world. Some commentators say “what is ahead of them” refers to the affairs of the Hereafter, and “what is behind them” refers to the worldly life they have passed. Others say it is about what is before the angels and creation. In any case, it signifies absolute, all-encompassing knowledge. No detail, no hidden intention, no future event escapes His awareness. The verse then states, “but they cannot encompass Him in their knowledge.” This is a fundamental principle of Islamic theology: Allah is beyond complete human comprehension. The finite mind cannot fully grasp the infinite. The word “yuḥīṭūna” implies encircling, encompassing, or fully comprehending. No matter how much knowledge humans attain, they can never fully know Allah’s essence, His attributes, or the full extent of His plans. This serves as a powerful rebuke to those who demand to understand the “how” of divine actions or who reject the unseen because it does not fit their logic. It also puts human knowledge in its humble place. The verse connects with the previous discussion: only those who are granted permission can intercede, and Allah’s knowledge is so vast that He knows who truly deserves mercy. The phrase “they cannot encompass Him” also offers comfort to the Prophet: the disbelievers’ arguments and mockery are rooted in ignorance; they cannot grasp the reality of the One they deny. The verse encourages believers to have intellectual humility and to trust the One Whose knowledge is unlimited. The balance between Allah’s complete knowledge of us and our limited knowledge of Him creates a proper relationship of awe and reliance.
111 | And all faces will be humbled before the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining. And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss. | On that Day, every human face will show abasement and submission before Allah. The phrase “all faces will be humbled” translates “‘anatil-wujūhu,” meaning they will be lowered, submissive, and full of humility. There will be no trace of the arrogance that characterized the disbelievers in the world. This humbling is before “the Ever-Living, All-Sustaining” (al-Ḥayy al-Qayyūm). These two names are among Allah’s greatest attributes: the Ever-Living who never dies, and the Self-Subsisting who sustains all existence without any need. The contrast is striking: mortal faces, once proud, are now humbled before the only One Who truly possesses life and power. The verse then singles out a group: “And those burdened with wrongdoing will be in loss.” The “wrongdoing” (ẓulm) here primarily refers to shirk and disbelief, as well as major sins. The word “khaba” means to fail, to be in manifest loss. This loss is absolute; they lose themselves, their families, their chances of Paradise, and any hope of escape. The punishment is not just physical but an existential failure. The humbling of faces is universal—even believers will experience awe—but for the wrongdoers, it is accompanied by loss, while for the believers, it will be followed by honor and safety, as the next verse will clarify. The verse serves as a severe warning, especially to the Meccan chiefs who strutted with pride. It teaches that the only true dignity is in submitting to the Ever-Living before death, so that on the Day of Judgment, one’s face is not humbled in punishment but radiant with joy. The mention of al-Qayyūm also reassures that while humans fail, Allah’s sustaining power never wavers, and He will establish justice.
112 | But whoever does good and is a believer will have no fear of being wronged or denied ˹their reward˺. | In beautiful contrast, the verse presents the state of the righteous. The condition is twofold: “does good” (righteous deeds) and “is a believer” (mu’min). Faith and action are inseparable. Such a person will have “no fear of being wronged or denied their reward.” The Arabic “ẓulman wa lā haḍman” covers two types of injustice: “ẓulm” is any form of oppression or unfair treatment, and “haḍm” means deprivation or reduction of due reward. So the believer is guaranteed that on that Day, they will not be treated unjustly in any way, nor will their good deeds be diminished in the slightest. This assurance is a source of immense comfort. Every human effort, every hidden tear, every sincere act will be accounted for and rewarded fully by the Most Just. The verse also implies that no intercession or favoritism will wrong the believer; their rights are secure. The use of “mu’minan” (believer) as a condition emphasizes that good deeds only benefit when rooted in imān. The earlier verse spoke of the burdened wrongdoers being in loss; here, the believers are in complete safety. This binary outcome is the core message of the surah and the Quran. The Prophet Muhammad would often recite such verses to encourage his followers who faced persecution, reminding them that their suffering would not be in vain. The reassurance extends to modern believers: any injustice suffered in this world will be redressed, and no act of goodness will be lost. The phrase “will have no fear” also addresses the emotional state; while the disbelievers are in terror, the believer will be calm and secure under the shade of the Throne. This is the culmination of the earlier promise in verse 75–76 regarding the highest ranks, offering a glimpse of the peace that awaits the purified. The verse thus ends the Judgment scene on a note of hope and justice.
113 | And so We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran and varied the warnings in it, so perhaps they will shun evil or it may cause them to be mindful. | After the vivid descriptions of the Hereafter, the surah returns to the Quran itself and its purpose. The phrase “And so We have sent it down as an Arabic Quran” emphasizes that the revelation is in the clear Arabic language, making it understandable to its first audience, the Quraysh. This is both a mercy and a proof: the message is not in a foreign tongue they could claim not to understand. It is also an honour for the Arabic language, chosen to carry the final revelation. The verse then mentions that Allah has “varied the warnings in it” (ṣarrafnā fīhi min al-wa‘īd). This means that the Quran presents warnings and threats in diverse ways—through stories, parables, arguments, and direct statements—to penetrate different types of hearts. The stylistic variety is a means to break through heedlessness and intellectual resistance. The purpose is given: “so perhaps they will shun evil or it may cause them to be mindful.” The verb “yattaqūna” implies attaining taqwā, a state of God-consciousness that leads to avoiding sin. The alternative “or it may cause them to be mindful” translates “yuḥdithu lahum dhikran,” meaning the Quran might create in them a remembrance, an awakening, a new consciousness of the truth. The use of “perhaps” (la‘alla) on Allah’s part expresses the intended effect, not uncertainty. It is as if to say, “We have done all this so that they might respond.” The verse encapsulates the Quran’s method: repetition and variation of warnings are not redundancies but pedagogical tools. It also implicitly answers the objection that the Quran contains repeated stories; the variation is deliberate to engender taqwā. For the Prophet, this verse explains the wisdom behind the different styles of the surah, which moved from the story of Moses to the Day of Judgment, all as diversified reminders. It calls upon the listener to engage with the Quran actively, allowing its variations to soften the heart and produce mindfulness.
114 | Exalted is Allah, the True King! Do not rush to recite ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran ˹O Prophet˺ before it is ˹properly˺ conveyed to you, and pray, “My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.” | This verse begins with glorification: “Exalted is Allah, the True King!” (fa-ta‘āla Allāhu al-Maliku al-Ḥaqq). It declares Allah’s transcendence above all false claims and His true, absolute sovereignty. All earthly kings are transient, but Allah is the King whose rule is eternal and real. This praise sets the tone for the crucial instruction that follows. The Prophet is then given a direct command: “Do not rush to recite the Quran before it is properly conveyed to you.” According to authentic reports, when the angel Gabriel brought revelation, the Prophet would hurriedly move his tongue, trying to memorize it on the spot, fearing he might forget it. He exerted himself greatly out of eagerness. Allah gently instructed him not to hasten; the responsibility of its collection and recitation rested with Allah. The verse parallels Surah Al-Qiyāmah, “Do not move your tongue with it to hasten it. Indeed, upon Us is its collection and its recitation.” Here, the command is coupled with a beautiful prayer: “and pray, ‘My Lord! Increase me in knowledge.’” This is a profound teaching: instead of anxiously rushing to grasp knowledge, one should turn to Allah and ask for an increase in knowledge. Knowledge (ilm) here encompasses the Quran, its meanings, and all beneficial knowledge. The prayer “Rabbi zidnī ‘ilman” is one of the most beloved supplications for students and scholars. It embodies humility, acknowledging that true knowledge is a gift from Allah and that no matter how much one learns, there is always more to seek. The verse connects with the opening of the surah where the Quran was not sent to cause distress; the Prophet is now shown how to receive it with tranquility, trust, and continuous supplication. For believers, it is a direct instruction: seek knowledge with patience and always ask Allah for increase. It frames the entire pursuit of learning as an act of worship, grounded in reliance on Allah rather than on one’s own intellectual power. The verse beautifully integrates praise, instruction, and supplication into a single transformative moment.
115 | And indeed, We once made a covenant with Adam, but he forgot, and ˹so˺ We did not find determination in him. | With this verse, the surah transitions to the story of Adam, the father of humanity, linking the trials of the earlier prophets to the very origin of human existence. Allah says, “We once made a covenant with Adam.” This covenant was the command not to eat from the forbidden tree. It was a direct instruction, a test of obedience. “But he forgot” — the Arabic “fa-nasiya” indicates that Adam did not deliberately disobey out of rebellion, but his memory lapsed, and his resolve weakened. This forgetfulness is part of human nature. Then the verse states, “and We did not find determination in him.” The word “‘azm” means firm resolve, steadfastness, strength of will. Adam, at that moment, lacked the determination to withstand the temptation. He was not obstinate like Iblīs; he simply faltered. This statement is not a condemnation but an explanation of his human vulnerability. It shows that even a prophet, created directly by Allah’s hands, was susceptible to forgetting and weakness. This serves to teach all of humanity that we are prone to error, and that perfection belongs to Allah alone. The verse also implicitly contrasts Adam’s forgetfulness with the stubborn refusal of Iblīs, who willfully disobeyed out of arrogance. Adam’s slip was followed by repentance, while Iblīs’s was followed by defiance. The mention of the covenant and its breach sets the stage for the subsequent narrative of the prostration of the angels, the refusal of Iblīs, and the temptation. For the Prophet Muhammad, this story was a reminder that his own community would make mistakes, but the door of repentance is open. The lack of determination in Adam is not a permanent flaw; it is a temporary state that was overcome through divine grace, as later verses will describe. The lesson is that human strength is always dependent on Allah, and forgetfulness can only be remedied by His remembrance.
116 | And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who refused ˹arrogantly˺. | The story unfolds further. Allah commanded the angels to prostrate before Adam, a prostration of honour and greeting, not of worship. This command was a test of obedience for the heavenly beings. The angels, being pure and obedient servants of Allah, all prostrated immediately, acknowledging the superiority of Adam’s knowledge which Allah had taught him. However, Iblīs—who was among the jinn but in the company of the angels due to his devotion—refused. The verse emphasizes “who refused arrogantly” (abā wa-stakbara). His refusal was not out of ignorance but pure arrogance. He considered himself superior, being made of fire, while Adam was made of clay. This arrogance is the first sin in creation, the root of all evil. The prostration of the angels symbolizes the proper human response to divine command, while Iblīs’s refusal symbolizes rebellion. The command itself established Adam as a khalīfah on earth, and the prostration was an acknowledgment of the honour Allah bestowed upon humanity. By recounting this event, the Quran reminds the Children of Adam that their enemy, Satan, declared his enmity from that very moment. The verse also contrasts with the previous mention of Adam’s forgetfulness: Iblīs did not forget; he actively and consciously rejected. This makes his sin far graver. For the Prophet Muhammad, this narrative served as a warning against the arrogance of the Quraysh, who refused to prostrate to the truth brought by a mere human from among them, just as Iblīs refused to honour Adam. The story illustrates that true nobility lies in obedience to Allah, not in one’s origin or substance. The prostration of the angels also foreshadows the eventual submission of all creation to the final message, while Iblīs’s refusal is the prototype of all disbelief.
117 | So We cautioned, “O Adam! This is surely an enemy to you and to your wife. So do not let him drive you both out of Paradise, for you ˹O Adam˺ would then suffer ˹hardship˺.” | After Iblīs’s refusal and his banishment, Allah turned to Adam and his wife Ḥawwā’ (Eve) with a clear warning. The address “O Adam!” is personal and full of care. The warning identifies Iblīs as “an enemy to you and to your wife,” establishing the permanent enmity between Satan and humanity. This enmity is not merely personal but extends to all descendants. The specific caution is “do not let him drive you both out of Paradise,” indicating that Satan’s goal is to strip them of their blessed abode. The consequence of being driven out is then explained: “for you would then suffer hardship.” The Arabic “fa-tashqā” means you will toil and experience misery. This hardship includes the difficulties of worldly life—earning a living, hunger, thirst, fatigue—as opposed to the ease of Paradise where all needs were met without effort. The warning is not a threat but a merciful revelation of the consequences. It shows Allah’s desire to protect Adam and his wife from suffering. The verse also explains why the world is a place of trial and toil: it is the result of that first transgression, yet it is also the arena for redemption. The phrase “to you and to your wife” emphasizes that both are equally targeted; Satan does not discriminate. The warning also implies that the couple had the capacity to resist, which is why they were responsible. This verse serves as a timeless caution for all believers: Satan is an active, cunning enemy who seeks to drive humanity out of the peace of Allah’s obedience into the hardship of sin and heedlessness. The mention of “your wife” also honors the marital bond and the shared responsibility of protecting each other from the enemy’s plots. The following verses describe the abundant provisions of Paradise that they would lose if they succumbed.
118 | Here it is guaranteed that you will never go hungry or unclothed, | Allah details the blessings of Paradise that Adam and his wife enjoyed, contrasting them with the hardships of the worldly life they would face if expelled. The first guarantee is that they will never go hungry or unclothed. Hunger is the most basic physical pain, and nudity is exposure and loss of dignity. In Paradise, food was abundant and available without toil, and clothing was provided as a covering of honour. The phrasing “it is guaranteed” (inna laka) emphasizes the certainty of this provision. This provision is a manifestation of Allah’s care. The absence of hunger implies complete satisfaction, and the absence of nakedness implies permanent covering. These two are among the most fundamental human needs, and their fulfillment in Paradise without effort contrasts sharply with the worldly condition where humans must toil for food and clothing. The verse implicitly points to the mercy of Allah, Who did not create humans to suffer but placed them in a place of total provision. The devil’s promise to Adam was of a kingdom that never fades, but what they already had was perfect sustenance and dignity. The warning is thus deepened: why risk guaranteed comfort for a deceptive promise? The mention of hunger and clothing also has symbolic dimensions: spiritual hunger is the absence of guidance, and spiritual nakedness is the exposure of sins. In Paradise, they were free from both. The lesson for believers is to appreciate the provisions Allah has given and to be wary of Satan’s whispers that make them dissatisfied with pure and lawful blessings, pushing them toward forbidden desires that bring hardship.
119 | nor will you ˹ever˺ suffer from thirst or ˹the sun’s˺ heat.” | The description of Paradise’s provisions continues with two more guarantees: freedom from thirst and from the sun’s heat. Thirst (ẓamā’) and heat (ḍuḥā) are significant afflictions, especially in the desert context of Arabia. Paradise provided perpetual hydration and a perfectly temperate climate, free from the scorching sun that causes fatigue and discomfort. The shade of Paradise is one of its most mentioned blessings in the Quran. The four guarantees—no hunger, no nakedness, no thirst, no heat—cover the most essential physical needs and comforts. Together, they paint a picture of complete well-being. This is the state that Adam and his wife would lose by heeding Satan. The contrast with the worldly life is stark: on earth, humans constantly struggle against thirst, sunstroke, and the elements. This serves as a reminder that the worldly life is inherently a place of toil, while the true home of ease is the Hereafter. The mention of these specific blessings also alludes to the story of Moses and the manna and quails, where the Israelites were provided food and shade in the desert; when they transgressed, they lost some of that ease. For believers, this reinforces the value of contentment with Allah’s lawful provisions and the danger of aspiring to what Satan falsely promises. The heat and thirst also point to the suffering of the disbelievers on the Day of Judgment, who will experience a blazing sun and extreme thirst, as described earlier. Thus, the choice is between the eternal shade of Paradise and the scorching punishment of Hell. The warning to Adam is a universal warning: do not exchange the guaranteed, pure blessings of obedience for the illusory temptations of sin.
120 | But Satan whispered to him, saying, “O Adam! Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that does not fade away?” | Despite the explicit warning, Satan approached Adam and his wife with a cunning temptation. The verb “waswasa” (whispered) is the origin of the term waswasah, indicating subtle, repeated, and insidious suggestions. Satan did not openly declare his enmity; he presented himself as a sincere advisor. His address “O Adam!” mimics the caring tone of the divine warning, which makes the whisper more seductive. He then made an attractive offer: “Shall I show you the Tree of Immortality and a kingdom that does not fade away?” The tree he referred to was the forbidden tree. He claimed that eating from it would grant them eternal life, making them angels or immortals, and that it would give them a kingdom that never decays or ends. This was a direct appeal to the human desire for permanence and power. The offer was a lie, because Allah had already guaranteed them a blissful life in Paradise. Satan’s strategy was to make them feel that what they had was insufficient and that they could attain something greater by disobeying. He planted the idea that Allah was hiding the tree from them out of a desire to prevent them from becoming superior. The phrase “kingdom that does not fade away” resonates with the human aversion to decline and loss. Adam and his wife, being free from sin, were susceptible to this deception because they could not imagine a lie. The verse illustrates the archetypal method of temptation: presenting evil as a means to a noble end (immortality, lasting kingdom), exploiting natural human aspirations, and casting doubt on the divine command. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a clear warning against the whisperings that would come to his community from Satan and his allies, who beautify sinful actions and make them seem like paths to success. The verse sets the stage for the fall, which is immediately followed by repentance and divine mercy, demonstrating the full cycle of human error and divine forgiveness.
121 | So they both ate from the tree and then their nakedness was exposed to them, prompting them to cover themselves with leaves from Paradise. So Adam disobeyed his Lord, and ˹so˺ lost his way. | The temptation reached its tragic conclusion. Despite the explicit warning and the clear identification of Satan as an enemy, Adam and his wife both ate from the forbidden tree. The phrase “So they both ate from the tree” confirms that both were deceived. The immediate consequence was not the promised immortality or kingdom, but a profound loss: “their nakedness was exposed to them.” Before this, they had been clothed in a state of innocence, but now the garments of Paradise were stripped away, and they became aware of their nakedness. This exposure is both physical and symbolic—it represents the loss of dignity and the cover of divine protection. Their instinctive reaction was to cover themselves, “prompting them to cover themselves with leaves from Paradise.” They frantically began plucking leaves and sticking them together to hide their shame. This is the origin of human modesty and the awareness of the body as something to be shielded. It also shows that even in their disobedience, they retained an innate sense of modesty. Then the verse states, “So Adam disobeyed his Lord, and so lost his way.” The Arabic “‘aṣā” (disobeyed) and “ghawā” (lost his way, became misguided) are used. This is not the disobedience of arrogant rebellion but a slip caused by forgetfulness and deception. Adam’s misguidance was temporary; he did not persist in it. The verse does not mention Eve separately, but Adam is addressed as the representative. Importantly, this event is not described as a “fall” carrying original sin upon all humanity, as in some other traditions. In Islam, it is a personal mistake, repented, forgiven, and without inherited stain. The covering with leaves teaches that human beings, when they sin, should immediately seek to correct their state and cover their faults, both physically and spiritually, through repentance. The verse closes the episode of the temptation, setting the stage for the divine mercy that immediately follows, demonstrating that human error does not sever the relationship with Allah when followed by sincere turning back.
122 | Then his Lord chose him ˹for His grace˺, accepted his repentance, and guided him ˹rightly˺. | Despite the disobedience, the verse opens with a powerful conjunction: “Then his Lord chose him.” The Arabic “thumma ijtabāhu rabbuhu” indicates that after the sin and the exposure, Allah turned to Adam with a special selection. The word “ijtabā” means to select, purify, and bring close. This choosing is an act of pure grace; it was not earned by Adam’s deed, because he had just erred. It demonstrates that Allah’s mercy can overtake a sin even before full repentance, preparing the heart to return. Then, “accepted his repentance” (fa-tāba ‘alayhi). Adam and his wife had learned words of supplication from Allah, as mentioned elsewhere: “Our Lord, we have wronged ourselves, and if You do not forgive us and have mercy upon us, we will surely be among the losers.” Allah taught them how to repent, and when they turned back, He accepted their repentance. The phrase emphasizes that Allah is the One who turns to His servant with forgiveness; human repentance is met with divine acceptance. Finally, “and guided him rightly” (wa-hadā). After forgiveness, Adam was given guidance to navigate the earthly life ahead. He was not left to wander in confusion; prophethood was granted to him. This sequence—sin, exposure, turning to Allah, forgiveness, and guidance—is the pattern for every human being. There is no sin too great for Allah’s mercy if the servant repents sincerely. The verse directly refutes any notion that Adam’s mistake permanently corrupted human nature. Instead, it shows that the default human relationship with Allah is one of mercy and restoration. For the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, this was a source of immense hope, and he said, “If you were not to sin, Allah would replace you with a people who would sin and then seek forgiveness so He could forgive them.” The verse beautifully rounds off the story of Adam in this surah by highlighting the triumph of divine mercy over human weakness, a central theme that ties together the stories of Moses, the magicians, and now the father of mankind.
123 | Allah said, “Descend, both of you, from here together ˹with Satan˺ as enemies to each other. Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray ˹in this life˺ nor suffer ˹in the next˺.” | After the repentance, the divine decree of descent to earth was issued. The command “Descend, both of you, from here” was addressed to Adam and Eve, or to Adam and Iblīs according to some interpretations, but the context here includes Satan as well—“together with Satan as enemies to each other.” The earthly life is established as an arena of enmity between humanity and Satan. This enmity is not a curse but a test; humans are warned that Satan is their sworn foe and will try to lead them astray, just as he did their parents. Then Allah pronounced the core promise that defines the human condition: “Then when guidance comes to you from Me, whoever follows My guidance will neither go astray in this life nor suffer in the next.” The phrase “guidance from Me” refers to the revelation sent through prophets, scriptures, and the innate human conscience. The promise is comprehensive: those who follow this guidance will be protected from “ghayy” (misguidance, spiritual error) in the world, and from “shaqā’” (wretchedness, misery) in the Hereafter. “Neither go astray nor suffer” is an absolute guarantee of a good life and an excellent eternal outcome. This is the foundational covenant for all humanity: guidance is provided, and following it leads to safety; rejecting it leads to loss, as the next verse will detail. The descent is not a punishment without remedy but the beginning of a journey in which every soul is equipped with the means to return to Paradise. The phrasing echoes the earlier story of Moses, where following guidance brought salvation and turning away brought destruction. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse reaffirmed that his message was the latest and most complete form of that “guidance from Me.” It offers a profound consolation to every human: if you hold fast to the divine guidance in a world of enmity and temptation, you will experience peace here and eternal bliss hereafter, free from the misery that Adam briefly encountered.
124 | But whoever turns away from My Reminder will certainly have a miserable life, then We will raise them up blind on the Day of Judgment.” | In contrast to the blessed promise, the verse warns of the consequences of rejection. “Whoever turns away from My Reminder” refers to those who deliberately ignore the Quran and the guidance of Allah. The word “dhikrī” (My Reminder) encompasses all divine revelation. The consequence is twofold: first, “will certainly have a miserable life.” The Arabic “ma‘īshatan ḍankan” means a life of hardship, constriction, anxiety, and spiritual poverty. This is not necessarily material poverty; many wealthy disbelievers live in inner turmoil, depression, and meaninglessness. The heart that turns away from Allah is constricted, no matter how much wealth surrounds it. This misery manifests as discontent, fear, and a sense of futility. Second, “then We will raise them up blind on the Day of Judgment.” The blindness here is literal and spiritual. They will be resurrected without sight, unable to see the path to safety or the face of the Prophet interceding, while the believers will have light. This blindness is the direct result of their willful blindness to the truth in this world. The verse aligns with the earlier description of the wicked being blue-faced and gathered in humiliation. The contrast between the “miserable life” and the “goodly life” promised to the righteous believer is a recurring Quranic theme. The Prophet Muhammad explained that the world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever; the believer’s true ease is in the Hereafter, but even in this world, faith provides a spiritual contentment that the disbeliever cannot find. The verse does not claim that every believer will be materially rich, but that the one who turns away from Allah will inevitably experience a constricted heart and a horrifying resurrection. The mention of “blind” also resonates with the story of the Sâmiri, who was made to cry “Do not touch me!”—a form of social blindness and isolation. This verse serves as a powerful warning, especially when placed after the story of Adam, who repented and was guided, showing that the way out of misery is always open through turning back to the Reminder.
125 | They will cry, “My Lord! Why have you raised me up blind, although I used to see?” | In the terror of the resurrection, the disbeliever, now blind, will plead with Allah. The cry “My Lord!” is poignant; they recognize His Lordship now, when it is too late. The question “Why have you raised me up blind, although I used to see?” expresses both bewilderment and a veiled protest. They had physical sight in the world, which they used to enjoy Allah’s blessings and witness His signs, but they refused to see the truth. Now, when sight is most desperately needed to navigate the horrors of judgment, they are deprived of it. The question implies that they think it unfair, as if they are being punished without reason. They do not yet fully grasp the connection between their worldly behavior and their current state. This verse captures the human tendency to demand justice without recognizing one’s own faults. The blindness is not arbitrary; it is a direct consequence of their own choices. The question also reveals the pain of the loss: they remember seeing, which makes the blindness more agonizing. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was asked about how people will be gathered on the Day of Judgment, and he explained that the disbelievers will be gathered blind, deaf, and dumb, while the believers will be with light. The verse sets the stage for the divine response in the next verse, which will provide the clear, logical connection between their worldly neglect of the revelations and their neglected state on that Day. The lesson is that the faculties we are given—sight, hearing, intellect—are tests, and their misuse will lead to their removal or transformation into sources of regret. Every act of ignoring a sign of Allah is, in reality, a step toward one’s own blindness on the Day when sight matters most. The plea “My Lord” also contains a flicker of hope, but for the unrepentant disbeliever, it is a cry that will receive the response of justice.
126 | Allah will respond, “It is so, just as Our revelations came to you and you neglected them, so Today you are neglected.” | The divine response is perfectly just and mirrors the crime. Allah says, “It is so” (kadhālika), affirming that the punishment fits the deed. The explanation follows: “just as Our revelations came to you and you neglected them.” The Arabic word “nasītahā” comes from the root for forgetting, but here it means willful neglect and abandonment. The revelations—the signs in creation, the prophets, the scriptures—came clearly, but the disbeliever chose to ignore them, to live as if they did not exist. They turned a blind eye to the truth. Therefore, the response is precise: “so Today you are neglected.” The passive form “tunsā” means you are left, forgotten, disregarded. Just as they treated Allah’s signs as unworthy of attention, they will be treated as unworthy of mercy. This is not an arbitrary punishment but a direct reflection of their own actions. The irony is profound: they who considered themselves important and self-sufficient will find themselves utterly disregarded. The verse teaches the principle of just recompense; Allah’s punishment is not a new, separate torment but the inevitable outcome of one’s own choices. It also clarifies that the blindness and neglect are not because Allah forgot them, but because they forgot His revelations. This echoes other Quranic statements: “They forgot Allah, so He forgot them.” The Prophet Muhammad would often emphasize that sins have consequences in this life and the next, and that neglecting the Quran leads to a neglected state. The verse offers a powerful psychological and spiritual insight: what you value and pay attention to shapes your ultimate reality. To neglect the Creator’s words is to court eternal obscurity. The conversation between the disbeliever and Allah reveals the perfect logic of divine justice, leaving no room for complaint.
127 | This is how We reward whoever transgresses and does not believe in the revelations of their Lord. And the punishment of the Hereafter is far more severe and more lasting. | After detailing the specific case, Allah generalizes the principle. “This is how We reward whoever transgresses and does not believe in the revelations of their Lord.” The punishment of a miserable life and a blind resurrection is the standard recompense for the combination of transgression (isrāf) and disbelief. Transgression refers to exceeding limits, indulging in sins, and wasting life in heedlessness, while disbelief is rejecting the divine signs. The two often go together; a heart that denies the truth will inevitably transgress moral bounds. The phrase “revelations of their Lord” emphasizes the personal lordship and mercy that they rejected. Then the verse adds a terrifying dimension: “And the punishment of the Hereafter is far more severe and more lasting.” The miserable life and blind resurrection are only the beginning; the ultimate and unimaginably worse punishment is the Fire. The severity refers to the intensity of the pain, and “more lasting” (abqā) emphasizes eternity. This answers any misconception that the hardship of a disbeliever’s worldly life, or the temporary terror of the gathering, is the full extent of their penalty. It is only a sample of what is to come. The phrase “more lasting” connects back to the magicians’ declaration to Pharaoh that Allah is “more lasting in punishment.” It ties the surah’s narratives together, showing a consistent divine law. For the believers, this verse reinforces gratitude for guidance and fear of straying. For the deniers, it is a warning that no temporary worldly pleasure is worth an everlasting punishment. The Prophet was commanded to convey this warning, not to force belief. The verse also highlights that divine justice operates on a universal scale; no exception is made for anyone who meets these criteria. It closes the section on the consequences of turning away from the Reminder, leading naturally into the next verse which reflects on the historical evidence of such punishment.
128 | Is it not yet clear to them how many peoples We destroyed before them, whose ruins they still pass by? Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment. | The surah now addresses the Meccan pagans directly, appealing to historical observation. The rhetorical question, “Is it not yet clear to them,” implies that the evidence has been repeatedly presented but they remain heedless. The phrase “how many peoples We destroyed before them” refers to the civilizations of the past—ʿĀd, Thamūd, the people of Lot, the Pharaoh and his army, and others—who were annihilated because of their rejection of the prophets. The Meccans traveled on their trade routes and passed by the ruins of these civilizations, such as the stone dwellings of Thamūd in the north and the traces of the people of Lot. The ruins were visible, undeniable testimonies to the destruction that came upon the unjust. The Arabic “ya‘mashūna fī masākinihim” means they traverse their dwelling places. The fact that they could walk through these ruins and still not take heed was a sign of their spiritual blindness. Then the verse concludes, “Surely in this are signs for people of sound judgment.” The word “nuhā” again appears, referring to intelligent minds that can restrain themselves from passion and see the deeper meaning. The lesson is clear: if past nations were destroyed for their denial, why should the present deniers feel immune? The Prophet Muhammad was sent as a mercy, but his people were repeating the same arrogance. This verse connects with the earlier story of Pharaoh, whose drowned army is one of the “peoples destroyed.” It also ties back to the opening themes of the surah, where the stories of the past are recounted as a reminder. The argument is rational: observe history, see the pattern, and use your intellect to avoid the same fate. The verse encourages believers to travel and reflect on the remnants of past civilizations, not as tourists but as contemplators of divine justice, thereby increasing their taqwā.
129 | Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord ˹O Prophet˺ and a term already set, their ˹instant˺ doom would have been inevitable. | This verse addresses a potential question: why are the Meccan deniers not immediately destroyed like the previous peoples? The answer lies in divine wisdom. “Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord and a term already set” refers to two things. The “prior decree” is the word of Allah that has preceded, which includes His decision to delay the punishment of this Ummah out of mercy, and specifically that the Prophet’s nation would not be annihilated with a general destruction like the earlier ones. The “term already set” (ajalun musamman) is the appointed time for the end of their individual lives or the collective punishment, which might be in the Hereafter or in a specific form. Because of this decree and appointed term, “their instant doom would have been inevitable” — otherwise, their denial was so severe that immediate punishment would have been justified. The verse serves multiple purposes. It comforts the Prophet, who might have yearned for a swift divine punishment against the mockers. It warns the disbelievers that they are only spared by Allah’s forbearance, not because they are right. It also establishes the principle that the Muslim Ummah, unlike some previous nations, would not face a single, overwhelming extermination; instead, its disbelievers might face individual deaths or defeat in battle, but the community as a whole would remain, and the ultimate punishment is deferred to the Hereafter. This is an aspect of the special mercy granted through the presence of the Prophet. The “prior decree” could also refer to the decree that punishment would only come after clear warning, and the Prophet was still among them. This verse is a direct parallel to other verses where Allah says He would not punish them while the Prophet was in their midst. The phrase “your Lord” adds a personal touch of reassurance: the Prophet’s Lord is in control, and His decisions are based on deep wisdom. The lesson for believers is to not be impatient for the punishment of the wicked, trusting that Allah’s timing is perfect and His decree is for the best.
130 | So be patient ˹O Prophet˺ with what they say. And glorify the praises of your Lord before sunrise and before sunset, and glorify Him in the hours of the night and at both ends of the day, so that you may be pleased ˹with the reward˺. | With the explanation that punishment is deferred, the Prophet is given a direct command: “So be patient with what they say.” The patience required is against the insults, mockery, and demands for signs. The Arabic “fa-ṣbir” is a command of steadfastness, which is the core prophetic virtue. Then, the remedy for bearing their speech is prescribed: continuous glorification of Allah. “And glorify the praises of your Lord” translates “wa-sabbiḥ bi-ḥamdi rabbika,” meaning to declare His transcendence while praising Him. The specified times are “before sunrise” (qabla ṭulū‘i ash-shams) and “before sunset” (qabla ghurūbihā), which refer to the Fajr and ‘Aṣr prayers respectively, or more broadly the times of morning and evening remembrance. “And glorify Him in the hours of the night” refers to the night prayers like Tahajjud, and “at both ends of the day” (wa-aṭrāfa an-nahār) could refer to the prayers at the edges of the day—Fajr, Maghrib, and perhaps Ẓuhr and ‘Aṣr together. The purpose is profound: “so that you may be pleased with the reward.” The Arabic “la‘allaka tarḍā” means that this regimen of worship and remembrance will lead to the satisfaction of the Prophet’s heart, knowing that Allah is pleased with him and that his reward in the Hereafter will be such that he is completely content. This is a direct response to the distress mentioned at the beginning of the surah: the Quran was not sent to cause distress, and these acts of glorification are the path to spiritual contentment. The Prophet’s contentment is tied to the glorification of his Lord, not to the acceptance of the people. This verse is a powerful prescription for anyone facing opposition and emotional pain: immerse yourself in the worship and praise of Allah, and you will find inner peace and the promise of a reward that will make you forget all hardship. The mention of specific times emphasizes regularity; the entire day is bracketed by divine remembrance, leaving no room for Satanic whisperings or worldly sorrow to dominate the heart.
131 | Do not let your eyes crave what We have allowed some of the disbelievers to enjoy; the ˹fleeting˺ splendour of this worldly life, which We test them with. But your Lord’s provision ˹in the Hereafter˺ is far better and more lasting. | The Prophet is further instructed to disregard the worldly attractions that the disbelievers enjoy. “Do not let your eyes crave” translates “lā tamuddanna ‘aynayka,” literally “do not stretch your eyes,” a vivid expression for longing and envy. The objects of craving are “what We have allowed some of the disbelievers to enjoy,” such as wealth, status, children, and luxuries. These are described as “the fleeting splendour of this worldly life” (zahra al-ḥayāti ad-dunyā). The word “zahrah” means bloom or flower, emphasizing its temporary, superficial beauty that quickly fades. Then the purpose is revealed: “which We test them with.” Their wealth is not a sign of Allah’s pleasure; it is a test to see if they are grateful or become more arrogant, and it will be a witness against them. This refutes the materialistic view that prosperity indicates divine favour. The verse then redirects the Prophet’s gaze: “But your Lord’s provision in the Hereafter is far better and more lasting.” The provision (rizq) of Paradise—peace, eternal youth, nearness to Allah—is incomparably superior in quality (khayr) and duration (abqā). This verse consoles the Prophet and all believers who might see the disbelievers flourishing while they themselves endure poverty or hardship. It realigns the heart from temporary glitter to eternal treasure. The command “do not let your eyes crave” is an essential discipline of spiritual focus. It echoes the lesson of Adam: Satan tempted him with a “kingdom that does not fade away,” but what was with Allah was better and lasting, while the tree was a test. The believer must internalize that whatever the disbelievers have is a fleeting test for them, and the believer’s true wealth is the contentment of faith and the promise of the Hereafter. The verse beautifully ties back to the magicians, who renounced Pharaoh’s rewards for the eternal provision of Allah. It is a call to elevate one’s aspirations beyond the material trinkets of this world.
132 | Bid your people to pray, and be diligent in ˹observing˺ it. We do not ask you to provide. It is We Who provide for you. And the ultimate outcome is ˹only˺ for ˹the people of˺ righteousness. | The Prophet is given a crucial instruction regarding his family and his community. “Bid your people to pray” (wa’mur ahlaka biṣ-ṣalāh) means command your family and your followers to establish the prayer. This emphasizes the central role of prayer in the life of faith. It is the first duty after belief, and the head of the household is responsible for ensuring its observance. The command is followed by “and be diligent in observing it” (waṣṭabir ‘alayhā), which means you yourself must be steadfast and patient in performing it. The Prophet is to lead by example. Then Allah reassures: “We do not ask you to provide.” Provision (rizq) is not the Prophet’s responsibility; he is not required to ensure his family’s sustenance or the economic well-being of his followers at the expense of his spiritual mission. This was a direct comfort, as the Prophet often faced poverty and the burden of his dependents. Allah says, “It is We Who provide for you.” The Lord of all creation takes direct responsibility for the provision of His servants, especially those engaged in His worship. The verse ends with a powerful principle: “And the ultimate outcome is only for the people of righteousness.” The Arabic “al-‘āqibatu lit-taqwā” means the good end in this world and the next belongs to those who have taqwā, who are mindful of Allah. This statement shifts the focus from material outcomes to spiritual ones. It echoes the earlier promise that those who believe and do good will have the highest ranks. The verse combines a command to worship with a promise of provision, liberating the believer from the anxiety of sustenance. It also corrects any misconception that the Prophet’s mission was about acquiring worldly power; his mission was to lead people to prayer and righteousness, and the ultimate victory—of the message and the believers—is tied to taqwā, not to wealth or numbers. This is a timeless lesson for anyone engaged in da‘wah: prioritize prayer and reliance on Allah, and leave the results and provisions to Him.
133 | They demand, “If only he could bring us a sign from his Lord!” Have they not ˹already˺ received a confirmation of what is in earlier Scriptures? | The Meccan pagans’ persistent demand for a tangible miracle is addressed. They said, “If only he could bring us a sign from his Lord!” They wanted something spectacular, like the staff of Moses or the she-camel of Ṣāliḥ, instead of the Quran. The verse dismisses this demand by pointing out an already existing and greater sign: the Quran itself. “Have they not already received a confirmation of what is in earlier Scriptures?” The Arabic “aw lam ta’tihim bayyinatu mā fī aṣ-ṣuḥufi al-ūlā” means that the Quran is a clear proof that confirms the truth contained in the ancient scriptures—the Torah, the Gospel, and others. The Quran narrates stories of previous prophets, aligns with core monotheistic teachings, and provides a clear, consistent message that matches what sincere scholars of those scriptures would recognize. Furthermore, the Quran’s own miraculous nature—its eloquence, its wisdom, its preservation—is a sufficient sign for those who reflect. The verse implies that demanding additional signs after the Quran is sheer obstinacy. The “confirmation” also points to the fact that the coming of Prophet Muhammad was foretold in earlier scriptures, so his presence and the Quran are themselves the fulfillment of those prophecies. This argument was especially relevant to the People of the Book who might recognize the truth, but the Quraysh also heard from travelers and scholars. The verse is a powerful logical rebuttal: you want a sign, but the sign you have—the Quran—is the ultimate sign, a timeless intellectual and spiritual miracle. It connects with the beginning of the surah, where the Quran is described as a revelation from the Lord of the heavens and the earth. The demand for physical miracles ignores the fact that those who witnessed such miracles in the past, like Pharaoh, still disbelieved. The lesson is that the greatest guidance is the Word of Allah, and those who reject it would not believe even if mountains were moved. The verse challenges the listener to engage with the Quran directly instead of making speculative demands.
134 | Had We destroyed them with a torment before this ˹Prophet came˺, they would have surely argued, “Our Lord! If only You had sent us a messenger, we would have followed Your revelations before being humiliated and put to shame.” | This verse presents a profound divine argument for sending the Prophet. Allah explains that if the Meccans had been annihilated by a divine punishment before the Prophet came, they would have protested on the Day of Judgment. They would have said, “Our Lord! If only You had sent us a messenger, we would have followed Your revelations before being humiliated and put to shame.” The words “before being humiliated and put to shame” refer to the disgrace of worldly punishment or the shame of the Hereafter. This imaginary argument shows that Allah, in His perfect justice, cuts off all excuses by sending messengers. No people are destroyed without a clear warning. The Quraysh could not claim they had no warner, because the Prophet Muhammad was in their midst, reciting the clear verses. The verse highlights the mercy in the advent of prophethood: it is a preventive grace that removes the pretext of ignorance. The same logic applied to previous nations; every community that was destroyed had received a messenger. For the Meccans to demand further signs after the messenger has come with the clear Quran is, therefore, an act of supreme ingratitude and exposes their insincerity. The verse also refutes the fatalistic objection that people might be predestined to disbelief; the sending of messengers proves that guidance is offered, and the choice is real. The phrase “before being humiliated” indicates that worldly punishment brings humiliation, and they would have pleaded for a chance. But since the chance is now given in the form of the Prophet, they have no argument left. This verse serves as a direct response to those who might wonder why the Meccans were not immediately punished; they are given respite and the messenger, so that on the Day of Judgment they will have no defense. The Prophet is comforted that his mission is an essential act of justice and mercy, filling a gap that would otherwise have left humanity with a legitimate complaint.
135 | Say ˹to them, O Prophet˺, “Each ˹of us˺ is waiting, so keep waiting! You will soon know who is on the Straight Path and is ˹rightly˺ guided.” | The surah concludes with a final, powerful challenge from the Prophet to his opponents. The command “Say” is directed to the Prophet, giving him words that embody a confident but patient stance. “Each of us is waiting” — the Prophet and the believers are waiting for Allah’s promise of victory and the ultimate outcome, while the disbelievers are waiting for the destruction of the Prophet and his message, or they are waiting to see what the future holds. The phrase “so keep waiting” (fa-tarabbaṣū) is a sharp, almost ominous instruction: wait on, for the truth will become manifest. Then the declaration: “You will soon know who is on the Straight Path and is rightly guided.” The “straight path” (aṣ-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm) is the path of Islam, the path of those who follow the guidance. The “rightly guided” (ihtadā) are those who have achieved the goal of guidance. This is a clear, confident assertion that the truth of the Prophet’s message will soon become undeniable. The waiting referred to could include the worldly outcome—such as the victory at Badr or the conquest of Mecca—or the final judgment in the Hereafter, or both. The tone is neither desperate nor confrontational; it is the calm certainty of a prophet who knows that his Lord’s word is supreme. The verse encapsulates the central conflict of the surah: truth versus falsehood, faith versus tyranny. It echoes the theme that ultimate victory belongs to the believers, as shown in the story of Moses and the magicians, and that the disbelievers’ waiting will end in disappointment. For the Prophet, this was a final reassurance: he did not need to force the outcome; he only needed to deliver the message and wait with patience and confidence. The surah, which began with the comforting words “Ṭâ-Hâ” and “We have not revealed the Quran to you to cause you distress,” ends on a note of ultimate triumph and clarity. The choice is laid before all: wait and see, for the truth will inevitably shine, and those on the straight path will be vindicated.
