1 | By those ˹angels˺ lined up in ranks, | The surah opens with a powerful oath in which Allah swears by certain aspects of His creation to draw attention to a fundamental truth. When the verse begins with “By those lined up in ranks,” the expression points specifically to the angels. The Arabic word used here for “lined up in ranks” is ṣâffât, a description of orderly rows, much like soldiers standing in perfect formation or worshippers arranged in prayer lines. The angels are presented as beings who stand in such disciplined rows before their Lord, ever ready to carry out His commands without hesitation or disorder. This image is not merely poetic; it carries a profound message about the nature of servitude and the absolute sovereignty of Allah. In the Meccan context, the pagans believed that angels were daughters of God and sometimes even worshipped them. By swearing an oath upon these very angels described as humble servants standing in ranks, the Quran immediately corrects that misconception. They are not divine offspring; they are created beings assigned their stations, displaying the highest form of obedience. The Prophet (peace be upon him) once told his companions that angels in the heavens line up in rows just as believers do in prayer, continuously glorifying Allah and waiting for His decree. This reminds us that the entire cosmos is structured upon submission to the One Creator. The oath also prepares the listener for the conclusion in the coming verses: that the God who commands such organized hosts must be One and Unique. When a person reflects on the perfect discipline of the angels, it becomes unreasonable to associate partners with the Lord who created and arranged them. Thus, even this opening phrase dismantles idolatry by presenting a glimpse of the unseen world where all beings willingly bow to Allah’s authority.
2 | and those who diligently drive ˹the clouds˺, | After mentioning the angels in stationary worshipful ranks, the oath moves to another group of angels: “those who diligently drive the clouds.” The word translated as “diligently drive” conveys a strong sense of forceful, determined pushing or herding. These angels are tasked with steering the clouds across the sky according to Allah’s precise command. Rain does not fall randomly; it is part of a meticulously managed system in which angels propel clouds to specific locations, sometimes bringing mercy and sometimes, by divine order, punishment. The polytheists of Mecca witnessed rain, yet they attributed natural phenomena to idols or impersonal forces. By swearing by the angels who drive the clouds, the Quran directs attention to a hidden reality behind what people see every day. The rain that revives barren land is not an autonomous process but a sign of the living God’s continuous involvement in His creation. This connects beautifully with the first oath: the same angels who stand in disciplined ranks in the heavens also actively execute Allah’s will in the physical world. Their tireless movement and diligence underscore that all natural occurrences are ultimately acts of worship performed by submissive creatures. The believer, therefore, should look at a rain-bearing cloud and perceive not a mechanical cycle but the obedient angels fulfilling their role, driving the cloud as a shepherd drives a flock. This awareness shifts one’s entire perspective, turning the sight of gathering clouds into a reminder of the unseen realm and a reason to thank the Lord who sends His servants to provide life-giving water. The oath links the hidden angelic realm with the tangible world, so that even a simple weather event becomes a visible link between the heavens and the earth, all of which declare the Oneness of their Maker.
3 | and those who recite the Reminder! | The third category in this opening oath consists of angels who “recite the Reminder.” The Reminder refers to the Quran itself or, more broadly, to the revelation and words of Allah that angels are honoured to recite. This shows that angels are not only beings of action, driving clouds and standing in ranks, but also beings of worship through recitation. They constantly engage with divine speech, glorifying and praising Allah without fatigue. Some scholars explain that the angels bring down the Reminder from the Preserved Tablet to the messengers, while others see them as reciting the scriptures in the heavens. Either way, this places the Quran, which was being revealed to the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) in Mecca, within a cosmic context. The very angels who form ranks and direct the clouds are the same ones intimately involved with the divine word. For the Meccan disbelievers who mocked the Quran as poetry or magic, this oath draws a stark contrast: the words they dismiss are so exalted that angels themselves recite them in the celestial realm. The phrase “recite” implies a continuous, melodious reading, indicating that the heavens are filled with the sound of revelation being proclaimed by pure beings. This naturally elevates the status of the Quran in the heart of the believer. It is not merely a book but the very Reminder that resonates through the highest assemblies of angels. By grouping the reciters of the Reminder with those who stand in ranks and drive the clouds, the oath unites the angelic functions of worship, administration, and transmission of knowledge. All of these duties harmoniously serve the ultimate purpose of declaring Allah’s Oneness. The listener is thus invited to recognize that the message brought by the Prophet is the same heavenly truth that the angels themselves honour.
4 | Surely your God is One! | After three solemn oaths describing the angels in their various functions, the central point of the surah is delivered: “Surely your God is One!” The oath serves as a buildup, and now the conclusion is stated with absolute clarity. The address “your God” speaks directly to the Prophet and his community, but it extends to all humanity. The word “One” does not merely mean numerically one; it means unique, singular in His essence, attributes, and actions, having no partner, equal, or rival. Everything the previous oaths portrayed—the perfect ranks of obedient angels, the precise driving of clouds, the heavenly recitation of the Reminder—all of it originates from a single divine will. Such an organized universe cannot have multiple conflicting authorities. This verse is a declaration of tawheed, the central doctrine of Islam, which the Meccan idolaters vehemently rejected. They acknowledged Allah as the Supreme Creator but associated other gods with Him, claiming these deities could intercede or bring them closer to Him. In response, the Quran establishes that the Lord of such magnificent angels and cosmic order is One alone, and He requires no partners. For believers, this short verse becomes a powerful affirmation. It reminds us that the angels in their breathtaking discipline, the clouds in their precise movement, and the revealed scriptures in their wisdom all point back to the same single source. When a person truly absorbs that “your God is One,” it transforms life: worship is directed solely to Him, hope and fear are attached to none other, and the entire existence is seen as a unified sign pointing to the One Creator. The use of “surely” intensifies the certainty, leaving no room for doubt after such grand witnesses have been presented.
5 | ˹He is˺ the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between, and the Lord of all points of sunrise. | After establishing that God is One, the surah expands on His lordship. The verse begins by identifying Him as “the Lord of the heavens and the earth and everything in between.” This phrase encompasses the entire cosmos, from the vast celestial spheres above to the ground beneath our feet and all that lies between them—the air, the clouds, the birds, the unseen creatures. Lordship means ownership, authority, nurturing, and governance. So the One God is not a distant, uninvolved creator; He actively sustains and rules over every particle of existence. Then the verse adds a specific description: “and the Lord of all points of sunrise.” The mention of “points of sunrise” in the plural is remarkable. The sun does not rise at the same exact spot each day from an earthly perspective; it shifts throughout the year, creating many different points of sunrise. Some commentators also understand it as referring to the many different places on earth from which the sun rises over various lands and peoples. By declaring Himself the Lord of all these rising points, Allah draws attention to His dominion over time, seasons, and geography. The pagan Arabs worshipped various local deities tied to specific regions, but here the Quran emphasizes that the same One Lord controls every sunrise everywhere. The daybreak that illuminates Mecca is under the same authority as the daybreak that shines on the farthest lands. This universal lordship refutes any notion that different gods manage different territories. It also reminds human beings of their complete dependence. We plan our days, but we cannot make the sun rise. That daily miracle is in the hands of the Lord of all points of sunrise. Thus, the verse builds a logical bridge from the angels’ worship described earlier to the visible creation, inviting reflection on the fact that the One who orders the heavens and the earth must be the sole object of worship.
6 | Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration | The surah now shifts attention to the visible cosmos as a sign of divine power and beauty. The verse states, “Indeed, We have adorned the lowest heaven with the stars for decoration.” The “lowest heaven” refers to the nearest celestial sphere, the sky that is directly observable to human eyes. The stars are described as an adornment, a dazzling decoration that fills the night with beauty. This is a profound shift in perspective: what people often take for granted as distant burning balls of gas is presented here as deliberate ornamentation placed by the Creator to beautify the sky. The purpose of this adornment is not merely aesthetic; it invites contemplation. When a person looks up at a star-filled sky, the natural response should be awe at the artistry and majesty of the One who arranged it. The use of “We” in the verse is the royal plural of majesty, not an indication of multiple gods. The verse counters the pagan practice of worshipping stars or considering them as having influence independent of Allah. By stating that the stars are a decoration of the lowest heaven, the Quran makes it clear that they are created objects, placed under Allah’s command, and subservient to His will. Moreover, this adornment is directly linked to the previous context of angels; the lowest heaven is the very realm where the angels descend with commands and where they are engaged in their duties. So the beauty above us is not empty; it is a domain populated by obedient servants. For the believer, the starry night becomes a reminder of Allah’s subtle artistry and a call to glorify Him. The concept of adornment suggests that Allah beautified creation as an act of generosity, providing humans with sights that bring tranquillity and inspire reflection, yet all this beauty is merely the lowest level of the heavenly realm.
7 | and ˹for˺ protection from every rebellious devil. | The stars do not only serve as decoration; the verse adds a second function: “and for protection from every rebellious devil.” This reveals a spiritual dimension to the cosmos that was hidden from the disbelievers. The “rebellious devils” are the shayateen from among the jinn who attempt to ascend to the heavens to eavesdrop on the conversations of the angels. Allah has stationed the stars—or meteoric flames produced from them—as missiles to repel these intruders. This protective function connects the visible night sky directly with the unseen battle between good and evil. For the Meccan pagans who often consulted soothsayers and believed that jinn had access to heavenly secrets, this verse dismantles that notion by showing that any attempt by devils to snoop is fiercely guarded against. The “protection” is not merely a wall but an active defense system driven by the same divine power that decorated the sky. It teaches that the heavens are secure; no unauthorized entity can penetrate the assembly of the angels. This has immediate implications for the Prophet’s message: the revelation he brings is pure and uncorrupted because no devil can intercept it. The words of the Quran are protected from above, and the Prophet is not a soothsayer receiving whispers from jinn, as his enemies accused him. Instead, he receives revelation through the trustworthy angel Gabriel. This verse also comforts the believer by showing that Allah’s control over the universe extends to safeguarding spiritual knowledge. The shooting stars we occasionally see are not random cosmic events but signs of devils being pelted and driven away. The same sky that looks tranquil and adorned is also a battlefield where Allah’s commands are enforced against the arrogant rebels who once refused to bow to Adam.
8 | They cannot listen to the highest assembly ˹of angels˺ for they are pelted from every side, | The description of cosmic protection continues with the statement that the devils “cannot listen to the highest assembly of angels.” The “highest assembly” (al-mala’ al-a‘la) refers to the noble gathering of angels in the heavens where divine decrees are discussed and commands are issued. The rebellious devils strive to overhear snippets of this celestial discourse to use that stolen information for misleading humans. But the verse declares that such eavesdropping is impossible because “they are pelted from every side.” The imagery is that of a fortress under complete siege; the devils are assailed from all directions by flaming meteors whenever they try to approach. This is a direct refutation of the belief held by some Arabs that soothsayers had informants among the jinn who brought them news of the unseen. The Quran clarifies that even if a devil manages to snatch a word, a piercing flame pursues it immediately. The “every side” phrase emphasizes total coverage; there is no blind spot in the heavens where a rebellious devil can hide. The lesson for humans is that the unseen is entirely under divine control. Whatever knowledge of future events or hidden matters reaches people through true revelation is only that which Allah permits the angels to convey to His prophets. This also reinforces the integrity of the Quranic message. The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) is not receiving confused, half-stolen information; he receives clear, guarded revelation delivered by Gabriel, who is part of that highest assembly. The verse thus subtly comforts the Prophet, whose opponents accused him of being a madman or a poet inspired by jinn, by showing that the real channel of heavenly knowledge is secure and the devils are utterly barred from it, pelted away in disgrace.
9 | ˹fiercely˺ driven away. And they will suffer an everlasting torment. | The devils are not only pelted and repelled but are also described as “fiercely driven away.” The repulsion is forceful and humiliating, indicating that they are expelled from the heavenly precincts with contempt. This immediate punishment in the sky is followed by a warning of something more severe: “And they will suffer an everlasting torment.” The verse links the present defensive measure with the future eschatological punishment. The rebellious devils are already experiencing the disgrace of being chased away from the light of divine assembly, but this is only a foretaste of the eternal suffering awaiting them in the Hereafter. This dual punishment demonstrates the consistency of Allah’s justice across both the unseen and the seen worlds. The devils, who were once honoured and then rebelled out of arrogance, now face continuous rejection and will ultimately be cast into Hell. For the human disbelievers who align themselves with the whisperings of these devils, there is a grave warning: if the devils themselves cannot escape the flames that pursue them now and the eternal fire later, how can those who follow them hope to be safe? The Meccan pagans who worshipped jinn or sought their aid are being alerted that their supposed allies are themselves hunted fugitives. The concept of “everlasting torment” also lays the groundwork for the later discussions in the surah about the fate of the wicked. It reminds the listener that this life’s hidden struggles are connected to final outcomes. The believer is reminded that the forces of evil, no matter how deceptive, are ultimately defeated and under constant divine assault. The sky, beautifully adorned, is simultaneously a place of relentless judgment against those who overstep their bounds, reflecting the perfect balance of mercy and justice in Allah’s creation.
10 | But whoever manages to stealthily eavesdrop is ˹instantly˺ pursued by a piercing flare. | After establishing that the devils are pelted from all sides, the verse adds a specific detail: “But whoever manages to stealthily eavesdrop is instantly pursued by a piercing flare.” This acknowledges that occasionally a devil may snatch a fragment of sound from the heavenly discourse. The verb “stealthily eavesdrop” suggests a sneaky, desperate attempt to grab something quickly. However, the response is immediate: a piercing flare, a shining meteor, chases and burns it. The word “piercing” indicates a flame that penetrates and overcomes, leaving no chance of escape. This precise imagery served a powerful purpose among the pagan Arabs, many of whom believed soothsayers received their information from jinn who overheard the angels. The Quran does not deny that a snatch of sound might occur, but it clarifies that such stolen information is partial, confused, and intercepted before the thief can benefit. The soothsayers then mix that single fragment with a hundred lies, making their predictions unreliable. The Prophet (peace be upon him), by contrast, receives the pure revelation through Gabriel without such interference. This effectively discredits the entire soothsaying profession. For the modern reader, this verse reinforces the reality that the unseen realm is not a chaotic spiritual free-for-all. It operates under strict laws and surveillance. The fact that a “piercing flare” pursues the eavesdropper demonstrates the speed and precision of divine enforcement. It also subtly answers those who question why bad things happen or why evil seems to have temporary success: even the devils, with all their cunning, can only snatch whispers before being burned. The forces of falsehood are always on the run, pursued by the truth. This image should fill the believer with confidence that whatever misinformation and deception circulate in the world, the ultimate source of truth remains guarded and uncorrupted by divine protection.
11 | So ask them ˹O Prophet˺, which is harder to create: them or other marvels of Our creation? Indeed, We created them from a sticky clay. | The discussion now turns from the cosmic scene to a direct challenge issued to the deniers of resurrection. Allah instructs the Prophet to ask them, “which is harder to create: them or other marvels of Our creation?” This rhetorical question targets the disbelievers who found the idea of being resurrected after death impossible. The logic is clear: if Allah created the vast heavens, the stars, the angels, the devils, and all the marvels mentioned earlier, is recreating human beings from their original substance really more difficult? The question uses their own reasoning against them. They accepted that Allah created the universe, yet they balked at the idea of a second creation. The phrase “other marvels of Our creation” could refer to the angels, the heavens, the earth, or even the enormous creatures and forces that Allah has brought into existence. Then the verse grounds this argument in human origin: “Indeed, We created them from a sticky clay.” The original creation of the human race was from a humble, sticky, mouldable clay. This origin is not a source of pride but a reminder of lowly beginnings. The one who fashioned a living, intelligent being from such simple material is certainly capable of bringing that being back to life. The mention of “sticky clay” also echoes the creation of Adam and reminds the Meccan idolaters that their own substance is from the earth they walk upon. This dual argument—comparing the resurrection to the more immense creation of the cosmos and pointing to the first creation from clay—leaves no intellectual escape. If someone acknowledges the greater act of creating the heavens and the earth, they cannot logically deny the lesser act of reconstituting bones and dust.
12 | In fact, you are astonished ˹by their denial˺, while they ridicule ˹you˺. | Having presented the logical argument about resurrection, the verse addresses the Prophet directly, but its message also flows to the believers. “In fact, you are astonished by their denial, while they ridicule you.” The Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) was amazed and perhaps bewildered that people could reject such clear reasoning. The evidence for Allah’s power was visible all around them, from the starry sky to the creation of human beings from clay, yet the disbelievers continued to mock the idea of life after death. The verse acknowledges the Prophet’s astonishment but contrasts it with the reaction of the deniers: they are not astonished; they simply ridicule. They do not engage intellectually; they turn to mockery. This dynamic is common throughout history: when truth is presented with compelling proofs, those who are determined to reject it often resort to belittlement and laughter rather than honest consideration. The verse consoles the Prophet by assuring him that his surprise at their stubbornness is natural, but their ridicule stems from arrogance, not from any weakness in the message. It also serves as a caution for believers: do not expect that every logical argument will be met with acceptance. Some hearts are sealed, and their response will be sarcasm. The Prophet is told, in effect, to recognize this pattern and not let their mockery distress him. The shift from the cosmic oaths and resurrection arguments to this intimate description of reactions makes the text deeply personal for the one delivering the message. It reinforces that the struggle between truth and falsehood involves emotional fortitude. The believers are reminded that they stand on the side of wonder at Allah’s signs, while the deniers stand on the side of scoffing.
13 | When they are reminded, they are never mindful. | This verse explains the deeper cause behind the ridicule. “When they are reminded, they are never mindful.” The reminder here refers to the Quranic verses, the teachings of the Prophet, and the signs in creation that remind people of Allah and the Hereafter. The disbelievers receive these reminders, yet they refuse to allow them to penetrate their hearts. The phrase “never mindful” indicates a persistent state of heedlessness. It is not that they fail to understand; they choose not to reflect and take heed. The word used in Arabic conveys the idea of actively paying attention and deriving spiritual benefit. They turn away from that. This is a severe critique of their attitude. Reminders are meant to awaken the innate human disposition that recognizes truth. Every human soul has a primordial covenant with its Creator, and a reminder should stir that memory. But when a person consistently blocks out the truth, they become desensitized. The verse thus diagnoses the spiritual disease of the disbelievers: they are unmindful by habit. The earlier mention of the Prophet’s astonishment is now explained; their denial is not based on intellectual lack but on willful negligence. For the believer, this verse is a warning to safeguard the heart against heedlessness. When we hear reminders, we should actively engage our minds and hearts, lest we fall into the same pattern of ignoring what could save us. The surah has already established immense signs—angels, stars, creation from clay—so to be unmindful of such reminders is to be truly lost.
14 | And whenever they see a sign, they make fun of it, | The verse moves from hearing reminders to seeing signs, completing the picture of their rejection. “And whenever they see a sign, they make fun of it.” A sign (ayah) can be a verse of the Quran, a miracle, or a natural phenomenon pointing to Allah. The Meccan idolaters witnessed the Prophet’s character, the Quran’s eloquence, and the splitting of the moon, among other signs. Their reaction was not contemplation but mockery. They turned these profound proofs into objects of jest. This behaviour is the defensive mechanism of pride. When faced with something that challenges their worldview, instead of submitting, they seek to belittle it to preserve their own ego and social standing. By making fun of the signs, they also pressure the Prophet and the early Muslim community, hoping to discourage them. The verse exposes the moral bankruptcy of such an attitude. It links back to the previous statement about being astonished versus ridiculing: the believers see in the signs a cause for awe; the disbelievers see a cause for laughter. This contrast continues throughout the surah, preparing for the scenes of the Day of Judgment when the mockers will be in utter regret. The practical lesson for believers is clear: never treat the signs of Allah with levity. When encountering a verse or a miracle, one should approach with humility and a readiness to learn. The verse also teaches that sometimes rejection is not due to lack of clarity but due to a rotten attitude that must be recognized and countered with patience and continued delivery of the message.
15 | saying, “This is nothing but pure magic. | Now the verse quotes their mockery directly: “saying, ‘This is nothing but pure magic.’” When the disbelievers saw the Quran’s effect on people and the signs the Prophet brought, they could not deny its power. So they resorted to labelling it “magic.” The addition of “pure” (or “clear”) intensifies the claim, as if to say it is obviously sorcery and nothing else. In their minds, this label served to discredit the Prophet and explain away the transformation they witnessed in his followers. Magic, in their cultural context, was understood to be a means of overwhelming and deceiving people through supernatural aid, often associated with jinn. By calling the Quran magic, they simultaneously admitted its extraordinary impact but refused to attribute that to divine origin. This accusation was not new; earlier prophets like Moses were also accused of sorcery when they brought clear signs. The verse thus places the Prophet in the company of previous messengers, normalizing his experience of rejection. For the believer, this teaches an important lesson about the nature of truth: when falsehood cannot match the evidence, it often tries to rename it. A person of sound intellect should look at the content and results of the message, not the labels thrown at it. The Quran invites this examination by itself. The verse also highlights the illogical nature of the accusation. Magic is associated with deception and harm, yet the Quran brings moral purification, justice, and monotheism. The very purity of its message testifies against the claim of magic.
16 | When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected? | The disbelievers’ mockery takes a specific form: they question the possibility of resurrection. “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?” This rhetorical question drips with incredulity and sarcasm. They present the stages of decomposition—first becoming dust, then scattered bones—and ask if life can possibly return to such remains. To their materialistic minds, the idea was absurd. The verse captures their mindset exactly, showing that the core of their denial was not just a rejection of the Prophet’s message but a deep-seated inability to conceive of divine power beyond what they saw in the physical world. The question “will we really be resurrected?” uses a grammatical form that expects a negative answer, revealing their certainty that such a thing cannot happen. This attitude is the root of much of human rebellion: if there is no accountability after death, one can live without moral restraint. The Quran frequently quotes this argument because it is the psychological barrier that must be broken for faith to enter the heart. The verse follows the pattern of presenting their speech verbatim, making the subsequent refutation more powerful. It also exposes that their earlier mockery and accusations of magic were all just smokescreens to avoid confronting the reality of the Hereafter. The mention of forefathers in the next verse will show how tradition reinforces this denial.
17 | And our forefathers as well? | They extend their question to include previous generations. “And our forefathers as well?” This addition is meant to make the idea of resurrection seem even more preposterous. If it is hard enough to imagine their own decomposed bodies being revived, how could people who died centuries ago, whose remains have completely disappeared, possibly be raised? This question also reveals a psychological dimension: the disbelievers hid behind the authority and customs of their ancestors. They thought, “If our ancestors lived and died without any resurrection happening to them, then they must have been on the correct path, and this talk of a Day of Judgment is just a new innovation.” The Quran repeatedly confronts the blind following of forefathers as a major obstacle to accepting truth. The specific mention of forefathers here ties the denial of resurrection to tribal pride and the desire to maintain the status quo. It shows that their rejection is not purely intellectual but deeply cultural and emotional. They were reluctant to admit that their beloved ancestors might have been wrong and might face punishment. This verse thus sets the stage for later passages in the surah that show how misled people will turn on those they followed. The question also allows for a comprehensive answer: Allah, who created the first human and has recorded every atom, can reassemble all generations just as easily as He created them initially. The mention of forefathers makes the question more personal and rooted in lineage, which the coming verses will address with a firm command to answer.
18 | Say, “Yes! And you will be fully humbled.” | The command to the Prophet is direct and unapologetic: “Say, ‘Yes! And you will be fully humbled.’” The affirmation “Yes” provides a blunt, clear answer to their sarcastic query. There is no hesitation or softening of the message. Yes, you will indeed be resurrected. The certainty of this declaration contrasts sharply with the doubt and mockery of the previous verses. Then the verse adds a crucial detail about the state in which they will rise: “you will be fully humbled.” The Arabic word used here (dākhirūn) means subdued, humiliated, and brought low. They had arrogantly scoffed at the idea of resurrection, so their rising will be in a state of abject humiliation, shattering their pride. This is not just a factual statement but a prophetic warning. The resurrection is not a neutral event for the deniers; it is the beginning of their disgrace. The answer addresses both the physical possibility and the moral consequence. The mention of being humbled also reassures the Prophet and the believers that the mockery they endured will be answered with divine justice. In the context of the surah, this short verse serves as a powerful pivot. It ends the dialogue of denial and asserts the truth, preparing the listener for the vivid scenes of the Day of Judgment that will follow. The Prophet is instructed not to engage in prolonged argument but to deliver this crisp, decisive response that combines affirmation of resurrection with a warning about its reality for the arrogant.
19 | It will only take one Blast, then at once they will see ˹it all˺. | After the decisive “Yes,” the surah describes how easily the resurrection will occur. “It will only take one Blast, then at once they will see it all.” The “one Blast” refers to the blowing of the Trumpet by the angel Isrāfīl. A single, immense sound will be enough to bring the entire creation back to life and assemble them. This underlines the absolute power of Allah. What the disbelievers considered impossible—the reviving of decayed bodies across all ages—requires from Allah nothing more than a single command, a single blast. The phrase “at once they will see” indicates the suddenness and the immediacy with which the reality they denied will become visible to them. There will be no gradual process; in an instant, they will be standing, witnessing the truth they mocked. The word “see” implies not only physical sight but also sudden, shocking realization. All their scepticism will evaporate the moment that Blast sounds. This verse, therefore, functions as a vivid corrective to the materialist mindset that measures Allah’s power by human limitations. It also inspires a sense of urgency in the believer. If the Day can come with a single sudden blast, then one must always be prepared. The imagery connects to the earlier oaths about the angels’ ranks; the same organized hosts will be involved in executing this event. The surah thus weaves together the unseen order and the eschatological climax, showing that the cosmos is moving according to a precise divine plan that culminates in a single, devastatingly swift moment of awakening.
20 | They will cry, “Oh, woe to us! This is the Day of Judgment!” | When that moment arrives, the deniers will not remain silent. The verse captures their desperate cry: “Oh, woe to us! This is the Day of Judgment!” The exclamation “Oh, woe to us!” is a phrase of extreme grief and self-reproach. It is the sound of a person who has suddenly realized the magnitude of their error and the horror of their situation. They recognize the Day for what it truly is, calling it by its right name: “the Day of Judgment.” In their worldly life, they had mocked this very term, but now they utter it with their own tongues in terror. The verse is deeply ironic: the people who once asked sarcastically if they would be resurrected are now the ones crying out in despair upon seeing that very Day. Their acknowledgment does not benefit them; it only compounds their regret. The word “Judgment” (dīn) also carries the meaning of recompense, indicating that they immediately understand this is a day when every deed will be weighed. The surah presents this scene not just as a prophecy but as a direct rebuke to the contemporary Meccan mockers. In effect, it tells them: “This is the cry you will utter.” For the believer, this verse evokes a mix of awe and a desire to be among those who will be joyful on that Day, not among those wailing in regret. It leads naturally into the descriptions of the fate of the wrongdoers that follow in the next verses, completing the passage’s movement from oath, to argument, to prophetic command, and finally to the vivid portrayal of the Day they refused to believe in.
21 | ˹They will be told,˺ “This is the Day of ˹Final˺ Decision which you used to deny.” | As the disbelievers cry out in anguish, recognizing the Day of Judgment, a voice responds to their wailing. They are told, “This is the Day of Final Decision which you used to deny.” The term “Final Decision” translates the Arabic word “faṣl,” which implies a decisive separation between truth and falsehood, right and wrong, the obedient and the rebellious. Throughout their worldly life, the deniers were warned repeatedly that a day would come when all matters would be settled conclusively, yet they consistently rejected that warning. Now, that very day has arrived, and they are reminded of their previous denial. The phrase “which you used to deny” is not just information; it is a rebuke that intensifies their regret. They are forced to confront the fact that their suffering is the direct result of their own arrogance and refusal to accept what messengers brought. The Meccan pagans used to mock the Prophet when he spoke of the Day of Decision, but now they stand in the middle of its unfolding and cannot escape. This statement also reveals the justice of Allah: He does not punish without making the truth clear beforehand. The denial was an active, repeated choice, and so the outcome is just. The verse teaches believers that the warnings of the Quran are not empty threats but an inevitable reality. When one hears about the Day of Decision, the heart should tremble and turn to obedience. The reply to the disbelievers also cuts off any hope of argument or appeal; it is the pronouncement that the time for debate is over and the time for recompense has begun. This sets the stage for the detailed scenes of judgment, where every alliance and false god will be shown to be utterly useless. It connects back to the oath at the beginning of the surah: the angels lined in ranks are now part of the cosmic tribunal that brings about this decisive day.
22 | ˹Allah will say to the angels,˺ “Gather ˹all˺ the wrongdoers along with their peers, and whatever they used to worship | The scene shifts from the disbelievers’ cry to the divine command initiating the sorting of humanity. Allah instructs the angels, “Gather all the wrongdoers along with their peers, and whatever they used to worship.” The command to “gather” indicates an authoritative, irresistible summoning. The angels, those same beings who were described at the surah’s opening as standing in obedient ranks, now become the executors of this enormous task. They are to assemble not just individuals, but groups. The “wrongdoers” are all those who committed shirk and oppression, and “their peers” refers to those who were like them in evil—partners in crime, leaders of misguidance, and followers who shared their mindset. This grouping signifies that on the Day of Judgment, associations and friendships based on sin will not help but will rather become a source of collective punishment. The command also includes “whatever they used to worship” besides Allah. Idols, false gods, jinn, and even revered humans will be physically gathered—according to many scholars, given a form of life—so that they can be seen by their worshippers and then cast into Hell along with them. This demonstration utterly humiliates the idolaters by proving that their objects of worship have no power to help or even defend themselves. For the Meccan pagans, who took pride in their ancestral idols and deities, this is a horrifying revelation. Their gods are not intercessors but co-defendants in the same punishment. The verse shows that no one will be left out; the gathering is comprehensive. This divine order also highlights the angels’ absolute obedience; they do not question but carry out the command instantly. The believer should reflect on the company they keep in this life, because those bonds may become a source of ruin if based on shared disobedience.
23 | instead of Allah, then lead them ˹all˺ to the path of Hell. | The command continues from the previous verse, adding a second step after the gathering: “then lead them all to the path of Hell.” The phrase “instead of Allah” clarifies that these worshipped entities were taken as deities in place of the One true God. This is the essence of their crime. Now they are to be led, like cattle being herded, towards Hell. The word “path” implies that Hell is a destination with a road leading to it, a road that they are now being forced to march upon. The inclusion of the false gods themselves in this parade to damnation is a profound display of humiliation. The Meccan idolaters expected their gods to intercede for them or save them. Instead, they find themselves and their idols being driven together towards the fire. The Arabic phrasing “lead them” carries a sense of driving and pushing, indicating forceful escort without any possibility of resistance. Scholars mention that the angels tasked with this are the same angels of punishment, fearsome in appearance, who will bind and drag the wrongdoers. The scene is terrifying, yet deeply logical: those who walked a path of misguidance in the world are now being led along the path to its final destination. The verse also completes the overturning of the pagan worldview. They thought their idols brought them closer to Allah; in reality, those idols are the very reason they are being thrust into destruction. For the believer, this is a stark reminder that whatever one places above Allah in love, fear, or obedience will become a source of ruin unless it is brought into alignment with His commands. Even permissible attachments, if they become idols in the heart, can lead one astray. The “path of Hell” is paved with the choices made in this life, and the only safe path is the straight path of sincere devotion.
24 | And detain them, for they must be questioned.” | The final part of the divine command adds, “And detain them, for they must be questioned.” Before they are cast into the fire, the wrongdoers and their false gods will be stopped and held in place. Detention here implies a forced pause in their march to Hell, not to offer hope but to add to their terror and accountability. The questioning is not to gain information—Allah knows all—but to establish guilt through their own confession or through the testimony of their own limbs and the angels. This interrogation will leave them with no excuses. In the worldly courts, a criminal may escape due to lack of evidence or clever arguments, but on that Day, the questioning is perfect and irrefutable. The Meccan pagans who mocked the idea of being held accountable will find themselves standing helplessly before their Lord, being asked about every deed and every false belief. The question will likely revolve around their worship: “Why did you worship other beings besides Me?” and “What answer do you have to the messengers who were sent to you?” The verse emphasizes that no one will be punished without a thorough establishment of their guilt. This is a manifestation of Allah’s perfect justice. The detention also serves as a moment of reflection—though too late for benefit—where they see the horror ahead and the pointlessness of their past arrogance. The believers should take this as a warning to constantly hold themselves accountable before that ultimate questioning arrives. The Prophet (peace be upon him) advised that a wise person is one who takes account of himself and prepares for death. The detention in the Hereafter is the unavoidable reality; the questioning we do of our own souls now can save us from its severity then.
25 | ˹Then they will be asked,˺ “What is the matter with you that you can no longer help each other?” | After being detained, the wrongdoers face a humiliating question: “What is the matter with you that you can no longer help each other?” In the world, they used to form alliances, support one another in wrongdoing, and boast about their collective strength. Their tribes, their wealth, their mutual backing gave them a sense of invincibility. Now, they stand powerless and isolated, even though they are gathered together. The question is deeply sarcastic; it reminds them of their former claims. They helped each other in defying Allah, they encouraged each other to mock the Prophet, and they promised each other that their gods would intercede. Yet in this decisive hour, none of that mutual assistance functions. They cannot lift a finger to aid one another, nor can their idols offer the slightest help. This sudden paralysis demonstrates that all worldly power is borrowed and vanishes when divine judgment arrives. The verse echoes the earlier argument about resurrection: just as they could not conceive of being raised, they also never imagined a day when their social bonds would dissolve into nothing. For the Meccan pagans who prided themselves on tribal solidarity, this scene dismantles that pride. Leaders and followers, rich and poor, will all be equally helpless. The believer learns that the only bond that will benefit on that Day is the bond of faith and righteous deeds. The mutual help we should cultivate is helping one another towards obedience to Allah, not in defiance of Him. The question “What is the matter with you?” also exposes the shock and confusion they experience; they themselves cannot comprehend why their previous strategies have failed so completely. The answer, which the verse does not state but is obvious, is that Allah’s power now dominates and all false supports have evaporated.
26 | In fact, on that Day they will be ˹fully˺ submissive. | The verse provides the stark reality behind their helplessness: “In fact, on that Day they will be fully submissive.” The word “submissive” translates a term that means surrendering completely, humbling oneself, and yielding without resistance. The very people who strutted arrogantly on earth, refusing to bow to Allah’s commands, now are brought to their knees. Their submission, however, comes too late to benefit them. It is not the voluntary submission of faith that earns reward, but the forced submission of encountering undeniable power. This reflects the Quranic principle that while Allah desires willing worship, the entire creation ultimately submits to Him by His irresistible authority. The disbelievers who mocked the call to prayer and the concept of prostration will find their bodies and hearts submitting involuntarily on the Day of Judgment. The addition of “fully” emphasizes the totality of their surrender; there is no defiance left, no eye that dares to lift, no voice that can argue. This humiliation is the direct consequence of their chosen arrogance. The believer is reminded that the voluntary submission of daily prayers and obedience spares one from the forced submission of that dreadful day. The scene also connects with the earlier verses where the deniers were scoffing and arguing; now they have no argument, only submissive silence. This transformation illustrates the complete reversal of fortunes. The surah consistently contrasts the state of the believers and the disbelievers, and this verse shows the ultimate end of the latter: a surrender stripped of any dignity. The lesson is profound: do not let your pride prevent you from submitting now, when submission brings mercy, for the Day will come when submission can no longer avert punishment.
27 | They will turn on each other, throwing blame. | The helplessness and submission do not create solidarity among the wrongdoers; rather, they dissolve all bonds of false friendship. “They will turn on each other, throwing blame.” Those who were allies in sin become enemies, each pointing the finger at the other to shift responsibility. The atmosphere in the place of detention becomes one of bitter recrimination. Followers blame leaders, and leaders blame followers. This mutual accusation is itself a part of their torment, because it shatters any illusion they had of loyalty and companionship in the worldly life. In their dunyā, they might have said, “We were together, we are in this together,” but now that the punishment looms, self-preservation takes over, though it is futile. The verse shows that alliances built on anything other than faith and truth are ultimately fragile. The blaming is not a calm discussion but an emotional, heated exchange of “you misled me,” “no, you chose your own path.” The scene echoes the Quranic theme that Satan himself will disown his followers on the Day of Judgment, telling them he merely invited and they followed. Here the human misleaders and misled follow the same pattern. For the believer, this is a strong warning against blindly following people of influence without examining their teachings against revelation. It also encourages sincere believers to cultivate relationships based on mutual advice to truth and patience, relationships that will endure in the Hereafter rather than becoming occasions of mutual cursing. The verse paints the psychological torment of regret, where one realizes that the very people they loved and trusted in this world were the cause of their eternal ruin.
28 | The misled will say, “It was you who deluded us away from what is right.” | The dialogue of blame begins with the followers, the misled, addressing those who led them astray. “It was you who deluded us away from what is right.” The misled here are the common people who followed the leaders of disbelief, the chiefs, and the instigators. They try to offload their culpability onto their leaders by claiming they were deluded. The word “deluded” implies being deceived, led on with false promises and clever arguments. They argue that they were not inherently inclined to reject truth, but the leaders wrapped falsehood in attractive packages, using persuasive speech, threats, and incentives to keep them away from the message of the prophets. They claim that the leaders blocked their access to the right path by sowing doubts and creating obstacles. This confession reveals that in their hearts, even during their worldly life, they might have recognized some glimmer of truth, but the social pressure and the influence of the powerful figures kept them from embracing it. The verse is a powerful lesson about the responsibility of those in positions of influence—scholars, politicians, celebrities, and community elders—who use their platform to discourage people from true faith. It also reminds the common person that excuses based on being misled will not absolve them if they chose to follow without sincere seeking. The misled are not innocent victims; they made the decision to listen and obey these leaders. The next verses will make that clear. This accusation sets the stage for the counter-response from the misleaders, creating a dramatic courtroom scene in the Hereafter where all parties try to escape blame, yet all will be held fully accountable.
29 | The misleaders will reply, “No! You disbelieved on your own. | The misleaders immediately reject the accusation. “No! You disbelieved on your own.” They flatly deny that they forced the followers into disbelief. Their defense is that the followers possessed their own free will and intellect, and they chose disbelief independently. The misleaders are essentially saying, “We may have invited you, but we did not compel you. You had the same prophets, the same scriptures, the same signs in creation that we did. You made your own decision to reject.” This response reflects a core Quranic principle: no soul bears the burden of another, and guidance or misguidance ultimately goes back to individual choice, even when external influences are strong. While the leaders are certainly guilty for their misleading invitations, the followers are also guilty for accepting those invitations without critical reflection. The verse dismantles the common psychological crutch of blaming the environment, society, or bad friends for one’s own disbelief. The leaders’ reply, though self-serving, contains a grain of truth that the Quran elsewhere affirms—that each person is accountable for their own response to truth. The mutual blaming reveals the breakdown of the false unity they shared in the world. They had stood together against the Prophet, unified in mockery, but now they tear each other apart. The believer should understand that on the Day of Judgment, following a misguided crowd will not be an acceptable excuse. Allah gave every person an innate disposition to recognize truth and sent messengers to clarify, so the individual cannot simply point to others. This interrogation scene serves as a mirror for us to examine our own willingness to take personal responsibility for our faith and actions.
30 | We had no authority over you. In fact, you yourselves were a transgressing people. | The misleaders continue their defense by stating, “We had no authority over you.” They point out that they were not rulers who could physically coerce belief. Their influence was merely verbal and social, not an irresistible force. Since the followers could have chosen to reject their invitations, the leaders claim innocence of direct compulsion. This statement is actually an admission that they themselves had no real power; it was all a bluff of arrogance. Then they turn the accusation back, saying, “In fact, you yourselves were a transgressing people.” They assert that the followers had a predisposition to transgression and eagerly embraced disbelief. They argue that the followers were not innocent lambs but willing participants who exceeded limits, enjoyed sins, and showed hostility towards the truth. The word “transgressing” suggests a deliberate crossing of boundaries set by Allah. This defense, while futile in absolving the leaders, exposes the true nature of both parties: the leaders misled, and the followers transgressed willingly. The verse provides insight into the psychology of collective disbelief. Often, corrupt leaders and corrupt followers create a symbiotic relationship where each reinforces the other’s deviance. The leaders enjoy power and adulation, the followers enjoy the license to indulge their desires under the banner of a respected authority. Thus both are guilty. The believer learns that following a leader in matters of faith requires verifying that leader’s conformity with divine guidance. No human authority is absolute. The fact that the misleaders and misled are now trapped in a blame game shows that none of them can produce a valid excuse. The “authority” they lacked over each other is now overshadowed by the absolute authority of Allah over them all.
31 | The decree of our Lord has come to pass against us ˹all˺: we will certainly taste ˹the punishment˺. | The argument between the misleaders and the misled reaches a grim conclusion as they jointly acknowledge a higher reality. They say, “The decree of our Lord has come to pass against us all: we will certainly taste the punishment.” After all the mutual blame, they realize that it is futile because Allah’s word of judgment has already been pronounced. The “decree” refers to the divine warning given through all prophets that disbelievers would be punished. Now that decree has materialized, and both groups are included in it without distinction. The phrase “we will certainly taste the punishment” is an admission of inevitable doom. The word “taste” gives a sensory immediacy to the suffering; they will directly experience it, not just observe it. This shared confession ends their dispute in a moment of bleak unity. The verse shows that on the Day of Judgment, the debate is finally silenced by the overwhelming reality of divine justice. In the world, they might have debated with the Prophet, but now they see the decree unfolding and can only acknowledge its truth. The scene is tragic because it demonstrates that recognition of truth came when it no longer profited them. The believer should take this as a motivation to accept the decree of Allah now, in faith and obedience, so that the decree of mercy is written for them rather than the decree of punishment. The verse also underlines the absolute sovereignty of Allah: His word cannot be overturned, and His warning is always fulfilled. The mutual blame game ends not because one side convinced the other, but because the Judge’s sentence silences all arguments.
32 | We caused you to deviate, for we ourselves were deviant. | After jointly acknowledging the decree, the misleaders make a candid admission, perhaps to their followers or to themselves: “We caused you to deviate, for we ourselves were deviant.” This is a moment of raw honesty extracted by the terror of the situation. They finally confess what they had denied before: yes, we misled you, but it was because we were already lost. It is not an excuse but an explanation of the chain of deviance. Misguidance propagates itself; a deviant person naturally leads others into deviation. The statement reveals the contagious nature of falsehood. The misleaders were not enlightened people consciously choosing to destroy others; they were themselves trapped in the darkness they spread. This recognition, however, does not lessen their crime; it only clarifies the mechanism of how evil multiplies in society. For the followers, this confession is devastating: they entrusted their eternal fate to people who now openly admit they were lost. The verse serves as a severe warning against following anyone who is not firmly guided by divine revelation. A teacher, a leader, or an influencer who is deviant will inevitably cause others to deviate, sometimes sincerely thinking they are on the right path. The test for believers is to measure everything against the Quran and authentic Sunnah. The phrase also echoes the Quranic portrayal of Satan, who admits he misled humans but also acknowledges his own deviation. This parallel suggests that the human misleaders are imitating the archetypal deceiver. The scene in the surah thus weaves a consistent narrative of shared guilt and shattered illusions, pushing the listener to evaluate their sources of guidance in this life before the day when all masks fall away.
33 | Surely on that Day they will ˹all˺ share in the punishment. | The mutual blame and confessions lead to the final verdict on this group: “Surely on that Day they will all share in the punishment.” The word “share” indicates that the punishment is a common lot; they are partners in suffering just as they were partners in sin. No one’s attempt to shift blame succeeds. The leaders and followers, the misleaders and the misled, are all immersed in the torment together. This verse reinforces the principle of individual responsibility while also recognizing collective accountability for collective crimes. The misleaders receive punishment for their misleading, and the misled receive punishment for their willing followership. The sharing of punishment adds a social dimension to the torment; they will see each other suffering, which may increase their remorse and mutual hatred. The phrase “on that Day” stresses the inevitability and specificity of the judgment. It is not a vague future but a definite appointed time. The verse ties a knot on the preceding dialogue, confirming that all the arguments are now over and only the consequences remain. The Meccan pagans who were united in opposing the Prophet will find themselves united in the fire, their worldly camaraderie transformed into eternal regret. For the believer, this is a sobering reminder to choose friends and associates wisely. The company we keep either leads us to share in Allah’s mercy or to share in His punishment. The surah will later contrast this with the believers who share in bliss and honourable mention. The starkness of “surely” leaves no room for doubt or hope of escape for those who die upon associating partners with Allah.
34 | That is certainly how We deal with the wicked. | Following the vivid scene of the misleaders and the misled sharing punishment, the verse delivers a universal principle: “That is certainly how We deal with the wicked.” The phrase “that is how We deal” establishes a divine pattern or sunnah that is consistent and unchanging. The punishment described is not an arbitrary act of anger but a deliberate, just recompense for wickedness. The “wicked” includes all those who commit kufr, shirk, and oppression, and who reject the messengers. The use of “We” emphasizes the majesty and authority of Allah in executing this justice. This verse serves as a transition from the specific story of the Meccan deniers and the past nations to a general law. It tells the Prophet and the believers that what they have just heard about the Day of Judgment and the punishment is not an exception; it is the standard operating procedure of divine justice. This should both terrify the stubborn disbelievers and comfort the oppressed believers. For the believers, it means that the apparent success of the wicked in this world is temporary and does not reflect their ultimate fate. The day of reckoning will certainly arrive, and Allah’s way with the wicked is humiliation and punishment. The verse also functions as a warning to anyone who might think they can escape accountability by clever arguments or by hiding behind influential leaders. The “deal” is a settlement of accounts, and no wicked person will be overlooked. It connects to the earlier rhetorical question about which is harder to create; the One who can create the heavens and earth can just as easily bring the wicked to justice. The finality of the statement reinforces the surah’s central theme of resurrection and judgment.
35 | For whenever it was said to them ˹in the world˺, “There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah,” they acted arrogantly | The surah now zooms in on a particular trait of the wicked that sealed their fate. “For whenever it was said to them in the world, ‘There is no god worthy of worship except Allah,’ they acted arrogantly.” The verse identifies the call to tawheed, the declaration of faith, as the core message they rejected. The phrase “There is no god except Allah” (lā ilāha illā Allāh) is the essence of Islam. When this call reached them, whether from the Prophet or from the earlier messengers, their immediate reaction was not contemplation but arrogance. The word “acted arrogantly” describes a psychological and behavioural state of considering oneself too grand, too important, or too sophisticated to submit to this simple truth. The Meccan pagans were particularly guilty of this: they perceived that accepting tawheed would mean abandoning the traditions of their forefathers and losing their social status as custodians of the idols. Their arrogance was a shield protecting their pride and interests. The verse teaches that arrogance is the primary barrier between a person and true faith. It is not usually lack of evidence that prevents belief, but an inflated ego that refuses to bow to a higher authority. The call to monotheism demands a revolution in one’s life—a transfer of ultimate allegiance from self, tribe, or nation to the Creator alone. The arrogant find this humiliating. The verse’s use of “whenever” shows that the call was repeated again and again, yet each time they persisted in pride. This demonstrates that their punishment is fully earned; mercy was offered repeatedly but rejected. The believer should examine their own heart for traces of this same arrogance when they hear reminders to obey Allah.
36 | and argued, “Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?” | The verse quotes their specific argument, which encapsulates both their arrogant attachment to ancestral worship and their ridicule of the Prophet. “Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?” The question is rhetorical and dripping with contempt. They frame the call to tawheed as an outrageous demand to “abandon our gods”—deities they inherited from their forefathers and around which their social, economic, and religious life revolved. To abandon them was unthinkable, a betrayal of their very identity. Then they belittle the source of the message by calling the Prophet “a mad poet.” The accusation of madness was a common slander against prophets. They could not attribute his eloquence and the Quran’s impact to normal human ability, so they labelled it either sorcery or the ravings of a madman. By adding “poet,” they tried to categorize the Quran as imaginative literature, denying its divine origin. The surah will refute this later by emphasizing that the Prophet came with the truth and confirming earlier messengers. For now, the verse simply records their speech to expose its foolishness. A “mad poet” does not produce a book of law, wisdom, and consistent monotheistic theology that transforms a society. The absurdity of their claim would be evident to any fair observer, but their arrogance blinded them. The believer learns from this that truth is often met with character assassination. When people cannot counter the message, they attack the messenger. Patience is required, as the Prophet himself endured such insults. The verse also highlights the emotional aspect of disbelief: they were not just intellectually unconvinced; they felt a deep personal attachment to their gods, making the call to abandon them feel like a loss.
37 | In fact, he came with the truth, confirming ˹earlier˺ messengers. | The immediate response to their claim is a divine assertion: “In fact, he came with the truth, confirming earlier messengers.” The verse does not engage in a lengthy debate but simply states the reality. The Prophet brought the truth—this is a definitive judgment from Allah, who knows the nature of the message. The truth he brought is the message of pure monotheism, the resurrection, and accountability, which aligns perfectly with what all previous messengers taught. The Quran is not a new, strange doctrine invented by a madman; it is a continuation and confirmation of the chain of prophethood stretching back through history. The phrase “confirming earlier messengers” serves multiple purposes. It validates the continuity of divine revelation, showing that Islam is not a break from the past but its fulfillment. It also refutes the pagans’ assumption that their forefathers were upon truth; the true forefathers in faith were the prophets like Abraham, Moses, and Jesus, whom the Quran confirms. For the Christians and Jews who may hear this, it calls them to recognize the truth in Muhammad’s message as consistent with their own scriptures’ original teachings. For the Meccan pagans, it undermines their claim that the Prophet invented something new. The verse also comforts the Prophet himself: despite being called a mad poet, Allah testifies that he is on the path of all noble messengers. The believer takes from this verse a clear criterion for evaluating any claim to truth: does it confirm the core message of all prophets? Any teaching that contradicts the fundamental principles of tawheed and resurrection is falsehood. The truth is unified across time, and the Prophet’s way is the final and complete expression of it.
38 | You will certainly taste the painful torment, | After affirming the truth of the Prophet’s message, the verse pivots to address the deniers directly with a solemn warning: “You will certainly taste the painful torment.” The address “You” points sharply at the Meccan pagans and, by extension, all who follow their path of arrogant denial. The word “taste” gives the torment a personal and experiential emphasis; it will not be a distant observation but a direct, internalized suffering. The adjective “painful” underscores that this is not a metaphorical or light punishment but an intense, agonizing reality. The certainty expressed by “certainly” leaves no room for the scepticism they displayed in the world. Just as they were certain in their mockery that resurrection would never happen, now the Quran gives them a contrary certainty: punishment will happen and they will experience it. This verse brings back the surah’s theme of reversal: what they denied becomes their unavoidable fate. The verse also serves as a direct answer to their rhetorical question about abandoning gods for a mad poet. The consequences of not abandoning those gods are this painful torment. The warning is an act of mercy even in its severity, because it is delivered in this world through the Quran, giving them a chance to repent. The torment is described as something they will “taste,” which implies a sampling of the consequences they have fully earned, with the full meal of eternal punishment to follow. Believers who hear this verse should feel a healthy fear that drives them to stay far from the actions and attitudes that lead to such an end.
39 | and will only be rewarded for what you used to do. | The verse adds a critical principle of justice to the warning: “and will only be rewarded for what you used to do.” The word “rewarded” here is used in the context of recompense, whether good or bad, but in this context it clearly refers to the punishment being the exact requital for their deeds. The phrase negates any possibility of injustice. They will not receive anything beyond what their own hands earned. Allah does not wrong anyone; the torment they taste is the direct product of their own disbelief, mockery, arrogance, and oppression. The phrase “what you used to do” reminds them of their habitual actions in the world. Their disbelief was not a one-time mistake but a pattern of behaviour that they repeated and persisted in. This cuts off any argument that they are being punished for something minor or unintentional. The verse also emphasizes individual moral responsibility, a theme the Quran consistently highlights. The recompense is tied strictly to deeds, which is both a warning and a comfort: a warning to those who do evil, and a comfort to those who do good that their good deeds will not be wasted. In the context of the surah, this verse forms a bridge to the next section, which contrasts the fate of the wicked with the reward of the devout servants of Allah. The divine justice system is perfectly balanced; punishment is earned, and so is reward. The believer is encouraged to focus on the quality of their deeds, knowing that every action, whether seen or hidden, will be presented on that Day. The logical outcome of such belief is a life of careful, conscious devotion.
40 | But not the chosen servants of Allah. | With this short verse, the surah introduces a massive shift in tone and content. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” After detailing the accusations, the blame game, the warning of painful torment, and the principle of just recompense, Allah makes a crucial exception. The “chosen servants” are those whom Allah has purified, guided, and selected for His mercy. The term “chosen” (mukhlaṣīn) can also be understood as those who have made their worship and devotion purely for Allah, i.e., the sincere ones. This group is completely exempted from the painful punishment described earlier. The verse serves as a ray of hope and a clear demarcation. It tells the listener that although the general pattern for the wicked is destruction, there is a category of people who are saved. The Meccan pagans thought they were the chosen ones due to their lineage or their custodianship of the Kaaba, but Allah clarifies that chosenness is based on sincere servitude, not tribal identity. The verses that follow will beautifully elaborate on what these chosen servants will receive in Paradise. For the believer, this verse is a lifeline. It reminds us that amidst all the terrifying scenes of judgment, there is a safe haven for those who purify their faith. It also connects back to the beginning of the surah, where the angels were described as lined up in ranks, reciting and driving clouds—they too are among the chosen servants. The exception “but not” is a small phrase with enormous implications; it separates humanity into two distinct destinations. It makes one eager to learn the description of these chosen ones and their reward, which the surah will now unfold in vivid detail, beginning with their known provision and honoured status.
41 | They will have a known provision: | The surah now begins detailing the reward of the chosen servants who were exempted from the painful torment. The verse opens by stating, “They will have a known provision.” This provision is “known” in a profoundly reassuring sense. It is not a vague hope or an uncertain promise but a defined, guaranteed, and specific sustenance that Allah has prepared for them. The term “known” implies that it has been measured, apportioned, and designated by divine knowledge, and the believers in Paradise are aware of what awaits them. Some scholars explain that the provision is known in its timing, its variety, and its continuous delivery without interruption. In the worldly life, provision is often a source of anxiety; a person may not know if they will have enough tomorrow. In Paradise, this anxiety is removed. The provision is constant and predictable in the most joyful way—every moment brings the delight they already anticipate. This also connects to the concept that the people of Paradise will have whatever they desire, and more. The provision includes all forms of sustenance: food, drink, clothing, companionship, and spiritual joy. The fact that it is “a provision” (rizq) ties back to the Quranic theme that Allah is the Provider; in the Hereafter, His provision for the chosen servants reaches its ultimate form. The psychological comfort for the believer is immense. When one hears about the horrors of judgment, this verse is like a gentle hand on the shoulder, whispering that a prepared, guaranteed, blissful provision awaits those who remain faithful. The Meccan pagans who hoarded wealth and prided themselves on their generous provisions in this life are being told that true, lasting provision is exclusively for Allah’s sincere servants. It also reminds us that our striving in this life is to earn that known provision through faith and righteous deeds, not through amassing worldly goods.
42 | fruits ˹of every type˺. And they will be honoured | Among the details of that known provision, the first mentioned is “fruits of every type.” The inclusion of fruits symbolizes abundance, variety, and the fulfillment of desires without limit. In the harsh desert environment of Arabia, fresh fruits were a luxury, often available only seasonally and for the wealthy. The promise of “every type” indicates that in Paradise, every kind of fruit imaginable—and even those beyond human imagination—will be readily available. This demonstrates that Paradise is a place where scarcity does not exist and where the believer’s longing for diverse delights is completely satisfied. Importantly, the verse does not stop at physical provision; it immediately adds, “And they will be honoured.” The honour (ikrām) is a spiritual and social elevation. The disbelievers in Mecca sought honour through tribal status, wealth, and the mockery of the Prophet, but true honour belongs solely to those whom Allah honours. In Paradise, this honour manifests in various ways: the angels greet them, their stations are elevated, their faces radiate with joy, and they are received with divine welcome. The combination of fruits and honour shows that the reward is holistic—it addresses the body’s pleasures and the soul’s dignity. The word “honoured” also implies that they are treated as VIP guests, not merely as inhabitants. For the believer who might have been humiliated, mocked, or oppressed in this world for their faith, this is a powerful consolation. The provision of fruits and the granting of honour together teach that the Hereafter compensates fully for any deprivation or disgrace suffered for Allah’s sake. The verse paints a picture of dignified satisfaction, far removed from the arrogant feastings of the world that were accompanied by boastfulness and neglect of the poor. In Paradise, honour is coupled with humility and gratitude.
43 | in the Gardens of Bliss, | The honoured provision and companionship are located “in the Gardens of Bliss.” The Arabic term “Jannāt an-Naʿīm” carries a depth of meaning. “Gardens” indicates lush, shaded, expansive spaces full of trees, rivers, and beauty. “Bliss” (naʿīm) refers to a state of complete well-being, comfort, and happiness that touches every dimension of existence. It is not merely physical pleasure but an all-encompassing felicity that includes peace of mind, security, and the absence of any distress. The Gardens of Bliss are the specific dwelling of the chosen servants, distinguished from other parts of Paradise perhaps by their exceptional delight. This description directly contrasts with the “painful torment” and the “path of Hell” mentioned earlier in the surah. While the wrongdoers are being dragged and detained in humiliation, the chosen servants are settled in the Gardens of Bliss. The word “Bliss” also negates the possibility of any negative emotion: no sadness, no regret, no boredom, no fear of losing the blessing. The Meccan disbelievers, who revelled in their worldly gardens and vineyards, are being told that the real, eternal Gardens belong to the believers they oppressed. Moreover, the phrase connects to the broader Quranic portrayal of Paradise as a place of ultimate return, where believers will hear the greeting “Peace” and dwell forever. The believer reading this verse is encouraged to visualize not just a physical garden but a state of existence where Allah’s pleasure is manifest in every detail. The Gardens of Bliss are the final home, and the journey of faith is the path leading to them, a path that many earlier messengers and their followers walked.
44 | facing each other on thrones. | Inside the Gardens of Bliss, the social aspect of Paradise is highlighted: “facing each other on thrones.” The image is that of a convivial gathering where people sit on elevated, comfortable seats, arranged so that they can see one another and converse. There is no turning of backs, no segregation due to animosity. In the worldly life, people might sit in circles of friendship, but even the closest gatherings can harbour hidden jealousies or boredom. In Paradise, Allah removes any ill feeling from the hearts of the believers, so their facing each other is a sign of pure love, mutual respect, and shared joy. The “thrones” (surur) indicate luxury and authority; they are not mere benches but seats of honour, suggesting that every believer is treated like a king or queen. The fact that they face each other shows that the social pleasure of Paradise is communal, not isolated. The believers will enjoy each other’s company, reminiscing about the worldly life with gratitude and discussing the blessings they have received. This scene is a beautiful reversal of the Hellfire scene earlier, where the misleaders and misled turned on each other with blame. There, they faced each other in mutual accusation; here, they face each other in love. The verse also reflects the culture of Arabian hospitality and social gatherings, but elevated to perfection. No one is left out; all are included in this circle of honour. The believer learns that Paradise is not merely individual enjoyment but a perfected community where the bonds of faith are forever celebrated. The thrones signify stability and permanence: they are seated, at rest, with no need to toil or move on.
45 | A drink ˹of pure wine˺ will be passed around to them from a flowing stream: | The description of delights continues with a specific drink. “A drink of pure wine will be passed around to them from a flowing stream.” The cup is passed around, indicating a generous, shared hospitality where servants or attendants circulate among the seated believers, offering them this exquisite beverage. The drink is identified as “pure wine,” yet its source is “a flowing stream.” This indicates that the wine of Paradise does not come from fermented fruits in limited barrels but flows continuously and freely, unpolluted and inexhaustible. The phrase “passed around to them” (yuṭāfu ʿalayhim) suggests a scene of celebration where goblets are brought again and again, yet without the negative consequences associated with worldly drinking. The Meccan pagans were familiar with wine gatherings, often accompanied by poetry, boasting, and sometimes indecency. Here, the Quran reclaims the symbol of wine and purifies it, presenting a heavenly version that brings only pleasure and no harm. The flowing stream is a direct contrast to the stagnant, murky water sources in the desert, and even to the limited supplies of earthly luxury. The drink being from a stream means it is ever-fresh, never running out. The believer listening to this verse understands that the lawful pleasures of this world are just a faint shadow of the perfect pleasures of the next, and that abstaining from intoxicants here for Allah’s sake leads to this incredible reward. The social dimension—passing the cup around—reinforces the themes of fellowship and mutual honour established in the previous verse.
46 | crystal-white, delicious to drink. | The wine’s qualities are then beautifully described: “crystal-white, delicious to drink.” The colour is a radiant white, unlike the usual dark red or golden earthly wines. “Crystal-white” suggests purity, clarity, and a luminous quality that appeals to the eye before it touches the lips. It resembles a pristine, gleaming liquid that reflects light perfectly. This visual appeal is the first level of enjoyment. Then the taste is described as “delicious to drink.” The Arabic term used conveys extreme pleasantness of taste, a smoothness that glides down the throat without any harshness or bitterness. There is nothing in it that would make a person wince; it is perfection in flavour. The combination of sight and taste encapsulates the complete sensory satisfaction. In the worldly life, even the most expensive drinks may have an aftertaste or a tinge of unpleasantness; this heavenly drink has none. The verse also implies that the drinkers will savour it consciously, with full appreciation, unlike the dulled senses of earthly intoxication. For the Arab pagans who were proud connoisseurs of wine, this description both challenges and transcends their experience. It tells them that what they indulge in is a defective, harmful imitation of the pure delight reserved for the righteous. The emphasis on “delicious to drink” is an invitation to reflect on the Reward’s perfection. The believer is reminded that every lawful pleasure given up in this world for the sake of Allah is replaced by something infinitely better in the Hereafter. This verse builds anticipation and strengthens the resolve to remain obedient. The white, delicious wine is a symbol of the purified delights that await, free from any imperfection or sin.
47 | It will neither harm ˹them˺, nor will they be intoxicated by it. | Immediately after describing the wine’s appealing qualities, the verse clarifies what it is not: “It will neither harm them, nor will they be intoxicated by it.” This is the critical difference that sets heavenly wine apart from its worldly counterpart. Earthly wine causes physical harm—headaches, liver damage, stomach sickness—and it clouds the mind, leading to loss of self-control, foolish speech, and regrettable actions. The wine of Paradise is completely free from these evils. It does not cause any bodily ailment or pain; it is a pure pleasure that nourishes without any negative side effects. Even more importantly, it does not intoxicate. The drinkers will not lose their intellect or consciousness. They will experience a delightful, euphoric sensation—some scholars mention a feeling of exhilaration and joy—but their minds remain clear, and their capacity to converse, remember, and appreciate remains fully intact. This is crucial because Paradise is a place of awareness and nearness to Allah; to be intoxicated would detract from that. The verse directly answers any criticism that a paradise including wine seems lowly or similar to sinful worldly behaviour. It shows that the substances in Paradise are of a completely different nature, created specifically to give pure pleasure without consequence. It also serves as an admonition to those who justify their drinking of earthly wine by imagining that since Paradise has wine, it cannot be so bad. The earth’s intoxicants are forbidden precisely because they harm and intoxicate, while the heavenly drink is sanctified. The believer finds in this verse a compelling reason to abstain now: the fleeting, harmful buzz is not worth forfeiting the eternal, harmless, and divine delight. The clarity and safety of the heavenly wine reflect the absolute perfection of Allah’s reward.
48 | And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and gorgeous eyes, | The description of the blissful gathering expands to include the companions of Paradise. “And with them will be maidens of modest gaze and gorgeous eyes.” The maidens, often referred to as ḥūr, are beings created for Paradise, perfectly beautiful in form and character. Their first described quality is “modest gaze.” This means their glances are restrained, directed only towards their spouses with love and devotion, never looking elsewhere with desire or causing any jealousy. Modesty here is not shyness rooted in fear but dignified, loving reserve that enhances intimacy and trust. The second quality is “gorgeous eyes.” The Arabic term ʿīn refers to eyes that are wide, with a stark, beautiful contrast between the intensely black pupil and the brilliant white sclera. Such eyes were considered the height of beauty in Arabian culture. These maidens are not merely physical companions; they represent perfect, pure, and faithful partnership. In the worldly life, relationships can be marred by wandering gazes, distrust, and disappointment. In Paradise, Allah provides companions who are a source of complete contentment and honour. The phrase “with them” indicates that these maidens are present in the same gatherings, sharing in the joy, and adding to the social bliss. This promise would resonate with the early Muslims, many of whom had left behind spouses or died unmarried for their faith. It assures them that their sacrifice will be compensated with unimaginably beautiful and loyal companions. The mention of modesty is significant: it shows that true beauty in Allah’s sight includes character and purity, not just appearance. This elevates the concept of companionship to a spiritual as well as physical level.
49 | as if they were pristine pearls. | The beauty and purity of these maidens are further emphasized by a striking simile: “as if they were pristine pearls.” The comparison to pearls is deeply evocative. A pristine pearl is one that has been hidden within its shell deep in the ocean, untouched by human hands, dust, or pollution. It retains its original lustre, smoothness, and perfection. In Arab culture, the pearl was a treasured, rare, and precious gem, often used as a metaphor for something extremely valuable and beautiful. The image of a pearl also conveys a sense of delicate, glowing whiteness, aligning with the purity of the maidens’ characters and appearance. Moreover, a pearl is formed through a process of patience and time within the oyster, hidden from view, which metaphorically reflects the preservation of these companions from any prior defilement or blemish. They are presented to the believers as untouched treasures, exclusive and sublime. The word “pristine” (maknūn) further means well-protected, guarded, and preserved in the best state. This simile silences any crude or base imaginations that might arise from the mention of female companions; it elevates the description to one of refined, artistic appreciation. The believer is invited to conceive of companions who are like hidden gems, now revealed for their delight in a setting of absolute purity. This contrasts sharply with the illicit relationships of this world, often characterized by exploitation, disappointment, and regret. The verse teaches that Allah’s reward includes aesthetic and relational beauty in a form that is completely clean, honouring both the giver and the receiver. For the Meccan pagans who devalued women and relationships, this presents a vision of paradise where purity and honour define even the most intimate joys.
50 | Then they will turn to one another inquisitively. | After establishing the general atmosphere of honour, bliss, and companionship, the surah moves to a specific scene of conversation. “Then they will turn to one another inquisitively.” The people of Paradise, seated on their thrones and enjoying their provisions, will begin to converse with one another. The word “inquisitively” indicates a friendly, curious manner of questioning and sharing. They are deeply interested in each other’s past experiences in the worldly life and how they came to this glorious destination. There is no envy, no competition, only loving curiosity and the joy of storytelling. This turning to one another signifies the complete openness and intimacy of the community of Paradise. They are not isolated in their bliss but actively engaged in a rich social life. The fact that they “turn” physically suggests that they are in a circle, facing one another as previously mentioned, and now they lean in to talk, creating an atmosphere of warmth and close friendship. This scene follows naturally from the honour and provisions; once the initial delight is savoured, they reflect and converse, adding a layer of intellectual and emotional fulfillment. The conversations in the subsequent verses will reveal that they remember their earthly life, the doubts they faced, and the grace that saved them. This remembrance is not painful but profoundly gratifying, as it magnifies their gratitude. For the believer, this is a beautiful vision of the afterlife: not just physical pleasure but meaningful connection with fellow believers, where stories of faith are shared and the mercy of Allah is celebrated. It also serves as encouragement to cultivate such pure, faith-based friendships in this world, for they may continue into the next in the most delightful form.
51 | One of them will say, “I once had a companion ˹in the world˺” | The conversation begins with one of the believers sharing a memory. “One of them will say, ‘I once had a companion in the world.’” The speaker is a person of Paradise who recalls a close associate he knew during his earthly life. The word “companion” (qarīn) can mean a close friend, a business partner, or someone constantly in one’s company. This companion was evidently a disbeliever who used to challenge and mock the believer’s faith. The use of “once” indicates that this friendship was limited to the world; it no longer exists in the Hereafter. The believer is not expressing sorrow but rather setting the stage for a story of divine rescue. This reflection will make the believer appreciate his salvation even more. The verse shows that the people of Paradise will remember the trials they faced, including the negative influences of certain friends. Their memory is complete, but any pain associated with it is removed; instead, it becomes a testimony to Allah’s grace. This is deeply relatable: many sincere believers have friends or family members who mock their faith or try to lead them astray. The verse assures that enduring such pressure and remaining steadfast is a path to a moment of profound gratitude in Paradise, where one can look back and see the disaster that was averted. The friendly, storytelling tone (“I once had a companion”) indicates the relaxed and happy state of the inhabitants, who can speak of past dangers without fear because they are now eternally safe.
52 | who used to ask ˹me˺, ‘Do you actually believe ˹in resurrection˺?’ | The believer now quotes his former companion’s words. “Who used to ask me, ‘Do you actually believe in resurrection?’” The phrase “used to ask” shows that this was a recurring theme. The companion would repeatedly challenge and ridicule the believer’s faith, attempting to sow doubt. The question “Do you actually believe?” is loaded with incredulity and sarcasm; the disbeliever found the idea of resurrection so absurd that he could not fathom a rational person accepting it. This directly mirrors the earlier verses in the surah where the Meccan pagans mocked the Prophet about being raised from dust and bones. The quotation connects the micro-level interpersonal pressure with the macro-level opposition faced by all messengers. It reveals that the denial of resurrection is not just a theoretical stance but can become a tool of social pressure against believers. The companion’s question was designed to make the believer feel foolish, isolated, and perhaps waver in his conviction. However, the believer held fast, and now in Paradise, he recounts this with a sense of triumph and relief. The verse also demonstrates that Allah is fully aware of the whispered doubts and mockeries that believers endure; these conversations are being recorded and will become part of the blessed remembrance in the Hereafter. The believer listening to this surah should recognize that the same questions thrown at them today by sceptical friends will one day be merely a story they tell in Paradise as they look upon the fate of those who asked them.
53 | ‘When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be brought to judgment?’” | The companion’s scepticism is quoted in full, echoing the exact words of the deniers earlier in the surah. “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be brought to judgment?” This detailed description of decomposition—dust and bones—was their supposed scientific proof that resurrection is impossible. The phrase “brought to judgment” is significant because it shows they understood the resurrection was not merely a return to life but a summons to account for deeds. The companion’s question was thus a rejection of moral accountability, not just a biological puzzle. By quoting this in Paradise, the believer highlights the flawed reasoning that nearly ensnared him. The irony is now complete: the companion who found the idea of judgment laughable is himself in the midst of that very judgment, suffering its consequences, while the believer who was mocked is enjoying the bliss of the Gardens. This verse is a powerful reminder that the truth does not depend on the number of people who believe it or the confidence with which falsehood is proclaimed. The companion was utterly convinced of his own rightness, yet his certainty did not change reality. The believer who listens to this verse today can find solace in the fact that the arrogant scepticism of the world will be reduced to a pitiful memory, while the simple, steadfast faith will be crowned with eternal honour and the opportunity to tell the story of one’s deliverance.
54 | He will ˹then˺ ask, “Would you care to see ˹his fate˺?” | After recounting his companion’s words, the believer turns to his fellow inhabitants of Paradise with a suggestion. “He will then ask, ‘Would you care to see his fate?’” The believer, now curious about the outcome of his former companion, invites the others to share in looking. This indicates that the people of Paradise have the ability, by Allah’s permission, to glimpse the state of the inhabitants of Hell. This seeing is not for the purpose of gloating in a malicious way, but to increase their gratitude and to witness the perfect justice of Allah. The phrasing “Would you care to see?” is gentle and inclusive; it turns this moment into a shared experience of reflection. The “fate” is the final destination of the disbeliever. The fact that the believer asks implies that he does not already have a constant view of Hell; rather, a means is provided upon request. This aligns with hadith literature describing that a door will be opened or a view granted so that the believer can see what his companion’s end became. The psychological effect is profound: the very person who tried to lead him astray is now a visual proof of Allah’s justice, and the believer feels an even deeper sense of mercy that he was saved. This also serves as a deterrent for those still in the world: the friendships we cultivate can become either a source of shared bliss or a subject of dreadful observation. The verse encourages believers to choose their close companions wisely and to worry less about the mockery of those who will one day be merely a cautionary sight.
55 | Then he ˹and the others˺ will look and spot him in the midst of the Hellfire. | The invitation is accepted, and the viewing occurs. “Then he and the others will look and spot him in the midst of the Hellfire.” The believer, along with his companions in Paradise, gazes into the depths of Hell and recognizes his former friend. The phrase “in the midst of the Hellfire” indicates that the disbeliever is completely engulfed by the punishment, not on the periphery but deep within it. The act of spotting him suggests that despite the vastness of Hell, the believer’s sight is enabled to locate this specific individual. This recognition is instantaneous and certain. The believer sees the horrifying reality that his companion is suffering, which starkly contrasts with his own surroundings of bliss. This moment is not described with any gleeful malice; rather, it is a moment of awe and realization. The believer fully comprehends what he was saved from. The surah’s earlier descriptions of the wrongdoers being gathered, detained, and led to Hell now find a concrete, personal illustration. The Meccan pagans who mocked the Prophet as a mad poet are being warned that those who follow them might one day be viewed in this way by the believers they oppressed. For the modern believer, this verse underscores the seriousness of disbelief and the immense value of guidance. It encourages us to keep our own faith strong and to be grateful for the environment and companions that help us stay on the path. It also reinforces that all pretenses will be stripped away; the true worth of people will be seen in their final abode.
56 | He will ˹then˺ say, “By Allah! You nearly ruined me.” | Upon seeing his former companion in such a state, the believer exclaims, “By Allah! You nearly ruined me.” This oath “By Allah” emphasizes the sincerity and intensity of the emotion. The phrase “you nearly ruined me” is a direct address to the disbeliever, although it may be spoken across a veil or simply uttered as an exclamation. The believer acknowledges how close he came to being led astray. The “ruin” refers to spiritual destruction leading to eternal punishment. The companion’s persistent mockery and arguments had almost shaken his faith. This is a powerful testimony to the danger of bad company. The believer was not passively immune; he felt the pressure and the pull of doubt, but Allah’s grace protected him. This exclamation does not express hatred as much as profound relief and a sober recognition of what could have been. The verse teaches that friendships can be a test, and even strong believers can be nearly ruined by persistent negative influence. The fact that the believer addresses him directly shows that the barrier between Paradise and Hell does not prevent a form of communication; the disbeliever might hear this statement, adding to his regret. For those of us still in the world, this verse is a wake-up call to evaluate our closest relationships. If we have companions who mock our faith or constantly challenge the truths of the Quran in a destructive way, we must guard our hearts and, if necessary, distance ourselves. The “nearly ruined me” is an honest admission that no one is completely safe from the effects of a bad environment, and only through holding tightly to Allah can one be saved.
57 | Had it not been for the grace of my Lord, I ˹too˺ would have certainly been among those brought ˹to Hell˺.” | Immediately after acknowledging the danger, the believer attributes his salvation entirely to Allah. “Had it not been for the grace of my Lord, I too would have certainly been among those brought to Hell.” This is the pinnacle of humility and gratitude. Even though the believer had faith and did righteous deeds, he does not boast of his own strength. He knows that his ability to remain steadfast and ultimately enter Paradise was a gift of Allah’s grace. The phrase “grace of my Lord” encompasses the guidance that was given to him, the protection from misguidance, the faith that was made beloved to his heart, and the final pardon that admitted him to Paradise. This recognition is exactly what the earlier verse meant by “the chosen servants of Allah”—their status is due to divine election and grace. The phrase “those brought to Hell” (muḥḍarūn) echoes the earlier command to the angels to gather and lead the wrongdoers. The believer realizes he could have been among that group. This moment perfectly captures the spirit of Paradise: the inhabitants are not arrogant about their achievement; they are eternally grateful. The exclamation also serves as an indirect rebuke to the disbeliever’s arrogance. The companion thought he was on a higher intellectual ground, but the reality shows he is in Hell while the one he mocked is in Paradise, and the one in Paradise credits it all to Allah. For the listener, this verse instills a deep reliance on Allah’s grace. While we must strive, we can never rely solely on our own deeds; we must constantly ask for Allah’s grace to complete our faith and save us from a terrible end.
58 | ˹Then he will ask his fellow believers,˺ “Can you imagine that we will never die, | The believer now turns away from the sight of Hell and addresses his fellow believers in Paradise with a joyful reflection. “Can you imagine that we will never die?” The question is rhetorical and filled with wonder. The people of Paradise have been granted eternal life, and they are marvelling at this reality. Death, which was an inescapable fact of worldly existence, has been removed from them permanently. The phrase “Can you imagine” expresses a delightful astonishment, as if the blessing is so immense that they are still savouring its reality. In the world, death is the ultimate source of anxiety, cutting short all pleasures and separating loved ones. In Paradise, this fear is abolished. The believer is not just told he will not die; he is given the consciousness of eternal life to enjoy forever. The question is shared among the group, enhancing their collective joy. This moment also links to a hadith where the Prophet (peace be upon him) described that after the judgment, death will be brought in the form of a ram and slaughtered before the inhabitants of Paradise and Hell, and it will be announced: “O people of Paradise, eternity for you and no death; O people of Hell, eternity for you and no death.” This will increase the delight of the people of Paradise and the despair of the people of Hell. The believer’s question, “Can you imagine that we will never die?” is a direct reflection of that announcement, savoured among friends.
59 | except our first death, nor be punished ˹like the others˺?” | The believer continues his reflection, adding two more astounding blessings. “Except our first death, nor be punished like the others?” The first part, “except our first death,” acknowledges the single death they experienced in the world. That death was the passage from the life of trial to the life of eternal reward. Now that death is permanently behind them. Some scholars note that even that first death will become a distant, non-painful memory, completely overshadowed by the eternal life. The second part, “nor be punished like the others,” is a statement of relief and gratitude. They see the punishment of the disbelievers and realize they have been spared from it. They are exempt from the torment that they are witnessing in Hell. This awareness makes their bliss even more profound, as contrast heightens appreciation. The phrase “like the others” refers to the disbelievers and wrongdoers whose fate they just glimpsed. The believer’s tone is not one of arrogant self-praise but of humble gratitude and wonder. He acknowledges that his own deeds could have led him there if not for Allah’s mercy. The verse encapsulates the complete security of Paradise: no death, no punishment, no fear, no sorrow. This is the final realization of the promise made to the chosen servants. For the living believer, these verses paint a picture of Paradise where one’s past trials are discussed with loving friends, and every difficulty of the world is remembered only to magnify the joy of the ultimate triumph. It encourages a life oriented towards that day when we can marvel together, “We will never die, nor be punished.”
60 | This is truly the ultimate triumph. | The passage concludes this reflection with a powerful, emphatic statement: “This is truly the ultimate triumph.” The scene of the believers in Paradise, with all its honours, provisions, companions, and the joyful realization of eternal safety, is declared the supreme success. The word “triumph” (fawz) in Arabic implies victory, achievement, and the attainment of one’s most desired goal. It is the prize that every human being should strive for. The addition of “ultimate” (al-ʿaẓīm) emphasizes that no other success in any world compares to this. Worldly triumphs—wealth, power, fame, even conquest—pale into insignificance when measured against the eternal triumph of entering Paradise and being saved from Hell. This verse acts as the climax of the description of the reward. It answers the earlier mocking question “Should we really abandon our gods for a mad poet?” with a resounding yes, because the result of abandoning false gods and believing in the truth is this unimaginable triumph. The Meccan disbelievers thought they were the successful ones, with their trade, their idols, and their tribal pride. The Quran redefines success entirely. The believer is invited to meditate on this verse and realign their ambitions. If one truly believes that this is the ultimate triumph, then the sacrifices of this life—avoiding forbidden things, enduring mockery, maintaining prayer—become not just bearable but eminently worthwhile. This verse also sets up the next section, which contrasts the bliss with the hospitality of Hell and warns that for such honour all should strive. It is a call to action: the triumph is described so vividly that any listener should be moved to earn it.
61 | For such ˹honour˺ all should strive. | After declaring the reward of the chosen servants to be the ultimate triumph, the Quran now issues a powerful call to action: “For such honour all should strive.” The word “honour” points back to the entire description of Paradise—the known provision, the fruits, the honour, the Gardens of Bliss, the thrones, the pure wine, the companions, and the eternal security from death and punishment. This is not a passive wish; it requires active effort. The verb translated as “strive” comes from the Arabic root that implies a vigorous competition, a race in which every participant pushes themselves to the utmost to win the prize. It is the same concept found elsewhere in the Quran, where believers are encouraged to “vie with one another in good deeds” and to “race towards forgiveness from your Lord.” The honour is described as something that must be competed for because it is the most valuable prize imaginable, and not everyone will attain it without sincere effort. In the Meccan context, the pagans competed in worldly matters—wealth, poetry, tribal glory—but this verse redirects human ambition towards the eternal. The Prophet (peace be upon him) and his companions were enduring hardship and mockery; this call reminded them that the struggle itself was part of the race. The verse also implies that the opportunity is open to all who are willing to strive. No one is excluded by birth or status; the only requirement is sincere effort in faith and righteousness. For the believer today, this verse is a daily motivator. Every prayer, every act of charity, every resisted temptation is a stride in that competition. The word “all” (al-mutanāfisūn) grammatically designates those who engage in this competition, and it suggests that there is an elite group of people who take the Hereafter seriously enough to make it their primary ambition. The verse connects beautifully with the preceding scene of the believer looking at his companion in Hell and realizing the value of divine grace. That realization should push every listener to compete for the honour that will never perish. The ultimate triumph is not awarded casually; it is earned through a lifetime of striving, and the Quran here sounds the starting gun for all who wish to run.
62 | Is this ˹bliss˺ a better accommodation or the tree of Zaqqûm? | Having described the bliss of Paradise and urged people to strive for it, the surah now poses a stark rhetorical question to the deniers: “Is this bliss a better accommodation or the tree of Zaqqûm?” The “bliss” refers to the detailed picture of Paradise just painted—the honoured seating, the delicious drinks, the eternal peace. The “accommodation” is the place of dwelling and hospitality. In Arabic custom, honouring a guest with a fine dwelling and generous food was a mark of nobility. So Allah asks the Meccan pagans, who prided themselves on their hospitality, to compare that heavenly hospitality with the “tree of Zaqqûm.” Zaqqûm is a specific tree in Hell, described in terrifying detail in the coming verses. Its very name sounded strange and ominous to the Arab ear. The disbelievers mocked the mention of Zaqqûm when it was first revealed. Abu Jahl, the arch-enemy of Islam, once heard the verses about Zaqqûm and sarcastically called for dates and butter, telling his companions to “eat zaqqûm,” insinuating that Muhammad was fabricating nonsense. But the Quran responds by taking the mockery head-on, asking them to seriously consider: which accommodation is truly better? The question is rhetorical because no sane person would choose the tree of Zaqqûm over the Gardens of Bliss. Yet the disbelievers, by their actions and rejection, were effectively making that choice. The verse forces the listener to engage in a mental comparison that leads to an inescapable conclusion. It is a teaching method that appeals to reason: look at the two options laid before you, one full of delight and honour, the other full of horror and pain, and then reflect on which one you are working towards. For the believer, this question reinforces gratitude for guidance and a renewed determination to remain on the path leading to the blissful accommodation. It also serves as a da‘wah tool: we can ask ourselves and others, what is our eternal accommodation going to be?
63 | We have surely made it a test for the wrongdoers. | The verse immediately explains the deeper purpose behind the terrifying tree: “We have surely made it a test for the wrongdoers.” The tree of Zaqqûm is not merely a punishment in the Hereafter; its very mention in the Quran serves as a trial for the disbelievers in this world. When the verses about a tree growing in the midst of fire were recited, the wrongdoers scoffed. They argued, how can a tree grow in fire? Fire burns trees. Muhammad must be crazy. This reaction exposed their materialistic mindset and their refusal to accept that Allah, who created the laws of physics, can make fire cool and safe for a tree if He wills, just as He made the fire cool and safe for Abraham. Their mockery of the unseen descriptions of Hell became a criterion separating the sincere from the arrogant. The test lies in the response: the believers say, “We believe; it is all from our Lord,” while the wrongdoers mock and reject, thereby increasing their own burden of sin. The phrase “for the wrongdoers” indicates that the test is specifically targeted at those who already incline towards oppression and disbelief. The description of Zaqqûm, instead of convincing them, becomes a stumbling block that confirms their rejection. This is a consistent pattern in the Quran: the same verses that are a healing and mercy for believers become a loss and misguidance for wrongdoers. For the believer, this verse teaches the proper attitude towards unseen realities described in revelation: we accept them as Allah describes them, without subjecting them to the limited standards of our worldly experience. The fire of Hell and the tree within it operate by different rules, governed by the Creator’s command. The early Muslims faced ridicule for these beliefs, yet they held firm, and their faith was strengthened by the test. Today, believers still face tests when divine descriptions seem beyond scientific comprehension; the correct response is humble submission and trust in the One who created all laws.
64 | Indeed, it is a tree that grows in the depths of Hell. | The surah now begins the actual description of Zaqqûm, starting with its origin: “Indeed, it is a tree that grows in the depths of Hell.” The word “depths” (aṣl) means the very bottom, the root, the foundation of Hell. This tree is not a superficial plant at the edge of the fire; it springs from the deepest, most intensely blazing core of Jahannam. Its roots are nourished by the molten fire and the foul substances that fuel Hell’s torment. This origin symbolizes the essence of evil and punishment. The tree draws its life from the place of ultimate suffering, and it becomes a manifestation of that suffering in a tangible form that the damned must interact with. The fact that it is a “tree” is itself a mockery of the trees of Paradise. In Paradise, trees provide shade and delicious fruits; in Hell, this tree provides agony. The contrast with the earlier “Gardens of Bliss” is deliberate and sharp. Some scholars, including Ibn Abbas, explained that Zaqqûm is a tree whose roots are in the bottom of Hell and whose branches rise up through its various levels, so that the inhabitants of different levels will encounter its fruits. The phrase “grows” indicates that it is a living organism, created by Allah for the specific function of punishment, renewing itself to provide a continuous supply of torment. For the Meccan pagans who were so attached to the date palms and lush gardens of their valleys, this image served as a horrifying antithesis. The believer reflecting on this verse feels a healthy fear that pushes them to seek refuge in Allah from Hell, and a renewed appreciation for the trees and fruits of this world, which are signs of mercy, not instruments of torture.
65 | bearing fruit like devils’ heads. | After describing the tree’s location, the verse depicts its fruit: “bearing fruit like devils’ heads.” This is one of the most striking and terrifying similes in the Quran. The fruit of Zaqqûm is not round, pleasant berries or dates; it resembles the heads of shayateen. The Arabs had a mental image of devils as hideous, grotesque beings with ugly, deformed heads. Some early commentators said that the fruit looks like the heads of serpents, which were commonly associated with evil. Others said it resembles a particularly ugly and thorny plant found in the desert. The point is that the fruit is visually repulsive, inspiring immediate disgust and terror. Yet, the damned will be driven by extreme hunger to approach and eat it. The comparison to devils’ heads also carries a symbolic meaning: just as the devils are the source of misguidance and evil whisperings, the fruit of Zaqqûm represents the consequence of following those whisperings. Those who took the devils as allies in the world will now find that their sustenance in Hell takes the very shape of those allies, as if to mock them. This verse also connects to the earlier passages about the devils being pelted with flames when they try to listen to the heavenly assembly. Now their image becomes the food of those who followed them. The detailed, almost nightmarish description is meant to shock the listener out of complacency. For the believer, it serves as a vivid deterrent: the temporary illicit pleasures of this world, which the devils beautify, will transform into something monstrous in the Hereafter if not repented from. The verse is a testament to the power of Quranic imagery to convey spiritual truths in a way that penetrates the heart and leaves a lasting impression.
66 | The evildoers will certainly ˹be left to˺ eat from it, filling up their bellies with it. | After the visual horror of the fruit, the verse moves to the actual consumption: “The evildoers will certainly be left to eat from it, filling up their bellies with it.” The phrase “be left to” carries a sense of being compelled or forced. The inhabitants of Hell are not sitting down to a meal by choice; extreme, unbearable hunger will drive them to eat this abomination. It is reported in hadith that the people of Hell will experience such starvation that they will have no option but to consume the filth and torment provided. They will eat the fruit of Zaqqûm, and instead of satisfying their hunger, it will cause more agony. The verse says they will fill their bellies with it, indicating that they will eat greedily, perhaps because the hunger is so intense that they desperately try to stuff themselves, yet the food brings no relief. As they eat, it will boil in their stomachs like molten metal. Another verse in the Quran describes that Zaqqûm will boil in their bellies like the boiling of scalding water. So the act of eating, which is normally associated with pleasure and sustenance, becomes an act of self-torment. The “evildoers” here are specifically those who committed crimes of disbelief and oppression. The word “certainly” assures that this is not a possibility but a guaranteed outcome. For the Meccan pagans who took great pride in their feasts and hospitality, this reversal is profound: the generous hosts of the world will become the desperate, humiliated eaters of poison-fruit. The believer is reminded of the value of hunger experienced in voluntary fasting; the temporary pangs of fasting in obedience to Allah are infinitely better than the eternal, forced eating of Zaqqûm. This verse should motivate a careful watch over what we spiritually consume in this world, ensuring we fill our souls with Quran and remembrance rather than the poison of sin that may foreshadow the fruit of Hell.
67 | Then on top of that they will be given a blend of boiling drink. | The torment does not stop with the solid food; it is compounded with a liquid punishment. “Then on top of that they will be given a blend of boiling drink.” After they have eaten the fruit of Zaqqûm and their stomachs are churning with its heat, they will be seized by a raging thirst. In response, they will be offered a drink, but it is not water to cool them down. It is a “blend” (shūb), a mixture of various foul and scalding fluids. Other Quranic verses describe this drink as ḥamīm—boiling water—and ghassāq—a festering pus. The blend indicates that several types of tormenting liquids are combined into one cup. The phrase “on top of that” shows the layered nature of the punishment: one agony piles upon another. The drink will burn their faces and tear their intestines as it goes down. In the world, the disbelievers enjoyed blending fine wines and creating delicious beverages; in Hell, the blend they receive is unimaginably horrific. The mention of being “given” the drink suggests that just as the people of Paradise are served with honour, the people of Hell are served with contempt. The angels of punishment will force them to drink, adding humiliation to the physical pain. The contrast with the “crystal-white, delicious to drink” wine of Paradise, passed around in flowing streams, is complete. The Meccan pagans who boasted about their drinking sessions are being warned that their next drink will be from the streams of Hell if they persist in disbelief. For the believer, this verse highlights the mercy of Allah in forbidding intoxicants in this world; He wants to save us from even the faintest reminder of the drink of the damned. The torment described is not sensationalism but a merciful warning meant to steer us towards the pure drinks of Paradise.
68 | Then they will ultimately return to ˹their place in˺ Hell. | After the cycle of eating and drinking, the verse says, “Then they will ultimately return to their place in Hell.” The word “return” indicates that this is a continuous, cyclical torment. They are in Hell, then they are perhaps driven out to certain stations for additional punishment like eating from Zaqqûm and drinking the boiling blend, and then they are herded back into the core of Hell. The “place” is their assigned abode where the fire continually engulfs them. The phrase “ultimately return” suggests that no matter what momentary variation the punishment takes, the constant underlying reality is the Fire. Some scholars mention that the inhabitants of Hell will be moved between different levels and types of punishment, but they always return to the primary blaze. This cyclical nature adds a psychological dimension to the suffering: there is no escape, no final destination of relief, only a return to the baseline torment. The Arabic word ma‘ād, translated as “return” or “place of return,” can also mean the final destination. It signals that Hell is their permanent home. The verse ties the previous descriptions together: the tree, the eating, the drinking, all are part of the journey within Hell that ultimately circles back to the heart of the fire. For the Meccan merchants who were used to travelling out on trade caravans and then returning home, this image would resonate; but their true return will be to Hell, not to the comforts of Mecca, if they die in disbelief. The believer reflects on the concept of “return” in a positive light: our ultimate return should be to the mercy and gardens of our Lord. The verse teaches us to prepare for our final return now, ensuring that our deeds lead us to the blissful accommodation rather than the repetitive torment.
69 | Indeed, they found their forefathers astray, | The surah transitions from describing Hell’s hospitality to diagnosing one of the root causes of disbelief that leads people there. “Indeed, they found their forefathers astray.” The “they” refers to the Meccan pagans and, by extension, to all who follow the same pattern. Their primary justification for their idolatry was that they were following the religion of their ancestors. They inherited a set of beliefs and practices without ever subjecting them to critical examination in the light of truth. The word “found” indicates a passive discovery; they were born into a society where misguidance was the norm, and they accepted it as the comfortable default. The Quran repeatedly identifies blind following of forefathers as one of the greatest obstacles to accepting divine guidance. When the Prophet called them to tawheed, their immediate response was, “We found our fathers upon a religion, and we are following in their footsteps.” This is a universal human tendency: traditions and ancestral customs carry immense emotional weight, and breaking away from them feels like a betrayal of family and identity. However, the Quran insists that truth is not determined by majority vote or antiquity; it is determined by revelation. The forefathers being described as “astray” means they were objectively misguided, regardless of their sincerity or the respect they command. This verse sets up a critical lesson: every individual is responsible for their own guidance. One cannot hide behind the mistakes of previous generations on the Day of Judgment. The Meccan pagans are being warned that their ancestors are not a valid excuse; rather, their ancestors’ misguidance should have been a reason for them to search for the truth. For the modern believer, this verse is a call to examine cultural and familial practices in the light of Quran and Sunnah, and to have the courage to abandon inherited errors.
70 | so they rushed in their footsteps! | Not only did they find their forefathers astray, but they actively and eagerly followed them. “So they rushed in their footsteps!” The verb “rushed” indicates haste, enthusiasm, and lack of deliberation. They did not pause to think or evaluate; they simply sped along the path their ancestors had laid out, as if racing to catch up. This depicts a kind of mindless imitation that goes beyond passive acceptance into active, energetic emulation. The footsteps suggest that they walked exactly where their forefathers walked, replicating their errors in precise detail. There is a tragic irony here: the same energy and commitment they should have used to seek the truth, they instead used to entrench themselves in falsehood. The verse also reflects the Meccan society where tribal pride was paramount. To question the ancestors was to question the tribe’s honour. So they rushed headlong into defending the ancestral ways against the Prophet’s message. For the believers, this verse is a warning against the psychology of the crowd and the pull of tradition. It reminds us that the path to Hell is often crowded with those who simply followed others without critical thought. The speed of their following indicates that they did not want to hear counter-arguments; they plugged their ears and accelerated. The lesson is to be a person of insight and deliberation, who weighs every tradition against the scale of divine guidance, even if that means walking against the current. The rushing also amplifies the tragedy: they were not slowly misled; they were sprinting towards their own destruction, thinking they were honouring their heritage.
71 | And surely most of the earlier generations had strayed before them, | The verse broadens the historical perspective to show that this phenomenon is not unique to the Quraysh. “And surely most of the earlier generations had strayed before them.” Throughout human history, the pattern has repeated: the majority of people in nation after nation fell into misguidance, worshipping idols, rejecting their messengers, and following their whims. This is a sobering reality check. The Quraysh might have felt secure in their majority status, thinking, “How could so many of our ancestors and so many nations be wrong?” The Quran answers that the majority is not a proof of truth; in fact, the majority throughout history have been astray. This is a crucial principle: truth is not established by popularity. The earlier generations include the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Lot, whose stories will soon be recounted in this very surah. Their misguidance and destruction serve as powerful signs. The verse also provides comfort to the Prophet and the small community of believers who might have felt isolated. Being a minority does not mean being wrong. The “earlier generations” (al-awwalīn) had their messengers, their warnings, and their chance, but they chose misguidance. The Quraysh are now following the same script. The believer should understand that standing for truth often means standing with the few, while falsehood enjoys the numbers. The verse encourages us to seek the company of the righteous, even if they are few, and to take warning from the fate of the misguided multitudes who came before. History is a teacher for those who reflect; the ruins and the stories are lessons that the majority path often leads to ruin when it deviates from revelation.
72 | although We had certainly sent warners among them. | To eliminate any possible excuse, the verse adds, “although We had certainly sent warners among them.” The earlier generations were not left in ignorance; Allah, in His mercy, sent messengers to warn them about the consequences of their misguidance. The “warners” are the prophets who came with clear signs and teachings, calling their people to monotheism and righteous living. The Quraysh themselves had received a warner—the Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him)—whose very presence among them was a fulfilment of this divine pattern. The phrase “certainly sent” emphasizes the consistency and justice of Allah’s approach. No nation is punished without first receiving a clear warning. This refutes any claim that the disbelievers might make on the Day of Judgment that they were unaware. The warners were sent “among them,” meaning from their own communities, speaking their language, understanding their culture, and living as examples. This made the message accessible and undeniable. The rejection of such warners is thus an act of extreme arrogance and ingratitude. The verse also serves as a reminder of the high status of prophets; they are the lifelines extended by Allah to humanity. When a people reject their warner, they sever their own connection to salvation. For the Meccan pagans, this was a direct rebuke: you have your warner among you, yet you rush after your astray forefathers. For the believer, it is a call to deeply appreciate the Prophet Muhammad and to respond to his warnings with obedience and love. The verse also indicates that the destruction of previous nations was not arbitrary but followed their deliberate rejection of the warners. This clarifies the justice behind the ruins the Quraysh passed by on their trade journeys.
73 | See then what was the end of those who had been warned. | The verse invites reflection on history with a direct command: “See then what was the end of those who had been warned.” The address is to the Prophet, the believers, and by extension the disbelievers who are being challenged to look at the evidence. The “end” refers to the final outcome and punishment that befell the previous nations after they rejected their warners. The ruins of the people of ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the towns of Lot were visible to the Meccan travellers. They would pass by these decimated remains on their journeys to Syria and Yemen. The command to “see” can be both a physical seeing and an intellectual seeing—that is, to observe and then ponder and take heed. History is not a series of random events; it is filled with divine signs showing the pattern of how Allah deals with those who reject His messengers after clear warnings. The verse directly challenges the notion that the ancestors should be blindly followed; their end was often destruction, so why emulate those who met a terrible fate? For the believer, this instills a sense of the importance of learning from history. The stories in the Quran are not mere tales but lessons to be internalized. The surah will soon recount several of these historical examples—Noah, Abraham, Moses, Lot, and others—to make the lesson concrete. The verse asks us to “see” the pattern: warnings were given, rejection followed, and then punishment descended. The only exception were those who believed. This pattern is unchangeable. It applies to individuals and nations. Thus, the believer must constantly evaluate their own response to the warnings they receive through the Quran and Sunnah. Are we heeding the warnings, or are we repeating the mistakes of those who saw the truth and turned away, only to face a terrible end?
74 | But not the chosen servants of Allah. | Once again, the surah inserts the crucial exception to the grim historical pattern. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” Among those earlier generations who received warners and were destroyed for their rejection, there was a group that was saved: the chosen servants. These are the prophets themselves and the sincere believers who followed them. When the flood drowned the people of Noah, the chosen servant Noah and the believers on the Ark were delivered. When fire was sent upon the people of Lot, the chosen servant Lot and his family—except his wife—were saved. This exception is a constant refrain in the surah, a thread that runs through the narratives of the prophets. It emphasizes that while divine punishment is certain for the rejecters, divine mercy and salvation are equally certain for the faithful. The “chosen servants” are those who purified their worship for Allah alone and held fast to the guidance brought by the messengers. This verse serves to prevent despair among the believers who live in corrupt societies. Even if the majority rushes after misguidance, the sincere minority are seen, valued, and will be delivered by their Lord. The Meccan Muslims, small in number and persecuted, could cling to this promise: they were the chosen servants of that era, just as the followers of Noah were in his. The exception also clarifies that the earlier verses about “most of the earlier generations” straying do not mean that everyone went astray without exception; Allah always preserved a remnant of truth. This is a source of hope and a call to be part of that remnant. The verse connects seamlessly to the upcoming stories, where the pattern of warning, rejection, punishment, and the salvation of the chosen servants will be illustrated in detail. It reminds the believer that the path of the chosen servants is the path of safety, and we should strive to be among them by emulating their sincerity and obedience.
75 | Indeed, Noah cried out to Us, and how excellent are We in responding! | With this verse, the surah begins a series of concise, powerful narratives about several prophets, starting with Noah. “Indeed, Noah cried out to Us, and how excellent are We in responding!” Noah is one of the greatest messengers, the first major prophet sent to a disbelieving people. He called his nation for 950 years with patience and persistence, yet they persisted in mockery and rejection. Finally, after centuries of abuse and seeing that no more would believe, Noah turned to Allah with a heartfelt supplication. He called out, asking for divine help and for the destruction of the disbelievers who had become a source of corruption. The verse does not detail the supplication here, but elsewhere in the Quran we hear his words: “My Lord, do not leave upon the earth from among the disbelievers an inhabitant.” The phrase “cried out to Us” indicates a direct, desperate, and intimate plea. It was a call born of exhaustion and complete reliance on Allah after exhausting all human effort. The second part of the verse, “how excellent are We in responding,” is a magnificent praise that Allah gives Himself for the quality of His response. It assures that Allah not only hears but answers in the best possible way. The response was swift, decisive, and perfectly suited to the situation. This teaches the believer the immense value of supplication, especially when all worldly means seem futile. Noah’s victory did not come through military might or majority support; it came through turning to Allah. The “excellent” response includes the careful planning of the Ark, the rescue of the believers, and the cleansing of the earth from rampant disbelief. The verse sets up the narrative with a focus on Allah’s attribute of perfect responsiveness, encouraging the listener to always turn to Him in times of distress, knowing that He is the best of responders.
76 | We delivered him and his family from the great distress, | The response to Noah’s cry is now summarized. “We delivered him and his family from the great distress.” The “great distress” (al-karb al-ʿaẓīm) refers to the overwhelming situation of the flood, but also to the long years of psychological distress Noah endured while being rejected and mocked by his people. When the flood waters rose and covered the mountains, it was a catastrophe of unimaginable scale. In that moment of universal drowning, Allah’s deliverance of Noah and the believers aboard the Ark was a spectacular manifestation of mercy. The mention of “his family” includes his believing wife and his three believing sons—Shem, Ham, and Japheth—and the small group of followers who believed with them. It does not include his other son who refused to board the Ark and was among the drowned, nor his wife if we consider some narrations that she was not among the saved (though the Quran does not explicitly state that about Noah’s wife; it is mentioned about Lot’s wife and Noah’s son). The main point is that Noah’s household of faith was saved. The deliverance from “great distress” echoes the recurring theme of the surah: the chosen servants are rescued from the collective punishment. This deliverance was a physical reality, but it also holds a spiritual lesson: faith in Allah is the true Ark that saves one from the floods of worldly and otherworldly punishment. The Meccan pagans, facing the Prophet’s warning of a coming judgment, are being told to board the Ark of faith before the flood of divine wrath arrives. The believer is reminded that no matter how severe the distress, Allah’s deliverance is near for those who remain faithful. The phrase “We delivered” emphasizes that salvation is entirely in Allah’s hands; human planning is only a means.
77 | and made his descendants the sole survivors. | After the flood, a new phase of human history began, and Allah highlights a critical outcome: “and made his descendants the sole survivors.” The flood was not merely a local disaster; it was a global reset in which all disbelievers were wiped out. Only Noah’s believing lineage remained. The verse states that his descendants (dhurriyyah) became the sole survivors, meaning that all human beings after the flood trace their lineage back to Noah. This is why Noah is often called the “second father” of humanity. The fact that the survivors were limited to his descendants shows that Noah’s faith became the seed of all future humanity. This was a tremendous honour granted to him in addition to his physical deliverance. It also signifies the victory of monotheism in that epoch: the earth was cleansed of idolatry, and the new civilization began on a foundation of tawheed, even though later generations gradually deviated. The Meccan pagans, who prided themselves on their noble lineage tracing back to Ishmael and Abraham, were actually descendants of Noah as well, so they were being reminded that their very existence was a product of a believer’s salvation. This placed an obligation on them to follow the faith of their forefather Noah. The phrase “sole survivors” underscores the totality of the destruction; no other group survived the flood. This magnifies the severity of the punishment that befell the rejecters and the exclusivity of the mercy for the believers. For the believer, this verse inspires the hope that even a small group of faithful can become the foundation for a righteous future. It also serves as a warning that pride in lineage is meaningless if one’s beliefs and actions contradict those of the righteous ancestors.
78 | And We blessed him ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations: | The surah now speaks of the legacy granted to Noah after his death. “And We blessed him with honourable mention among later generations.” This is a profound gift: Allah ensured that Noah would be spoken of with reverence, praise, and respect by all future nations. When anyone among Jews, Christians, or Muslims mentions Noah, they speak of him as a prophet, a righteous man, a saviour of humanity. The phrase “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) literally means a truthful and praiseworthy reputation. No matter how much his enemies mocked him during his life, his name is eternally honoured. This is a comfort for all who are ridiculed for their faith: your reputation among people is not what matters; the lasting honour granted by Allah is what counts. The Meccan pagans were calling the Prophet a mad poet, trying to tarnish his name, but Allah is promising him a similar, if not greater, honourable mention. Today, millions of believers around the world send peace and blessings upon Muhammad every day. This is the fulfillment of that pattern. The verse teaches that a person’s true legacy is not in the momentary praise of their contemporaries but in the enduring recognition that Allah bestows. The later generations include all the prophets who came after Noah and all the believers who read his story. This is part of the “excellent response” to Noah’s supplication; his name was elevated instead of being erased. For the believer, the lesson is to focus on earning Allah’s lasting praise rather than the fleeting approval of people.
79 | “Peace be upon Noah among all peoples.” | The honourable mention takes a specific, beautiful form: the divine greeting of peace. “Peace be upon Noah among all peoples.” This is the very salutation that Allah Himself bestows, and that the angels and believers extend. The phrase “Peace be upon Noah” is a timeless blessing that echoes through the heavens and the earth. It means safety, honour, and divine mercy are invoked for him forever. The addition “among all peoples” (fī al-ʿālamīn) indicates the universal scope of this peace. It is not limited to one nation or one era; it embraces all nations and all times. Noah’s peace and noble remembrance transcend every boundary. This greeting is not merely a historical note; it is part of the Islamic creed. Muslims are taught to say “peace be upon him” whenever they mention any prophet, including Noah. By sending peace upon Noah, Allah elevates him and consoles him for all the abuse he suffered during his long mission. The verse also indirectly instructs the believers to send peace upon the prophets, as Allah Himself does. In the context of the surah, which repeatedly emphasizes the distinction between the chosen servants and the wrongdoers, this peace is the ultimate gift for the chosen servant Noah. The deniers and mockers of his time perished in disgrace, while Noah sails eternally on a sea of divine peace. This should inspire the believer to seek the company of those upon whom peace descends. The verse also creates a beautiful link to the later verses in the surah that will conclude with “Peace be upon the messengers,” showing that Noah is part of this blessed brotherhood.
80 | Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers. | The narrative of Noah concludes with a principle that echoes throughout the surah: “Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers.” The deliverance, the honour, the lasting peace—all of it is the standard reward from Allah for those who excel in goodness (muḥsinīn). The “good-doers” are those who perfect their faith and their actions, who worship Allah as if they see Him, and who strive with patience and excellence in the face of trials. Noah epitomized such excellence through his 950 years of unwavering da‘wah and his ultimate trust in Allah. The verse assures that Noah’s case is not an exception; it is the rule. Every good-doer will receive a reward proportional to their sincerity and effort, both in this world and the Hereafter. The principle “this is how We reward” speaks of an unchanging divine sunnah, a law of mercy and justice that applies to all. This connects perfectly to the earlier verses about the chosen servants and the ultimate triumph. It tells the Meccan believers that their good deeds, no matter how small they seem under the shadow of persecution, are seen and will be rewarded in the same magnificent pattern. It also warns the oppressors that the opposite pattern applies: just as the good-doers are rewarded with salvation and honour, the evildoers are rewarded with destruction and disgrace. The verse serves as a transition, setting up the stories of the other prophets that follow—Abraham, Moses, Elias, Lot, and Jonah—all of whom will receive the same refrain, emphasizing the consistency of Allah’s reward. For the believer, this verse is a powerful motivator: become a muḥsin, a good-doer, and the same Lord who rewarded Noah will reward you in ways that far exceed your imagination.
81 | ˹For˺ he was truly one of Our faithful servants. | After declaring that this is how Allah rewards the good-doers, the surah adds a final commendation of Noah that explains why he deserved such a magnificent reward. The verse states, “For he was truly one of Our faithful servants.” The word “truly” emphasizes the certainty and depth of this quality. Noah did not merely perform acts of worship; he was a servant through and through, in every dimension of his being. The phrase “faithful servants” translates the Arabic “ʿibādinā al-muʾminīn,” which combines servitude with faith. Faithfulness here means he was a true believer whose belief permeated his actions, his patience, and his complete reliance on Allah. This description is the highest honour a human can receive. It signifies that Noah’s entire life was oriented around obedience to his Lord. He was not a servant of his own desires, nor of public opinion, nor of his tribe. When his people mocked him for building an ark on dry land, his faithfulness kept him steady. When his own son refused to believe and was drowned, Noah’s faithfulness led him to accept Allah’s decree without protest. The verse teaches that the ultimate distinction is not lineage, wealth, or worldly success, but being counted among the faithful servants of Allah. This title connects Noah directly to the “chosen servants” mentioned repeatedly in the surah, those who are exempted from punishment and granted paradise. For the believers of Mecca, who were being told by the pagans that they had abandoned the religion of their forefathers, this verse reassures them that their new identity as faithful servants of Allah places them in the noblest lineage of all—the lineage of Noah, Abraham, and all the prophets. The verse also prepares the ground for the story of Abraham, who is described in the next verses as one who followed Noah’s way. Faithfulness is thus shown to be a continuous chain linking all true believers across history. Every believer should ask themselves whether their life reflects this quality of being a truly faithful servant, not merely a cultural or nominal one. The reward of such faithfulness is not just deliverance in this world, but eternal honour.
82 | Then We drowned the others. | The verse closes the account of Noah with a brief, stark statement that contrasts sharply with the honour given to the faithful servant. “Then We drowned the others.” There is no lengthy description here, as the surah’s focus is on the pattern and the lesson, not on graphic detail of the punishment itself, which has already been touched upon. “The others” refers to all those who rejected Noah’s call, including his own son who chose to seek refuge on a mountain instead of boarding the ark. The drowning was a total, comprehensive punishment that wiped out an entire civilization of disbelievers. The simplicity of the phrase, set against the elaborate praise of Noah, drives home the reality of divine justice. Those who are not among the chosen servants, who persist in disbelief and mockery, will face an end that is swift and final. The water, which could have been a source of life and mercy, became an instrument of destruction by Allah’s command. In the context of the Meccan pagans who were being warned of a similar punishment—perhaps a drought, a military defeat, or the ultimate Day of Judgment—this brief verse carries a heavy threat. They are the “others” of their time unless they embrace the message brought by the Prophet. The verse also highlights a recurring Quranic theme: the separation between truth and falsehood. The flood physically separated Noah and his followers from the disbelievers, and that separation became eternal. The believer learns that there is no middle ground; one is either with the faithful servants or among the others. The drowning of the others was not an act of cruelty but the necessary consequence of their sustained rebellion and their rejection of the clear warning. Noah called them for centuries, but they chose their path. The lesson is to never assume that Allah’s patience means indifference. The punishment, when it comes, is decisive. The verse also serves as a transition to the next prophet, Abraham, who lived in a later generation and faced his own people’s idolatry with the same faith that Noah embodied.
83 | And indeed, one of those who followed his way was Abraham. | The surah now introduces the next great prophet, Abraham, linking him directly to Noah. “And indeed, one of those who followed his way was Abraham.” The phrase “his way” can be interpreted in two beautiful ways. The pronoun “his” refers to Noah, meaning that Abraham was a follower of Noah’s religious path—the path of pure monotheism, patience, and calling to Allah. Alternatively, some scholars read it as referring to the Prophet Muhammad, meaning Abraham was of his spiritual party, a predecessor who shared his core message. Both interpretations are valid and harmonious. The point is that the struggle for tawheed is a single continuous movement across history. Abraham is not presented as an innovator of a new religion but as a link in the chain of faithful servants. This was a powerful argument against the Meccan pagans who claimed Abraham as their ancestor and the founder of their religion. The Quran corrects them: Abraham’s true way was the worship of Allah alone, not the idolatry they practiced. By saying “one of those who followed his way,” the verse also indicates that there were other believers between Noah and Abraham whose names are not mentioned but who kept the flame of monotheism alive. This continuity is a source of comfort for the believers: they are not a lonely minority in history but part of a vast brotherhood of faithful servants. The verse elevates Abraham’s status while humbling him as a follower of a previous messenger, showing that all prophets are united in their mission. For the believer, this teaches the importance of seeing oneself as part of an ummah that stretches back to the beginning of humanity. We should feel a deep connection with Noah’s patience and Abraham’s uncompromising faith. The introduction of Abraham also prepares the ground for the detailed scenes of his debate with his people, his destruction of the idols, and his great sacrifice, all of which will be recounted in the following verses to illustrate the pattern of a chosen servant facing a hostile, idolatrous society.
84 | ˹Remember˺ when he came to his Lord with a pure heart. | The narrative now focuses on Abraham’s personal spiritual state. “Remember when he came to his Lord with a pure heart.” The command “Remember” is addressed to the Prophet and the believers, calling them to reflect on Abraham’s exemplary approach to Allah. The phrase “came to his Lord” does not refer to a physical journey but to the orientation of his entire being towards Allah. It is a coming of devotion, sincerity, and submission. Abraham turned away from the falsehood of his people and directed his soul wholly towards his Creator. The quality that defined this approach is “a pure heart” (qalb salīm). A pure heart, in Quranic terminology, is one that is free from shirk, doubt, hypocrisy, and love of evil. It is a heart that loves what Allah loves and hates what Allah hates, a heart that is untainted by the diseases of arrogance, envy, and worldly attachment. Abraham’s heart was pure because he had cleansed it from the idolatry of his father and people through his deep contemplation and his innate recognition of the One God. The purity of his heart was the foundation for all his subsequent actions, from his fearless debate with his people to his willingness to sacrifice his son. This verse teaches that Allah looks at hearts, not outward appearances. The journey to Allah begins with cleansing the heart. For the Meccan pagans whose hearts were filled with tribal pride and attachment to idols, this was a call to purify themselves. For the believers, it is a reminder that the ultimate goal of all worship is to present oneself before Allah with a heart that is sound and pure. The concept of qalb salīm is so central that the Quran describes the Day of Judgment as a day when neither wealth nor children will benefit, except one who comes to Allah with a pure heart. Abraham achieved this, and thus he became a beloved friend of Allah, worthy of mention in the divine scripture forever.
85 | and said to his father and his people, “What are you worshipping? | With a pure heart, Abraham could not remain silent about the falsehood around him. The verse describes his da‘wah: “and said to his father and his people, ‘What are you worshipping?’” Abraham began with his own father, Azar, who was a maker and seller of idols, and then extended his call to the wider community. The question “What are you worshipping?” is a rhetorical challenge designed to make them reflect on the absurdity of their practices. It is not a request for information; Abraham knew very well what they worshipped. The question invites them to articulate their beliefs so that their irrationality becomes evident. By asking this, Abraham places the burden of proof on the idolaters. He forces them to consider the worthiness of the objects they devote themselves to. In the Meccan context, the Prophet was doing the same: asking his people what they worshipped and why, when the Lord of the heavens and earth is the only One deserving of worship. The mention of “his father” shows that the call to truth must start with one’s closest relatives, even if they are deeply entrenched in falsehood. It also demonstrates that blood relations do not override the obligation to speak the truth. Abraham’s approach is both compassionate and bold; he desires to save his father and his people from the fire, so he confronts their core error directly. For the believer, this verse is a model of da‘wah: begin with what is closest, ask thought-provoking questions, and address the fundamental issue of worship. The pure heart of Abraham compelled him to speak, and his speech was not aggressive but reasoned, as the next verses will show. It is a lesson that true faithfulness often requires challenging the deeply held beliefs of one’s own family and community with wisdom and courage.
86 | Is it false gods that you desire instead of Allah? | Abraham continues his questioning, sharpening the focus. “Is it false gods that you desire instead of Allah?” The phrase “false gods” translates a word that implies fabricated deities, things that have no true divinity but are given divine status out of human desire and falsehood. The question contrasts these false gods with Allah, the One true God. The key word is “desire” (tamānan). It exposes the psychological root of idolatry: it is not based on reason or evidence but on whim, desire, and wishful thinking. They desire these gods either because their forefathers worshipped them, or because they serve their worldly interests and lusts. There is no intellectual basis for their worship; it is simply what they prefer. This is a profound insight into human psychology. Often, people reject the truth not because of a lack of evidence but because accepting it would require them to abandon their desires and the social structures that benefit them. The Meccan pagans profited from the pilgrimage to the idols, and their tribal identity was tied to the idols. Their “desire” was for the status quo. Abraham’s question strips away all pretenses and exposes the emptiness of their worship. For the modern believer, this verse is a mirror: what are we truly desiring and prioritizing in our lives? Do we have false gods—money, status, ego—that we desire more than Allah? Idolatry is not limited to stone statues; anything that takes the place of Allah in one’s heart becomes a false god. Abraham’s challenge is timeless: examine your priorities and see if your desires have led you away from the pure worship of the Creator. The rhetorical nature of the question also implies that no reasonable person would openly admit they desire falsehood over truth, thus cornering them into self-reflection or defensive arrogance.
87 | What then do you expect from the Lord of all worlds?” | Having challenged their worship, Abraham shifts their attention to the true God with another powerful question. “What then do you expect from the Lord of all worlds?” This is an appeal to their innate sense of justice and consequence. Abraham is saying: if you reject the true God, the Lord of all creation, what do you think His reaction will be? Do you expect Him to ignore your rebellion? Do you think He will not hold you accountable? The phrase “Lord of all worlds” emphasizes Allah’s absolute sovereignty over everything. He is not a tribal deity or a local god; He is the Master of the entire universe. To worship others besides Him is a direct affront to His lordship. The question implies that a terrible reckoning awaits those who persist in such an affront. It is a warning wrapped in a question, inviting them to consider the consequences of their actions. Abraham’s approach is logical: first establish the absurdity of idol worship, then establish the reality of the Creator, and then point to the inevitable judgment. This matches the structure of the entire surah, which begins with oaths to establish the truth of monotheism, then argues for resurrection and judgment, and then presents the fates of the believers and disbelievers. Abraham, in his brief speech, summarized the entire Quranic message. For the believer, this verse inspires a sense of accountability. We should constantly ask ourselves: what do we expect from the Lord of all worlds? If we disobey Him, do we not fear His punishment? If we obey, do we not hope for His reward? The Lord of all worlds is not indifferent; He is just and powerful. Abraham’s question is left hanging in the air, unanswered by his people, because they have no coherent answer. Their silence reveals their intellectual bankruptcy. This verse teaches the da‘wah technique of turning the attention of the heedless towards their Lord and His impending judgment.
88 | He later looked up to the stars ˹in contemplation˺, | The surah now recounts a specific incident in Abraham’s life that demonstrated both his wisdom and his strategy. “He later looked up to the stars in contemplation.” This occurred after his debate with his people, likely during a festival when they were preparing to leave the city. Abraham, who was known for his intelligence, looked up at the sky. The phrase “in contemplation” indicates that he was not merely gazing but thinking and planning. Some scholars explain that his people were astrologers and star-worshippers, so Abraham used their own frame of reference as a strategy. He looked at the stars and then, as the next verse says, declared that he was sick, using the observation of the stars as a pretext that they would understand. His looking at the stars was not an act of worship or divination; it was a legitimate means of deduction and planning, using the knowledge of the celestial bodies that Allah created. Others interpret that his looking at the stars was a sign of his natural contemplation of the heavens, leading him to realize the Creator, but in this context, it was part of a ruse to stay behind while his people went to their festival, so he could destroy their idols. This event shows that sometimes wisdom requires using strategies that are permissible and intelligent to achieve a greater good. Abraham did not lie about being sick; he made a statement that was ambiguous but true in a certain sense, or it was a form of ma‘āriḍ (indirect speech) to avoid direct lying while still achieving a righteous aim. The verse teaches that a believer should be thoughtful and clever, using his intellect in the service of faith, while never compromising on truth and sincerity. Abraham’s pure heart did not prevent him from being strategic; rather, his pure heart guided his strategy for the sake of Allah alone.
89 | then said, “I am really sick.” | Following his contemplation of the stars, Abraham made a statement to his people: “I am really sick.” This declaration was part of his plan to avoid going with them to their pagan festival, thus giving him the opportunity to confront their idols alone. Scholars have debated the nature of this statement. It was not a lie. Some say he meant he was sick at heart due to their idolatry, as the Quran elsewhere describes the hypocrites as having a disease in their hearts. Others say he was physically unwell, perhaps with a fever, and that was true. Still others note that he used a form of speech that was technically true but intended to be understood by them as a physical illness that prevented him from attending the celebration. The pagan custom was to go out to a feast, and Abraham claimed weakness so he could stay behind. The key lesson here is not that deception is permissible in all cases, but that in the face of idolatry and the potential to eradicate falsehood, a believer may use legitimate indirect means that do not involve committing a sin. Abraham’s goal was to demonstrate the powerlessness of the idols by destroying them, thereby making a profound point to his people. Staying behind was necessary for that. The statement “I am really sick” must be understood in its context. The most cautious scholarly view is that he was genuinely sick in some sense—perhaps sick of their shirk—and the words he chose conveyed a truth while serving his righteous purpose. This incident is a lesson in balancing truthfulness with wisdom. The believer should always speak truth, but sometimes the full context allows for statements that serve a higher good without harming anyone. Abraham’s pure heart and his desire to guide his people justified his action, and Allah recorded it in the Quran without censure, showing divine approval of his overall conduct.
90 | So they turned their backs on him and went away. | Abraham’s statement had its intended effect. “So they turned their backs on him and went away.” His people, thinking he was physically sick or weak, left him behind in the city while they went off to their festival. The phrase “turned their backs on him” indicates that they abandoned him without care or concern. They did not investigate his condition further; they simply left him alone. This neglect gave Abraham the perfect opportunity to execute his plan. The verse highlights how the disbelievers were heedless and arrogant; they did not take Abraham seriously. They probably thought he was just a sick young man with strange ideas. This underestimation is a recurring theme: the forces of falsehood often dismiss the believers as weak or irrelevant, only to find that truth has a power they cannot comprehend. The turning of their backs also symbolizes their turning away from the truth Abraham represented. They were more interested in their festival and their idols than in listening to his message. For the believer, this verse offers hope: sometimes, when it seems that opponents are ignoring you or walking away, that very neglect may be part of Allah’s plan to give you space to strengthen your faith and take positive action. Abraham used the solitude to confront the idols directly. The lesson is to be patient and perceptive; Allah can create openings in unexpected ways. The Meccan pagans similarly turned their backs on the Prophet, mocking him and going about their business. This verse foreshadows that their neglect will not last forever, and the truth will eventually confront them when they least expect it. Abraham’s solitary stand is a model for all believers who find themselves alone in a society dominated by falsehood.
91 | Then he ˹stealthily˺ advanced towards their gods, and said ˹mockingly˺, “Will you not eat ˹your offerings˺? | With the people gone, Abraham took action. “Then he stealthily advanced towards their gods, and said mockingly, ‘Will you not eat your offerings?’” The stealth indicates that he went quietly and deliberately, perhaps to avoid any remaining guards, though the temples were likely empty. He approached the idols that had been set up, with food offerings placed before them as a sign of devotion. The pagans believed their gods consumed or blessed the offerings. Abraham, knowing these idols were lifeless stone and wood, addressed them with biting sarcasm. He asked them why they were not eating the food sitting right in front of them. The mockery was not for the sake of mockery itself; it was a pedagogical method to expose the absurdity of idol worship. By speaking to the idols as if they were alive, he highlighted the contradiction: their worshippers treat them as gods, yet they cannot perform the simplest act of a living being, like eating. This method of using irony and rhetorical questions is a legitimate form of da‘wah when dealing with clear falsehood, as it forces the audience to think. Abraham’s pure heart did not prevent him from using sharp wit; rather, his wit was a tool in service of truth. The Meccan pagans similarly placed offerings before their idols in the Kaaba. The Prophet later destroyed those idols, following in the footsteps of his father Abraham. For the believer, this verse teaches that confronting falsehood sometimes requires bold, dramatic actions that capture attention and challenge the status quo. However, such actions must be guided by wisdom and the right timing, as Abraham’s was. His question, “Will you not eat?” remains unanswered, highlighting the lifelessness of false gods.
92 | What is wrong with you that you cannot speak?” | Abraham continued his mock interrogation of the idols. “What is wrong with you that you cannot speak?” After asking them about eating, he now questioned their muteness. The inability to speak is a clear proof of lack of life, intellect, and power. A god who cannot answer a simple question or defend itself is no god at all. The question “What is wrong with you?” is a rhetorical device that exposes the defect of the idols. It suggests that there is something fundamentally broken about these so-called deities. Abraham knew they could not speak, but by publicly asking them, he was building a case that would later be presented to his people. The silence of the idols would condemn them and their worshippers. In the Quranic account in Surah Al-Anbiya, when the people returned and found their gods smashed, they asked Abraham if he did it. He replied, “Rather, this one, the biggest of them, did it. Ask them, if they can speak.” That beautiful retort used the same principle: if the idols can speak, they can identify the culprit; if not, why worship them? The lesson for the believer is clear: anything worshipped besides Allah is mute, deaf, and powerless. Whether it is an idol, a human leader, wealth, or desire, none of them can truly speak, guide, or benefit. They are silent in the face of real need. Abraham’s question is a timeless challenge to every false object of worship. It also demonstrates the use of logic and humour in da‘wah. Rather than delivering a dry lecture, Abraham set up a scenario that made the absurdity of idol worship vividly obvious. The Meccan pagans hearing this would recognize the force of his argument and the shame of their own similar practices. The believer is encouraged to use reason and clarity when explaining tawheed.
93 | Then he swiftly turned on them, striking ˹them˺ with his right hand. | After verbally mocking the idols, Abraham escalated his action to a physical demonstration of their helplessness. “Then he swiftly turned on them, striking them with his right hand.” The word “swiftly” indicates a sudden, decisive movement. He did not hesitate or waver; he acted with full conviction. He struck the idols, likely using an axe or a tool, breaking them into pieces. The use of “his right hand” is significant: the right hand symbolizes strength, honour, and righteous action. Abraham employed his strongest hand for this act of destroying falsehood, showing his complete commitment. He left only the largest idol untouched, as we know from other surahs, to later use it as a lesson. The act of striking the idols was not mere vandalism; it was a prophetic act of iconoclasm, a physical removal of spiritual corruption. Abraham, as a prophet, had the authority to do this, guided by divine wisdom. His action demonstrated that idols are not just false in theory but vulnerable in practice; they cannot defend themselves against even a single man. This visual proof was far more impactful than a hundred speeches. The Meccan believers, who were longing to see the Kaaba cleansed of idols, could find in Abraham’s action a powerful precedent. The lesson for believers is not to go around destroying property in contemporary pluralistic societies, as the legal and prophetic context differs, but to understand the principle: falsehood must be challenged actively, not just passively. The idols in our lives—the ideologies, the material attachments, the ego—must be “struck” with the right hand of sincere faith and decisive action. Abraham’s courage is a model: once the truth is clear, one should act upon it with strength, not remain in a state of passive acceptance of surrounding falsehood.
94 | Later, his people came rushing towards him ˹furiously˺. | News of the destroyed idols reached the people, and they reacted with predictable rage. “Later, his people came rushing towards him furiously.” The festival ended, and they returned to find their sacred idols shattered into pieces, save for the largest one. The sight filled them with anger and humiliation. The word “rushing” indicates a chaotic, emotional stampede. They did not come calmly to investigate; they came in a fury, ready to punish the one who had insulted their gods. Their anger reveals the deep psychological hold the idols had on them, despite their utter powerlessness. They had been publicly embarrassed, and their instinct was to retaliate with violence rather than to reflect on the truth that Abraham’s action demonstrated. The phrase “his people” shows that the entire community was united in their opposition to Abraham. He stood alone against a furious mob. This is the station of the prophets: solitary truth-bearers facing the collective wrath of misguidance. The verse reflects the experience of the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, where the Quraysh would rush to persecute him and his followers whenever their idols were criticized. For the believer, this verse is a reminder that speaking truth and dismantling falsehood often provokes intense backlash. One should not expect a calm, rational response from those whose vested interests and deep-seated beliefs are challenged. The rush of the people shows that they had no rational counter-argument; their only response was emotional rage. Abraham’s courage is contrasted with the mob’s irrationality. The believer must prepare psychologically for such reactions and remain steadfast, trusting that the truth will ultimately prevail, just as it did for Abraham. The verse also shows that the destruction of idols was the catalyst that forced the issue, moving the confrontation from debate to a decisive showdown that would eventually prove Abraham’s prophethood.
95 | He argued, “How can you worship what you carve ˹with your own hands˺, | When the furious people confronted Abraham, he did not panic or retreat. Instead, he seized the moment to deliver his ultimate argument. “He argued, ‘How can you worship what you carve with your own hands?’” This is the crowning logical blow. Abraham pointed out the simple, undeniable fact that the idols were man-made. They were products of human craftsmanship, carved from wood or stone by the very hands of the worshippers themselves. A thing that is created by human hands cannot possibly be the creator of humans or the controller of the universe. The argument is devastating in its simplicity: a maker is superior to what he makes. If you fashion an idol with your own hands, how can you then bow down to it and expect it to benefit or harm you? The word “carve” emphasizes the artistic, manual process, reducing the idols from objects of reverence to mere human artefacts. This verse exposes the absurdity of all forms of human-made religion, where people create concepts or objects and then enslave themselves to them. For the Meccan pagans who intricately carved their idols from dates or stone, Abraham’s argument was directly applicable. It stripped away any mystical awe surrounding the idols and revealed them as lifeless lumps of matter. For the believer, this argument is a timeless proof of tawheed: Allah alone is the Uncreated Creator, while everything else, including the most impressive human constructions, is created and subordinate. Abraham’s rational, fearless debate in front of a hostile crowd is an inspiration. He used a single, undeniable premise—you carve these things—to demolish their entire belief system. It teaches that the truth is inherently powerful and simple, requiring no complex philosophy, just clear thinking and the courage to state it.
96 | when it is Allah Who created you and whatever you do?” | Abraham extends the argument from the specific to the universal. “When it is Allah Who created you and whatever you do?” Not only are the idols made by them, but the very people who made the idols, along with all their actions, are created by Allah. The phrase “whatever you do” can be understood as including the carving of the idols themselves. Thus, even the act of making the false gods is under Allah’s creative power and permission. The Creator of the universe is the one who gave humans the hands, the intelligence, and the materials to carve. So how can they take what ultimately belongs to Allah and use it to defy Him? This verse establishes the ultimate basis of worship: gratitude and recognition of the Creator. Worship should be directed only to the One who originated our existence and sustains it. Abraham’s logic connects the creation of the heavens and the earth, the creation of human beings, and the creation of their actions, all pointing to the absolute sovereignty of Allah. It is a profound theological statement that refutes not only idol worship but also any belief that humans are fully autonomous. Everything we do, including our so-called creations, is within the domain of Allah’s creation and will. This humbles human pride and directs all praise to the true Doer. For the Meccan pagans, who acknowledged Allah as the supreme Creator but associated partners with Him, this argument closed any loophole. If Allah is your Creator and the Creator of your deeds, He alone deserves your worship, not the pieces of stone you shape. For the believer, this verse fosters a deep awareness of Allah’s encompassing power, inspiring reliance on Him and humility. It also serves as a response to modern forms of idolatry where human achievements are glorified, forgetting that the ability to achieve itself comes from Allah. Abraham’s speech is the essence of tawheed al-rubūbiyyah leading necessarily to tawheed al-ulūhiyyah.
97 | They said ˹to one another˺, “Build him a furnace and cast him into the blazing fire.” | Confronted with an argument they could not refute, the people resorted to brute force. “They said to one another, ‘Build him a furnace and cast him into the blazing fire.’” The logical debate was over; their only response was to kill Abraham. They conspired among themselves, inciting each other to take this drastic action. The “furnace” (bunyān) here refers to a great structure or pit in which a massive fire would be kindled. This was to be a public execution, a spectacle to deter anyone else from challenging their gods. The phrase “blazing fire” emphasizes the intensity of the punishment they planned. This is a classic reaction of falsehood when cornered: it turns to violence to silence the truth. The Quraysh listening to this would see a direct parallel to their own treatment of the Prophet, whom they also tried to kill. The verse reassures the believers that the threat of fire is nothing against the protection of Allah. Abraham’s people thought they could eradicate his message by burning him, but they forgot that the God Abraham called upon is the Lord of the fire and everything else. Their conspiracy shows that they recognized the existential threat Abraham posed to their system; they had no answer, so they sought to eliminate the questioner. For the believer, this verse is a lesson that the path of truth often leads through extreme danger, and the forces of falsehood will not hesitate to use violence. Yet, the outcome is always in Allah’s hands. The verse also highlights the wickedness of a society that would rather burn a man alive than reconsider its inherited beliefs. Abraham’s calm in the face of this sentence, not recorded here but known from tradition—he said, “Allah is sufficient for me, and He is the best disposer of affairs”—demonstrates ultimate trust. The believer should cultivate such trust, knowing that when all human means fail, Allah’s help is certain.
98 | And so they sought to harm him, but We made them inferior. | The verse summarizes the outcome of their plot with majestic brevity. “And so they sought to harm him, but We made them inferior.” They intended to burn Abraham and thereby prove their gods’ superiority and his weakness. Their plot was elaborate and their conviction was strong. However, Allah’s intervention turned the tables completely. The fire was kindled, and Abraham was cast into it. But Allah commanded the fire, “O fire, be coolness and safety upon Abraham.” The fire lost its nature and became a garden of peace for him. The people watched in astonishment as their greatest effort to destroy him resulted in a miracle that exposed their impotence. The phrase “We made them inferior” means that Allah humiliated them and gave victory to Abraham. They were the ones who were shown to be weak and defeated, not Abraham. Their attempt to assert dominance backfired, and Abraham emerged with even greater honour and credibility. This is the divine sunnah for all who oppose the prophets: they plot, but Allah plans, and Allah is the best of planners. The verse serves as a direct message of hope to the Prophet and the believers in Mecca, who were facing the Quraysh’s plots. It reassures them that no matter how powerful the opposition seems, Allah can make them inferior in an instant. The fire of persecution cannot harm a believer unless Allah wills it. For the believer, this verse is a source of immense comfort and courage. When one is faced with threats, bullying, or oppression for the sake of faith, one should remember Abraham and know that the harm intended by the enemies can be transformed into a source of coolness and safety by the One who controls all elements. The inferiority of the disbelievers is not just a future event in the Hereafter; it can manifest in this world as a sign of divine support for His faithful servants.
99 | He later said, “I am leaving ˹in obedience˺ to my Lord. He will guide me. | After his miraculous deliverance from the fire and the continued rejection of his people, Abraham made a momentous decision. “He later said, ‘I am leaving in obedience to my Lord. He will guide me.’” Abraham chose hijrah—emigration for the sake of Allah. When a land becomes hostile to faith and its people persist in disbelief despite clear signs, the prophets and righteous are permitted to leave. Abraham’s departure was not an act of cowardice but of obedience. He recognized that his mission in that particular place had reached its limit, and Allah had a greater plan for him elsewhere. The phrase “in obedience to my Lord” shows that his migration was an act of worship, done at Allah’s command or in pursuit of a place where he could freely worship Allah. He did not leave out of frustration or despair; he left with complete trust in divine guidance. The statement “He will guide me” reflects his certainty that Allah would direct him to a land of blessing and a community of righteousness. Indeed, he journeyed from Ur or Babylon to the blessed land of Syria and Palestine. This hijrah paved the way for the birth of a great nation of monotheists from his descendants. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this verse was a clear parallel and a prophecy of his own upcoming hijrah to Medina. The Meccan believers were also later commanded to emigrate from the persecution of the Quraysh. The lesson for believers is that sometimes the best form of worship is to physically remove oneself from an environment of sin and oppression in order to preserve faith and build a new, God-conscious community. Abraham’s emigration was not the end; it was a new beginning. The trust that “He will guide me” is the motto of the muhājir. It teaches us to step into the unknown for Allah’s sake, confident that the One who commands the migration will also provide the destination and the path.
100 | My Lord! Bless me with righteous offspring.” | Upon his departure, or perhaps during his journey or settlement, Abraham turned to his Lord with a heartfelt supplication. “My Lord! Bless me with righteous offspring.” This is the prayer of a man who, despite his old age and his barren wife Sarah, hoped in Allah’s mercy. He asked not just for any child, but for a righteous one—a child who would be among the ṣāliḥīn, those who are virtuous and obedient to Allah. Abraham’s primary concern was not lineage or personal comfort but the continuation of the message of tawheed. He wanted an heir who would carry the banner of monotheism after him. This supplication reflects the deep foresight of a prophet. He had left his people and his father; he now needed a new family of believers. This prayer was answered with the gift of Ishmael and later Isaac. The story of the sacrifice will soon follow, showing how that righteous offspring proved his worth. The verse teaches the importance of prioritizing righteousness in our children over worldly success. Many people pray for children who are healthy, wealthy, or successful, but the most important quality is righteousness. Abraham’s prayer is a model for all parents. It also demonstrates that while one must take means, the ultimate source of righteous children is Allah’s blessing. Abraham was a prophet, yet he still supplicated humbly for this gift. The word “bless me” (hab lī) implies a gift, something freely bestowed by divine grace. The believer learns that even the most pious cannot guarantee the righteousness of their offspring; they must constantly turn to Allah in prayer. This verse sets the stage for the beautiful narrative of Ishmael’s birth, his father’s dream, and the great test of sacrifice that both father and son would undergo, demonstrating the fruit of that righteous offspring.
101 | So We gave him good news of a forbearing son. | Abraham’s heartfelt supplication for a righteous offspring was answered in the most beautiful way. “So We gave him good news of a forbearing son.” The good news was delivered by angels, as detailed in other surahs, who visited Abraham in human form and announced the birth of a boy. This was a moment of immense joy for the aged prophet who had left his homeland and had no child to inherit his prophetic mission. The son is described as “forbearing” (ḥalīm), a quality that combines patience, gentleness, intelligence, and the ability to control anger. Forbearance is one of the most beloved traits to Allah, and it is particularly fitting for a prophet and a son who would be tested. This description of the son, who is Ishmael according to the predominant view, foreshadows the great test of sacrifice that would soon come. A forbearing son would accept the divine command with patience and submission, just as his father would. The word “forbearing” also indicates that the boy would grow into a man of remarkable emotional maturity, a leader among his people. The good news was given to Abraham and, through the mention in the Quran, to all believers, showing that Allah’s mercy responds to sincere prayer even when all material circumstances suggest impossibility. Sarah, Abraham’s wife, was barren and old, yet the promise was fulfilled. This teaches that no situation is beyond Allah’s power. For the Meccan believers, many of whom were separated from their families or had lost children, this verse was a comfort that Allah can bestow the gift of righteous offspring in the most unexpected ways. It also set the stage for the story of the great sacrifice, demonstrating that the birth of Ishmael was not just a personal joy but a crucial part of divine planning for the future of monotheism. The forbearing son would be the forefather of the Prophet Muhammad, linking the Meccan pagans’ lineage to this tested and faithful servant of Allah.
102 | Then when the boy reached the age to work with him, Abraham said, “O my dear son! I have seen in a dream that I ˹must˺ sacrifice you. So tell me what you think.” He replied, “O my dear father! Do as you are commanded. Allah willing, you will find me steadfast.” | The narrative now moves to one of the most profound moments in the Quran: the test of sacrifice. “Then when the boy reached the age to work with him,” meaning Ishmael had grown old enough to walk alongside his father, assist in daily tasks, and engage in meaningful conversation. The affection between them had deepened through years of shared life and waiting. At this pinnacle of fatherly love, the test arrived. Abraham said, “O my dear son! I have seen in a dream that I must sacrifice you.” The phrase “my dear son” (yā bunayya) is an expression of the utmost tenderness in Arabic. Abraham does not hide the command but shares it gently, showing the transparency and trust between them. The dream of a prophet is a form of revelation; it is a command from Allah, not a random thought. Abraham knew that the dream was true and must be acted upon, yet he asked, “So tell me what you think.” This was not a request for permission, as the command was divine and binding, but an inquiry to prepare his son’s heart and to test his willingness. It also demonstrates the Islamic principle of shura (consultation) even in the most difficult matters. The son’s reply is nothing short of heroic: “O my dear father! Do as you are commanded. Allah willing, you will find me steadfast.” The son mirrored his father’s tenderness and added absolute submission. The phrase “do as you are commanded” shows that he recognized the dream as a divine order. Then he added, “Allah willing, you will find me steadfast,” invoking Allah’s name and promising patience. This is the hallmark of the forbearing son: immediate acceptance, trust in Allah, and a firm resolve not to waver. The entire exchange is a masterclass in faith, parenting, and communication. There is no argument, no panic, only serene submission. The believer learns that true righteousness in offspring means raising them to love Allah above all else, so that even the most daunting command is met with “do as you are commanded.” This test prefigured the tests the Meccan Muslims would face, and it remains a timeless model of sacrifice and family unity in obedience to the Creator.
103 | Then when they submitted ˹to Allah’s Will˺, and Abraham laid him on the side of his forehead ˹for sacrifice˺, | The verse describes the actual execution of the command, capturing the intense emotional and physical moment. “Then when they submitted to Allah’s Will,” meaning both father and son had fully surrendered their wills to the divine decree. The word “submitted” (aslamā) is from the same root as Islam, indicating that this act was the very essence of Islam: complete and peaceful submission. There was no internal resistance left. “And Abraham laid him on the side of his forehead for sacrifice.” Abraham placed his beloved son down, positioning him so that the forehead—the most noble part of the face—was on the ground, perhaps to avoid seeing his son’s eyes during the act or as a gesture of complete prostration. The forehead touching the earth symbolized the son’s humility before Allah even at the moment of sacrifice. This scene is incredibly poignant. Abraham, the affectionate father, had sharpened the knife, and the son lay still. The submission was not a theoretical belief but a physical act carried to its final step. Everything was in place for the slaughter. This demonstrates that faith is not merely sentiment but action. Both of them passed the test internally and externally. The early Muslims, some of whom were tortured or killed for their faith, could find in this image the ultimate example of holding nothing back for Allah. The verse also refutes any notion that the command was symbolic only; the physical action was required to prove their sincerity. It teaches that when Allah commands, the believer does not hesitate even when the command seems to contradict human affection or logic. The son’s willingness to be laid down and Abraham’s willingness to perform the act highlight the unique relationship they had with their Lord. The forehead placed on the ground was a silent testimony that the greatest love is for the One who gave life.
104 | We called out to him, “O Abraham! | At the very precipice of the sacrifice, divine intervention arrived. “We called out to him, ‘O Abraham!’” The call came directly from Allah, either through a voice or through the angel Gabriel, halting the act at its final moment. The fact that Allah called out by name—“O Abraham!”—is a mark of intimacy and honour. It is a personal address that conveyed immediate comfort and recognition. Abraham, knife in hand, fully intent on completing the command, heard his Lord’s voice calling him. The call came after the submission was proven, not before. The test was not to actually shed the son’s blood but to demonstrate the willingness to do so for Allah’s sake. Once that willingness was fully actualized, the objective was achieved. This teaches that Allah does not burden a soul beyond its capacity, and that the culmination of a test is often immediate relief and divine nearness. The call “O Abraham!” resonated through the valley, transforming a scene of potential tragedy into one of triumph and divine friendship. The use of the intimate address also echoes the earlier relationship between Allah and Abraham, the khalīl (friend) of Allah. The believers in Mecca, who were being called to sacrifice their tribal ties and worldly attachments for Islam, would take immense solace from this moment. The same Lord who stopped the knife for Abraham sees their struggles and will provide relief in due time. The call reminds every believer that Allah is fully aware of the difficulty of the test and that He is closer than one’s jugular vein. The moment of greatest trial is often the moment just before the greatest relief.
105 | You have already fulfilled the vision.” | The divine call continued with words of confirmation and release. “You have already fulfilled the vision.” This statement is profoundly merciful. Allah declared that the purpose of the dream had been accomplished without the actual death of the son. Abraham had translated the vision into action to the fullest extent of his human ability. He did not hesitate or hold back; thus, in the sight of Allah, the vision was fulfilled. The word “vision” (ruʾyā) reaffirms that it was a prophetic dream, a true form of revelation. The fulfillment was in the sincere effort and complete submission, not in the spilling of blood. This established a principle in Islamic theology: Allah’s commands are achieved by the sincerity of the heart and the effort of the limbs, and He may, out of His mercy, substitute the original requirement once the test is passed. The phrase also removes any doubt that Abraham might have failed or that the command was somehow invalid. The reassurance “you have already fulfilled” soothed Abraham’s heart, which was undoubtedly aching at the thought of harming his beloved son. The test was graded and passed with distinction. For the believer, this is a source of immense hope. The struggles we go through for the sake of Allah, the sacrifices we make, are seen and accepted even if the outcome is different from what we expected. The vision was fulfilled because the essence of worship—unconditional obedience—was perfected. This verse is a capstone on the lesson of the sacrifice, teaching that the ultimate aim of all divine commands is to purify the soul and deepen its connection to the Commander, and that aim was brilliantly achieved on that hillside.
106 | Indeed, that was truly a revealing test. | After affirming the fulfillment, the Quran comments on the nature of the event. “Indeed, that was truly a revealing test.” The word “test” (balāʾ) indicates a trial that distinguishes the true from the false, the sincere from the pretender. The test was “revealing” (mubīn) because it made manifest the extraordinary degree of faith possessed by Abraham and Ishmael. It laid bare their hearts for all of history to witness. No one could doubt their total devotion after this. The test was not a secret; it became a public legacy through the Quran, revealing the highest level of human submission. The word “truly” emphasizes the magnitude of the trial. There is no test more severe for a father than to sacrifice his own beloved son with his own hands. By passing it, Abraham demonstrated a love for Allah that superseded all natural loves. This test also revealed Allah’s mercy, as He did not desire the child’s death but the father’s heart. The Meccan pagans, who claimed to honour Abraham, are being reminded that the real Abraham was not an idolater but a man whose faith was proven through the ultimate trial. They are challenged to measure their own devotion against his. For the believer, this verse frames trials in a new light: they are opportunities for the true nature of one’s faith to be revealed to oneself and to others. The test was not a punishment but a gift that elevated Abraham to the rank of khalīlullah. Every difficulty a believer faces can be a revealing test that, when passed with patience, strengthens the soul and brings one closer to Allah. The verse serves as a conclusion to the sacrifice narrative, setting up the reward and ransom that immediately follow.
107 | And We ransomed his son with a great sacrifice, | With the test successfully completed, Allah’s mercy manifested as a tangible substitute. “And We ransomed his son with a great sacrifice.” The knife was removed, and in the place of Ishmael, a ram was provided to be slaughtered instead. The word “ransomed” (fadaynāhu) implies a payment or substitution that secures release. The son’s life was spared, and the blood that was to be shed was now shed through the ram. The “great sacrifice” refers to a magnificent ram, which according to Islamic tradition was a fat, horned ram that had been prepared in Paradise or was one of the best of its kind, sent by Allah to be offered. The greatness of the sacrifice lies not only in the quality of the animal but in the divine origin of the substitute. This event became the foundation of the ritual of udhiyah (sacrifice) on Eid al-Adha, where Muslims worldwide slaughter an animal in commemoration of Abraham’s obedience and Allah’s mercy. The ransom demonstrated that Allah does not desire human suffering but rather the submission of the heart. The son was a gift, and the ram was a gift; both were from Allah. The Meccan pagans who offered sacrifices to idols are shown that the true legacy of sacrifice is in the worship of the One God, following the footsteps of their forefather Abraham. For the believer, the ritual sacrifice is a yearly reminder of this event and an opportunity to revive the spirit of submission. The verse also carries a deeper spiritual meaning: when we sacrifice something beloved for Allah’s sake, He replaces it with something better. The ram was a “great sacrifice” because it was a symbol of divine acceptance and a means of drawing closer to Allah. The lesson is that nothing we give up for Allah is ever lost; a greater compensation awaits.
108 | and blessed Abraham ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations: | As with Noah before him, Abraham was granted an eternal legacy of praise. “And blessed Abraham with honourable mention among later generations.” The word “blessed” (taraknā) implies that Allah left upon him a lasting, beautiful remembrance. Abraham is revered by Jews, Christians, and Muslims as the father of prophets and the friend of Allah. His name is mentioned with honour in scripture, in prayer, and in the daily speech of billions. The phrase “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) is the same used for Noah, indicating a truthful and praiseworthy reputation. This blessing is a direct answer to Abraham’s earlier prayer for a righteous legacy. Not only was he given righteous offspring, but his name itself became synonymous with faith, hospitality, and submission. The Meccan pagans who boasted of their descent from Abraham are being told that true connection to Abraham is not through bloodline alone but through following his pure monotheistic faith. The honourable mention was for his tawheed and his sacrifice, not for idol worship. For the believers, this verse shows that if one sincerely serves Allah, He will take care of their reputation in ways they never imagined. Abraham was a lone stranger in a land of idolaters; now he is beloved by the whole world. This is a consolation for those who feel isolated or marginalized for their faith. The “later generations” include all the prophets who came after him, all the believers, and even we who recite the Quran today are part of that honourable mention. The verse builds a bridge between the stories of the prophets, showing that each of them shares in this eternal blessing, and the phrase will be repeated for others in the surah.
109 | “Peace be upon Abraham.” | The honourable mention is concretized in the divine salutation: “Peace be upon Abraham.” This greeting is the highest honour. “Peace” (salām) comes directly from Allah and is also pronounced by the angels and the believers. It is a prayer for safety, mercy, and blessings. When Allah sends peace upon a servant, that servant is secured from all harm, his heart is at rest, and his station is elevated. This salutation is part of the enduring legacy: every time a believer recites the Quran or sends blessings upon the prophets, they say “peace be upon Abraham.” It is a timeless connection of love and respect. The verse also echoes the greeting the people of Paradise will receive, as mentioned earlier in the surah. Abraham, the friend of Allah, is already in that state of divine peace. The Meccan pagans, who might have thought that their idols could bring them peace, are shown that true peace is only with the prophets and their way. For the believer, sending peace upon Abraham is an act of worship and a means of drawing closer to Allah. It reminds us of the brotherhood of prophets and the single chain of tawheed. This verse is a beautiful pause in the narrative, allowing the listener to internalize the greatness of Abraham and to join in the salutation. The peace that Abraham earned through his immense trials is a peace that no worldly disturbance can shake. It is the peace of a heart that has fully submitted and been accepted. Believers long for a share of that peace in this life and the next.
110 | This is how We reward the good-doers. | The refrain appears again, linking Abraham’s reward to a universal principle. “This is how We reward the good-doers.” The deliverance from the fire, the gift of righteous offspring, the ransom from the sacrifice, the honourable mention, and the eternal peace—all of this is the standard reward for those who achieve the rank of iḥsān, spiritual excellence. Abraham was a muḥsin, one who worshipped Allah as if seeing Him, and his reward is a template for all who follow. The repetition of this refrain after each prophet in the surah drives home the consistency of Allah’s justice and mercy. It assures the believers that their good deeds are never lost, and the pattern of divine generosity is unchanging. The Meccan Muslims, who were being persecuted and had little worldly reward, are reminded that the real reward comes in forms that far surpass material wealth. The refrain also connects Abraham back to Noah, and forward to the prophets yet to be mentioned, weaving a golden thread through the tapestry of prophethood. For the contemporary believer, this verse is a powerful motivational tool: strive to be a good-doer, and the same Lord who rewarded Abraham will reward you in ways appropriate to your own test and context. The “good-doers” are not necessarily those who achieve great fame, but those who perfect their faith and action, wherever they are.
111 | He was truly one of Our faithful servants. | As with Noah, the final commendation of Abraham is that he was a faithful servant. “He was truly one of Our faithful servants.” The phrase “faithful servants” (ʿibādinā al-muʾminīn) combines complete servitude with deep, unwavering belief. Abraham was not only a believer in heart but a servant in every action. His entire life was a testament to this dual quality. The word “truly” emphasizes the certainty and depth of this description. This is the pinnacle of human achievement: to be recognized by Allah as a faithful servant. It is not a title one can claim for oneself; it is a divine attestation. Abraham’s faithfulness was proven through the fire, the exile, the sacrifice, and the endless hospitality. He never wavered. For the believers, this verse is an invitation to examine their own faith and servitude. Are we truly faithful servants, or do we serve our desires and the pressures of society more than we serve Allah? The legacy of Abraham calls us to a life of complete dedication. The verse also serves as a bridge to the mention of Isaac and the continuation of Abraham’s lineage, showing that his faithfulness bore fruit in a line of prophets and righteous people. The highest honour is not in wealth or power but in being inscribed in the register of Allah’s faithful servants.
112 | We ˹later˺ gave him good news of Isaac—a prophet, and one of the righteous. | After the narrative of the sacrifice, the Quran mentions the second major son of Abraham. “We later gave him good news of Isaac—a prophet, and one of the righteous.” The word “later” indicates that the glad tidings of Isaac came after the events of the sacrifice and after Ishmael’s birth. This was a further blessing. Abraham, now very old, and his wife Sarah, who was also advanced in age, were given the good news of another son, Isaac. The good news was delivered by the same angels who were on their way to destroy the people of Lot. The verse describes Isaac with two magnificent qualities: “a prophet” and “one of the righteous.” This shows that Isaac was not only a prophet himself but was also deeply rooted in personal piety. His prophethood was a fulfillment of Allah’s promise to Abraham that his progeny would include prophets and righteous leaders. The mention of Isaac here also sets the record straight for the Jewish and Christian communities who trace their spiritual lineage through Isaac. The Quran honours Isaac as a noble prophet and does not diminish his status, while also affirming Ishmael’s honoured place. The “good news” of a prophet son is a tremendous gift, showing that Abraham’s legacy would be carried on through multiple channels. For the believer, this verse demonstrates that Allah often grants more than what we initially ask or imagine. Abraham had prayed for righteous offspring; he was blessed with Ishmael and then Isaac, and through them, countless prophets. It also teaches that the righteous are an interconnected family, and the greatest joy for a believing parent is to see their children walking the path of prophethood and righteousness, even if they themselves are not prophets.
113 | We blessed him and Isaac as well. Some of their descendants did good, while others clearly wronged themselves. | The verse summarizes the legacy of Abraham and Isaac. “We blessed him and Isaac as well.” The blessing (baraknā) encompassed prophethood, righteous progeny, scripture, and lasting honour. The “him” refers to Abraham, and Isaac is specifically named again, emphasizing that both father and son were jointly blessed. Then the verse states a profound principle of justice and individual responsibility: “Some of their descendants did good, while others clearly wronged themselves.” This indicates that being from the lineage of prophets does not automatically guarantee righteousness. Among the descendants of Abraham through Isaac and Ishmael, there were those who did good—the prophets and the sincere believers—and those who clearly wronged themselves through disbelief, idolatry, and sins. This was a direct message to the Meccan pagans who claimed Abraham as their ancestor and assumed that this lineage would save them. The Quran tells them that Abraham’s true legacy is followed only by those who do good, while those who deviate and do wrong are accountable for their own actions, regardless of their ancestry. The phrase “clearly wronged themselves” indicates that the evidence was manifest, and they had no excuse. It also applied to the Jews and Christians who deviated from the original teachings of their prophets. For the believer, this verse severs any reliance on family background or saintly lineage as a means of salvation. Each soul is responsible for its own deeds. The blessing of being born into a Muslim family or a righteous community is a favour, but it must be matched with personal faith and good actions. The verse also highlights the justice of Allah: He does not punish the descendants for the sins of their forefathers, nor does He reward them merely for ancestral piety. Everyone stands on their own record.
114 | And We certainly showed favour to Moses and Aaron, | The surah now moves to the next set of chosen servants. “And We certainly showed favour to Moses and Aaron.” The word “certainly” emphasizes the abundance and certainty of the favours bestowed upon them. Moses (Mūsā) and his brother Aaron (Hārūn) were jointly granted prophethood, divine support, and a tremendous mission to confront Pharaoh and liberate the Children of Israel. The “favour” includes the revelation of the Torah, the miracles of the staff and the shining hand, the parting of the sea, the destruction of their enemy, and the guidance provided to their people. Mentioning them together highlights their partnership in prophethood, as Moses asked Allah to appoint Aaron as his helper and Allah granted that request. This demonstrates the beauty of brotherly cooperation in the path of Allah. The favour shown to them was not because of their personal merits alone but was purely from Allah’s grace. The Meccan pagans were aware of the story of Moses and Pharaoh from the Jews and Christians; the Quran presents it concisely, focusing on the pattern of deliverance. For the believers, this verse is a reminder that Allah’s favour upon His prophets is immense and that those who follow their guidance share in that favour. The mention of Moses and Aaron leads into a series of brief vignettes that highlight different aspects of divine assistance and honour, consistent with the refrain that follows. The believers who were a small, oppressed minority in Mecca could see in Moses and Aaron’s struggle against the mighty Pharaoh a parallel to their own struggle against the Quraysh, and find hope that Allah’s favour would eventually grant them victory and deliverance.
115 | and delivered them and their people from the great distress. | The specific favour is now detailed. “And delivered them and their people from the great distress.” The “great distress” refers to the long period of enslavement, humiliation, and slaughter that the Children of Israel suffered under Pharaoh. The baby boys were being killed, and the people were forced into hard labour. This distress was both physical and psychological. Allah’s deliverance came through the mission of Moses and Aaron, culminating in the Exodus: the parting of the Red Sea, the crossing, and the drowning of Pharaoh’s army. The delivery was not just from an abstract difficulty but from a life of utter degradation to a life of dignity and freedom to worship Allah. The phrase “their people” shows that the deliverance was collective; Moses and Aaron were saved along with the believers among their nation. This mirrors the deliverance of Noah and his family from the flood and connects to the overall theme of the surah: the chosen servants are saved, while the wrongdoers are destroyed. The “great distress” can also be seen as a type of all the worldly and spiritual trials that afflict believers, with Allah’s deliverance always near. For the Meccan Muslims, suffering under the persecution of the Quraysh, this verse whispered the promise of a coming deliverance. The word “We delivered them” underscores that the source of salvation is Allah alone; the prophets and their strategies were only means. This gives believers confidence that no matter how powerful the oppressor seems, the One who drowned Pharaoh can deliver His servants from any distress.
116 | We helped them so it was they who prevailed. | The deliverance was followed by a decisive victory and superiority. “We helped them so it was they who prevailed.” The help from Allah came in multiple forms: the miracles that confounded Pharaoh’s magicians, the plagues that struck Egypt, the safe passage through the sea, and the destruction of the pursuing army. The result was that Moses, Aaron, and the Children of Israel became the victors, while Pharaoh and his hosts became a lesson for the worlds. The word “prevailed” (gālibīn) indicates they were the dominant, triumphant party. They were no longer slaves but a free nation under divine guidance. This victory was a manifestation of the earlier promise in the surah that the forces of Allah will certainly prevail. The verse teaches that the believers’ path, though fraught with trials, ends in ultimate success, whether in this world or the Hereafter. The Meccan Muslims, who were at that moment a weak and persecuted group, received a clear promise that they too would be helped and would prevail. This promise was later fulfilled in the conquest of Mecca and the spread of Islam. For the individual believer, this verse is a reminder that any struggle for the sake of Allah comes with divine support. The conditions for this help are faith, patience, and taqwa. Moses and Aaron did not prevail by their own numbers or weapons; they prevailed because Allah helped them. Thus, the believer should always seek Allah’s help and trust in His plan, rather than despairing at the apparent power of falsehood.
117 | We gave them the clear Scripture, | After physical deliverance and victory, Allah granted Moses and Aaron a spiritual treasure. “We gave them the clear Scripture.” The “clear Scripture” is the Torah, the book of guidance, law, and light that was revealed to Moses. The word “clear” (mustabīn) means that the Scripture was self-evident in its truth, detailed in its instructions, and unambiguous in its core message. It clearly distinguished between right and wrong, lawful and unlawful. This gift was the culmination of their mission; liberation from Pharaoh was not just for political freedom but for the establishment of a community guided by divine law. The Scripture was the constitution of the new nation, providing them with a comprehensive way of life. The verse connects to the earlier mention of angels reciting the Reminder; the Torah was part of that chain of divine reminders. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this was a reminder that his own gift of the Quran was following the same pattern: after trials and persecution, a clear Scripture is given to guide humanity. The believers were longing for the complete revelation and the formation of a community under its guidance; this verse showed that their situation had a precedent. For us, the clear Scripture is the Quran, and we must treat it with the same reverence and obedience that Moses’ people were expected to show the Torah. The clarity of the Scripture is a mercy that leaves no excuse for ignorance. The believer should immerse themselves in it to find light in every distress.
118 | and guided them to the Straight Path. | The gift of the Scripture was accompanied by active divine guidance. “And guided them to the Straight Path.” The “Straight Path” (al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm) is the path of pure monotheism, righteous deeds, and the way of life that leads to Allah’s pleasure. The guidance was both in the form of the revealed text and in the prophetic example of Moses and Aaron. Allah did not just hand them a book and leave them; He guided their understanding and their steps. The Straight Path is the one that avoids the extremes of excess and deficiency, the path of moderation and truth. This verse is directly connected to Surah Al-Fatiha, where believers daily ask to be guided to the Straight Path. Moses and Aaron and their followers were shown that path clearly. The “them” includes all who believed with them, not just the prophets. The guidance was a continuous process throughout their desert journey. For the Meccan believers, this verse was a reassurance that the Prophet Muhammad was also guiding them to the Straight Path, which would lead them out of the darkness of idolatry into the light of tawheed. The lesson for us is that possessing a Scripture is not enough; we need Allah’s ongoing guidance to understand and implement it correctly. We must constantly seek that guidance through prayer, reflection, and companionship with the righteous. The Straight Path is the path of all the prophets, and the sequence in the surah shows that Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Aaron all walked and guided to this same path.
119 | And We blessed them ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations: | The refrain that appeared for Noah and Abraham now appears for Moses and Aaron. “And We blessed them with honourable mention among later generations.” The pair of prophets are granted a lasting, truthful, and praiseworthy reputation that extends throughout history. Jews, Christians, and Muslims all honour Moses and Aaron as great prophets. Muslims in particular are required to believe in them and speak of them with respect. The phrase “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) is identical to what was granted to their predecessors. This consistency reinforces the unity of the prophetic mission and the equality of their core message. It also serves as a motivation for believers: a life spent in sincere service to Allah results in a legacy that outlives earthly existence. The “later generations” include us today. When we recite this verse or speak of Moses and Aaron with reverence, we are part of the fulfillment of this divine blessing. The Meccan pagans, who mocked the Prophet and attempted to erase his message, are being warned that they cannot erase the honourable mention Allah bestows. The believers who are unknown and oppressed can take heart that Allah can make their names honoured in ways they cannot foresee. The verse also subtly refutes the negative propaganda that Pharaoh’s regime must have spread about Moses; Pharaoh’s name is cursed, while Moses’ name is blessed. Truth always prevails in the long term.
120 | “Peace be upon Moses and Aaron.” | The honourable mention crystallizes into the divine salutation: “Peace be upon Moses and Aaron.” As with Noah and Abraham, the peace of Allah descends upon these two faithful servants. The peace is eternal and unshakeable. It encompasses their souls, their memory, and their station. When a believer hears this, they are encouraged to echo the salutation in their hearts and upon their tongues. The mention of peace is particularly poignant for Moses and Aaron, who faced a life of struggle, defiance from their own people, and the immense burden of leadership. Now, after their passing, they are in a state of perfect peace, far removed from the turmoil of the world. This salutation is also a refutation of any claims that might belittle them; Allah Himself sends peace upon them, so no believer can harbour anything but love and respect for them. The early Muslims, living in a hostile environment, would find in this verse a reminder that the peace of Allah is the ultimate goal, far more valuable than the fleeting peace offered by compromise with falsehood. The salutation also links the prophets together in a chain of peace, which will be extended at the end of the surah to all messengers. This consistent structure in the surah creates a rhythm that embeds the lesson deeply: the chosen servants are those who receive divine peace, and we must strive to be among those upon whom the angels descend with peace.
121 | Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers. | The refrain appears yet again, this time concluding the account of Moses and Aaron. The verse states, “Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers.” The word “Indeed” imparts absolute certainty, leaving no room for doubt that the magnificent treatment granted to Moses and Aaron—the deliverance from great distress, the help that made them prevail, the gift of the clear Scripture, the guidance to the Straight Path, the honourable mention among later generations, and the divine salutation of peace—is not an isolated occurrence. It is the established, unchanging pattern of divine recompense. The phrase “this is how” draws attention to the specific way Allah deals with those who achieve the rank of iḥsān. The good-doers are those who perfect their worship and their character, worshipping Allah as though they see Him, and who combine deep faith with meticulous action. Moses and Aaron exemplified this through their tireless struggle against Pharaoh, their patient leadership of the Israelites, and their profound reliance upon Allah in the face of immense hardship. When Moses stood before the Red Sea with Pharaoh’s army closing in, his iḥsān was manifested in his words, “No, my Lord is with me, He will guide me.” When Aaron managed the rebellious Israelites, he demonstrated forbearance and commitment to unity. The reward was comprehensive: they were saved, empowered, given divine law, and eternally honoured. For the Meccan believers, this refrain was a powerful reassurance that their own struggles, sacrifices, and good deeds, however small they seemed, would be rewarded in the same magnificent pattern. It taught them to look beyond immediate circumstances and trust in the consistent, generous justice of Allah. For the contemporary believer, this verse serves as both motivation and comfort. Every act of kindness, every prayer performed with concentration, every temptation resisted, and every patience shown in adversity is seen by the same Lord who rewarded Moses and Aaron. The reward may come in this world as victory, honour, or peace of heart, and it will certainly come in the Hereafter in the Gardens of Bliss. The verse also warns that the opposite pattern holds true for the wicked, making it a call to strive for iḥsān in all aspects of life, so that one may be counted among those whose reward is divine friendship and eternal peace.
122 | They were truly ˹two˺ of Our faithful servants. | The final seal on the story of Moses and Aaron is this divine testimony: “They were truly two of Our faithful servants.” The emphasis on “truly” (innahumā) affirms the depth and authenticity of their servitude. This is the highest praise a human can receive—to be described by the Creator as a faithful servant. The phrase combines two essential dimensions: servitude (ʿibādah) and faith (īmān). They were not only believers in their hearts but also servants in every action and moment of their lives. Their will was fully aligned with Allah’s will. The word “two” acknowledges their unique partnership in prophethood. Moses had asked Allah to make his brother Aaron a helper and a sharer in his mission, and Allah granted that, elevating both together. This shows the beauty of brotherly cooperation in the path of truth, and how Allah honours those who support one another in righteousness. Being a faithful servant meant that Moses did not rely on his own strength when confronting Pharaoh; he turned entirely to Allah. Aaron, likewise, was a steadfast supporter who did not seek personal glory. Their faithfulness was tested in the crucible of oppression, in the wilderness, and in dealing with a stubborn people, yet they remained unwavering. For the believers in Mecca, this testimony provided a powerful lens through which to view their own situation. The Quraysh mocked them as abandoning the ways of their ancestors, but they were walking the path of Moses and Aaron, the faithful servants. True honour is not in tribal lineage but in this divine attestation. The verse also connects to the series of prophets mentioned in the surah: Noah, Abraham, and later Elias, Lot, and Jonah all receive similar commendations. This creates a golden thread of faithfulness across history. The believer aspires to this title—to be counted among the faithful servants of Allah, those whose inner faith and outer actions are perfectly harmonized, and whose ultimate reward is the divine peace and honourable mention that never fade.
123 | And Elias was indeed one of the messengers. | The surah now shifts to another honoured prophet, introducing him with the same formula. “And Elias was indeed one of the messengers.” The “indeed” (wa-inna) serves to confirm the prophethood of Elias, known in Arabic as Ilyās, who is identified with the biblical Elijah. The Meccan pagans might have heard of Elias from the Jews and Christians, but they did not necessarily honour him as they should. The Quran here places him firmly within the chain of divine messengers, emphasizing that his message was the same core truth delivered by all prophets: the call to tawheed. Elias was sent to a people who had deviated into idol worship, specifically to the worship of an idol called Baʿl. The historical context, as understood from various traditions, places him in the region of Baalbek or among the Israelites after the time of Solomon. The people had become prosperous but had abandoned sincere worship of Allah, turning to a fertility god and indulging in moral corruption. Elias stood as a lone voice calling them back. The phrase “one of the messengers” connects him directly to Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Aaron, showing that he was not a reformer with a new philosophy but a link in the unbroken chain of divine guidance. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this mention was a source of comfort; he was following in the footsteps of these noble messengers who all faced similar rejection. The verse also subtly refutes those who might deny Elias’s prophethood or reduce him to a mere storyteller figure. Allah Himself testifies to his station. For the believer, every prophet mentioned in the Quran is a role model, and Elias’s story, though briefly told, holds profound lessons about the courage required to confront a society deeply entrenched in false worship and the patience needed when the truth is rejected.
124 | ˹Remember˺ when he said to his people, “Will you not fear ˹Allah˺? | The narrative begins with the core of Elias’s message, placed in a rhetorical question full of reproach and love. “Remember when he said to his people, ‘Will you not fear Allah?’” The command “Remember” invites the Prophet and the believers to reflect on Elias’s approach. He began with the call to taqwā, the fear and consciousness of Allah. This is the foundation of all prophetic missions: if a person truly fears Allah, they will refrain from shirk and sin. The question “Will you not” carries a tone of gentle yet firm admonishment, implying that their lack of fear is astonishing given the clear signs of Allah’s power and mercy around them. Elias was not asking them to fear a distant, indifferent deity; he was asking them to fear the One who created them, sustained them, and would hold them accountable. The fear of Allah is not a cowering terror but a reverent awe that inspires obedience and love. In the context of his people who had turned to Baʿl, this fear had been replaced by a careless attachment to a false god that could neither benefit nor harm. Elias’s question also highlights that the root problem of his people was spiritual heedlessness. They had lost the sense of accountability, so they worshipped a lifeless idol. The Meccan pagans were in exactly the same state, and the Prophet Muhammad similarly asked them, “Will you not fear Allah?” For the believer, this verse is a timeless reminder that the first step to reform is to awaken the consciousness of Allah in the heart. We must constantly ask ourselves: do we truly fear Allah in our private and public lives? That fear is what guards against sin and motivates towards good. Elias’s simple yet profound question remains as relevant as ever.
125 | Do you call upon ˹the idol of˺ Ba’l and abandon the Best of Creators— | Elias now makes the contrast explicit, exposing the absurdity of their worship. “Do you call upon the idol of Ba’l and abandon the Best of Creators?” The phrase “call upon” (tadʿūna) means to invoke, to worship, and to seek help from. Baʿl was a well-known idol of the ancient Near East, often associated with storms, fertility, and the sun. The people of Elias had dedicated temples and rituals to this lifeless object, praying to it for rain, children, and protection. Elias’s question is designed to shock them into reflection. They were leaving the worship of Allah, the Best of Creators—the One who perfected everything He made, who created the heavens and the earth with infinite wisdom, and who fashioned human beings in the finest form—for a piece of stone or wood that their own hands had carved. The title “Best of Creators” (Aḥsan al-Khāliqīn) is a powerful emphasis on Allah’s unmatched creative perfection. All other so-called creators—whether human artisans who carve idols or the false deities themselves—are utterly inferior. The word “abandon” (tadharūna) implies a wilful forsaking of something vastly superior for something worthless. The verse cuts deep into the psychology of idolatry: it is a trade of the real for the illusory, of the Benefactor for the beneficiary. For the Meccan pagans who called upon Hubal, Lāt, and ʿUzzā while abandoning the Lord of the Kaaba, this was a direct and humbling challenge. The verse teaches the believer to evaluate all attachments and priorities. Do we, in any way, abandon the Best of Creators—His commands, His pleasure—for the sake of modern idols like wealth, status, or base desires? The rhetorical question frames this choice as utterly irrational, inviting the listener to immediately recoil from such a foolish exchange and return to the worship of the One who alone deserves it.
126 | Allah, your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers.” | Elias immediately answers the implied question, identifying the Best of Creators. “Allah, your Lord and the Lord of your forefathers.” The verse uses the name “Allah,” the personal name of the one true God, combining it with the concept of Lordship. The word “Lord” (Rabb) signifies the Creator, Sustainer, Owner, and Ruler. By saying “your Lord,” Elias reminds them that Allah is not a distant, foreign deity; He is their own Lord who has been caring for them from birth. Then he adds “and the Lord of your forefathers.” This is a strategic and profound addition because the pagans’ primary justification for idol worship was that they were following the religion of their ancestors. Elias dismantles that argument by stating that Allah, not Baʿl, was the Lord of their forefathers. True ancestors, the ones who were rightly guided, worshipped Allah alone. If the forefathers had later fallen into idolatry, that was a deviation, not a tradition to be proud of. The chain of true tradition leads back to monotheism. The Meccan pagans made the same claim, saying they found their fathers upon a religion and were following their footsteps. The Quran, through Elias’s words, refutes this: Allah is the Lord of the forefathers, and they should have worshipped Him. This verse thus establishes that ancestral custom is no excuse for shirk when it contradicts divine Lordship. The believer learns to honour the true heritage of tawheed passed down through the prophets and to evaluate cultural traditions in light of this ultimate truth. The Lord of the forefathers is the same Lord of the later generations, and His worship is the only constant that unites the righteous across time. This unbroken continuity is the foundation of the Muslim ummah’s identity.
127 | But they rejected him, so they will certainly be brought ˹for punishment˺. | Despite the clear and compassionate call of Elias, the response of his people was rejection. “But they rejected him, so they will certainly be brought for punishment.” The Arabic word for “rejected” (kadhdhabū) implies that they called him a liar and denied the truth of his message. They not only refused to abandon Baʿl but actively opposed Elias, likely mocking him and threatening him. The consequence is stated with certainty: “they will certainly be brought.” The verb “brought” (muḥḍarūn) means they will be forcibly presented, herded like prisoners to the place of reckoning and punishment. They will not be able to escape or hide. This applies to the punishment in the Hereafter, and according to many scholars, it also refers to a worldly punishment that befell them, such as a severe drought and then destruction. The verse reflects the unchanging divine law: when a people persistently reject the messenger sent to them after clear warning, punishment descends. This law was seen with Noah’s people, Pharaoh, and the people of Lot, and the Meccan pagans are being warned that the same law applies to them. The phrase “will certainly be brought” also links to the earlier scenes in the surah where the angels are commanded to gather and lead the wrongdoers to the path of Hell. There is a terrifying continuity between the rejection in this world and the humiliation in the next. For the believer, this verse is a stark reminder of the seriousness of denying the truth once it has been made clear. It also provides solace: the prophets are not defeated when their people reject them; the victory belongs ultimately to the truth, and the rejecters will face a sure and inevitable reckoning. The faithful servants, however, are exempted, as the next verse reminds.
128 | But not the chosen servants of Allah. | As has become the beautiful rhythm of this surah, the announcement of punishment is immediately followed by the exception. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” Among the people of Elias, not everyone rejected him. There was a group of sincere believers who responded to his call, purified their worship for Allah alone, and held fast to the truth despite the hostile environment. These were the chosen servants (al-mukhlaṣīn), those whom Allah had purified, guided, and selected for His mercy. They were saved from the punishment that overtook their people. The word “chosen” implies both divine election and personal sincerity. They were sincere in their devotion, so Allah chose them. This exception provides a crucial balance to the narrative: it shows that Allah’s justice does not sweep away the innocent with the guilty. Even in the most corrupt society, the righteous remnant is preserved. The believers in Mecca, small in number and surrounded by idolaters, were exactly such a chosen remnant. This verse was a direct reassurance that they would be saved while their persecutors would be brought to punishment. It also teaches that being a chosen servant is not a passive state but is achieved through sincere worship and steadfastness in the face of rejection. The refrain also connects Elias’s story to the overarching theme of the surah: the ultimate triumph of the chosen servants of Allah, whether through worldly deliverance or through honourable mention and peace after their passing. The exception serves as a call to every listener to strive to be among these chosen ones, by purifying their faith and clinging to the rope of Allah, so that when the day of distinction arrives, they stand among the saved.
129 | We blessed him ˹with honourable mention˺ among later generations: | The reward for Elias’s steadfastness, like that of the prophets before him, was not limited to his own lifetime but extended across history. “We blessed him with honourable mention among later generations.” The phrase “blessed him” (taraknā ʿalayhi) means that Allah left upon him a lasting, beautiful legacy. The “honourable mention” (lisāna ṣidq) is a tongue of truth and praise, meaning that future generations speak of Elias with reverence, respect, and love. His name is recorded in the Quran, recited in prayers, and honoured by millions of believers. This is a direct compensation for the rejection and slander he faced from his own people. They may have called him a liar or a troublemaker, but Allah ensured that his true reputation would be one of honour until the Day of Judgment. The “later generations” include the Israelites, the Christians who revere Elijah, and the Muslim ummah, which is required to believe in all prophets and speak of them with respect. This verse underscores a profound principle: a believer’s true reputation is not in the fleeting opinions of contemporaries but in the enduring judgment of Allah. Many prophets were dismissed as mad or dangerous in their time, yet their names are now blessed, while the names of their arrogant opponents are cursed or forgotten. The Meccan pagans were trying to tarnish the Prophet Muhammad’s name, calling him a mad poet, but this verse reassured him that Allah would similarly bless him with honourable mention. Today, the adhan echoes around the world with Muhammad’s name, fulfilling that promise. For the believer, this inspires a detachment from seeking human approval and a focus on earning Allah’s lasting praise. The righteous legacy is not material wealth or power but a name that is remembered with good by the community of faith.
130 | “Peace be upon Elias.” | The honourable mention is crystallized into the divine salutation: “Peace be upon Elias.” This is the same greeting of peace that was sent upon Noah, Abraham, Moses, and Aaron. The word “Peace” (Salām) is a comprehensive prayer for safety, security, mercy, and blessings. When Allah sends peace upon a servant, it means that servant is in the abode of peace, free from all harm, fear, and grief. Elias, who faced the enmity of his people and the hardships of daʿwah, now dwells in a state of perfect, eternal peace. This salutation is not merely a historical note; it is a living reality. The angels greet him with peace, and the believers are taught to repeat this salutation when they read or hear his name. The verse invites the heart of the listener to join in this divine greeting, fostering a personal connection of love and respect for the prophet Elias. In the context of the surah, the repeated salutation upon each prophet builds a powerful sense of the brotherhood of messengers and the single, harmonious path they all walked. The peace upon Elias is a testament to his success; the mission that seemed so difficult in the world has resulted in eternal tranquility. For the Meccan believers, this was a reminder that the peace they were denied in their city by the persecutors would be granted to them by the Lord of the worlds, just as it was granted to Elias. The verse also teaches the believer to value the peace that comes from Allah above all worldly peace. The peace of a clear conscience, the peace of faith, and the ultimate peace in Paradise are the true goals. By saying “Peace be upon Elias,” we affirm our place in the long line of believers who honour and love all the prophets.
131 | Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers. | The refrain repeats, anchoring Elias’s story in the universal divine principle. “Indeed, this is how We reward the good-doers.” All the honours bestowed upon Elias—the prophetic mission, the deliverance of the chosen servants, the honourable mention among later generations, and the eternal peace—are presented as the standard reward for those who attain the rank of iḥsān, spiritual excellence. Elias was a good-doer because he combined deep, sincere faith with courageous action. He stood alone against a society that had normalized idol worship, calling them to fear Allah with wisdom and compassion. His good-doing was not a passive inner state but an active struggle to establish tawheed. The verse reaffirms that Allah’s reward system is consistent and just; no act of goodness, no matter how small, is overlooked, and no good-doer is ever left without a magnificent recompense. For the believers who heard this in Mecca, the refrain was a constant injection of hope. Their own acts of patience, their secret prayers, their charity, and their endurance of abuse were all part of the same pattern of iḥsān, and the same Lord who rewarded Elias would reward them. The emphasis on “this is how” draws attention to the specific manner of divine reward: it is comprehensive, covering both this world and the next, and it often includes the transformation of hardship into lasting honour. The verse challenges the listener to elevate their own deeds to the level of iḥsān—to worship Allah as if seeing Him, to perfect their character, and to call others to goodness—so that they may deserve this magnificent reward. It is a call to excellence in all facets of life, rooted in the certainty that the rewarder is the Best of Creators.
132 | He was truly one of Our faithful servants. | The story of Elias concludes with the highest commendation, identical to that given to the prophets before him. “He was truly one of Our faithful servants.” The phrase “faithful servants” (ʿibādinā al-muʾminīn) joins complete servitude with unwavering belief. Elias’s entire being was oriented towards Allah; he was a servant in thought, word, and deed, and a believer whose heart was filled with the light of certainty. The word “truly” (inna) emphasizes the reality and depth of this quality. This is not a title that Elias claimed for himself; it is a testimony from the Lord of the worlds, which makes it the most profound validation possible. Elias’s faithfulness was proven in his willingness to stand against the current, to deliver the difficult message, and to endure the loneliness of rejection without compromising the truth. His servitude meant that his will was entirely merged with Allah’s will; he desired what Allah desired and opposed what Allah opposed. This verse also subtly refutes any suggestion that Elias was a fiery, impatient figure; the Quranic picture is of a man of deep faith and complete submission, despite his people’s stubbornness. For the believers in Mecca, this description of Elias provided a mirror for their own aspirations. They too were being called to be faithful servants amidst a hostile environment. The verse teaches that the ultimate identity of a believer is not based on tribe, nation, or social status, but on being a faithful servant of Allah. It is an identity that transcends time and connects the believer to the most noble company: the prophets and the righteous. The subsequent stories of Lot and Jonah will continue this pattern, each adding unique dimensions to the portrait of the faithful servant, whether through steadfastness in a wicked society or through the journey of repentance and return.
133 | And Lot was indeed one of the messengers. | The surah now moves to the next prophet in the series, Lot (Lūṭ). “And Lot was indeed one of the messengers.” The “indeed” again confirms his station emphatically. Lot was the nephew of Abraham, and he accompanied his uncle on the journey to the blessed land. He was later sent as a messenger to the people of Sodom and Gomorrah, cities known for their moral depravity, especially the abominable act of homosexuality that they were the first among humankind to practice openly. The verse places Lot squarely within the brotherhood of messengers, showing that his mission was divinely ordained and his message was the same call to tawheed and moral purity. The Meccan pagans were aware of the story of Lot’s people from the passing caravans and from Jewish and Christian traditions. By mentioning Lot, the Quran highlights a different type of test: one where the corruption was not just idolatry but extreme moral perversion. The faithful servant must confront all forms of deviation. For the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca, this mention provided another model of patience and trust. Lot endured the wickedness of his people for years, offering them guidance and warning them of the consequences. The verse sets the stage for a concise yet powerful summary of his deliverance and the destruction of his people. It reminds the believer that the call to Allah includes the call to moral and social righteousness, and that the faithful servant must remain pure in the most corrupt of environments. Lot’s story, like the others, is a testament to the fact that the chosen servants are saved, while the defiantly wicked are utterly destroyed, leaving behind ruins as a sign for those who travel and reflect.
134 | ˹Remember˺ when We delivered him and all of his family, | The core of Lot’s narrative in this surah focuses on deliverance. “Remember when We delivered him and all of his family.” The command “Remember” invites reflection on this specific act of divine salvation. The deliverance of Lot occurred when the angels, who had first visited Abraham in the form of handsome guests, came to Sodom to execute the punishment. They instructed Lot to leave the city with his family during the night and not to look back. The phrase “all of his family” indicates that the door of salvation was open to his entire household. Lot’s family, primarily his two believing daughters, was included in this deliverance. This mirrors the earlier pattern of Noah, where the prophet and his family were saved from the great distress. The deliverance was an act of pure mercy, snatching the righteous out of a society that had become utterly corrupt and was about to face a catastrophic punishment. The word “We delivered” emphasizes that the rescue was entirely by Allah’s power and planning, not by any worldly means. The believers were a tiny minority in a deeply perverse city, yet they were not forgotten. For the Meccan Muslims, this was a direct parallel: they were a small, persecuted community in a city steeped in idolatry and immorality. The verse promised that just as Lot and his family were delivered from the heart of corruption, they too would be delivered. The lesson for every believer is that no matter how dark the environment, Allah’s hand of rescue is always near for those who maintain their faith. Deliverance may come in the form of migration, a change of circumstances, or the ultimate salvation in the Hereafter. The emphasis on family also highlights the importance of striving to keep one’s household upon faith, so that all may be saved together.
135 | except an old woman, who was one of the doomed. | The comprehensive deliverance had one tragic exception, stated with brevity. “Except an old woman, who was one of the doomed.” The old woman was Lot’s wife, who did not share his faith. Despite living in the household of a prophet and witnessing his righteousness, her heart was with the sinners. She conspired with the people of Sodom, informing them of the visiting guests, and she had no intention of leaving her wicked ways. When the command to depart came, she lingered or looked back, and she was thus caught in the punishment. The description “one of the doomed” (al-ghābirīn) means she was among those who remained behind and were destroyed. This exception is deeply sobering: marriage to a prophet or proximity to righteousness does not guarantee salvation if the individual’s own faith is absent. It illustrates the principle that no soul bears the burden of another, and that familial ties are severed when based on disbelief. The verse serves as a stark warning to the Meccans who claimed that their lineage to Abraham or their association with the Prophet’s tribe would save them. Lot’s wife was of the same household as the messenger, yet she perished. For the believers, this exception reinforces the urgency of personal faith and the danger of being attached to a righteous community outwardly while harbouring disbelief or hypocrisy inwardly. The mention of her age as “an old woman” perhaps highlights that years of living with a prophet had not softened her heart, making her fate a cautionary tale about the hardening of the heart over time. The verse also magnifies the perfection of Allah’s justice: the deliverance was only for the true believers, and the disobedient were excluded without any favouritism, emphasizing that the title “faithful servant” is earned, not inherited.
136 | Then We ˹utterly˺ destroyed the rest. | After the deliverance of Lot and his believing daughters, the divine punishment was unleashed. “Then We utterly destroyed the rest.” The “rest” refers to the entire population of Sodom and the surrounding cities that had rejected Lot’s message and persisted in their heinous sins. The word “utterly” (dammarnā) indicates a thorough, catastrophic annihilation that left nothing standing. The destruction involved the cities being turned upside down and a rain of stones of baked clay pelting them. This was a direct, miraculous punishment that matched the enormity of their crime, which included not only sexual perversion but also highway robbery and open defiance of the prophetic warning. The verse is concise because the surah’s focus is on the pattern and the lesson, not the graphic details, which are elaborated in other surahs. The destruction of the rest is the flip side of deliverance: the chosen servants are saved, and the rejecters are wiped out. This consistent outcome reinforces the gravity of rejecting a messenger. The people of Lot had been warned repeatedly, and when they even tried to assault the angelic visitors, their measure of sin was full. The verse thus shows that divine patience has a limit. For the Meccan pagans, who were also rejecting their messenger and plotting against him, this was a direct threat. They are the “rest” of their time if they do not change. The believer learns from this that the power of Allah to destroy is as real as His power to save, and that one must never feel secure from the divine plan. The ruins of the destroyed cities, as the next verse points out, were left as a visible sign for all who pass by, a permanent reminder etched into the geography of the region.
137 | You ˹Meccans˺ certainly pass by their ruins day | The surah now turns the historical narrative into a direct, present-tense address to the Meccan disbelievers. “You Meccans certainly pass by their ruins day.” The Arabs of Quraysh conducted regular trade caravans to Syria, a journey that took them along the route near the Dead Sea, where the ruins of Sodom and Gomorrah were located. The verse tells them that they “certainly pass by” these ruins, making the destruction of Lot’s people not a distant legend but a visible, tangible reality integrated into their travel experience. The mention of “day” indicates their daytime journeys, when the ruins were plainly visible. The destroyed cities were a silent testimony to the truth of what the Quran was narrating. This direct address is a powerful rhetorical strategy: it brings the warning into the immediate lived reality of the audience. They could not deny the existence of these ruins; their own eyes had seen them. The verse implicitly asks: you see the punishment that befell those who rejected Lot, yet you reject Muhammad and repeat the same sins? The ruins serve as an open-air classroom, but the lesson requires a heart willing to reflect. For the believer, this teaches that the signs of Allah’s power and justice are all around us in history, archaeology, and the natural world. We should not be like those who travel through life seeing the remnants of destroyed nations without internalizing the message. The verse also emphasizes the credibility of the Quranic narrative; it appeals to empirical observation that the Meccans could verify. The ruins were a standing proof of the pattern the surah relentlessly highlights: rejection of the warners leads to utter destruction.
138 | and night. Will you not then understand? | The verse completes the temporal dimension of their passing by the ruins, adding “and night,” meaning they pass by both during the day and during the night—perhaps on their return journeys, or it simply emphasizes that the ruins are always there, impossible to miss. The constant presence of this evidence makes their heedlessness even more blameworthy. Then the verse ends with a piercing question: “Will you not then understand?” This is an appeal to the intellect (ʿaql). The ruins are not just stones; they are a message. The faculty of understanding should connect the visible effect (the destroyed cities) to its cause (the rejection of the messenger and the commission of vile sins), and then apply that causal chain to one’s own situation. The question is rhetorical and laden with rebuke. They had minds, but they were not using them to grasp the most crucial truth. The Meccans prided themselves on their intelligence and their poetic wit, yet they failed to make the simplest logical deduction from the archaeological evidence before their eyes. This verse teaches that true understanding is not merely intellectual accumulation but the ability to derive moral and spiritual lessons from the signs that Allah has placed in the world. For the believer, it is a call to be a person of understanding, who reflects on the stories of the past, the patterns of history, and the consequences of actions, and then adjusts their life accordingly. The question “Will you not then understand?” echoes across the centuries, challenging every reader and listener to avoid the fate of those who see the signs but remain blind to their meaning. It transitions seamlessly into the next story, that of Jonah, which will present a different type of trial and a unique lesson.
139 | And Jonah was indeed one of the messengers. | The surah now introduces the final prophet in this sequence, Jonah (Yūnus), with the same formula of confirmation. “And Jonah was indeed one of the messengers.” The “indeed” (wa-inna) affirms his prophethood with certainty, even though his story contains an episode of apparent flight from duty. Jonah is also called “Dhun-Nun” (the Man of the Whale) and “Ṣāḥib al-Ḥūt” (the Companion of the Fish) in the Quran. He was sent to the people of Nineveh, a great city in Mesopotamia. His mention here places him in the same honoured line as Noah, Abraham, Moses, Aaron, Elias, and Lot, emphasizing that his status as a messenger is not diminished by the trial he underwent. The Meccans, like the people of Jonah, were being warned. The story that follows will illustrate a different aspect of the prophetic experience: not only patient endurance, but also the moment of human weakness, followed by repentance, and then Allah’s immense mercy. This provides a more complete picture of the faithful servants; they are not infallible robots but human beings who, when they err, turn back to Allah with intense devotion. The Prophet Muhammad was told in another surah to be patient like the “Companion of the Fish,” indicating that this story was a source of comfort and instruction. For the believer, Jonah’s story is a powerful reminder that even the chosen ones can face overwhelming pressure, but the door of repentance and Allah’s mercy is always open. The verse sets the stage for the concise yet deeply moving account of his flight, the storm, the whale, and the eventual deliverance and success, all of which encapsulate the surah’s theme of reward for those who ultimately turn to Allah.
140 | ˹Remember˺ when he fled to the overloaded ship. | The narrative of Jonah’s trial begins with his departure. “Remember when he fled to the overloaded ship.” The command “Remember” draws attention to this critical moment. Jonah, after calling his people to Allah for a long time and seeing no response but rejection, became angry and frustrated. He left his city without Allah’s explicit permission, essentially abandoning his assigned post. The word “fled” (abaqa) is particularly striking; it is the same verb used for a slave who runs away from his master. This indicates that Jonah’s departure was a lapse from the complete servitude expected of a prophet, a momentary escape from the burden of his mission. He fled “to the overloaded ship,” meaning he went to the sea and boarded a ship that was already full of passengers and cargo. The detail of the ship being “overloaded” (mashḥūn) sets up the tension and the subsequent events. The ship, carrying too much weight, would soon face a storm that threatened to sink it. Jonah’s flight was from duty to a place of seeming escape, but it led him directly into a more constricted trial. This teaches that attempting to flee from Allah’s decrees or responsibilities only leads to tighter corners, as the divine plan cannot be outrun. For the Meccans, this was a subtle warning: they could not flee from the truth brought by the Prophet. For the believers, Jonah’s story is relatable; sometimes the pressures of life and daʿwah become so intense that one wants to withdraw. Yet, the lesson is that true relief comes not from fleeing the situation, but from turning back to Allah in sincere repentance, as Jonah would ultimately do in the belly of the whale. The verse captures the beginning of a journey that would bring the prophet to the depths of the sea and then to the heights of divine mercy, illustrating the profound journey of the soul from error to redemption.
141 | Then ˹to save it from sinking,˺ he drew straws ˹with other passengers˺. He lost ˹and was thrown overboard˺. | After Jonah fled his people and boarded the overloaded ship, the journey took a perilous turn. The verse says, “Then to save it from sinking, he drew straws with other passengers.” A violent storm struck, and the heavily laden ship began to founder. In the ancient world, when a ship was in danger and all efforts failed, the crew would sometimes resort to drawing lots to determine if someone on board was the cause of the misfortune, perhaps an escaped slave or a person under divine wrath. By casting lots, they sought to identify and throw that person overboard to lighten the ship and appease whatever force they believed was angry. Jonah participated in this drawing of lots, and “he lost and was thrown overboard.” The lots singled him out. This was not a matter of random chance; it was the operation of Allah’s decree. Jonah, who had fled his prophetic responsibility, was now being drawn out and exposed by a seemingly neutral process. The casting of lots is mentioned in the Quran as a lawful means of decision-making in some contexts, and here it was the means by which the divine will manifested. The fact that Jonah was the one identified indicates that his situation was no secret to the Creator who controls the storm and the lots. The passengers and crew, not knowing Jonah’s true identity, saw the result and acted upon it, throwing him into the raging sea. This moment marks the lowest point of Jonah’s physical trajectory: from the security of the ship to the engulfing darkness of the water. Yet, it was also the beginning of his rescue, for the depths would become the place of his seclusion with Allah. The verse teaches that one cannot run from Allah’s decree; Jonah fled to the sea but found himself face to face with the very trial that would bring him back. For the believer, it is a lesson that when we make mistakes, Allah may orchestrate circumstances that corner us, not to destroy us, but to bring us to a state of humility and reliance. The apparent defeat of being thrown overboard was, in reality, the first step towards a magnificent deliverance. The surah’s theme of the chosen servant being saved is about to unfold in the most literal way, within the belly of a great fish.
142 | Then the whale engulfed him while he was blameworthy. | As Jonah hit the water, divine intervention took a startling form. “Then the whale engulfed him while he was blameworthy.” The Arabic word used is “ḥūt,” which can mean a large fish or a whale. By Allah’s command, this great creature swallowed Jonah whole, without tearing his flesh or breaking his bones. The whale became a living prison, a dark, constricted space beneath the waves. The verse adds a crucial moral detail: “while he was blameworthy.” Jonah was not swallowed as an innocent victim of circumstances; he was in a state of being blameworthy (mulīm) for leaving his people without divine permission, for his impatience and anger. This word carries the sense of doing something that calls for blame, though not necessarily a grave sin that invalidates prophethood. Prophets are protected from major sins but may experience minor lapses or leave what is more preferable, and Allah, in His mercy, educates them through trials. Jonah’s blameworthiness was that he fled before receiving the command to do so, giving up on his people prematurely. The whale’s belly became the crucible in which that blame would be purified. This image is the ultimate symbol of constriction after flight. Jonah had sought to escape the confines of his difficult mission, only to be confined within a much tighter space, under the sea, in the dark, alone. The physical darkness of the whale’s belly, the darkness of the ocean, and the darkness of the night combined to create a threefold darkness that he would later describe in his supplication: “There is no god except You; exalted are You. Indeed, I have been of the wrongdoers.” The lesson is profound: when a believer, especially one in a position of responsibility, makes a mistake, the way to redemption is not further flight but immediate repentance and glorification. The whale was not a punishment in the sense of damnation, but a divine detention designed to bring Jonah to his knees in humble admission. The Meccan believers, some of whom might have wavered under persecution, are reminded that even a prophet experienced a moment of frailty, yet the door of return was wide open. The whale, a creature that normally evokes fear, became an instrument of divine tutelage, preparing Jonah for a greater mission.
143 | Had he not ˹constantly˺ glorified ˹Allah˺, | The surah now reveals the key that unlocked Jonah’s rescue. “Had he not constantly glorified Allah.” The verse introduces a conditional statement that will continue in the next verse, but this clause alone is packed with meaning. It points to the spiritual habit that Jonah had cultivated throughout his life before this trial: he was among those who constantly perform tasbīḥ, glorifying Allah by saying “Subḥān Allāh” (Glory be to Allah) and engaging in prayer and remembrance. The word “constantly” (musabbiḥīn) indicates a frequent, sustained practice, not just a one-time utterance. This glorification is what eventually saved him. In his normal life, Jonah had filled his days and nights with the remembrance of Allah, and when the darkness of the whale’s belly closed in, that deep-rooted habit surfaced. He turned not to despair but to glorification and the acknowledgment of his own fault. The famous supplication he made inside the whale is taught by the Prophet Muhammad as a powerful prayer for distress: “Lā ilāha illā anta subḥānaka innī kuntu min aẓ-ẓālimīn” (There is no god but You, glory be to You; I have been among the wrongdoers). This was his glorification: declaring Allah’s transcendence and his own shortcoming. The verse teaches that the daily, consistent worship and remembrance we practice are not just for good times; they build a spiritual reservoir that sustains us in the darkest moments. Jonah’s glorification was his lifeline. If he had not been among those who habitually glorify, he might have reacted with panic or anger, but his tongue and heart were trained to turn immediately to Allah. For the believers in Mecca, enduring persecution and the temptation to despair, this was a powerful encouragement to maintain their prayers, their recitation of the Quran, and their remembrance, for these are the deposits that will save them in times of crisis. The verse also highlights the mercy of Allah, who looks at the heartfelt glorification of a blameworthy servant and grants him a way out. It is not the magnitude of the mistake that determines the outcome, but the sincerity and constancy of the servant’s connection to his Lord.
144 | he would have certainly remained in its belly until the Day of Resurrection. | The conditional statement is completed with a terrifying hypothetical. “He would have certainly remained in its belly until the Day of Resurrection.” If not for his prior habit of glorification and the sincere repentance it produced, Jonah’s prison would have become his grave, and the whale’s belly would have been his holding place until the end of time. This does not necessarily mean the whale would have lived until the Resurrection; rather, it means the belly would have been his tomb, and he would have remained dead and entombed there, his fate sealed without deliverance, only to be raised on that Day for judgment. Some scholars interpret that the whale’s belly would have been his abode of punishment, a prison until the Resurrection, as a recompense for his blameworthy state. The severity of this image underscores the absolute seriousness of abandoning a prophetic mission without divine leave, and it magnifies the mercy that actually occurred. The verse serves a dual purpose. On one hand, it highlights the virtue and saving power of glorification. Jonah’s tasbīḥ was so valuable that it transformed a potential eternal prison into a temporary trial and a place of spiritual rebirth. On the other hand, it is a stark warning that no one, not even a prophet, can escape the consequences of their actions without sincere return to Allah. The “until the Day of Resurrection” phrase also connects this narrative to the overall theme of the surah: the resurrection is a reality, and the deeds of this life have enduring consequences. The Meccan pagans, who denied resurrection, are indirectly told that even a prophet inside a whale would have faced that Day, so their denial does not change the reality. For the believer, the verse inspires immense hope and immense fear—hope that glorification can rescue from the darkest pit, and fear that without it, even a prophet would be lost. It is a call to never abandon the rope of remembrance, especially in moments of weakness, because the alternative is unthinkable. The verse thus sets up the actual deliverance, which follows immediately, as an act of pure divine grace responding to sincere repentance.
145 | But We cast him onto the open ˹shore˺, ˹totally˺ worn out, | After the whale’s belly and the glorification, Allah’s mercy physically manifests. “But We cast him onto the open shore, totally worn out.” The word “But” signals the turning point from the hypothetical horror to the actual salvation. Allah commanded the whale to eject Jonah and deposit him safely on a beach. The phrase “cast him” (fa-nabadhnāhu) indicates a forceful expulsion; the whale was an obedient instrument casting him out onto the land. The location was an “open shore” (ʿarāʾ), meaning a barren, empty stretch of land without shelter, trees, or shade. Jonah was “totally worn out” (saqīm). His body had been affected by the digestive fluids of the whale, leaving him weak, hairless, and with extremely sensitive skin, like a newborn baby. He was physically broken, yet he was alive and free. This moment of deliverance is a visual mercy: the vast open space contrasts with the constricting darkness of the whale, and the fresh air contrasts with the stifling interior. However, being cast onto an open shore, exposed to the sun, and in a state of total physical exhaustion created a new form of vulnerability. He needed shelter and nourishment. The divine care that had delivered him from the sea would continue to provide for him. The sequence teaches that deliverance from one trial often places us in a situation of need that drives us to rely even more on Allah. Jonah could not pat himself on the back; he was utterly helpless, a naked, sick man on an empty beach. This was a complete reset of his strength, preparing him for the second phase of his mission. For the Meccan believers, who might have been physically weakened by torture, hunger, and exile, this verse was a picture of hope: the same Lord who cast Jonah onto the shore will restore you and provide for you. The humbling state of “totally worn out” is not a sign of Allah’s displeasure but often a prelude to His nurturing care, as the next verse shows with the plant. The lesson is that after a period of severe trial and repentance, restoration comes, but the servant remains acutely aware of his total dependence on Allah.
146 | and caused a squash plant to grow over him. | The divine care after deliverance is beautifully illustrated with a specific detail of nature. “And caused a squash plant to grow over him.” The Arabic word “yaqṭīn” refers to a squash, gourd, or pumpkin plant with large, broad leaves. This plant was miraculously caused to grow rapidly over Jonah to provide him with essential protection and healing. The broad leaves acted as a canopy, shielding his sensitive, weakened skin from the scorching sun. The plant also likely attracted insects away, provided a cooling microclimate through its broad leaves, and perhaps yielded fruit that he could eat. The fact that it was specifically a squash plant is significant; the Prophet Muhammad mentioned that it was a gourd, and he had a fondness for it. Squash plants are known for their leaves’ ability to provide shade and their natural resistance to flies and insects, making them an ideal covering for a person in a vulnerable state. This small but profound detail shows the meticulous and tender mercy of Allah. He did not just deliver Jonah to dry land and leave him; He provided immediate, customized care through a plant that addressed his exact needs at that moment. The lesson is that when we are at our weakest, having been delivered from a major crisis, Allah’s mercy extends to the smallest details of our recovery. The believer learns to see in the natural world the hand of the Provider. A simple gourd plant becomes a sign of divine love and attention. The plant growing “over him” suggests that Jonah lay under its shade, recovering his strength, both physically and spiritually, and reflecting on the immense grace that had been shown to him. For the Meccan Muslims, who were in a harsh desert environment often lacking shade and food, this verse was a promise that Allah, who shaded a prophet on an empty shore, would provide for them in their hardship. It also serves as a reminder that the signs of Allah are everywhere in creation; even the shade of a leaf can be a reminder of the One who sent it. The story now moves from Jonah’s personal recovery to the renewal of his public mission.
147 | We ˹later˺ sent him ˹back˺ to ˹his city of˺ at least one hundred thousand people, | After Jonah recovered, his mission was reinstated. “We later sent him back to his city of at least one hundred thousand people.” The word “later” indicates a passage of time for healing and divine pardon. Once Jonah was fully restored, Allah commanded him to return to the very city he had fled. This shows that his repentance was accepted and his prophetic commission was renewed. The city is described as having “at least one hundred thousand people” or even more (the Arabic “miʾati alfin aw yazīdūn” implies a large number, possibly more than a hundred thousand). This detail emphasizes that Jonah had been sent to a major urban centre, a massive population, and his abandoning them was a serious matter because of the great number of souls at stake. His return was not to a small, easy task but to a huge city, yet this time he went not on his own impulse but by Allah’s clear instruction. The surah previously mentioned that the chosen servants are saved and honoured; here we see that Jonah’s honour included being sent back as a prophet to a vast nation that would now believe in him. This was a greater triumph than any he could have imagined. The verse also hints at the magnitude of the mercy that was to descend: over a hundred thousand people would be guided. For the Prophet Muhammad, who was facing a city of stubborn idolaters, this was a reminder that the outcome is in Allah’s hands; a nation that seems hopeless today may tomorrow become a nation of believers. For the believer, the reinstatement of Jonah teaches that failure and mistakes do not define our end if we repent. Allah can restore us to even greater service than before. The mission is not over until Allah says it is, and His mercy can transform a deserter into a successful guide of multitudes. The sheer number of people also underscores the value of every soul; Allah cared about those hundred thousand and sent Jonah back to save them.
148 | who then believed ˹in him˺, so We allowed them enjoyment for a while. | The fruit of Jonah’s returned mission is now stated. “Who then believed in him, so We allowed them enjoyment for a while.” When Jonah returned and called his people again, this time, they responded with faith. The verse says they believed “in him,” meaning they believed in his message and in Allah. The entire city, over a hundred thousand people, embraced monotheism and turned from their idols. This was an extraordinary mass conversion, a unique event in prophetic history where a whole nation accepted the message without experiencing destruction. In other stories, the rejection of a messenger led to annihilation, but Jonah’s people, upon seeing the signs of impending punishment, repented and were saved. The Quran elsewhere mentions that the people of Jonah were the only ones who believed at the last moment and were spared. The phrase “so We allowed them enjoyment for a while” indicates that after their belief, Allah extended their lives in comfort and security, letting them enjoy the good things of the world until their appointed term. Their enjoyment was not an eternal reward—that is in the Hereafter—but a temporary respite in this life because of their faith. This demonstrates that collective faith can bring worldly blessings and the lifting of divine punishment. For the Meccan pagans, this was both a warning and an invitation: unlike Jonah’s people, who believed before it was too late, they were still persisting in denial. If they would believe, they too could be granted enjoyment for a while instead of facing the imminent punishment that the Prophet was warning them about. For the believer, this verse shows the power of sincere repentance on a societal level. It also highlights that the ultimate goal is not just worldly enjoyment, but the enjoyment is a natural consequence of aligning with divine will. The “while” is the period of their lifespan, after which they would face the final judgment as believers, with their eternal reward secure. The story of Jonah ends here in this surah on a note of mercy and triumph, perfectly illustrating the refrain: the chosen servants are rewarded, and even those who falter are welcomed back when they glorify Allah and repent. It is a fitting conclusion to the series of prophets, showing the full spectrum of the faithful servant’s journey, from trial to redemption to massive success.
149 | Ask them ˹O Prophet˺ if your Lord has daughters, while the pagans ˹prefer to˺ have sons. | The surah transitions from the stories of the prophets to a direct confrontation with the Meccan pagans’ absurd beliefs about Allah. The verse commands, “Ask them O Prophet if your Lord has daughters, while the pagans prefer to have sons.” This is a rhetorical question designed to expose the deep contradiction and insult in their theology. The pagans of Mecca believed that the angels were female beings and that they were the daughters of Allah. They worshipped these angels as intercessors. At the same time, these same pagans considered daughters to be a source of shame and disgrace for themselves. When one of them received news of the birth of a daughter, his face would darken, and he would hide from people in embarrassment, as the Quran describes elsewhere. They even practiced female infanticide, burying their baby girls alive. So the Prophet is instructed to ask them: how can you attribute to Allah what you yourselves despise and reject? If daughters are so lowly in your eyes that you bury them, how dare you claim that Allah has daughters? The question is a devastating logical and moral blow. It shows that their belief is not only false but also hypocritical and insulting to the Divine Majesty. They assign to the Creator the very thing they consider inferior. The phrase “your Lord” reminds them that He is their own Lord, the One who provides for them, yet they ascribe to Him what they detest for themselves. The verse forces a moment of introspection: they must either admit that daughters are not shameful—which contradicts their entire culture—or admit that their attribution of daughters to Allah is a monstrous falsehood. Either way, their idolatry stands exposed. For the believer, this verse trains us to recognize logical inconsistencies in false beliefs and to worship Allah in a way that is worthy of His perfection. We must never attribute to Allah anything we would not consider perfect.
150 | Or ˹ask them˺ if We created the angels as females right before their eyes. | The surah follows up the first question with a second, even more pointed one. “Or ask them if We created the angels as females right before their eyes.” This is a challenge to their claim of knowledge about the angels. The pagans asserted that the angels were female, as if they had witnessed their creation. Allah asks: were you present when We created the angels to know their gender? Did you see them being formed? The obvious answer is no. They have no eyewitness testimony, no revelation, and no evidence whatsoever. Their claim is pure speculation and a fabrication inherited from their forefathers. The phrase “right before their eyes” highlights the absurdity of their certainty. They speak with such conviction about the unseen, yet they were never there. This is a recurring Quranic method: to challenge baseless claims by demanding evidence that the claimant logically cannot provide. It exposes that the pagan belief system is built on nothing but assumption and blind following. The mention of “We created” also establishes that the angels are created beings, not divine offspring. Allah is the sole Creator; the angels are His handiwork, submissive servants who are neither male nor female in the sense of having gender as a biological function, or they are a creation whose reality is known only to Allah, but the crucial point is that assigning them a specific gender and calling them Allah’s children is a fabrication without any foundation. For the Meccans, this question stripped away any pretense of divine authority for their idolatry. For the believer, this verse underscores the principle that matters of the unseen must be based on revelation, not on speculation or inherited myths. We accept what Allah tells us about the angels—that they are honoured servants—and we reject what has no evidence. It also instills humility about the unseen; we cannot speak of it without knowledge from the Creator.
151 | Indeed, it is one of their ˹outrageous˺ fabrications to say, | After asking the rhetorical questions, the Quran now issues a direct verdict on their claims. “Indeed, it is one of their outrageous fabrications to say.” The word “indeed” (a-lā) is an emphatic particle drawing attention to the seriousness of the statement. The Arabic word “ifk” translated as “fabrications” denotes a severe lie, a slander, something completely made up and contrary to reality. The phrase “outrageous” captures the additional nuance that this is not a small mistake but a monstrous falsehood. The pagans’ claim that angels are Allah’s daughters is labelled as pure ifk. This is a harsh condemnation, but it is justified by the gravity of slandering the Creator. Fabricating lies against Allah is one of the greatest sins. By calling it a fabrication, the Quran dismisses any possibility that there is truth or even a reasonable misinterpretation behind it. It is not a matter of scholarly difference; it is a lie. The verse also notes that this is “one of” their fabrications, implying they have many other false beliefs, but this one is particularly egregious. The directness of the language is meant to shock the pagans into realizing what they are actually doing: they are inventing lies about the Lord of the worlds. For the believers who were constantly bombarded with these pagan claims, this verse provided clear, authoritative language to refute them. It also serves as a timeless warning against attributing to Allah anything that He has not revealed. In our time, people may fabricate ideas about God based on philosophy, mysticism, or cultural myths; all such fabrications are condemned by the Quran. The truth about Allah is only what He has taught us through His messengers. The verse sets up the exact quote of their lie, which follows immediately, leaving no doubt about what is being denounced.
152 | “Allah has children.” They are simply liars. | The specific fabrication is now quoted: “Allah has children.” This concise statement sums up the claim that the angels are Allah’s daughters, and by extension it includes any claim of divine offspring, such as the Christian claim that Jesus is the son of God, or the Jewish claim that Ezra is the son of God. The Quranic rejection is absolute: “They are simply liars.” There is no softening of the language. The word “simply” (innahum la-kādhibūn) uses an emphatic structure, affirming without any doubt that they are deliberate liars. The claim that Allah has children is a falsehood that strikes at the very heart of tawheed. It implies that the Creator has a need, a partner, or a lineage, all of which are impossible for the Self-Sufficient, Eternal One. The Meccan pagans did not necessarily believe that Allah had children in the biological sense through procreation, but they called the angels His daughters as a term of honour, yet the Quran refutes even that metaphorical usage because it implies division in the divine essence and leads to worship of the creation. The accusation of lying is severe because the pagans had no evidence for their claim; they were merely repeating what their forefathers said. Furthermore, they themselves were inconsistent, as the next verse will highlight. The verse reinforces that truth is not determined by majority opinion or cultural heritage but by revelation and reason. For the believers, this clear declaration fortified their faith against the pressure to compromise with polytheistic beliefs. It also teaches that when it comes to the fundamental doctrines of faith, there is no room for ambiguity or polite hedging; falsehood must be called out as falsehood. The starkness of “they are simply liars” is a divine judgment that should inspire a healthy fear of inventing anything about Allah without knowledge.
153 | Has He chosen daughters over sons? | The surah continues to dismantle the pagan claim by exposing its sheer irrationality. “Has He chosen daughters over sons?” The question is rhetorical and loaded with sarcasm aimed at the pagans’ own values. As mentioned earlier, the pagan Arabs regarded daughters as inferior and shameful for themselves, while sons were a source of pride, strength, and honour. If they themselves prefer sons over daughters, how can they believe that the Lord of the worlds, the Most High, would choose for Himself what they deem to be the lesser, inferior option, leaving the superior for them? Is Allah, in their imagination, picking the daughters and leaving the sons for the humans? This would imply either that Allah is foolish or that they are more discerning than Allah—both conclusions being blasphemous. The question forces them to confront the contradiction between their cultural values and their theological claims. It also subtly defends the dignity of women: the Quran does not say that daughters are inherently inferior, but it uses the pagans’ own prejudice against them. If they insist on their prejudice, their doctrine is absurd. If they abandon their prejudice, then the whole foundation of their idolatry collapses because they no longer have a reason to worship what they consider equal. The question is a masterful use of logic to corner the opponent. For the believer, it shows the importance of consistency in one’s worldview. False beliefs often contain internal contradictions that, when exposed, reveal their bankruptcy. It also teaches that Allah’s attributes are perfect and beyond any comparison with human attributes. He is far above having children, needing nothing, and His choices are based on infinite wisdom, not on human biases. The verse is part of a sustained attack on the idea of divine offspring, leading to the next challenge to use reason.
154 | What is the matter with you? How do you judge? | Following the exposure of their contradictory logic, the verse expresses astonishment at their thought process. “What is the matter with you? How do you judge?” This is a sharp rebuke, a rhetorical expression of bewilderment at their irrational judgment. The phrase “What is the matter with you?” (mā lakum) is used in Arabic to express amazement at someone’s strange behaviour or faulty reasoning. It suggests that there is something deeply wrong with their mental or spiritual state that has led them to such a preposterous conclusion. Then “How do you judge?” questions the very basis of their decision-making. On what criteria are they basing their judgment that Allah has daughters, that these daughters are worthy of worship, and that they are intercessors? Their judgment is completely skewed because it is not based on evidence, revelation, or sound reason, but on blind imitation and whims. The verse calls them to step back and critically evaluate their own cognitive process. This is an appeal to the innate human intelligence (fiṭrah) that recognizes contradiction and falsehood. The pagans were not unintelligent people; they were merchants, poets, and orators. But their intelligence was clouded by tradition and self-interest. The verse essentially says: use your minds! How can you come to such a verdict? For the believers, this verse is an encouragement to be people of sound judgment who base their beliefs on clear evidence, not on cultural pressures. It teaches that we must constantly audit our own judgments: are we judging according to the Quran and Sunnah, or according to what is popular and inherited? The question “How do you judge?” is timeless, challenging every individual to examine their standards and to abandon any judgment that contradicts divine guidance. It leads naturally into the next verse, which asks if they will not be mindful, appealing to the same faculty of reason and conscience.
155 | Will you not then be mindful? | After the sharp rebuke, the verse softens into an invitation to reflection. “Will you not then be mindful?” The Arabic word “tadhakkarūn” comes from dhikr, meaning remembrance, mindfulness, and taking heed. The question suggests that the truth is already imprinted in their souls and that the arguments presented should be enough to awaken that dormant knowledge. Mindfulness means connecting the dots: you know that you prefer sons; you claim Allah has daughters; you have no evidence; you realize the contradiction; so will you not then be mindful and correct your belief? It is a merciful pause in the relentless logical assault, offering them a chance to save themselves by simply being mindful and returning to the truth. The Quran frequently uses this approach: after presenting proofs and exposing falsehood, it appeals to the heart and conscience. The faculty of mindfulness is what separates the guided from the heedless. The pagans had become so engrossed in their idolatrous traditions that they had stopped being mindful of the simple truths of reason and the innate knowledge of Allah’s oneness. This verse calls them back to that mindfulness. For the believer, it is a reminder that the Quran is full of signs that should increase our mindfulness. Reading these verses should not be a mechanical recitation but a catalyst for deep reflection on the greatness of Allah and the absurdity of any belief that detracts from His perfection. The question also implies that if they do not become mindful, there is something blocking their fitrah—perhaps arrogance or vested interest. The believer must guard against such blocks by constantly engaging in dhikr and self-evaluation. The progression from “What is the matter with you?” to “How do you judge?” to “Will you not then be mindful?” is a perfect psychological and spiritual trajectory: exposing the error, questioning the judgment, and inviting the healing of mindfulness. It is a model of how daʿwah should progress, combining logical rigor with heartfelt appeal.
156 | Or do you have ˹any˺ compelling proof? | The surah now issues a direct challenge: produce your evidence. “Or do you have any compelling proof?” The word “compelling proof” translates “sulṭān mubīn,” which literally means a clear authority or a manifest warrant. This refers to a definitive proof, whether rational or revealed, that can justify their claims about Allah having daughters or angels being female. It is an open invitation to bring forth their strongest argument. The rhetorical structure “Or do you have” implies that after all the logical contradictions exposed, the only thing that could possibly salvage their position is a solid proof. But the challenge is laced with the certainty that they possess no such proof. If they had any scripture from a previous prophet, any authentic tradition, any rational demonstration, they would have presented it. Their silence is deafening. This verse establishes a fundamental Islamic principle: beliefs, especially those concerning the divine, must be based on proof, not on conjecture, blind imitation, or cultural heritage. Allah Himself, through the Quran, provides proofs for tawheed, while the polytheists have nothing but “ifk” (fabrications). The challenge also highlights the mercy of Allah, who does not demand belief without evidence but rather provides clear proofs and then asks the deniers to bring their own if they are truthful. For the Meccan pagans, this was a humiliating exposure of their intellectual poverty. They prided themselves on their eloquence and wisdom, yet they could not produce a single compelling proof for the core of their religion. For the believer, this verse instills confidence that Islam is a faith rooted in clear proofs—the Quran, the miracles, the rational arguments for tawheed—and it is not a blind faith. It also serves as a methodology for evaluating any claim: demand a “sulṭān mubīn.” If a person makes a religious claim, ask for their proof from divine revelation. If they cannot produce it, their claim is worthless. This protects the faith from innovations and superstitions.
157 | Then bring ˹us˺ your scripture, if what you say is true! | The challenge is intensified by specifying the type of proof they might claim to have. “Then bring us your scripture, if what you say is true!” The pagans had no revealed book, but the challenge is framed as a taunt and a demonstration of the impossibility of their task. If they were truthful in claiming that their idolatrous beliefs were sanctioned by God, they must have received some divine scripture. Allah demands that they produce this supposed scripture. The word “your scripture” (kitābakum) implies a book from Allah that authorizes their polytheism. The very idea is absurd because all revealed scriptures, from the scrolls of Abraham to the Torah and the Gospel, preach pure monotheism. There is no scripture that says Allah has daughters or that angels are female and should be worshipped. By asking them to bring such a book, the Quran highlights that their religion is entirely man-made, devoid of any heavenly origin. This also indirectly vindicates the Prophet Muhammad, who came with a clear Scripture—the Quran—which consistently condemns shirk. The challenge “if what you say is true” is a standard Quranic phrase that puts the burden of proof on the claimant. It also implies that their words are false, as they will never be able to meet the challenge. For the Meccans, who were familiar with the concept of scripture from the Jews and Christians, this argument was devastating because they had always admitted they had no book. For the believer, this verse underscores the centrality of scripture as the source of authentic religious teaching. Any belief or practice that contradicts the Quran and Sunnah is baseless. The verse also encourages the study and preservation of the Quran, as it is the true Scripture that refutes all falsehood. The challenge to bring their scripture is ultimately an invitation to abandon their fabricated religion and embrace the divine book that has been sent.
158 | They have also established a ˹marital˺ relationship between Him and the jinn. Yet the jinn ˹themselves˺ know well that such people will certainly be brought ˹for punishment˺. | The surah now adds another dimension of the pagans’ false beliefs. “They have also established a marital relationship between Him and the jinn.” Some factions of the pagan Arabs, particularly from certain tribes, claimed that the jinn were related to Allah, even that He had taken spouses from among the jinn and that the angels were the offspring of this union. This was an even more grotesque fabrication, attributing to the Lord of the worlds a marital bond with a creation. The word “marital relationship” (nasab) signifies kinship through marriage or lineage. By claiming such a thing, they were anthropomorphizing Allah in the most degraded way, imagining Him as a being who takes a spouse and begets children, similar to pagan mythologies where gods marry and have divine offspring. The verse declares that the jinn themselves know well that those who say this will be brought for punishment. The jinn are the very beings they are slandering, yet the jinn, who are accountable creatures, know that this claim is a lie and that those who spread it will face divine judgment. This is a powerful refutation: even the jinn they supposedly revere disown this belief and know its consequences. It indicates that the truth about Allah is known among the unseen creation, and the pagans are far more astray than the jinn in this regard. The phrase “will certainly be brought” echoes the surah’s earlier language about the disbelievers being brought to punishment. The jinn are witnesses against them on the Day of Judgment. For the believers, this verse reinforces that shirk can take many vile forms, and all of them are far removed from the majesty of Allah. It also reminds us that the unseen beings, like the jinn, are not to be worshipped or elevated; they themselves are servants of Allah who will be judged. The verse serves to completely dismantle the interconnected pagan myths, leaving no shred of legitimacy to any of their claims about divine progeny or partnership.
159 | Glorified is Allah far above what they claim! | After exposing the fabrications, the absurdities, and the contradictions, the surah breaks into the declaration of tanzīh (divine transcendence). “Glorified is Allah far above what they claim!” The Arabic “Subḥāna Allāh” is a profound phrase meaning that Allah is utterly perfect, free from any flaw, defect, or association. This glorification is the natural and necessary response to hearing such vile claims about the Creator. It is as if the entire discourse leads up to this moment of clearing Allah’s name from all the slander they have uttered. The phrase “far above” (ʿammā yaṣifūn) indicates a complete, unbridgeable distance between His perfect reality and their false descriptions. Whatever they claim—daughters, sons, spouses, partners, weaknesses—Allah is elevated far above it all, in His essence, attributes, and actions. This glorification is not just a statement of belief; it is an act of worship. By saying “Subḥāna Allāh,” the believer purifies his heart from any improper thought about Allah and affirms His absolute perfection. In the context of the surah, which began with angels glorifying Allah and lining up in ranks, this glorification brings the thematic arc full circle. The angels themselves glorify Allah, and now the believers are taught to do the same in response to the pagans’ blasphemies. For the Meccans, this was a direct refutation of their beliefs and a call to join in the glorification of the One true God. For the believer, this verse is a constant reminder to maintain the purity of tawheed in the heart and on the tongue. Whenever we encounter ideas that compromise Allah’s oneness or perfection, we should immediately resort to tasbīḥ, seeking refuge in the glorification of our Lord. The verse also carries a tone of reassurance: no matter what lies people invent, Allah’s majesty remains untouched and undiminished. It is the lie that will perish, and the truth of Subḥāna Allāh will abide forever.
160 | But not the chosen servants of Allah. | The surah inserts the crucial exception once more, but here its meaning is slightly nuanced by the context. “But not the chosen servants of Allah.” This phrase has appeared as a refrain throughout the surah, indicating that the chosen servants are saved from punishment and granted honourable mention. In this immediate context, scholars offer two interconnected interpretations. First, it means that the chosen servants of Allah—the prophets, the angels, and the sincere believers—do not ascribe such false claims to Allah. While the pagans fabricate lies about Allah having children or consorts, the chosen servants glorify Him and declare Him far above such things. They are the ones who say “Subḥāna Allāh” sincerely. Second, it can be seen as a continuation of the refutation: the pagans who are brought for punishment do not include the chosen servants, who are exempt from that fate. Both meanings are harmonious. The verse thus draws a clear dividing line between the liars and the truthful, between those who slander Allah and those who glorify Him. The chosen servants are the ones with pure hearts, like Abraham, who came to his Lord with a sound heart, and like the angels who are lined up in ranks glorifying Allah. They are the ones who know the truth about their Lord and speak it. The Meccan Muslims, by reciting and believing these verses, are counted among these chosen servants, as they disown the pagan claims. The verse encourages the believer to ensure they are on the side of the chosen servants in both word and deed, actively glorifying Allah and refuting falsehood. It also beautifully sets up the following verses where the angels themselves will speak, declaring their stations and their glorification, proving that they are indeed the chosen servants who would never claim divinity or kinship with Allah. The exception provides a moment of clarity and hope amidst the condemnation, reaffirming that the path of safety and truth is the path of sincere worship and transcendence.
161 | Surely you ˹pagans˺ and whatever ˹idols˺ you worship | The surah now turns to address the Meccan idolaters directly, drawing a decisive conclusion from the preceding arguments about their false beliefs. The verse states, “Surely you pagans and whatever idols you worship.” The word “surely” (fa-innakum) conveys absolute certainty, leaving no room for doubt. Allah speaks directly to the polytheists, grouping them together with the objects of their worship. The phrase “you pagans” addresses the human worshippers, while “whatever idols you worship” encompasses all their false deities—the stone statues, the angels whom they claimed were daughters of Allah, the jinn they associated with Him, and any other beings or objects they called upon besides Allah. This grouping is deeply significant because it places the worshipper and the worshipped on the same level of powerlessness. The idols are not presented as mighty gods who might defend themselves; they are merely “whatever,” a dismissive term that strips them of any pretense to divinity. In the earlier verses, the surah demolished the intellectual basis of idolatry, showing that these false gods can neither speak, nor eat, nor create, nor benefit, nor harm. Now, having established their utter impotence, Allah lumps the pagans together with their idols as equally helpless before His power. The Meccan pagans who took pride in their noble lineage and their mighty gods are being told that they and their gods are, in reality, nothing before the Lord of the worlds. This sets the stage for the next two verses, which will describe the absolute limitation of their power to mislead. The verse also functions as a transition from the refutation of their creed to the affirmation of Allah’s sovereign control over guidance and misguidance. By addressing them directly, the Quran shakes them out of the role of distant observers and forces them to see themselves as the subjects of this divine judgment. For the believer, this verse reinforces the understanding that all false gods—whether ancient idols or modern-day attachments—are ultimately powerless, and those who cling to them share in their weakness.
162 | can never lure ˹anyone˺ away from Him | The verse continues the thought, revealing the crucial limitation of the pagans and their idols. “Can never lure anyone away from Him.” The word “lure” translates a verb that carries the meaning of enticing, seducing, and leading astray through temptation. The pagans actively tried to lure believers away from the worship of Allah. They used social pressure, mockery, economic threats, and even promises of reward to try to make the early Muslims revert to idolatry. However, Allah declares here that they “can never” succeed in luring away anyone whom Allah protects. The negation is absolute and eternal. It is not that they might have limited success; rather, their entire effort is futile against those whom Allah has willed to guide. This is a powerful reassurance for the oppressed believers in Mecca who were facing immense pressure to abandon their faith. The verse tells them that no matter how persuasive or threatening the pagans become, the faithful heart that Allah has filled with the light of tawheed cannot be stolen by idolaters and their lifeless gods. The idols themselves have no power to lure; they are inert. The human pagans have tongues and schemes, but they too are powerless to change the hearts that Allah has secured. This reflects the Quranic principle that guidance and misguidance are ultimately in Allah’s hands. The Prophet could not guide those he loved; likewise, the enemies of faith cannot misguide those Allah loves. The verse also implies that the pagans and their idols are themselves lured away from the truth; how then can they lure others to it? They are the blinded leading the blind. For the believer today, this verse is a fortress of comfort. When we face pressures to compromise our faith—whether from society, media, or personal relationships—we must remember that no created power can lure us away from Allah unless we willingly follow. The protection is in holding firmly to the rope of Allah. This verse also serves as a warning to those who actively try to lead believers astray: their efforts are doomed, and they will only increase their own burden. The power to lure is an illusion; true hearts are in the hand of the Merciful.
163 | except those ˹destined˺ to burn in Hell. | The absolute statement of the previous verse is now qualified with a crucial exception. “Except those destined to burn in Hell.” This does not mean that the pagans and their idols have the power to lure even these people; rather, it explains that the only ones who will fall into the trap of idolatry and misguidance are those who are already written by Allah’s foreknowledge to be among the inhabitants of Hell. Their being lured away is the manifestation of their own choice and their predetermined fate based on that choice. The phrase “destined to burn” (ṣālū al-jaḥīm) refers to those who have sealed their own doom through persistent rejection of truth, arrogance, and love of falsehood. They are not innocent victims of powerful seduction; they are willing participants whose hearts are already inclined to Hell. The pagans might think they are successfully luring people, but in reality, they are only attracting those who were already on the path to Hell. The true believers are immune. This exception is a profound theological point: Allah’s knowledge encompasses all things, and He knows who will choose disbelief and who will choose faith. The “luring” is a test that reveals the inner state of a person. The one who is destined to burn will find the lure attractive and will follow it; the one who is destined for Paradise will see through its falsehood. This does not negate human free will; rather, it asserts that Allah’s knowledge is perfect, and He has already written the outcomes based on the choices people will freely make. For the Meccan believers, this was a clarification that those who apostatized under torture or persuasion were not truly guided in the first place; their faith was not rooted. The believers should not grieve over them. For the contemporary believer, this verse inspires a healthy fear and self-reflection. We should never be complacent, but we trust that if we sincerely seek Allah, He will protect us from the lures that lead to Hell. The verse also underscores the justice of Allah: those who burn in Hell are not there because the pagans overpowered them, but because they themselves chose the path of destruction. The lures are only effective on the hearts that have already turned away from the truth.
164 | ˹The angels respond,˺ “There is not one of us without an assigned station ˹of worship˺. | After exposing the pagan claims about the angels, the surah now allows the angels themselves to speak, through divine revelation, to clarify their true nature. The verse says, “The angels respond, ‘There is not one of us without an assigned station of worship.’” The pagans claimed the angels were daughters of Allah and worshipped them as divine intercessors. The angels, who are the chosen servants of Allah mentioned in the exception earlier, now refute this blasphemy in the most beautiful way. They declare their complete servitude. The phrase “There is not one of us” (wa-mā minnā illā) is an absolute negation, meaning every single angel, without exception, has a specific, assigned station. The word “assigned station” (maqām maʿlūm) refers to a fixed position of worship and service. The angels are not aimless beings; they are organized in a perfect hierarchy, each one knowing its place and its duty, never overstepping it. This echoes the very opening of the surah, where the angels are described as being “lined up in ranks.” They do not descend to earth without permission, they do not intercede without leave, and they certainly do not claim divinity. Their “station” is one of complete obedience and humility before the Majestic Lord. This response is both a rebuke to the pagans and a lesson for humanity. The angels, despite their immense power and proximity to Allah, are utterly submissive. They do not consider themselves worthy of worship; they are worshippers. For the Meccans who elevated these angels to divine status, the angels themselves declare, “We are only servants with assigned duties.” The believer learns that true honour lies in knowing one’s station before Allah and fulfilling it with devotion. Every human being also has an assigned station in the sense of their role, responsibilities, and the path laid out for them by Allah. The key is to embrace servitude as the angels do. This verse also provides a powerful model of monotheism: even the highest creatures in the universe are but slaves. So how can a human worship another human, a stone, or a celestial body? The angels’ testimony dismantles the foundation of all shirk by revealing that the entire unseen realm is structured upon the principle of servitude to the One God.
165 | We are indeed the ones lined up in ranks ˹for Allah˺. | The angels continue their testimony, now specifying the nature of their station. “We are indeed the ones lined up in ranks for Allah.” This is a direct reference to the first verse of the surah, “By those lined up in ranks,” creating a beautiful literary and theological loop. The angels confirm that the oath taken by Allah at the beginning of the surah was about them. They stand in disciplined rows, perfectly aligned, waiting for the divine command to issue forth. The phrase “lined up in ranks” (ṣāffūn) paints a picture of orderly, humble servitude. There is no jostling, no competition, no rebellion. Each angel knows its place in the row, and the rows extend as far as the divine will determines. This formation indicates their readiness for any command—whether it be worship, delivering revelation, executing punishment, or managing the natural world. The addition of “for Allah” emphasizes that their lining up is exclusively for His sake, in His service, and by His command. They are not a chaotic assembly of autonomous spirits; they are a perfectly disciplined army of worshippers. For the pagans who claimed the angels were independent gods or daughters, this statement is a devastating refutation. Gods do not stand in rows waiting for orders; servants do. The angels themselves reject any divinity and affirm their total subordination. The believer, reading this, should reflect on the beauty of order and discipline in worship. The angels’ ranks are a model for the rows of worshippers in the mosque, where believers stand shoulder to shoulder, equal and united, before their Lord. This verse also instills a sense of awe at the unseen world: above us, there are beings of light who are perpetually arrayed in ranks, glorifying Allah without fatigue. The cosmic order is not random but highly structured. It further reinforces that the ultimate purpose of existence is worship, and the angels are the perfect exemplars of that purpose. We, as humans, are called to join them in spirit, aligning our lives in obedience to the One they serve.
166 | And we are indeed the ones ˹constantly˺ glorifying ˹His praise˺.” | The angels conclude their testimony with the second part of their station, which is the very activity that defines them. “And we are indeed the ones constantly glorifying His praise.” The word translated as “glorifying” is “musabbiḥūn,” derived from tasbīḥ, which means to declare Allah free from all imperfections. The addition of “constantly” indicates that this is their perpetual state. They never cease from glorifying Allah and praising Him. This echoes the earlier verse of the surah, “and those who recite the Reminder,” linking the three opening oaths to this angelic confession. The angels glorify and praise Allah in unison, and their glorification is itself an act of worship that they perform without boredom or lapse. Their praise of Allah is inseparable from their glorification; they praise His attributes of beauty and perfection while declaring Him above any defect. This stands in stark opposition to the pagan claims, which attributed to Allah the imperfection of having daughters and consorts. The angels, who were the very subjects of those claims, testify that they spend their existence negating such imperfections. They are the first to pronounce “Subḥāna Allāh,” clearing His name from all the slander the polytheists utter. For the believer, this verse invites participation in the cosmic chorus of tasbīḥ. When we say “Subḥāna Allāh” after our prayers, when we reflect on His creation and feel our hearts moved to glorify Him, we are harmonizing with the angels. The Prophet taught that the angels surround the gatherings of remembrance, and that the tasbīḥ is a light and a treasure. The constant glorification of the angels also reminds us of our own deficiency; we are distracted and forgetful, while they are in perpetual, perfect remembrance. This should motivate us to increase our own dhikr. The verse also reassures the believers in Mecca that the universe is not silent about the pagan blasphemies; the highest assemblies of angels are continuously countering those falsehoods with glorification and praise. Truth, at the cosmic level, is overwhelmingly upheld. The angels’ testimony is thus a powerful conclusion to the refutation of shirk, as it comes from the very beings the pagans slandered, and it affirms the absolute transcendence and oneness of Allah.
167 | They certainly used to say, | After the angelic testimony, the surah returns to expose yet another contradiction in the Meccan pagans’ position. The verse says, “They certainly used to say.” The phrase “certainly” (wa-in kānū) indicates that this was a habitual, well-known saying of the Quraysh before the Quran was revealed to them. They would repeatedly make a specific statement, which will be quoted in the next verse. This serves to remind the Prophet and the believers of a claim the Arabs used to make before Islam, and then to contrast it with their actual response when that claim was fulfilled. The fact that they “used to say” this indicates that deep in their hearts, they recognized a deficiency in their own tradition. They looked at the Jews and Christians, the “Peoples of the Book,” who possessed scriptures like the Torah and the Gospel, and they felt a sense of inferiority. They wished they too had a revealed book, a prophet, a divine law, so that they could be guided and attain a status like those nations. This desire was not necessarily a sincere longing for truth but a sort of competitive jealousy. They thought that having a scripture would make them a great nation with a clear path. The verse sets the stage for the irony that follows: when Allah actually answered that unspoken wish and sent them a messenger from among themselves with a clear Arabic Quran, they rejected him. Their previous words now stand as a witness against them. For the believer, this is a lesson about the danger of empty wishes. Many people say, “If only I had guidance, I would be devout,” yet when guidance comes to them, they turn away because it challenges their desires. The verb “used to say” emphasizes that this was not an isolated statement but a recurring sentiment. It also demonstrates Allah’s knowledge of what is in the hearts; He knew what they were saying among themselves. The verse serves as a prelude to the dramatic revelation of their hypocrisy, which will be laid bare in the subsequent verses.
168 | “If only we had a Reminder like ˹those of˺ earlier peoples, | The specific statement of the pagan Arabs is now quoted. “If only we had a Reminder like those of earlier peoples.” The word “Reminder” (dhikr) refers to a divine scripture, a revealed book that reminds people of their Creator, their purpose, and the Day of Judgment. The Meccans used to look at the Jews who had the Torah and the Christians who had the Gospel, and they would say, “If only we had a book like theirs, we would be guided.” They knew these earlier peoples had received divine revelation containing laws, wisdom, and stories of the prophets. The Arabs, as an unlettered nation that had not received a major prophet or book since Ishmael’s time, felt this absence keenly. They envied the scriptural communities and imagined that possessing such a book would elevate them. However, this wish was not entirely innocent; it was often accompanied by a sense of self-justification. They used it as an excuse: “We have not received a warner, so we are not to blame for our idolatry.” But deep down, they had the legacy of Abraham and Ishmael, the original monotheism of the Kaaba, which they had corrupted. The “earlier peoples” refers to the nations who received prophets, like the people of Moses and Jesus. The verse captures a moment of spiritual longing that could have been a stepping stone to faith. There was a flicker of recognition that their own traditions were lacking and that divine guidance was valuable. For the believer, this verse reminds us that Allah answers the sincere desires of hearts. The Arabs, even with mixed motives, expressed a wish for guidance, and Allah, in His mercy, sent them the greatest Reminder, the Quran. This is a manifestation of the divine name “The Responsive.” It should make us reflect on our own wishes: if we truly desire closeness to Allah, He will provide the means, but we must be ready to accept them. The tragedy of the Quraysh is that the very thing they claimed they wanted was given to them, and they spurned it.
169 | we would have truly been Allah’s devoted servants.” | The Arabs completed their wish with a bold claim of what they would do if they received a Reminder. “We would have truly been Allah’s devoted servants.” The phrase “devoted servants” (ʿibāda Allāh al-mukhlaṣīn) means they claimed they would be sincere, pure worshippers of Allah, dedicating their religion entirely to Him, unlike the Jews and Christians who, in their view, had gone astray or fallen into internal conflicts. They boasted that if they had a scripture, they would be the best of nations, more guided, more obedient, and more united. The word “truly” (la-kunnā) adds emphasis to their self-confident assertion. They presented themselves as people who only lacked the opportunity; given the right tool, they would excel. This boast reflects a common human trait: blaming external circumstances for one’s spiritual failures while overestimating one’s own virtue. The pagan Arabs thought their idolatry and moral corruption were due to the absence of a divine book, as if the innate fitrah, the signs in creation, and the legacy of their forefather Abraham were insufficient to guide them. The verse thus exposes a profound self-deception. They imagined that if a heavy, demanding scripture with laws, responsibilities, and prohibitions came to them, they would embrace it wholeheartedly. But when it actually came, their reaction proved otherwise. For the believer, this is a cautionary tale: it is easy to imagine how devout we would be if circumstances were different, but the test is how we respond to the guidance that is already in our hands. Many people today say, “If I had more knowledge, I would be more pious,” while neglecting to act upon what they already know. The sincerity of one’s devotion is proven by obedience, not by hypothetical scenarios. The verse also sets up the crushing irony that these people who claimed they would be “devoted servants” are now calling the actual devoted servant, the Prophet Muhammad, a mad poet and a liar. Their own words condemn them, showing that their problem was never the lack of guidance but the arrogance and attachment to their ancestral ways that prevented them from accepting the guidance when it arrived.
170 | But ˹now˺ they reject it, so they will soon know. | The irony reaches its climax with this devastating revelation. “But now they reject it, so they will soon know.” The “it” refers to the Reminder, the Quran, which Allah sent to them in answer to their supposed longing. When the very thing they said they wished for was granted to them—a clear Arabic scripture, a messenger from their own lineage, a book more eloquent and comprehensive than any previous revelation—they turned their backs on it. Instead of becoming “devoted servants,” they denied it, mocked it, and called it magic or poetry. The word “now” emphasizes the present, shocking contrast. The very people who had expressed envy of the earlier peoples for having scriptures are now the rejecters of the final and greatest scripture. The phrase “they will soon know” is a severe warning. It is not an empty threat; it points to the immediate future in this world—the Battle of Badr, the conquest of Mecca, the eventual triumph of Islam—and more importantly, to the punishment of the Hereafter. They will know the truth of what they rejected when the Blast of the Trumpet sounds, and they cry out, “Woe to us!” This is a classic Quranic technique: after exposing a contradiction or a false claim, a warning is issued that the deniers will soon see the consequences of their actions. For the Meccan Muslims who had heard these very pagans say they wished for guidance, this verse was a vindication and a source of strength. It showed that the pagans were not sincere seekers but hypocritical boasters. Their rejection was not based on intellectual difficulty but on stubbornness. For the believer, this verse teaches that we should never envy the spiritual status of others while neglecting the guidance we have received. We have the Quran and the Sunnah; the question is whether we are living as devoted servants or whether we, too, are rejecting the Reminder through heedlessness and lack of action. The “soon will know” should resonate in our hearts as a call to take our present guidance seriously before the time comes when knowledge will no longer benefit.
171 | Our Word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers, | The surah now shifts to a message of reassurance for the Prophet Muhammad, grounding it in the unchanging divine decree concerning all messengers. “Our Word has already gone forth to Our servants, the messengers.” The “Word” (kalima) refers to the divine decree, the unalterable promise and judgment that Allah pronounced in His eternal knowledge. This Word “has already gone forth,” meaning it was established and settled in the Preserved Tablet long before the creation of the heavens and the earth. It is not a new decision; it is the sunnah of Allah that has been in operation since the first prophet was sent. The recipients of this Word are “Our servants, the messengers,” a title of honour that combines their servitude with their mission. The messengers are the closest to Allah, and this Word is a covenant of support and victory for them. The content of this Word is not fully detailed here but is summarized in the next two verses: they will be helped, and Allah’s forces will prevail. This verse comes as a soothing balm to the Prophet, who was facing intense opposition and the apparent success of the deniers. It reminds him that he is not alone in this struggle; he is part of an ancient and honoured company of messengers, and the same decree that applied to them applies to him. The phrase “Our Word” emphasizes the authority and certainty of the promise. What Allah says is the truth, and His Word cannot be changed or thwarted. For the believers in Mecca, who might have wondered if the small community of Muslims would ever overcome the mighty Quraysh, this verse assured them that victory was not a matter of numbers or weapons but of divine decree. The Word had already gone forth, and history was unfolding according to that Word. The believer learns to trust in the promises of Allah, even when circumstances seem hopeless. The messengers were all tried, but the outcome was always in their favour. This verse also connects beautifully to the earlier stories of Noah, Abraham, Moses, and the others; they all experienced the fulfillment of this Word. The Meccans who rejected the Prophet are thus warned that they are not fighting a lone man but opposing a divine decree that has been set in stone.
172 | that they would surely be helped, | The first part of the divine Word is now explicitly stated. “That they would surely be helped.” This is the promise of divine aid and victory for the messengers. The word “helped” (mansūrūn) is in the emphatic plural form, indicating a definite, guaranteed assistance. This help encompasses many forms: sometimes it is visible, like the physical salvation of Noah from the flood, the deliverance of Abraham from the fire, the defeat of Pharaoh’s army, and the destruction of the people of Lot. Sometimes it is less spectacular but equally real, like the firmness of heart granted to a prophet in the face of persecution, the conversion of a tyrant, or the establishment of the messenger’s community after his passing. For the Prophet Muhammad, this help materialized in various ways: the miraculous victory at Badr, the gradual spread of Islam despite the Quraysh’s opposition, the eventual conquest of Mecca, and the preservation and triumph of his message after his death. The word “surely” leaves no room for doubt. It is an iron-clad guarantee from the Lord of the worlds. The help does not necessarily come on the timeline humans expect, nor in the form they imagine, but it comes. The believers might suffer, some might even be martyred, but the ultimate victory of the truth is assured. This verse was a direct challenge to the Meccan pagans who thought they could extinguish the message by killing or mocking the Prophet. It told them that the forces of history were against them, and the divine plan would be executed regardless of their efforts. For the believer in any era, this promise is a source of immense courage and patience. When one strives for the sake of Allah, facing obstacles and setbacks, they should recall this verse and know that Allah’s help is on the way, conditioned upon their continued faith and righteousness. The help is not for the arrogant or the hasty, but for those who follow the path of the messengers with sincerity. The verse also ties back to the surah’s opening depiction of the angels as disciplined forces; these forces are part of the divine help, always at the ready to assist the messengers when the command is given.
173 | and that Our forces will certainly prevail. | The second part of the divine Word complements the first. “And that Our forces will certainly prevail.” The “forces” (junūd) of Allah refer to all the means of support He employs to establish the truth and defeat falsehood. This includes the angels who fought alongside the believers at Badr, the natural elements like the wind that destroyed ʿĀd, the water that drowned Pharaoh, the birds that pelted the army of Abraha, and even intangible forces like the fear cast into the hearts of enemies or the arguments that render falsehood intellectually bankrupt. The forces of Allah are not limited to physical armies; they encompass every instrument of divine will in the cosmos. The word “certainly prevail” (la-hum al-ghālibūn) is an emphatic declaration of their undefeatable nature. Whatever Allah sends to support His messengers, whether it be a storm, an angel, a plague, or a shift in political fortunes, that force will overcome all opposition. This statement is an ultimate reassurance for the Prophet and the believers. It tells them that they are not a small, isolated band of people but part of a grand cosmic movement backed by the Lord of the worlds. The battle between truth and falsehood is not a fair fight; it is weighted decisively in favour of the truth because the Creator of the universe has decreed its victory. The Meccan pagans, with their wealth and numbers, thought they were the powerful ones, but the verse declares that the real power belongs to Allah’s forces, and they will be the ones who prevail. For the believer, this cultivates a deep trust in Allah. It does not mean passivity; the believers are themselves part of Allah’s forces when they strive in His path. But it does mean that the ultimate outcome is not in doubt. The forces of falsehood may have temporary ascendancy, but the overall trajectory of prophetic history is their defeat and the triumph of tawheed. This verse also connects to the eschatological promise: the Day of Judgment will be the final, absolute victory of Allah’s forces, when the chosen servants enter Paradise and the wrongdoers are cast into Hell. It is a verse that fills the heart with hope and steadfastness, enabling the believer to endure the darkest hours with the certainty of the coming dawn.
174 | So turn away from the deniers for a while ˹O Prophet˺. | Having established the certainty of divine support and eventual victory, Allah now gives the Prophet a strategic and psychological instruction. “So turn away from the deniers for a while O Prophet.” This command to “turn away” (tawalla) does not mean to abandon the mission of daʿwah completely, but to temporarily withdraw from the exhausting and fruitless arguments with those who are obstinate. The phrase “for a while” (ḥattā ḥīn) indicates a limited time, a divinely appointed respite. The Prophet was deeply grieved by his people’s rejection, and he exhausted himself trying to convince them. Allah, in His mercy, commands him to step back, to give his heart a break, and to focus his energy on those who are receptive. This “turning away” is not out of fear or despair but out of wisdom and obedience. It is a strategic withdrawal to preserve spiritual strength for the long struggle ahead. The deniers are to be left in their arrogance until the appointed time when the divine punishment or victory arrives. This mirrors the earlier command to Abraham, who physically left his people, and to other messengers who separated themselves from the stubborn rejecters. The “while” refers to the period before the hijrah to Medina, or before the major battles, or before the conquest of Mecca. In a broader sense, it teaches that there are times when engaging with hostile people is counterproductive; one should focus on building the community of believers and conveying the message to those who might listen. For the contemporary believer, this verse provides guidance on dealing with relentless argumentation and toxicity. Sometimes the most spiritually healthy course is to disengage, to protect one’s faith and energy, and to leave the outcome to Allah. It does not mean giving up on people forever, but recognizing when the heart of the other is sealed and further debate only hardens it. The command is accompanied by a promise in the next verse that both the Prophet and the deniers will see the outcome, so the turning away is not a retreat but a repositioning while waiting for the unfolding of the divine decree.
175 | You will see ˹what will happen to˺ them, and they too will see! | The command to turn away is immediately followed by a promise of mutual witnessing. “You will see what will happen to them, and they too will see!” The repetition of “see” emphasizes the certainty and the imminence of a visible outcome. The Prophet is told that he will witness the consequences of their denial—whether in this world through their defeat at Badr, their humiliation at the conquest of Mecca, or in the Hereafter through their punishment. Similarly, the deniers will see the truth of what the Prophet brought: they will see his victory, the spread of Islam, and on the Day of Judgment, they will see the reality of resurrection and the recompense. The phrase is a powerful declaration that the current state of affairs is temporary. The Prophet is not to be discouraged by their mockery, because a day is coming when both sides will see the truth with their own eyes. The deniers’ sarcastic questions about the punishment will be answered when it descends upon them. This verse also contains a subtle warning and a note of hope. The warning is for the deniers: “you will see” means your eyes will be opened forcibly to what you now deny. The hope is for the Prophet: “you will see” means your patience will be rewarded by witnessing the triumph of your mission. The mutual seeing creates a dramatic tension that the surah resolves in the final verses. It also connects to the earlier scene of the believers in Paradise seeing the disbeliever in Hell; that seeing is the ultimate fulfillment of this promise. For the believer today, this verse instills patience. When we are in the midst of a struggle and the forces of falsehood seem dominant, we must remember that a time of clear seeing is coming. We will see the victory of Allah’s religion in ways perhaps we cannot imagine, and the deniers will see their error. The verse frees the heart from the need to immediately vindicate itself; Allah will handle the vindication. All we need to do is remain steadfast and turn away from fruitless conflict, focusing on our own salvation and the daʿwah to those who are open.
176 | Do they ˹really˺ wish to hasten Our punishment? | The surah now turns back to the deniers with a rhetorical question brimming with rebuke. “Do they really wish to hasten Our punishment?” The Meccan pagans, in their arrogance and mockery, would often challenge the Prophet by saying, “If you are truthful, then bring upon us the punishment you threaten us with.” They thought that the delay in punishment was proof that it would never come, and they sarcastically asked for it to be sped up. This verse exposes the foolishness of such a request. Wishing to hasten punishment is like a criminal demanding the executioner to hurry up, not realizing the horror that awaits. The phrase “Our punishment” reminds them that it is not a light matter; it is the punishment of the Lord of the worlds, which is utterly devastating. The question “Do they really wish” (a-fa-bi-ʿadhābinā yastaʿjilūn) carries a tone of incredulity. How can any sane person wish to hasten something that will destroy them? The verse is a warning that they should be praying for mercy and more time to repent, not foolishly challenging the divine power. This is a common theme in the Quran: when people mock the warning of punishment, Allah reminds them that once the punishment arrives, there is no escape, and their mockery will turn into desperate regret. The verse also serves to reassure the believers that the delay is not out of weakness but out of mercy, giving the deniers a chance to turn back. The haste of the disbelievers is contrasted with the patience of the believers, who await Allah’s decree with hope and fear. For the believer today, this verse is a reminder never to be flippant about divine warnings. When we hear about the punishment of the grave or the fire of Hell, our response should be to seek refuge in Allah, not to question why it has not come. The wish to hasten punishment is born out of a heart that is hardened; it is a sign of being far from Allah. The verse thus calls us to take the warnings of the Quran with utmost seriousness, to repent before the day when the punishment does arrive, and it is no longer possible to return.
177 | Yet when it descends upon them: how evil will that morning be for those who had been warned! | The surah now paints a chilling picture of the moment the punishment actually arrives, answering their foolish wish. “Yet when it descends upon them: how evil will that morning be for those who had been warned!” The word “when” (fa-idhā) shifts the scene from their current arrogance to the inevitable future. The punishment is described as “descending” (nazala), a verb that implies it comes from above, sudden and inescapable, like a torrent or a divine strike. The specific mention of “morning” (ṣabāḥ) is significant. In Arabic culture, armies would often attack at dawn, and the morning was associated with the unveiling of events. Many divine punishments in history, like the destruction of Lot’s people, occurred at or just before the break of day. The morning, which usually brings light and hope, becomes a morning of horror and despair for the warned deniers. The verse exclaims, “how evil will that morning be” (fa-sāʾa ṣabāḥu al-mundharīn), a rhetorical expression of the utmost dreadfulness. The phrase “those who had been warned” (al-mundharīn) is a painful reminder that they were not taken by surprise; they were amply warned by the messengers, and they chose to mock and challenge the warning. The morning of punishment is thus the bitter harvest of their own rejection. For the Meccan pagans, this was a direct prophecy of a coming disaster in this world—perhaps the famine, the defeat at Badr, or the conquest—and certainly the Day of Judgment. The verse is designed to strike fear into the hearts of the listeners. It asks them to imagine waking up to the punishment they used to scoff at. There would be no escape, no chance to go back and believe. For the believer, this verse is a powerful deterrent from complacency. It reminds us that the warnings we hear in the Quran are not distant theories; they are a reality that can descend suddenly. We must live in a state of preparedness, grateful for the time we have been given to repent and do good. The verse also comforts the oppressed believers that the mockers will not have the last laugh; their morning of reckoning will be so evil that their previous laughter will seem like a distant, cruel joke. The contrast between the “morning” of punishment and the “Gardens of Bliss” awaiting the believers could not be starker.
178 | And turn away from them for a while. | The command given earlier in verse 174 is repeated almost verbatim, demonstrating its importance and providing a rhythmic refrain in this concluding section. “And turn away from them for a while.” The repetition serves to comfort the Prophet and reinforce the strategy. It is as if Allah is telling him, “I have told you to turn away, and I tell you again: turn away. Do not let your heart be weighed down by them. Entrust their affair to Me for this appointed period.” The “while” is a divinely measured interval before the punishment or the victory. This repetition also indicates that the Prophet’s natural compassion and desire to save his people might keep pulling him back into fruitless, draining arguments. So Allah reiterates the command to protect his mental and spiritual well-being. The command is not abandonment of the mission; the Prophet continued to invite people to Islam, but he was to withdraw from engaging with the hardcore mockers who only wanted to argue and insult. This is a lesson in time management and spiritual economy: one should invest energy where there is hope of receptivity, not exhaust it on those who have made a conscious decision to reject. The repetition also creates a literary effect, building anticipation for the final outcome described in the next verses. For the believer, this verse is a reminder that sometimes the most obedient thing to do is to step back, to be silent, and to let Allah handle the matter. In our personal lives, when we face persistent hostility or mockery for our faith, we are not always obliged to engage. We can “turn away for a while,” preserving our peace and waiting for Allah’s decree to unfold. This turning away is not cowardice; it is an act of trust and a following of the prophetic example. The command is bracketed by the mutual seeing of the outcome, which underscores that the final judgment is not our responsibility but Allah’s.
179 | You will see, and they too will see! | The promise of mutual witnessing is repeated, almost as an echo of verse 175, but now it stands as a firm conclusion to the command to turn away. “You will see, and they too will see!” The brevity and symmetry of this verse add to its powerful impact. The Prophet is addressed directly, and the deniers are referred to in the plural. Both sides will be spectators of the unfolding truth. The repetition reinforces certainty. The Prophet will see the punishment that befalls the deniers and the victory granted to the believers. The deniers will see the fulfillment of the Prophet’s warning and the truth of the resurrection they denied. This dual seeing encompasses both the worldly events—like the Battle of Badr where the leading mockers were killed, and the conquest of Mecca where the idols were smashed—and the scenes of the Hereafter, where the Prophet will witness his followers entering Paradise and the deniers will witness the fire. The verb “see” (fa-sa-yubṣirūn) carries a connotation of insight and realization. It will not be a superficial glance; it will be a profound understanding of the reality that they had rejected. For the believer, this verse is a source of consolation. There are many situations in life where we feel wronged or where the truth seems to be losing. This verse assures us that the ultimate clarification is coming. We do not need to force people to see the truth; Allah will make them see it, either in this world through signs and events, or definitively in the Hereafter. The repetition also serves as a rhetorical closure to the section, leaving the deniers with a final, ominous warning, and the Prophet with a final, uplifting promise. It is a verse that strengthens the heart to remain patient and steadfast, knowing that the blindness of the rejecters is temporary and will be removed in a moment when it is too late for them. The believer’s seeing, however, will be a source of joy and gratitude, as they witness the fulfillment of Allah’s Word.
180 | Glorified is your Lord—the Lord of Honour and Power—above what they claim! | The surah now reaches its majestic conclusion, beginning with a glorification that echoes the themes of the entire passage. “Glorified is your Lord—the Lord of Honour and Power—above what they claim!” This is a sublime declaration of tanzīh, declaring Allah’s transcendence and perfection, far removed from the false descriptions and fabrications the pagans have uttered throughout the surah. The phrase “your Lord” (rabbika) is personal and intimate, addressed to the Prophet, reminding him of the close, nurturing relationship he has with his Master. The Lord is then described with two magnificent attributes: “the Lord of Honour” (rabbi al-ʿizza) and “the Lord of Power.” Honour (ʿizza) refers to might, dignity, invincibility, and exaltedness. Allah possesses all honour; none can humiliate Him or overpower Him. Power (in the Arabic phrasing included in the verse, though the translation here renders “Honour and Power” as the combined meaning, it encompasses jabarūt or strength that compels submission). He is the Compeller, the One whose will is irresistible. These attributes directly counter the pagan claims of weakness and neediness implied by attributing daughters and partners to Him. A Lord of such absolute Honour and Power is far above having children, consorts, or rivals. The phrase “above what they claim” is the recurring refrain that dismisses all the slander of the polytheists—their claims about angels, jinn, idols, and the denial of resurrection. The glorification at the end of the surah mirrors the glorification of the angels at the beginning and their testimony in the middle. The entire surah is framed by tasbīḥ. This structure teaches the believer that the ultimate response to falsehood is to glorify Allah and affirm His transcendence. We may refute arguments, but our hearts should rest in the glorification of the One who is far above all imperfections. For the Meccans, this was a final, thunderous declaration that their beliefs were not just wrong but a vile affront to the Majestic Lord. For the believer, it is an invitation to join in this glorification, to fill our hearts with the awe of the Lord of Honour and Power, and to cleanse our faith of any concept that does not befit His majesty. This verse sets up the final two verses of the surah, which extend peace to the messengers and praise to Allah, bringing the entire magnificent discourse to a serene and powerful close.
181 | Peace be upon the messengers. | After the powerful glorification of the Lord of Honour and Power, the surah concludes with a beautiful, universal greeting of peace that encompasses the entire brotherhood of prophets. The verse states simply yet profoundly, “Peace be upon the messengers.” This is not merely a passing salutation; it is a divine decree of safety, honour, and eternal serenity for all those noble souls who carried the message of tawheed to humanity throughout the ages. The word “Peace” (salām) here comes directly from Allah, making it the highest form of greeting and blessing. It is the same peace that was individually bestowed upon Noah, Abraham, Moses and Aaron, Elias, and implied for the other prophets mentioned in the surah. Now, at the end, all the messengers are collectively bathed in this divine peace. This demonstrates the unity of their mission and their shared destiny. They faced rejection, mockery, persecution, and immense hardship from their respective peoples, yet they stood firm. The peace of Allah is their ultimate reward, a state of perfect tranquility, security from all harm, and the bliss of divine nearness. This salutation also serves as an instruction to the believers: we are to extend this greeting to all the prophets. Muslims are required to believe in all the messengers without distinction and to honour them with the words “peace be upon him” whenever their names are mentioned. The verse thus shapes the consciousness of the ummah, linking us in love and respect to the great line of chosen servants. It refutes any notion that the Prophet Muhammad was in conflict with previous prophets; rather, he is their brother, and his message is the completion of their collective call. The greeting “Peace be upon the messengers” also resonates with the scene of Paradise earlier in the surah, where the chosen servants are in Gardens of Bliss, experiencing peace. The messengers are the forerunners of that peace. For the believer, this verse is a source of comfort and hope. It assures us that the path of the messengers, however difficult, leads to the ultimate peace from the Lord of all worlds. It also serves as a farewell from the surah, wrapping up the historical narratives and the arguments with a gentle, unifying blessing. After the intensity of the refutations and the vivid descriptions of punishment, the surah leaves the listener with the serene image of the messengers resting in divine peace. It is as if the entire storm of the surah settles into this calm, reminding us that the goal of all the warnings, arguments, and stories is to lead humanity to the peace that comes from submission to the One God. The believers are implicitly included in this salutation, for they are the followers of the messengers, and the peace of Allah descends upon those who follow their guidance. The verse is a simple sentence, yet it carries the weight of the entire history of prophethood, offering a moment of reflection and a deep breath of spiritual tranquility before the final word of praise that closes the surah.
182 | And praise be to Allah —Lord of all worlds. | The surah concludes with the most comprehensive and fitting declaration of praise, bringing the entire discourse to a majestic close. “And praise be to Allah—Lord of all worlds.” The word “praise” (al-ḥamd) is a definitive, all-encompassing term that signifies gratitude, extolment, and the acknowledgment of perfection. It is not a request or a description but a statement of reality: all praise, in every form, from every tongue, in every time, belongs solely to Allah. This is the same phrase that opens the Quran in Surah Al-Fatiha, and here it serves as the final seal of Surah As-Saffat, creating a beautiful symmetry. The phrase “Lord of all worlds” (rabbi al-ʿālamīn) proclaims Allah’s absolute Lordship over every realm of existence—the world of angels, the world of humans, the world of jinn, the world of animals, the celestial spheres, the unseen, and the seen. After a surah that has taken the listener on a journey through the ranks of angels, the adornments of the lowest heaven, the depths of Hell, the Gardens of Bliss, the stories of the prophets across different nations, and the refutation of the pagan myths about jinn and angels, this conclusion is profoundly fitting. All of these “worlds” are under the Lordship of Allah, and He alone is deserving of unceasing praise. The placement of this verse as the final word is deliberate. It teaches that the culmination of all knowledge, all reflection, and all revelation is the praise of Allah. After hearing about the destruction of the deniers and the salvation of the chosen servants, the natural response of the heart is to praise the One who is perfectly just and perfectly merciful. After witnessing the logical arguments against idolatry, the mind is compelled to praise the One who is the sole Creator and Sustainer. The verse transforms the entire surah into an act of worship, because the listener is invited to end the recitation with this declaration of praise. It is a verbal act of aligning oneself with the angels who are “constantly glorifying His praise,” and with the messengers who lived and died in His praise. For the Meccan pagans, this was a final call to abandon the false praise they offered to idols and to direct all praise to the true Lord of the worlds. For the believers, it is a daily creed: whatever happens, in ease or hardship, praise belongs to Allah. The hardship of the Meccan period was severe, yet the surah instructs the believers to seal their recitation with “praise be to Allah,” acknowledging that even in the struggle, there is wisdom and mercy that warrants praise. This verse also connects to the Hereafter, where the final call of the people of Paradise will be “Praise be to Allah, Lord of all worlds.” Thus, the surah’s end is a preview of the eternal joy of the believers. It leaves the soul in a state of serene gratitude, focused on the majesty and mercy of the Creator, and ready to face the trials of life with the knowledge that the ultimate reality is the praise of the Lord of all worlds. This completes the thematic arc from the oath of the angels to the glorification of the Lord and the praise that will echo for eternity.
