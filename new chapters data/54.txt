1 | The Hour has drawn near and the moon was split ˹in two˺. | The surah opens with a thunderous declaration that links a cosmic event witnessed by the Quraysh with the ultimate cosmic event that is yet to come. The Hour, the Day of Judgment, has drawn near. Its approach is not a distant speculation but an impending reality, and one of the signs of its nearness is that the moon was split. The Arabic verb "inshaqqa" means to be split apart, to be cleaved. According to numerous authentic hadiths narrated in both Bukhari and Muslim, this verse refers to a miracle that occurred in Mecca during the lifetime of the Prophet Muhammad. The Quraysh came to the Prophet and demanded a sign to prove his prophethood. They asked him to split the moon. The Prophet turned to Allah in supplication, and Allah caused the moon to split into two distinct halves, with Mount Hira visible between them. The Prophet called out to the Quraysh, "Witness!" They saw the miracle with their own eyes. Yet, despite witnessing this undeniable sign, they turned away and called it magic, as the next verse will state. The splitting of the moon is thus both a miracle and a warning. It is a miracle because it demonstrated the Prophet's truthfulness and Allah's absolute power over the celestial bodies that the pagans sometimes worshipped. It is a warning because it signalled that the natural order is not permanent, that the cosmos is obedient to its Creator, and that the great disruption of the Hour is both possible and near. The verse pairs the nearness of the Hour with the splitting of the moon because the latter is a foretaste of the former. If the moon can be split by divine command, then the heavens can be rent asunder, the stars can fall, and the entire universe can be dismantled when the Hour arrives. The believer reflects on this and feels a profound sense of awe. The same moon that hangs serenely in the night sky is not an independent deity or an eternal fixture; it is a creature under the command of its Lord, and its splitting was a sign of both mercy and warning to a people who were given every chance to believe.
2 | Yet, whenever they see a sign, they turn away, saying, “Same old magic!” | This verse captures the tragic and obstinate response of the Quraysh to the miracle of the moon splitting. Despite seeing an undeniable sign, something that defied the laws of nature and could only be the work of the Creator, they turned away. The Arabic "yu'ridu" means to turn one's side, to show disregard, to walk away. They did not pause to reflect, they did not fall down in prostration, they did not acknowledge the truth. Instead, they immediately reached for a label that would allow them to dismiss it. They called it "same old magic." The Arabic "sihrun mustamirr" can mean continuous magic, powerful magic, or magic that is passing away and will soon vanish. Each of these meanings captures a different aspect of their desperate psychology. By calling it continuous magic, they implied that the Prophet was a persistent and skilled magician whose tricks were becoming tiresome. By calling it magic that would pass away, they comforted themselves with the thought that this sign, like all supposed magic, was temporary and its effect would fade. This reaction reveals the terrifying power of a heart that has sealed itself against the truth. When a person is determined not to believe, no evidence is sufficient. They will always find a label, an explanation, a rationalisation that allows them to continue in their state of denial without confronting the reality. The verse is also deeply instructive for believers. It shows that the demand for signs is often not a sincere search for truth but a tactic of avoidance. The Quraysh asked for a sign, received the most dramatic of signs, and still rejected it. The problem was never the lack of evidence; the problem was the state of their hearts. The believer learns from this that faith is not simply the passive result of seeing miracles. It is a choice of the heart, a choice to submit to the truth when it is presented, rather than to turn away and invent comfortable excuses. The repetition of this pattern throughout history, where every nation that rejected its prophet did so with similar dismissive labels, is a source of comfort for the believers who face mockery and a warning to those who mock.
3 | They rejected ˹the truth˺ and followed their own desires—and every matter will be settled— | This verse diagnoses the root cause of the Quraysh's denial and delivers a decisive verdict on its consequences. They rejected the truth, and they followed their own desires. The Arabic "kadhdhabu" means they called it a lie. Their rejection was not a passive disbelief but an active denunciation. They did not simply withhold belief; they declared the truth to be false. The second half of the diagnosis is even more revealing: they followed their desires. The Arabic "ahwa'ahum" is the plural of "hawa," the whims, inclinations, and passions of the self. This is the engine that drives rejection. People do not disbelieve primarily because of intellectual objections; they disbelieve because the truth conflicts with their desires. The Quraysh wanted the freedom to worship their idols, to maintain their social status, to engage in their lucrative but sometimes unjust trade practices, and to live without the accountability that the Day of Judgment implies. The pure monotheism and moral discipline of Islam threatened their entire way of life. Following their desires was easier than submitting to the truth. The verse then delivers a short, powerful sentence that closes the matter: "and every matter will be settled." The Arabic "kullu amrin mustaqirr" means that every affair has a point of settlement, a final outcome, a conclusion that will inevitably be reached. The matter of their rejection will not remain in suspense forever. It will be settled, either in this world through punishment or on the Day of Judgment when they face the full consequences of their choice. This phrase is a profound source of comfort for the oppressed and a dire warning for the oppressor. The injustice, the mockery, and the denial may persist for a time, but they are moving toward a definitive conclusion. The believer trusts that the matter is in Allah's hands and that the final settlement will be perfectly just. Nothing remains unresolved in the divine plan.
4 | even though the stories ˹of destroyed nations˺ that have already come to them are a sufficient deterrent. | This verse presents an additional layer of evidence that the Quraysh had no excuse for their rejection. The stories of the destroyed nations had already come to them. They knew about the people of Noah, the people of 'Ad, the people of Thamud, the people of Lot, and the people of Pharaoh. These were not obscure tales from distant lands. The ruins of 'Ad and Thamud were in their own peninsula. The caravans of the Quraysh passed by the devastated region of Lot's people on their way to Syria. The stories of these nations, detailing how they rejected their prophets and were subsequently annihilated, were a sufficient deterrent, a "muzdajar," meaning something that should have stopped them in their tracks, a warning that should have prevented them from following the same path. The historical evidence was right before their eyes. Yet, they looked at the ruins of the past and did not take heed. They saw the empty dwellings of Thamud and did not connect their own behaviour to the behaviour of Thamud's deniers. The verse implies that the Quraysh had all the information they needed to make the right choice. The stories of the destroyed nations served as a clear and sufficient warning. If a person ignores a warning sign that says "Danger: Cliff Ahead" and walks off the cliff, the fault is not with the sign but with the person who ignored it. The Quran is here stating that the historical evidence was a clear sign, and their disregard of it is wilful. For the believer, this verse underscores the importance of studying history with the eye of faith, not merely as a collection of dates and events but as a repository of divine lessons. The stories of the Quran are not entertainment; they are warnings and guidance. The believer who reads about 'Ad and Thamud should feel a shiver of fear and a renewal of commitment to obey the prophets. The surah is about to recount these very stories, reminding the Quraysh of what they already know but refuse to internalise.
5 | ˹This Quran is˺ profound ˹in˺ wisdom, but warnings are of no benefit ˹to them˺. | This verse comments on the nature of the Quran and the tragic state of the deniers. The Quran is described as "hikmatun balighah," wisdom that is profound, complete, far-reaching, and that has reached its ultimate point of perfection. The word "hikmah" is profound, encompassing knowledge, wisdom, sound judgment, and the ability to put things in their proper place. The Quran is not a collection of myths or poetry; it is the distillation of divine wisdom, a guidance that addresses every aspect of human life and provides the most rational, most just, and most beneficial teachings. The adjective "balighah" adds that this wisdom is not partial or incomplete; it has reached its goal, it is fully mature and effective. If a person approaches it with an open heart, it will transform them utterly. Yet, the verse immediately follows this description with a lament: "but warnings are of no benefit." The Arabic "nudhur" refers to the warnings, the wake-up calls, the admonitions that are meant to stir the heedless from their sleep. Despite the profound wisdom of the Quran, these warnings do not benefit the deniers. The problem is not with the Quran; the problem is with the recipients. Their hearts are so sealed, so hardened by their desires and their arrogance, that the most powerful wisdom in existence bounces off them like rain off a stone. This is a profound and sobering truth. The same Quran that transforms a savage, drunken, tribal society into the most righteous and unified community in history, the same Quran that makes grown men weep and souls soar, is utterly ineffective for those who have chosen to close themselves off from it. The verse teaches the believer that guidance is ultimately a gift from Allah. The external means, the Quran, is perfect and complete. But the internal receptivity, the opening of the heart, is what determines whether the wisdom takes root or is rejected. The Prophet is being comforted; he is not failing in his delivery. The wisdom he brings is perfect, but the hearts he addresses have turned to stone.
6 | So turn away from them ˹O Prophet˺. ˹And wait for˺ the Day ˹when˺ the caller will summon ˹them˺ for something horrifying. | After establishing that the deniers are impervious to warnings, the Quran gives the Prophet a difficult but necessary command: turn away from them. The Arabic "fa tawalla 'anhum" means to turn aside, to leave them, to cease the exhausting effort of trying to convince those who have made it abundantly clear that they will not listen. This command is a mercy to the Prophet. His heart was heavy with grief over their rejection, and he was in danger of destroying himself with sorrow because they would not believe. Allah, in His care for His messenger, relieves him of that burden. The responsibility of the messenger is to convey, not to compel. Once the message has been delivered with clarity and the response is persistent mockery and denial, the messenger is justified in withdrawing his emotional investment and entrusting the matter to Allah. However, this turning away is not a cancellation of the warning; it is a transition to a different phase. The verse then instructs the Prophet to wait for the Day when the caller will summon them to something horrifying. The caller is the angel Israfil, or another appointed angel, who will blow the trumpet on the Day of Judgment. The call will summon all of humanity to the plain of gathering. The word "nukur" describes something horrifying, unprecedented, terrifying beyond all human experience. The Day of Judgment is a day unlike any other, a day whose horrors the mind cannot fully comprehend. The command to wait is both a comfort and a subtle threat. The Prophet waits with serene patience, knowing that the justice of Allah will manifest. The deniers, meanwhile, are being given a final warning. The turn away from them is a sign that their case has now been deferred to the ultimate court, and the court date is set for a Day that will be horrifying beyond their worst nightmares. For the believer, this verse teaches the wisdom of disengagement from fruitless arguments. There comes a point when the best thing to do for one's own spiritual health is to turn away, leave the judgment to Allah, and focus on those who are receptive.
7 | With eyes downcast, they will come forth from the graves as if they were swarming locusts, | This verse paints a vivid and terrifying picture of the resurrection. The deniers will emerge from their graves with their eyes humbled and downcast. The Arabic "khushsha'an absaruhum" means their eyes will be in a state of extreme humility, abasement, and terror. They will not dare to look up. The arrogance that characterised their worldly life, the strutting and the proud glances, will be utterly extinguished. They will be like the lowest of the low, their eyes fixed to the ground in shame and fear. The second part of the verse compares their emergence to swarming locusts. Locusts, when they swarm, are a chaotic, teeming mass that spreads across the land in overwhelming numbers, moving with a frantic, undirected energy. The comparison is profoundly apt. Like locusts, the resurrected human beings will be countless, emerging from every corner of the earth, covering the plains. Like locusts, they will be in a state of confusion and dispersion, driven by the call to gather, unable to resist. Like locusts, they will rise from the ground, from their graves, after a period of being hidden in the earth. The imagery also conveys a sense of weakness and vulnerability. Locusts are fragile creatures, easily crushed. The mighty kings and tyrants of history will rise from their graves as helpless, teeming beings with downcast eyes, stripped of all their power and pretense. The believer, reading this, is filled with a sense of the majesty of Allah and the utter insignificance of human power before Him. The verse also provides a contrast: the believers will rise with faces radiant and eyes full of hope, as described in other surahs. But for the deniers who laughed and mocked, the resurrection will be a scene of unmitigated horror and humiliation. The comparison to locusts is a sharp reminder that the human being, no matter how impressive in this world, is but a creature, and when the Creator commands, the creature rises in a state of complete subjection.
8 | rushing towards the caller. The disbelievers will cry, “This is a difficult Day!” | This verse continues the scene of the resurrection, describing the movement of the resurrected and their verbal response. They will be rushing headlong, necks stretched out, hastening towards the caller. The Arabic "muhti'in" means speeding eagerly or being driven compulsively, unable to stop or slow down. They are drawn by the irresistible summons of the trumpet, like iron filings to a magnet. There is no choice, no hesitation, no possibility of refusal. The call of the divine summons overrides all other wills. The scene is one of total, unstoppable motion toward the place of judgment. Then comes the cry of the disbelievers: "This is a difficult Day!" The Arabic "yaumun 'asir" means a day of hardship, severity, and constriction. This is their first verbal admission of the truth they spent their lives denying. In the world, they mocked the idea of the Day of Judgment. They asked when it would come and scoffed at its terrors. Now, faced with its reality, they can only utter this cry of anguish. The word "difficult" barely scratches the surface of the horrors they are experiencing. The admission comes too late. In the world, they could have avoided this difficult Day by believing and doing good. Now, the acknowledgment of its difficulty does not alleviate its suffering. The verse captures the essence of the tragedy of disbelief: the truth is denied when it could save, and it is acknowledged when it can only condemn. For the believer, this verse serves as a powerful motivation to take the warning seriously while there is still time. The Day will indeed be difficult, but for the believers, Allah will make it easy. The Prophet said that the Day of Judgment will be made easy for the believer to the extent that it will be like the time between the noon and afternoon prayers. The contrast could not be greater. The same Day that is "difficult" beyond imagination for the disbelievers will be a period of ease and relief for the righteous. The cry of the disbelievers, "This is a difficult Day," is the sound of a soul encountering the reality it refused to prepare for. The surah is about to recount the stories of nations who faced a foretaste of this difficult Day in their worldly destruction.
9 | Before them, the people of Noah denied ˹the truth˺ and rejected Our servant, calling ˹him˺ insane. And he was intimidated. | The surah now begins the series of historical examples that serve as warnings to the Quraysh. The first is the people of Noah. They denied the truth and rejected Our servant, meaning Noah. The title "Our servant" is an honour. Despite being rejected and mocked by his people, Noah was elevated by Allah with this noble designation. The Arabic "'abdana" signifies a close, devoted servant, someone who belongs entirely to Allah. The people of Noah did not merely disagree with him; they accused him of being insane. The word "majnun" was hurled at Noah, just as the Quraysh hurled it at Muhammad. This is the first of many parallels that the Quran draws between the Prophet and his predecessors. The accusation of insanity is the last refuge of the denier who cannot refute the message. The verse adds that Noah was intimidated. The Arabic "wazdujira" means he was rebuked harshly, threatened, driven back with insults and menaces. His people did not just ignore him; they actively bullied him. They told him that if he did not stop his preaching, he would be stoned or expelled. The pressure on Noah was immense. He was one man standing against an entire society entrenched in idolatry and sin. This description serves to comfort the Prophet Muhammad. He is not the first messenger to be called insane. He is not the first to face threats and intimidation. His brother Noah, a mighty prophet, endured the same for nearly a thousand years. The believer learns from this that the path of da'wah is often a path of persecution. The truth is rarely welcomed by those whose power and desires it threatens. The reaction of Noah's people, calling him insane and trying to silence him, is the classic response of falsehood when it cannot counter the argument. The verse sets the stage for Noah's supplication and the divine response, which will demonstrate the pattern of patient calling followed by ultimate deliverance for the messenger and destruction for the deniers.
10 | So he cried out to his Lord, “I am helpless, so help ˹me˺!” | After centuries of patient preaching, after enduring mockery, threats, and physical intimidation, Noah finally turned to his Lord with this deeply moving supplication. The Arabic "da'a rabbahu" means he called upon his Lord, he supplicated earnestly and desperately. The words he used are recorded in other surahs and summarised here: "I am helpless, so help me." The word "maghlub" means overpowered, defeated, vanquished. Noah was not admitting a personal failure of effort; he was stating the reality of his situation. He had exhausted every possible means of persuasion. He had called his people openly and secretly, by day and by night. He had used every argument, every approach. But they had overpowered him with their numbers, their ridicule, and their threats. He was helpless to change their hearts, because guidance is ultimately in Allah's hand. So he turned to the only source of help. The supplication is a beautiful model of prophetic humility. Noah did not ask for their destruction out of personal vengeance. He simply laid his state before his Lord and asked for help. The Arabic "fantasir" means to grant victory, to come to the aid of the oppressed, to exact justice on behalf of the one wronged. It is a cry for divine intervention. This supplication marks a turning point. After it, the era of warning came to an end, and the era of punishment began. The verse teaches the believer that there is a time for patient endurance and a time to pour out one's heart to Allah in desperate supplication. Allah is the only true helper. When all human means are exhausted, the believer turns to Allah, not with despair but with hope, knowing that His help is near. Noah's supplication was answered in the most dramatic way imaginable, as the following verses will describe. The believer learns that no situation is hopeless when one has a direct line to the Lord of the worlds. The cry of the oppressed is heard, and the response may come in ways that shake the very foundations of the earth.
11 | So We opened the gates of the sky with pouring rain, | The divine response to Noah's supplication was swift and overwhelming. The gates of the sky were opened. The Arabic "fatahna abwab as-sama'" paints the picture of the heavens being thrown wide open, not a gentle shower but a deluge. The rain that came down was not ordinary rain. It was "ma'in munhamir," pouring water that descended in torrents, in sheets, without pause or mercy. This was not the rain that brings life to the earth; this was the rain of destruction. The image of gates being opened is a powerful one. Gates imply control. Normally, Allah sends down rain in a measured amount, a blessing that sustains life. But when the gates are opened, the measure is removed, and the water becomes an unstoppable flood. This verse and the next one describe the two sources of the floodwater: the sky and the earth. Water came from above and water gushed from below, surrounding the disbelievers from every direction. There was no escape. The flood was not a random natural disaster; it was a precisely timed act of divine retribution. The opening of the gates of the sky is also a sign of Allah's absolute power over the elements. The same sky that holds back its water during a drought can release it in a torrent that destroys a civilization. The Quraysh, who sometimes experienced both drought and floods, were being reminded that the natural world is not a self-regulating machine. It is under the direct command of its Creator. The rain that they took for granted or that they sometimes feared is entirely in Allah's hand. The verse also carries a symbolic meaning. The flood was not just physical water; it was a cleansing of the earth from the filth of disbelief and sin. The earth was being washed of a corrupt humanity, making way for a new beginning. The believer reflects on this and recognises that what appears as a natural phenomenon is often a divine act, and that the boundary between mercy and punishment is crossed when human beings transgress the limits set by their Lord.
12 | and caused the earth to burst with springs, so the waters met for a fate already set. | The second source of the flood was the earth itself. The earth burst with springs. The Arabic "fajjarna al-ard 'uyunan" means the earth was split open, and water gushed forth from every fissure. It was as if the entire planet had become a fountain. The combination of torrential rain from above and gushing springs from below meant that the water rose rapidly, covering everything. There was no high ground to flee to, no mountain that could provide refuge. The waters met, the rain and the spring water, converging to fulfil a command that was already decreed. The phrase "for a fate already set" translates the Arabic "li amrin qad qudir." This means that the flood was not a chaotic, random event. It was a matter that had been preordained, written in the divine decree. The amount of water, the duration of the flood, the exact moment it began, and the exact number of those who would perish were all determined by Allah's precise measure. This is a profound theological point. The destruction of the disbelievers was not a loss of control; it was the execution of a perfect, preordained plan. The universe operates on divine law, and the flood was part of that law. The verse also highlights the helplessness of human power against the forces of nature when they are unleashed by divine command. The people of Noah may have been strong, wealthy, and numerous, but they could not close the gates of the sky or seal the springs of the earth. Their technology, their buildings, and their physical strength were utterly useless. For the Quraysh, this was a clear warning. They too were subject to the same Lord who drowned the people of Noah. The flood was a historical event, but the principle behind it remains. When a people persist in their rebellion, they are at the mercy of a universe that is entirely obedient to its Creator. The believer learns to fear the Lord who controls the sky and the earth and to seek His protection rather than relying on any human security.
13 | We carried him on that ˹Ark made˺ of planks and nails, | Amidst the overwhelming description of the flood, the Quran turns to the means of salvation for Noah and the believers. Allah says "We carried him," taking direct credit for the rescue. The ark was not merely Noah's construction; it was a vessel that sailed by divine will and protection. The ark is described in humble, tangible terms: it was made of planks and nails. The Arabic "dhat alwahin wa dusur" strips the ark of any mythical grandeur. It was a simple wooden boat, built with boards and fastened with nails or ropes. This is a lesson in itself. The greatest salvation does not require the most sophisticated technology. What matters is that the construction is done under divine guidance and that the vessel is boarded by those who have faith. The simplicity of the ark also highlights the contrast between the material resources of the deniers and the believers. The deniers had grand buildings, lofty towers, and all the wealth of their civilization. The believers had a wooden boat held together with nails. But the boat survived, and the grand civilization was utterly destroyed. The mention of planks and nails makes the story tangible and credible. It is not a fairy tale about a magical ship; it is a historical account of a real vessel whose remains some traditions say were seen on Mount Judi. The verse also emphasises Allah's direct involvement. "We carried him" is a statement of intimate care. Noah was not abandoned to the flood. He was personally transported to safety by his Lord. The ark, with its planks and nails, became the locus of divine mercy. For the believer, this verse is a reminder that material simplicity, when combined with faith and obedience, is a fortress that no flood can destroy. The planks and nails of the ark teach us that what makes something a means of salvation is not its outward form but the command of Allah that accompanies it. The believer trusts in the planks and nails of their own life, the simple acts of obedience and the straightforward faith, knowing that Allah can carry them through any storm.
14 | sailing under Our ˹watchful˺ Eyes—a ˹fair˺ punishment on behalf of the one ˹they˺ denied. | The ark sailed under the watchful eyes of Allah. The Arabic "bi a'yunina" means under Our eyes, under Our direct gaze and protection. This is the same phrase used to comfort the Prophet in a previous surah. The ark was not drifting aimlessly on a chaotic sea. It was under the constant supervision and care of Allah. Every wave that rose, every current that flowed, every movement of the vessel was known and controlled. This is an image of profound intimacy and security. In the midst of the greatest flood the world has ever known, a small wooden boat was the safest place on earth because it was under the eyes of the Lord. The second half of the verse states the purpose of this event: it was a punishment on behalf of the one they denied. The one they denied was Noah. The flood was divine justice executed in response to their rejection and mockery of Allah's servant. The punishment was not arbitrary; it was a response to a specific crime. The Arabic "jaza'an li man kana kufir" means a recompense for the one who was disbelieved. This shows the honour that Allah bestows upon His prophets. To reject a prophet is to invite divine wrath. To support a prophet is to board the ark of salvation. The verse masterfully combines the themes of mercy and justice. For Noah and the believers, the flood was an act of rescue and protection. For the deniers, the same flood was an act of punishment. The water that carried the ark to safety drowned the mockers. The believer learns from this that Allah's actions in the world are not one-dimensional. A single event can be a punishment for one group and a mercy for another, depending on their relationship with Allah. The eyes of the Lord that watched over the ark are the same eyes that saw the drowning of the wicked. The believer seeks to be on the side of the ark, under the watchful eyes of mercy, not on the side of the flood, under the eyes of justice.
15 | We certainly left this as a sign. So is there anyone who will be mindful? | The flood of Noah was not just an event that happened and was forgotten. Allah deliberately left it as a sign for future generations. The Arabic "taraknaha ayatan" means We left it, the story or the ark itself, as a sign. The sign is multi-layered. The story itself, preserved in scripture and oral tradition, is a sign that teaches about the consequences of disbelief. The physical remains of the ark, which according to the Quran rested on Mount Judi, were left as a sign for people to see and reflect upon. Some scholars mention that the early generations knew the location of the ark's remains. The entire narrative, with its vivid details, is a sign that points to the reality of divine justice and the truth of the prophetic message. Then the verse poses a powerful, recurring question that will become the refrain of this surah: "So is there anyone who will be mindful?" The Arabic "fa hal min muddakir" is an invitation, a challenge, a plea. Muddakir comes from dhikr, meaning remembrance, reflection, taking heed. The question is: is there any soul out there who will take this sign to heart? Who will remember the lesson of Noah and apply it to their own life? Who will recognise that the same Lord who drowned the mockers of old is the Lord who will judge the mockers of today? This refrain is a direct appeal to the human conscience. It bypasses intellectual argument and addresses the heart's capacity for taking warning. The question implies that the signs are abundantly clear and that the only thing missing is a receptive heart. The believer, upon hearing this verse, should feel a stirring in the heart, a desire to be among those who are mindful, who do not let the signs pass them by as mere stories but who internalise their lessons and change their lives accordingly. The repetition of this refrain throughout the surah will hammer this invitation home, giving the listener multiple opportunities to respond. It is one of the most beautiful and haunting features of Surah Al-Qamar.
16 | Then how ˹dreadful˺ were My punishment and warnings! | This verse is the second refrain of the surah, and it complements the first. While the first refrain invited reflection, this one expresses the severity of what happens when the warning is ignored. "How dreadful were My punishment and warnings!" The Arabic "kayfa kana 'adhabi wa nudhur" is a rhetorical question that implies an answer too terrible to fully articulate. The punishment was dreadful beyond description. The warnings that preceded it were clear, persistent, and merciful. The combination of the two creates a picture of complete justice. The warnings gave every opportunity for repentance. The punishment was the inevitable consequence of rejecting those warnings. The verse is spoken in the first person by Allah, which adds to its power. It is not a narrator describing the punishment; it is the Lord of the worlds Himself reflecting on the fate of those who defied Him. This personal involvement underscores the reality of the punishment. It was not an impersonal natural disaster; it was "My punishment." The use of "My" also highlights that the warnings were an act of mercy. Allah sent "My warnings" through Noah, a gesture of care and concern. When those warnings were mocked, "My punishment" became an act of justice. For the believer, this refrain inspires a healthy fear of Allah. The punishment of Noah's people is not a myth; it is a historical demonstration of what happens when warnings are ignored. The believer hears this and renews their commitment to heed the warnings of the Quran, knowing that the same Lord who punished the people of Noah is the Lord who will judge all humanity. The refrain also serves as a comfort to the oppressed believers. The punishment that fell on the mockers of Noah was a vindication of Noah and his followers. The same Lord who punished the oppressors then will punish the oppressors now. Justice may be delayed, but it is never cancelled.
17 | And We have certainly made the Quran easy to remember. So is there anyone who will be mindful? | This verse is the third refrain and perhaps the most central to the surah. It declares that Allah has made the Quran easy for remembrance, for taking heed, for being stored in the heart and recalled in the mind. The Arabic "yassarna al-Qur'ana li dhikr" is a profound statement about the accessibility of the divine word. The Quran is not a book of obscure mysteries that only a scholarly elite can understand. Its core message is simple, clear, and easily remembered. Its stories are vivid, its arguments are logical, its moral teachings are intuitive, and its language is rhythmic and memorable. Millions of people, young and old, literate and illiterate, have memorised the entire Quran, a phenomenon unique among the world's scriptures. This ease of remembrance is a miracle in itself and a fulfilment of the divine promise. The purpose of making the Quran easy is "li dhikr," for remembrance, for being a constant reminder. The Quran is meant to be a living companion, a book that is frequently recited, reflected upon, and used as a source of guidance in daily life. It is not meant to sit on a shelf gathering dust. The verse then repeats the question: "So is there anyone who will be mindful?" The ease is provided, the sign is clear, the reminder is accessible. The only missing component is a human being who is willing to take heed, to remember, to internalise. The question is a gentle reproach and an urgent invitation. What excuse does anyone have? The book has been made easy. The path to guidance has been smoothed. If a person still fails to be mindful, the fault lies entirely within their own soul. For the believer, this verse is a tremendous encouragement. It assures them that the Quran is not beyond their reach. They can understand it, memorise it, and live by it. It also instils a sense of responsibility. Since the Quran has been made easy, the believer has no excuse for ignorance. For the Quraysh, who also prided themselves on their fortifications and their martial prowess, this was a clear warning. The wind that destroyed 'Ad was not a myth. The ruins of their civilization bore witness. The believer reads this and is reminded of the fragility of human power. The same wind that cools the skin on a hot day can, by Allah's command, become an instrument of annihilation.

They had inverted the natural order, so a punishment rained down from the sky, turning the very earth upside down as well, as the Quran mentions elsewhere. The unleashing of the stones was a manifestation of divine wrath, a deliberate and targeted act of destruction. Then, the verse immediately provides the crucial contrast: the family of Lot, the believers, were delivered. The Arabic “illa ala Lutin najjaynahum” means that Lot and his household were saved. The exception is clearly stated. The punishment was not indiscriminate. The righteous were separated from the wicked. This is a consistent principle in all the stories of destruction. Noah and the ark, Salih and the believers, and here Lot and his family, all were saved before the punishment descended. The verse adds the specific timing: “before dawn.” The Arabic “bi sahar” refers to the last part of the night, the time just before the break of dawn. It was in the darkness before the first light that the believers were led out of the city to safety. This timing is significant. The punishment came with the morning, as the next verses will mention. The believers were rescued in the quiet, dark hours, while the criminals slept, unaware that their doom was moments away. This is a lesson in the way Allah’s help arrives. Often, deliverance comes in the darkest hour, when hope seems thinnest. The believer is reminded that the mercy of Allah operates with perfect timing, separating the righteous from the wicked with precision that no human judge could achieve.
35 | as a blessing from Us. This is how We reward whoever gives thanks. | The deliverance of Lot and his family is further explained: it was a blessing from Allah. The Arabic “ni’matan min ‘indina” means a favour, a grace, a gift directly from Us. The believers did not save themselves through their own cunning or strength. Their salvation was an act of divine generosity. The verse frames this deliverance as part of a general divine principle: “This is how We reward whoever gives thanks.” The Arabic “kadhalika najzi man shakar” establishes a direct link between gratitude and reward. Lot and his family were grateful to Allah. They believed in Him, worshipped Him, and followed His messenger. Their gratitude was not just a verbal expression but a life lived in obedience. Therefore, Allah rewarded them with salvation from the most horrific punishment. The concept of shukr, gratitude, is central to the Quranic worldview. The opposite of gratitude is kufr, which is also the word for disbelief. The disbelievers are those who are ungrateful for the blessings of Allah. The believers are those who recognise the blessings, attribute them to Allah, and use them in His service. The deliverance of Lot’s family is presented as a reward for their gratitude. This teaches the believer that gratitude is not a passive emotion; it is an active force that attracts more blessings and provides protection from calamity. When a believer thanks Allah sincerely, especially in times of difficulty, they are aligning themselves with the divine current of mercy. The verse also serves as a reassurance to the believers in Mecca. They were a small, persecuted minority, surrounded by a hostile majority. The story of Lot tells them that no matter how small the group of the grateful, Allah’s reward for them is certain and complete. They should continue to be grateful, continue to worship, continue to obey, and their deliverance will come, just as it came for Lot’s family in the darkness before dawn. The verse transforms the historical narrative into a timeless promise.
36 | He had already warned them of Our ˹crushing˺ blow but they disputed the warnings. | This verse adds a crucial detail about the events leading to the destruction of Lot’s people. Lot had already warned them of Allah’s crushing blow. The Arabic “wa laqad andharahum batshatana” means that Lot explicitly warned them of the severity and power of divine seizure. The term “batsha” refers to a sudden, violent seizing, a crushing grip. Lot did not merely give vague spiritual advice. He warned them of a specific, terrible punishment that would befall them if they did not repent. He used strong language, invoking the fear of Allah’s might. This warning was an act of mercy, giving them every opportunity to change. Yet their response was to dispute the warnings. The Arabic “fa tamaraw bi an-nudhur” means they argued about them, they cast doubt upon them, they engaged in intellectual and verbal gymnastics to dismiss them. They did not simply ignore Lot; they actively disputed his message, probably mocking the idea that stones could rain from the sky or that their cities could be overturned. This is the same behaviour exhibited by the Quraysh, who disputed the Quranic warnings, asking when the punishment would come and calling it old magic. The verse highlights the tragedy of a people who are warned clearly and directly, and who use the gift of speech and reason not to accept the warning but to argue against it. Dispute becomes a tool of self-destruction. The believer learns from this that when a clear warning from Allah reaches the heart, the proper response is not to argue or to rationalise it away but to accept it with humility and to change one’s behaviour. The story of Lot shows that the time for dispute eventually runs out. When the crushing blow comes, the arguments evaporate, and all that remains is the terrible reality they denied. The verse is a call to take the warnings of the Quran with the utmost seriousness, bypassing the impulse to dispute and heading directly to the response of obedience.
37 | And they even demanded his angel-guests from him, so We blinded their eyes. ˹And they were told,˺ “Taste then My punishment and warnings!” | This verse describes the specific event that sealed the fate of Lot’s people. The angels had come to Lot in the form of handsome young men. Lot received them with hospitality and protected them in his home. The wicked people of the city, upon hearing of the visitors, rushed to Lot’s house and demanded that he hand the guests over to them for their evil purposes. The Arabic “wa laqad rawaduhu ‘an dayfihi” means they sought to seduce or forcefully take his guests. This was the ultimate act of depravity. They had reached a level where they would violate even the most sacred laws of hospitality and morality. They saw no shame in publicly demanding the guests for their sinful acts. At this moment, the angels revealed their true nature to Lot and reassured him. Then Allah blinded their eyes. The Arabic “fa tamasna a’yunahum” means We wiped out their sight, We sealed their eyes. The exact nature of this blinding is a miracle. Some reports say the angels struck their faces, causing them to lose their sight, and they stumbled around, unable to find the door. The physical blindness was a fitting punishment for their spiritual blindness. They could not see the truth of Lot’s message, so their physical sight was taken as an immediate prelude to the greater punishment to come. After they were blinded, they were told, “Taste then My punishment and warnings!” This is the first time in the surah that the deniers are directly addressed with these words during the punishment itself. The taste of punishment is no longer a distant threat; it is a present reality. The command to “taste” is deeply experiential. The punishment is not just a concept; it is a flavour, a sensation that fills their entire being. For the believer, this verse is a terrifying illustration of how close punishment can be. The people of Lot moved from demanding the guests to blindness to total destruction in a single night. There was no long delay. The divine response was swift and decisive. The lesson is clear: when sin reaches a certain level of brazenness, the punishment can descend with shocking speed.
38 | And indeed, by the early morning they were overwhelmed by an unrelenting torment. | The timeline of the punishment is now specified. The events of the previous verse, the blinding and the warning to taste the punishment, occurred at night. Then, by the early morning, the final, total destruction came. The Arabic “wa laqad sabbahahum bukratan” means the morning came upon them, and with it, the punishment that settled and remained. The word “bukrah” refers to the early part of the day, the time just after dawn. The punishment was an “adhab mustaqirr,” an unrelenting, established torment that took hold of them permanently. This was not a temporary disaster that they could recover from. It was a terminal punishment that ended their existence in this world and transferred them to the punishment of the Hereafter. The contrast between the night and the morning is stark. The night was filled with the crime and the blinding. The morning brought the final cataclysm. The people of the city probably woke up thinking it was a normal day, but for them, the sun rose on a scene of utter devastation. The stones rained down, and the cities were flipped. The torment settled upon them and never lifted. The word “mustaqirr” echoes the earlier verse about every matter being settled. The matter of Lot’s people was settled on that morning. Their long record of sin, their rejection of the warnings, their assault on the honour of the guests, all was settled in one devastating morning. For the believer, this verse instils a profound sense of the suddenness of divine punishment. A person can go to sleep in sin and wake up in the grip of torment. The only safety is to have one’s record settled in a state of repentance and faith before the morning of punishment arrives. The verse also highlights the mercy shown to Lot and the believers, who were far away by that morning, safe and witnessing the smoke of the destruction from a distance. The same morning that brought death to the wicked brought freedom to the righteous.
39 | ˹Again they were told,˺ “Taste now My punishment and warnings!” | The command to taste is repeated, but with a subtle and significant variation. In verse 37, the command was given after the blinding: “Taste then My punishment and warnings!” The word “then” implied a sequence. Now, after the full destruction has occurred, the command is given again: “Taste now My punishment and warnings!” The repetition emphasises that the tasting is an ongoing experience. The punishment is not a one-time event. It continues, from the immediate blinding through the destruction and into the eternal torment of the Hereafter. The deniers are being made to taste the full, cumulative consequence of their rejection. The use of “My punishment and My warnings” together is a powerful rhetorical device. The warnings and the punishment are paired. The very warnings they mocked have now become part of the punishment, because they will remember those warnings forever as the missed opportunities for salvation. Every moment of their suffering will be accompanied by the bitter memory of Lot’s voice calling them to safety, and their own laughter in response. The command “Taste!” is an address from a position of absolute authority and justice. It is a declaration that the punishment is deserved, that it is the direct result of their own choices. There is no room for appeal. For the believer, this repeated command is one of the most chilling parts of the surah. It is a reminder that the words of the Quran are not just information; they are a living address. The same Lord who commanded the people of Lot to taste the punishment is the Lord who speaks to us through these verses. The believer hears this and prays for protection from ever hearing those words addressed to them. The repetition also serves to reinforce the theme of the surah: the warnings have been delivered, the punishment is real, and the only way to avoid tasting it is to taste the sweetness of faith and obedience now, in this life, while the door of mercy is still open.
40 | And We have certainly made the Quran easy to remember. So is there anyone who will be mindful? | The refrain returns for the fourth time, after the harrowing account of Lot’s people. The placement is again an act of profound mercy. The listener has just heard about the storm of stones, the blinding, the unrelenting torment, and the command to taste the punishment. The heart is trembling. The mind is picturing the terror. And then, immediately, the Quran offers the balm and the solution: the reminder has been made easy. This refrain, appearing after each story of destruction, is the consistent lifeline. It says to the listener: you have seen what happens to those who reject the warnings. Now, will you use the tool I have given you to avoid that fate? The Quran is easy to remember. Its stories, like the ones you have just heard, are vivid and unforgettable. Its lessons are clear. Its guidance is practical. There is no excuse for heedlessness. The question “So is there anyone who will be mindful?” is asked again, and with each repetition, the urgency and the pathos of the question increase. It is as if a merciful teacher, having shown a student a series of catastrophic failures, turns to the student and asks, with sincere concern, “Will you not learn from their mistakes?” The word “muddakir” is related to dhikr, remembrance. The Quran is the ultimate reminder. To be mindful is to remember Allah, remember the Hereafter, remember the stories of the past, and let that remembrance transform one’s actions. The believer, hearing this refrain for the fourth time, feels the weight of the invitation. They renew their commitment to be among the mindful, to not let the Quran be mere words that pass through the ears but a living reality that shapes the life. The refrain is a pause, a deep breath, a moment of decision, before the surah continues to the next example, the people of Pharaoh, and then to the direct address to the Quraysh, driving the message home with relentless power.
41 | And indeed, the warnings ˹also˺ came to the people of Pharaoh. | The surah now presents the fifth and final example of a destroyed nation before turning its direct address to the Quraysh. The people of Pharaoh also received the warnings. The Arabic “wa laqad jaa’a ala Fir’awna an-nudhur” uses the verb “jaa’a” to indicate that the warnings came to them, they arrived, they were delivered. The warnings did not bypass Pharaoh’s people. They were not left in ignorance. Prophet Moses, accompanied by his brother Aaron, was sent to them with clear signs and a powerful message. The warnings delivered to Pharaoh’s people were among the most comprehensive and dramatic in the history of prophethood. Moses did not just preach verbally; he was given miracles that defied the greatest magicians of the age. The staff that became a serpent, the hand that turned white, the plagues of blood, frogs, lice, locusts, and darkness, all were warnings, nudhur. Each plague was a sign, a divine alarm bell calling Pharaoh and his people to release the Children of Israel and to submit to the Lord of the worlds. The mention of Pharaoh’s people as a group is significant. The sin was not just Pharaoh’s alone, though he was the leader and the worst of them. The people followed him, supported his tyranny, and participated in the rejection of Moses. They were all complicit. The warnings came to them all, from the highest noble to the lowest peasant. They heard the message. They witnessed the signs. Yet they collectively refused to believe. The verse, in its brevity, sets the stage for the description of their rejection and punishment. The pattern is now deeply familiar to the listener: warnings come, they are rejected, destruction follows. The Quraysh are being shown that the most powerful empire of the ancient world, the kingdom of Pharaoh, which represented the pinnacle of human civilization and military might, was not immune to this pattern. If Pharaoh could not escape, neither can they. The believer reading this reflects on the universal reach of divine justice. No nation, no matter how powerful, is beyond the reach of Allah’s warning, and no tyrant is beyond the reach of His punishment. The verse is a prelude to the specific description of Pharaoh’s sin and his seizure, reminding us that the warnings were clear and abundant.
42 | ˹But˺ they rejected all of Our signs, so We seized them with the ˹crushing˺ grip of the Almighty, Most Powerful. | This verse reveals the response of Pharaoh’s people and the consequent punishment. They rejected all of the signs. The Arabic “kadhdhabu bi ayatina kulliha” emphasises the totality of their rejection. It was not that they disbelieved in some signs and accepted others. Every single miracle, every plague, every warning was met with denial. Moses presented sign after sign, and each time they dismissed it as magic or illusion. Even when the plagues became unbearable and they begged Moses to pray for relief, promising to release the Children of Israel, they broke their promise the moment the plague was lifted. This pattern of serial rejection exhausted their chance for mercy. Then came the seizure. “So We seized them with the crushing grip of the Almighty, Most Powerful.” The Arabic “akhadhna akhdha ‘azizin muqtadir” is a powerful cluster of attributes. The grip is that of the Almighty, Al-‘Aziz, the One whose might is unchallengeable. And it is the grip of the Most Powerful, Al-Muqtadir, the One who has absolute power over everything. The punishment was not a random event. It was a deliberate, measured act of divine force. The grip seized Pharaoh and his army as they pursued Moses and the Israelites across the parted sea. The waters, which had stood like walls by the command of Allah, collapsed upon them, and they drowned. Their mighty chariots, their heavily armed soldiers, their royal pomp, were all crushed by the simple element of water. The description of the grip as belonging to both the Almighty and the Most Powerful reinforces the absolute helplessness of human power before divine might. Pharaoh, who had declared himself the supreme lord of Egypt, was reduced to a gasping, drowning man, his power stripped away in an instant. For the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their own strength and their fortified city, this was a sobering reminder. The Lord who crushed Pharaoh with a grip of irresistible power is the same Lord they are defying. The believer feels a reverent fear of this divine grip, and a profound gratitude that the same Almighty and Most Powerful God is their protector and guardian, provided they remain in His obedience.
43 | Now, are you ˹Meccan˺ disbelievers superior to those ˹destroyed peoples˺? Or have you ˹been granted˺ immunity ˹from punishment˺ in divine Books? | Having completed the survey of the five destroyed nations, the surah now turns its full rhetorical force directly upon the Quraysh. The question is devastating in its logic. “Are you Meccan disbelievers superior to those destroyed peoples?” The Arabic “a kuffarukum khayrun min ula’ikum” asks whether the Quraysh are better, stronger, more powerful, or more virtuous than the people of Noah, ‘Ad, Thamud, Lot, and Pharaoh. The implied answer is an emphatic no. The Quraysh were a small, quarrelling tribe living in a barren valley. They were not mightier than the empire of Pharaoh. They were not stronger than the giant ‘Ad. They were not more technologically advanced than Thamud. If those nations, with all their power and civilization, could not withstand the punishment of Allah, what chance do the Quraysh have? The question exposes the irrationality of their arrogance. They act as if they are immune, as if the laws of divine justice that operated throughout history have been suspended for their benefit. The second question drives the point further: “Or have you been granted immunity from punishment in divine Books?” The Arabic “am lakum bara’atun fi az-zubur” asks if they have some special exemption, some get-out-of-jail-free card, written in the scriptures. Do they possess a document from Allah that guarantees they will never be punished, no matter what they do? They have no such immunity. They have no special status. They are subject to the same divine law as all peoples. This rhetorical question is a brilliant dismantling of any sense of false security. The Quraysh, like many later groups, believed in a kind of chosenness, that their lineage, their custodianship of the Kaaba, or their wealth placed them beyond the reach of divine retribution. The verse shatters that illusion. The believer learns from this that no nation, no group, no individual is inherently immune from Allah’s justice. Righteousness is the only protection. The verse is a call to abandon false sources of confidence and to seek security in obedience and faith, not in inherited status or material strength.
44 | Or do they say, “We are all ˹a˺ united ˹front˺, bound to prevail.”? | The surah now addresses another potential source of the Quraysh’s false confidence. They might say, “We are a united front, bound to prevail.” The Arabic “nahnu jami’un muntasir” captures their boast that they are a solid, unified group, a coalition that is victorious and will always come out on top. The Quraysh derived a sense of security from their tribal solidarity. They were a powerful confederation, allied with other tribes, and they believed that their unity made them invincible. In their councils, they prided themselves on their ability to mobilise a formidable force and to defend their interests. This boast is a classic expression of reliance on human numbers and collective strength. The Quran exposes this as another empty illusion. The verse frames their statement as a question, as if to say, “Is this what you are relying on?” The implication is that their united front is of no consequence in the face of divine power. The previous nations also had their alliances and their armies. Pharaoh had his hosts. ‘Ad had their mighty warriors. Thamud had their strong tribes. Their unity did not save them. Their collective strength melted away when the punishment descended. The verse sets up the next verse, which will deliver the crushing response: their united front will be shattered, and they will flee. For the believer, this is a powerful lesson about the nature of true strength. The unity of the disbelievers, no matter how formidable it appears, is ultimately fragile. It is built on worldly interests and shared arrogance, and it cannot withstand the test of divine intervention. The only unity that has lasting value is the unity of the believers, which is rooted in faith and the love of Allah. The Quraysh were confident in their coalition, but the Battle of Badr and subsequent events would prove that their united front was a paper shield. The verse is a timeless warning against placing trust in human alliances instead of in Allah. Empires and coalitions rise and fall, but the word of Allah and His promise to the believers remain forever.
45 | ˹Soon˺ their united front will be defeated and ˹forced to˺ flee. | The boast of the Quraysh is answered with a definitive prophecy. Their united front, their “jam’,” will be defeated, and they will turn their backs and flee. The Arabic “sa yuhzamu al-jam’u wa yuwalluna ad-dubur” is a precise and confident prediction. The word “sa” indicates the near future. It will happen soon. The collective body of the disbelievers, which they thought was invincible, will be routed and scattered. This prophecy was fulfilled in stunning fashion at the Battle of Badr, which occurred about a year or two after the revelation of this surah. At Badr, the Quraysh assembled a large, well-equipped army to confront the small, poorly armed Muslim force. They were confident of an easy victory, boasting of their numbers and their prowess. Yet, as the battle unfolded, they were decisively defeated. Many of their leaders were killed, and the survivors fled in disarray, turning their backs to the battlefield. The very thing they had relied upon, their military might and unity, failed them completely. The fleeing, described as “turning their backs,” is a specific humiliation in Arab warfare. A warrior who turns his back in battle is admitting defeat and abandoning his honour. The verse thus not only predicts the defeat but the manner of it, a shameful rout. For the Prophet and the believers, this verse was a source of immense comfort and a promise of victory. They were outnumbered and outmatched by worldly standards, but Allah was telling them that the enemy’s united front was an illusion that would soon dissolve. For the Quraysh, it was a final warning. The very thing they boasted about would become a source of humiliation. The believer learns from this that the promises of Allah are true. When the world seems overwhelmingly opposed to the truth, and the forces of falsehood appear united and unstoppable, this verse reminds the heart that the united front of the disbelievers has an expiration date. The victory belongs to Allah, and He grants it to whom He wills, often through the most unlikely means. The defeat of the Quraysh at Badr became a defining moment in Islamic history, a testament to the truth of this Quranic prophecy.
46 | Better yet, the Hour is their appointed time—and the Hour will be most catastrophic and most bitter. | The surah now shifts from the near-term defeat to the ultimate appointed time. Even if the Quraysh suffer a worldly defeat, that is only a foretaste. The real appointment, the true rendezvous with justice, is the Hour. The Arabic “bali as-sa’atu maw’iduhum” means that the Hour is their appointed time, the promised meeting they cannot avoid. The worldly battle of Badr is just a minor skirmish compared to the cosmic catastrophe of the Day of Judgment. The verse then describes the Hour with two powerful adjectives: “most catastrophic and most bitter.” The Arabic “adha wa amarr” contrasts the two qualities. “Adha” means more destructive, more painful, more catastrophic. “Amarr” means more bitter in taste, more grievous in experience. The Hour is not just physically devastating; it is emotionally and spiritually bitter, a taste of unrelenting anguish. The Quraysh mocked the idea of the Hour, asking when it would come. This verse tells them that it is their appointment, and it will be far worse than any defeat they can imagine in this world. The fleeing at Badr will seem like a mild inconvenience compared to the terror of that Day. The verse serves to refocus the listener’s attention from the temporary to the eternal. The disbelievers are worried about their worldly power and prestige, but the real danger is the Hour that looms over them. For the believer, this verse is a powerful reminder that the ultimate accountability is not in this world. Even if justice is not fully served in the worldly life, the Hour is the great equaliser. The believer does not need to see the complete defeat of the oppressors now, because the Hour is their appointed time, and it will be catastrophic and bitter for them, while it will be a moment of relief and joy for the righteous. The verse instils patience and long-term perspective, anchoring the heart in the certainty of the Hereafter.
47 | Indeed, the wicked are ˹entrenched˺ in misguidance, and ˹are bound for˺ blazes. | This verse provides a summary diagnosis of the spiritual state of the deniers and their ultimate destination. The wicked, the “mujrimun,” are those who commit crimes, who sin persistently, who reject the truth. They are described as being in “dalal,” misguidance, and “su’ur,” which is often translated as blazes, insanity, or raging fire. The Arabic “fi dalalin wa su’ur” is a powerful pairing. The word “dalal” indicates that they are lost, wandering in error, cut off from the path of truth. The word “su’ur” can refer to the flames of Hell, or to a state of madness and frenzy. Many commentators understand it here as a reference to the Fire, meaning they are in misguidance in this world and will be in the blazing Fire in the next. This dual meaning captures their state in both realms. In this life, they are in the burning fever of false beliefs and desires, a spiritual madness that consumes them. In the Hereafter, they will be in the literal burning of Hell. The verse is a concise but complete description of the fate of the criminals. They are not merely mistaken; they are entrenched in their misguidance, meaning it is a settled condition, a deep-rooted state from which they refuse to emerge. They have chosen the blazing path over the cool, peaceful path of guidance. The verse stands as a stark warning to the Quraysh and to all who follow their way. The believer, in contrast, is someone who has been pulled out of the fire of misguidance and placed on the path of light. The verse fosters gratitude for guidance and a deep aversion to the state of the mujrimun. It also clarifies that the punishment of the wicked is not arbitrary; it is the natural culmination of their inner state of misguidance. The external fire of Hell is the manifestation of the internal fire of their rejected truth. The believer seeks refuge in Allah from being among those described in this verse and strives to ensure that their own inner state is one of guidance, not misguidance.
48 | On the Day they will be dragged into the Fire on their faces, ˹they will be told,˺ “Taste the touch of Hell!” | The surah now provides a horrifying glimpse of the punishment of the wicked on the Day of Judgment. They will be dragged into the Fire on their faces. The Arabic “yawma yushabuna fi an-nari ‘ala wujuhihim” paints a scene of utter humiliation and violent treatment. To be dragged on one’s face is the epitome of disgrace. It is how the most contemptible criminals might be treated. The faces, which in their worldly life were lifted in arrogance and pride, will now be scraping the ground as they are hauled toward the flames. This is the physical manifestation of their spiritual humiliation. They refused to bow their faces to Allah in prostration, so their faces will be debased in the Fire. The angels of punishment, mighty and stern, will drag them without mercy. As they are dragged and cast into the Fire, they will be told, “Taste the touch of Hell!” The Arabic “dhuqu massa saqar” uses the imperative “taste,” the same command heard in the stories of previous nations. The “touch” of Hell refers to the direct, sensory contact with its torment. The word “mass” implies a physical touching, an experience that is immediate and undeniable. The command to taste it is a bitter irony. In the world, they tasted the pleasures of sin, the delights of arrogance, the sweetness of mockery. Now, they are made to taste the true, searing reality they denied. The naming of Hell as “Saqar” is significant. It is one of the names of Hell in the Quran, evoking intense heat that melts and scorches. The verse leaves the listener with a visceral, terrifying image. For the believer, it inspires a profound fear of Allah’s punishment and a renewed determination to avoid the actions that lead to such a fate. It also provides a sense of justice; the oppressors who seemed untouchable will be dragged in the most degrading manner, and the believers who were humiliated in the world will witness the divine vindication. The verse is a powerful deterrent, using graphic imagery to convey the reality of the unseen punishment.
49 | Indeed, We have created everything, perfectly preordained. | This verse shifts from the description of the punishment to a foundational statement about divine creation and decree. Everything has been created by Allah with a precise measure, a preordained decree. The Arabic “inna kulla shay’in khalaqnahu bi qadar” contains the word “qadar,” which means measure, proportion, destiny, decree. This is a cornerstone of Islamic belief. The universe is not random. Every star in its orbit, every leaf that falls, every event that unfolds, is part of a divine plan measured with perfect wisdom. The creation itself is an act of qadar; everything is given its specific form, function, size, and lifespan. The decree extends to the events of human history, including the stories of the destroyed nations and the punishment of the wicked that the surah has just described. None of it happened by chance. The destruction of Noah’s people, ‘Ad, Thamud, Lot, and Pharaoh were all measured, written, and executed according to a divine plan. This verse is a powerful response to those who question divine justice. The punishment is not a capricious outburst of anger. It is part of a precisely measured system of cause and effect, reward and punishment, that is embedded in the very fabric of creation. For the believer, this verse brings immense peace and trust. Knowing that everything is created by Allah with qadar means that one’s life is not a chaotic series of accidents. The difficulties, the joys, the trials, and the triumphs all have meaning and purpose within the divine measure. It also calls the believer to align their own life with the divine measure, to accept the decree with patience and gratitude, and to act responsibly within the limits set by Allah. The verse also refutes the pre-Islamic Arab belief in a blind, impersonal fate. The Quranic qadar is not blind; it is the deliberate, wise measure of a personal Creator. The believer is not a helpless victim of fate but a servant of the All-Wise who acts within the framework of His decree while making genuine choices for which they are accountable. The verse stands as a majestic reminder of Allah’s sovereignty and the perfect order that underlies all existence.
50 | Our command is but a single word, done in the blink of an eye. | This verse elaborates on the ease and speed with which Allah’s decree is executed. “Our command is but a single word.” The Arabic “wa ma amruna illa wahidatun” means that the divine command, the act of bringing something into being or executing a decree, is just one thing, one word. There is no process, no delay, no gathering of resources for Allah. When He wills something, He simply says “Be,” and it is. This is the ultimate expression of omnipotence. The second part of the verse, “done in the blink of an eye,” translates the Arabic “ka lamhin bi al-basar,” meaning like a glance of the eye, instantaneous. The resurrection of all humanity, the creation of the universe, the destruction of nations, all are equally effortless for Allah. The blink of an eye is one of the fastest motions a human can perceive. It conveys the idea that what seems impossibly vast and complicated to the human mind is trivially easy for the Creator. This verse serves as a direct refutation of the deniers who found the resurrection impossible. How can dead bones be brought back to life? How can an entire world be destroyed and recreated? The answer is that Allah’s command is a single word, taking no more time than a glance. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on their craftsmanship and their labour, could not fathom a creation that required no effort. This verse corrects their limited perspective. It also provides profound comfort to the believer. The help of Allah, when it comes, can arrive in the blink of an eye. The long periods of waiting and hardship are not signs of Allah’s inability or forgetfulness; they are part of the measured decree. When the command comes, the situation can change instantly. The defeat of the united front, the rescue of the oppressed, the fulfilment of the promise, all can happen in a single moment by a single divine word. The believer is called to trust in this divine speed and not to grow impatient with the apparent slowness of worldly events. The Lord who created the cosmos with a word can resolve any problem with a word. The verse leaves the heart in a state of awe at the sheer power and majesty of Allah.
51 | We have already destroyed the likes of you. So will any ˹of you˺ be mindful? | The surah now delivers a direct and personal warning to the Quraysh, drawing the historical lessons to their doorstep. “We have already destroyed the likes of you.” The Arabic “wa laqad ahlakna ashya’akum” means that your counterparts, your fellows in disbelief, those who behaved just as you are behaving, have been destroyed. The Quraysh are not unique. They are just the latest iteration of a recurring type. The people of Noah, ‘Ad, Thamud, Lot, and Pharaoh were all “the likes of you” in their rejection, their mockery, and their arrogance. Their destruction is a matter of historical record, visible in the ruins of their civilizations and preserved in the stories passed down through generations. The verse uses the past tense to emphasise the certainty of the event. It is not a future possibility; it is an established pattern. Then, the Quran asks the question that has been the refrain of the surah, but with a slight variation: “So will any of you be mindful?” The Arabic “fa hal min muddakir” is the same call to remembrance, but here the “any” is specifically directed at “you,” the contemporaries of the Prophet. After all the stories, all the refrains, all the invitations, the question is posed one final time in this context: will any of you take heed? The implication is that the opportunity is still open, but it is slipping away. The destruction of the likes of them is a fact. The only thing standing between them and joining those destroyed peoples is their willingness to be mindful. The verse is a masterful conclusion to the historical section of the surah. It transforms the distant stories into an immediate, personal ultimatum. The believer, reading this, feels the weight of history and the urgency of the present moment. They answer the question with a resounding affirmation of mindfulness, committing themselves to be among those who remember, who learn, and who change. The verse is an open door for repentance, held open even as the shadow of destruction looms.
52 | Everything they have done is ˹listed˺ in ˹their˺ records. | The surah now moves to the theme of precise divine record-keeping. Everything that the deniers have done, every action, every word, every thought, is listed in the records, the “zubur.” The Arabic “wa kullu shay’in fa’aluhu fi az-zubur” indicates that their deeds are written down in books, in scrolls, in the registers kept by the recording angels. The word “zubur” is the plural of “zabur,” meaning a written book or scroll. This is a powerful reminder that no deed is lost, no sin is forgotten, no injustice is overlooked. The deniers might think that their mockery, their persecution of the believers, their plotting against the Prophet, their rejection of the signs, and their indulgence in worldly pleasures are all passing events that will leave no trace. This verse corrects that illusion. Everything is recorded. The records are comprehensive and precise. On the Day of Judgment, these records will be presented, and the deniers will be confronted with the complete account of their lives. They will see their deeds in front of them, undeniable and inescapable. The verse echoes similar themes throughout the Quran, where the book of deeds is mentioned, and the individual is told to read their own record, with no need for an external judge because their own limbs will testify against them. For the believer, this verse inspires a profound sense of accountability. Every moment of life is being written. Every act of kindness, every word of truth, every struggle against the self, as well as every sin, is recorded. This awareness should shape the believer’s conduct, making them mindful of even the smallest deeds. It also brings comfort, because the injustices they suffer are also being recorded, and not a single tear or sigh of the oppressed is missed. The divine bureaucracy of the unseen is flawless. The verse is a bridge between the warning of destruction and the detailed descriptions of the Hereafter that follow, anchoring the moral order in the certainty of perfect record-keeping.
53 | Every matter, small and large, is written ˹precisely˺. | This verse intensifies the previous one by specifying that the record includes everything, small and large. The Arabic “wa kullu saghirin wa kabirin mustatar” means that every minor matter and every major matter is written down, recorded in lines, set down in a register. The word “saghir” refers to the small, the insignificant, the things people might consider trivial and forgettable. The word “kabir” refers to the large, the major, the significant events. Nothing escapes the divine record. The speck of dust, the fleeting thought, the muttered word, the grand public deed, all are inscribed. This comprehensiveness is both terrifying and comforting. It is terrifying for the sinner, who realises that no sin, no matter how secret or small, is overlooked. It is comforting for the righteous, who know that no good deed, no matter how minor, is wasted. The widow’s sigh, the orphan’s tear, the small act of charity done in secret, all are written down and will be rewarded. The verse also carries a profound theological message about the nature of Allah’s knowledge. It is not limited to the general or the grand. It encompasses the minute and the particular. The fall of a leaf, the crawling of an ant in the darkness, the whisper of a soul, all are within the scope of His knowledge and are recorded in the divine archives. This understanding has a transformative effect on the believer’s heart. It means that one is never alone, never unseen, never unheard. Every moment is lived in the presence of a Witness who records everything. This awareness is the essence of ihsan, the spiritual excellence that the Prophet defined as worshipping Allah as if you see Him. The verse thus serves as a powerful motivator for mindfulness and a restraint against heedlessness. The deniers who thought their actions would disappear into the past are informed that their deeds are eternally preserved in a precise record, waiting for the Day of Reckoning. The believer, in contrast, looks forward to seeing their record filled with the mercy and forgiveness of Allah, and the good deeds that will outweigh any mistakes.
54 | Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and rivers, | The surah, having delivered its powerful warnings and described the fate of the wicked, now turns in its closing verses to the beautiful reward of the righteous. The contrast is deliberate and follows the Quranic pattern of pairing warning with glad tidings. The righteous, the “muttaqun,” are those who possessed taqwa, God-consciousness, who lived their lives in awe of their Lord and in obedience to His commands. Their destination is described as being amid Gardens and rivers. The Arabic “fi jannatin wa nahar” places them in the heart of Paradise, surrounded by lush gardens and flowing rivers. The Gardens represent the ultimate environment of beauty, peace, and abundance, free from the scorching winds and barren deserts that the deniers faced in their worldly punishment and will face in the Hereafter. The rivers are a symbol of perpetual life, purity, and delight. In the arid landscape of Arabia, flowing water was the most vivid symbol of luxury and divine favour. The righteous will not merely have a garden with a river; they will be amid multiple gardens and rivers, an entire landscape of beauty. The preposition “fi” indicates that they are immersed in this environment. It surrounds them. They are not visitors or observers; they are the permanent, honoured residents. This verse is the answer to the earlier refrain. Those who were mindful, those who remembered, those who took heed of the warnings, are the ones who end up here. The Gardens and rivers are the fruit of their mindfulness. The believer reads this and feels a surge of hope and motivation. The path of taqwa may involve difficulty, sacrifice, and swimming against the current of a heedless society, but its destination is a place of unimaginable bliss. The verse is short, but it opens a window into the eternal home of the believers, a home that makes every worldly hardship seem insignificant. It is the divine invitation, extended to all who will accept it: come, be mindful, be righteous, and you will be amid Gardens and rivers.
55 | at the Seat of Honour in the presence of the Most Powerful Sovereign. | The surah concludes with a verse of breathtaking beauty and majesty, describing the pinnacle of the believers' reward. The righteous will be at the Seat of Honour, in the presence of the Most Powerful Sovereign. The Arabic “fi maq’adi sidqin ‘inda Malikin Muqtadir” contains layers of profound meaning. The “Seat of Honour” translates “maq’ad sidq,” which literally means a seat of truthfulness, a place of firm and established honour, a station that is real and not illusory, a position of complete integrity and dignity. In the courts of earthly kings, the seats of honour are reserved for the most favoured nobles. In Paradise, every believer is given a seat of honour, a position of closeness and favour that is absolutely real and eternal. This seat is “in the presence of” the King, “Malikin,” the Most Powerful, “Muqtadir.” The nearness to Allah is the greatest reward, surpassing even the Gardens and rivers. The believer is not placed in a distant corner of Paradise; they are brought near to the Divine Presence. This nearness is one of spiritual vision, intimacy, and honour. The name “Malik” emphasises Allah’s absolute sovereignty and ownership. The believers are granted an audience with the King of all kings. The name “Muqtadir” emphasises His perfect power, echoing the earlier verse where Pharaoh’s people were seized with the grip of the Almighty, Most Powerful. The same power that destroyed the tyrants is the power that will honour the believers with a seat in His presence. The combination of these two names inspires both awe and intimacy. He is the King, so the believer is in the presence of ultimate majesty. He is the Most Powerful, so the seat of honour is secure, unassailable by any enemy. The surah thus ends on a note of sublime hope and aspiration. It began with the splitting of the moon and the nearness of the Hour, proceeded through the terrifying accounts of destroyed nations, issued urgent refrains to be mindful, and now rests at the feet of the Divine Throne, where the righteous sit in honour. For the believer, this final verse is the ultimate motivation. It is not just about escaping the Fire; it is about attaining the Seat of Honour in the presence of the Beloved. The entire journey of the surah, from the cosmic sign to the divine presence, is completed. The invitation stands, echoing in the heart of every listener: be mindful, be righteous, and your seat is waiting.
