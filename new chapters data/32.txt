1 | Alif-Lãm-Mĩm. | These are among the disjointed letters that open a number of surahs in the Quran, and their full meaning is known only to Allah. Yet they carry profound lessons. The most prominent understanding is that these letters are drawn from the very alphabet of the Arabic language—the same letters that form the speech of the Arabs. By beginning the surah with them, Allah immediately draws attention to the fact that this Quran is composed of familiar letters, yet no one can produce anything like it. It is a direct challenge to the eloquent masters of the Arabic tongue and a proof that the Book is not the invention of a human being. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, was unlettered and could not have authored such a miraculous text. These letters also serve as a divine signature, arresting the listener’s attention and signaling that what follows is of the highest importance. Some scholars have noted that these disconnected letters often appear before a mention of the Quran’s revelation, as if to say: “From these simple letters, this mighty Book is formed.” Thus Alif-Lãm-Mĩm introduces the grand theme of the surah: the absolute certainty of divine revelation. The opening creates an atmosphere of awe and reflection, preparing the heart to receive the truths that follow about creation, resurrection, and guidance. In this way, even three letters become a gateway into a world of meaning and a compelling invitation to accept the message.
2 | The revelation of this Book is—beyond doubt—from the Lord of all worlds. | The phrase “The revelation of this Book” affirms that the Quran is being sent down from on high; it is not a product of the Prophet’s mind or the culture of the Arabs. The word translated as “revelation” implies a gradual descent guided by divine wisdom. Then comes the powerful emphasis “beyond doubt,” a forceful removal of any uncertainty. In Arabic, this negation of doubt is absolute: there is no place in a sound heart for suspicion regarding the origin of this scripture. The verse crowns this certainty by declaring that the revelation is “from the Lord of all worlds.” The title “Lord of all worlds” encompasses every creation, every era, and every nation, indicating that the message is universal and not limited to a particular tribe. The very same Lord who nurtures the cosmos, who provided earlier scriptures, has now revealed this final guidance. This verse was a direct answer to the Meccan chieftains who accused the Prophet of fabricating the Quran. It reassures the believers and the Prophet himself that the truth they hold is securely anchored in the Sovereign of the universe. Moreover, by mentioning His lordship over all worlds, the verse reminds every reader that the One who sent the Book is also the One who will hold everyone to account, linking the revelation to the coming judgment.
3 | Or do they say, “He has fabricated it!”? No! It is the truth from your Lord in order for you to warn a people to whom no warner has come before you, so they may be ˹rightly˺ guided. | The verse opens with a rhetorical question that exposes the slander of the disbelievers: “Or do they say, ‘He has fabricated it!’?” This accusation was repeatedly hurled at the Prophet, peace be upon him, by those who could not otherwise dismiss the Quran’s impact. The response comes with the emphatic particle “No!”—a sharp and complete rejection of their claim. Then follows the affirmation: “It is the truth from your Lord,” meaning the Quran embodies absolute reality and originates from the One who possesses all authority. The next part states the purpose of this truth: “in order for you to warn a people to whom no warner has come before you.” The Arabs had been without a prophet for a long interval since the time of Ishmael, peace be upon him, and they had sunk into idolatry and ignorance. Thus the sending of Muhammad was a pure act of mercy to lift them from darkness. The verse concludes with “so they may be rightly guided,” highlighting that the ultimate divine intent is not punishment but guidance. The entire statement refutes the fabrication charge by showing the logical necessity of a warner for a people left without divine instruction, and it frames the Prophet’s mission as a continuation of a long chain of mercy.
4 | It is Allah Who has created the heavens and the earth and everything in between in six Days, then established Himself on the Throne. You have no protector or intercessor besides Him. Will you not then be mindful? | The verse opens with a declaration that the very Being who revealed the Book is the One “Who has created the heavens and the earth and everything in between.” This vast creation is brought into existence “in six Days,” a period whose length only Allah knows, demonstrating purposeful, gradual creation rather than instantaneous production, which teaches wisdom and patience. The statement “then established Himself on the Throne” refers to Allah’s highness and supreme authority in a manner that befits His majesty, without likening it to the settling of created beings. The Throne is the greatest of created things, and His establishment over it signifies His absolute control. Immediately after this, the verse undercuts the entire basis of Meccan idolatry: “You have no protector or intercessor besides Him.” The pagans worshipped idols claiming they would intercede, but here Allah negates any guardian or intercessor operating independently of His will. This leaves humanity with no refuge but to turn directly to Him. The verse concludes with the searching question “Will you not then be mindful?”—an invitation to use the intellect to draw the obvious conclusion: the Creator alone deserves worship and obedience, and His revelation must be heeded.
5 | He conducts every affair from the heavens to the earth, then it all ascends to Him on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting. | This verse explains how Allah’s authority over creation operates. “He conducts every affair” means He plans, decrees, and manages all matters—the descent of angels with revelation, the distribution of provisions, life and death, and the unfolding of destinies. The affairs descend “from the heavens to the earth” by His command. Then “it all ascends to Him”—the outcomes of those affairs, the deeds of His servants, and the records of all that occurred return to Him for judgment. This ascent takes place “on a Day whose length is a thousand years by your counting.” Many scholars interpret this Day as the Day of Resurrection, which will feel like a thousand years of earthly time to those experiencing it, emphasizing its immense severity. Others understand it as the time it takes for the angels to ascend with the records, a distance that by human reckoning is a thousand years, showing the vastness of the heavens. The phrase “by your counting” contrasts human perception of time with divine reality, reminding us that Allah is not bound by time. This verse gives the Prophet and the believers unshakable confidence: the One who manages every affair will certainly bring all matters to a just conclusion, and no mockery of the disbelievers can alter that.
6 | That is the Knower of the seen and unseen—the Almighty, Most Merciful, | The demonstrative pronoun “That” points back to the One who conducts all affairs, identifying Him as “the Knower of the seen and unseen.” His knowledge encompasses everything that is observable to creation and everything that is hidden—inner thoughts, future events, the realities of the heavens, and the secrets of the hearts. This attribute proves that His management of the universe is perfect, for a being without comprehensive knowledge could not govern such a complex creation. The verse then pairs two magnificent names: “the Almighty,” highlighting irresistible power and dominance, and “Most Merciful,” highlighting an endless, tender mercy. This combination is crucial. His might means that He can enforce His will without hindrance, while His mercy means that His power is not exercised tyrannically. For the believers, this pairing brings immense comfort: their Lord is strong enough to protect them and merciful enough to forgive and provide. For the deniers, it serves as both a warning and an invitation—fear His power but hope in His mercy by turning back. The verse thus presents a complete picture of the divine being behind creation and revelation, encouraging awe and trust alike.
7 | Who has perfected everything He created. And He originated the creation of humankind from clay. | This verse continues to describe the Creator by highlighting the excellence of His work: “Who has perfected everything He created.” Nothing in Allah’s creation is flawed or random; every entity is given the precise form and function suited to its purpose. The word “perfected” indicates beauty, proportionality, and wisdom woven into every level of existence, from the orbiting stars to the intricacies of a living cell. This perfection is a manifest sign for those who reflect. Then the verse narrows the focus specifically to humanity: “And He originated the creation of humankind from clay.” This refers to the creation of Adam, peace be upon him, fashioned from clay. By mentioning the humble origin of the human body, the verse lays the groundwork for the argument for resurrection. The One who began the human race from simple clay, shaping it into a perfect form, can just as easily bring them back after death. The mention of clay also serves to humble human beings, reminding them of their lowly material origin so that they do not become arrogant toward their Maker. The transition from the vast creation to the particular creation of man connects the cosmic signs with the personal reality each soul must face.
8 | Then He made his descendants from an extract of a humble fluid, | After mentioning the creation of the first human from clay, this verse traces the continuation of the human race: “Then He made his descendants from an extract of a humble fluid.” The word “descendants” refers to the progeny of Adam, propagated through the normal process of reproduction. The “extract of a humble fluid” describes a small, selected portion of the sperm-drop, which in Arabic is called sulalah. The fluid is described as “humble” or “despised” to highlight its insignificance in the eyes of people, yet from this weak origin Allah brings forth a complex human being with intellect and faculties. This progression from clay to water-based fluid demonstrates layers of divine wisdom. The description serves a dual purpose: it humbles human pride by reminding every person of their lowly biological beginning, and it provides a powerful proof of Allah’s ability to resurrect. If Allah can cause a fully formed human to emerge from a mere drop of fluid, reviving the dead from scattered particles is certainly within His power. The word “then” links the stages, showing that the same Creator who shaped Adam from clay also designed the ongoing system of procreation, leaving no room for any alternative explanation.
9 | then He fashioned them and had a spirit of His Own ˹creation˺ breathed into them. And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect. ˹Yet˺ you hardly give any thanks. | The verse continues the stages of human creation inside the womb: “then He fashioned them,” meaning Allah proportioned the developing form with precise limbs and features. Then comes a moment of immense honor: “and had a spirit of His Own creation breathed into them.” The spirit is a created entity, but it is attributed to Allah as a mark of nobility, much like the Kaaba is called the House of Allah. This breathing transforms the formed body into a living, conscious soul. After that, the verse draws attention to the gifts given: “And He gave you hearing, sight, and intellect.” These faculties are mentioned in a deliberate order, with hearing coming first, then sight, and then the heart or mind, which processes understanding. They are the primary tools for acquiring knowledge and recognizing the truth. Yet despite these immense blessings, the verse ends with the sorrowful observation: “Yet you hardly give any thanks.” Most human beings use these faculties for worldly purposes but fail to employ them in gratitude and in seeking their Lord. The verse thus combines a profound description of human dignity with a sharp reminder of the ingratitude that often accompanies it, setting the stage for the denial of resurrection that follows.
10 | ˹Still˺ they ask ˹mockingly˺, “When we are disintegrated into the earth, will we really be raised as a new creation?” In fact, they are in denial of the meeting with their Lord. | Despite the clear signs of creative power described in the previous verses, the disbelievers continue to ask their question, not out of sincere inquiry but with mockery. “When we are disintegrated into the earth” refers to the body’s complete decomposition after death, its particles scattering and mixing with the soil. They find it incredible that “a new creation” could be raised from such a state. Their question implies that a second genesis is impossible because they see no precedent in their daily experience. Allah immediately exposes the deeper issue: “In fact, they are in denial of the meeting with their Lord.” Their problem is not a lack of evidence or a genuine intellectual difficulty; it is a moral and spiritual rejection of being held accountable. The phrase “meeting with their Lord” signifies the Day of Judgment when they will stand before Him. This verse turns the tables on them by pointing to the heart of their disbelief. The earlier stages of creation already provide a sufficient answer to the “how” of resurrection, but because they do not want to face the consequences of their deeds, they cling to the supposed impossibility. This diagnosis is crucial for understanding the entire surah’s argument.
11 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death, who is in charge of you. Then to your Lord you will ˹all˺ be returned.” | Instead of engaging the mockers in a theoretical debate about the possibility of resurrection, the Prophet is instructed to bring the discussion to an undeniable reality: death itself. “Your soul will be taken by the Angel of Death” reminds them that their own exit from this world is certain and in the hands of an angel specifically entrusted with this task. The Angel of Death has been “put in charge of you,” meaning he is appointed over every human being, and no one can escape his grasp or negotiate a delay. This truth was well known to the Arabs, who acknowledged death but sought to ignore what comes after it. The verse then draws the direct conclusion: “Then to your Lord you will all be returned.” If the soul is already being taken by a divine agent and brought into another realm, the return to the Lord for judgment is a natural continuation of that same divine control. The verse skillfully bypasses the objection about disintegrated bodies by focusing on the soul, whose fate is entirely in Allah’s hands. It confronts every listener with the immediate reality of their own mortality, making denial of the Hereafter seem utterly irrational.
12 | If only you could see the wicked hanging their heads ˹in shame˺ before their Lord, ˹crying:˺ “Our Lord! We have now seen and heard, so send us back and we will do good. We truly have sure faith ˹now˺!” | The verse paints a vivid scene of the Day of Judgment for the Prophet and his followers to reflect upon. “If only you could see” is a rhetorical expression that brings the future event vividly before the mind’s eye. The wicked will be “hanging their heads in shame,” a posture of utter humiliation and defeat. Their arrogance and mockery from the worldly life will be replaced by complete subjugation. From this state of disgrace, they cry out: “Our Lord! We have now seen and heard.” They acknowledge that they have witnessed the realities of the unseen that they used to deny and have heard the truth of the warnings they once ridiculed. Their request, “so send us back and we will do good,” betrays a desperate desire for a second chance, a life in which they would act righteously. They even add, “We truly have sure faith now,” but faith that comes only after seeing the punishment is not accepted. The verse serves as a heart-shaking warning to those still alive that the opportunity for voluntary belief is only in this world, and that delaying until certainty is forced upon them will only lead to eternal regret.
13 | Had We willed, We could have easily imposed guidance on every soul. But My Word will come to pass: I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together. | This verse addresses a profound question about divine will and human free choice. “Had We willed, We could have easily imposed guidance on every soul” means that Allah possesses the power to compel every human and jinn to believe, just as He does with the angels, leaving them no choice. However, such forced faith would not serve the purpose of the test for which creation was brought into existence. Instead, He gave humans and jinn free will and sent guidance through messengers, making them morally accountable. “But My Word will come to pass” refers to the divine decree pronounced in eternity based on His perfect foreknowledge: that Hell will be filled with those who freely choose rebellion after the truth has been made clear to them. The mention of “jinn and humans all together” shows that both species with free will are subject to this decree. This verse should not be misunderstood as an arbitrary predestination to evil. Rather, Allah’s knowledge of who would reject faith and who would accept it does not eliminate their choice. The filling of Hell is a just consequence of those choices, not a compulsion, and it serves as a warning that divine patience should not be mistaken for inability or indifference.
14 | So taste ˹the punishment˺ for neglecting the meeting of this Day of yours. We ˹too˺ will certainly neglect you. And taste the torment of eternity for what you used to do! | On the Day of Judgment, the deniers will be addressed with these biting words. “So taste the punishment” is an order that starts their unending suffering. The reason given is “for neglecting the meeting of this Day of yours.” While on earth, they lived as if this Day would never come, pushing it out of their thoughts and mocking its possibility. Their deliberate neglect is now repaid in kind: “We too will certainly neglect you.” This means Allah will leave them in their torment, paying no merciful attention to their cries. It is a just recompense—since they forgot their Lord, they are now forgotten in the place of punishment. The command continues, “And taste the torment of eternity for what you used to do.” The punishment is eternal because their primary crime, disbelief and rejection of the truth, was not a temporary lapse but a settled, lifelong stance. The phrase “for what you used to do” ties the suffering directly to their actions, affirming divine justice. This verse ends the warning section of the surah with a heavy and sobering finality, preparing the listener to contrast this fate with the reward of the believers described in the next verses.
15 | The only ˹true˺ believers in Our revelation are those who—when it is recited to them—fall into prostration and glorify the praises of their Lord and are not too proud. | After the severe warnings to the deniers, the surah now turns to the beautiful qualities of the sincere believers. “The only true believers in Our revelation” indicates that mere verbal profession is not enough; faith must manifest in action. The defining sign is that “when it is recited to them,” meaning when the verses of the Quran are heard, “they fall into prostration.” This falling is not a slow, reluctant descent but a swift, humble dropping to the ground in immediate submission. Accompanying this physical act, they “glorify the praises of their Lord,” declaring His perfection and expressing gratitude while in the position of greatest humility. The final clause, “and are not too proud,” identifies the barrier that prevents the disbelievers from doing the same. The Quraysh leaders refused to bow, considering it beneath their dignity. This verse gave the surah its name, As-Sajdah, because it encapsulates the believer’s response to divine words. The prostration here is both a physical act of worship and a symbol of the heart’s surrender, demonstrating that true faith engages the body, the tongue, and the soul together, without any trace of arrogance.
16 | They abandon their beds, invoking their Lord with hope and fear, and donate from what We have provided for them. | The description of true believers continues, now moving from their public response to revelation to their private devotion. “They abandon their beds” paints a picture of people whose sides forsake the comfort of warm sleep. They rise in the depths of the night to stand before their Lord in voluntary prayer, or tahajjud, when no human eyes are watching. In that seclusion, they are “invoking their Lord with hope and fear,” meaning their supplication and prayer are driven by a beautiful balance—hoping earnestly for Allah’s mercy and Paradise, while trembling with fear of His punishment and displeasure. This balance keeps them from complacency and despair. Alongside this spiritual devotion, they “donate from what We have provided for them,” fulfilling the financial rights of others through obligatory charity and voluntary spending. The phrase “from what We have provided” reminds them that their wealth is a trust from Allah, not an absolute possession. This verse thus combines the two major dimensions of worship: the personal, hidden connection with the Lord in the stillness of the night, and the outward expression of care for others through spending, both performed solely for Allah’s sake.
17 | No soul can imagine what delights are kept in store for them as a reward for what they used to do. | This is one of the most hope-inspiring verses in the Quran, promising a reward that surpasses human imagination. “No soul can imagine” declares the complete inability of any human mind to conceive of the delights awaiting the believers. The word used for “delights” suggests the ultimate joy and comfort that will cool the eyes of the righteous—a bliss so profound that it has no parallel in worldly experience. These delights are “kept in store for them,” hidden away by Allah as a treasured secret, which increases the longing and anticipation of the believers. The reward is “as a recompense for what they used to do,” linking it directly to their secret night prayers, their humble prostrations, and their selfless charity described just before. A famous authentic hadith qudsi explains this verse: Allah says, “I have prepared for My righteous servants what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has ever imagined.” This hidden nature of the reward is perfectly suited to the hidden nature of their best deeds, performed away from the gaze of others. It provides immense comfort to believers facing hardship in this world, assuring them that their labor is not in vain and their future joy is beyond all calculation.
18 | Is the one who is a believer equal ˹before Allah˺ to the one who is rebellious? They are not equal! | After detailing the spiritual beauty of the believers and the terrible fate of the disbelievers, the surah poses a rhetorical question that demands reflection. “Is the one who is a believer equal before Allah to the one who is rebellious?” The contrast is between a faithful person whose life is shaped by obedience and worship, and a fasiq, a rebellious sinner who has stepped outside the boundaries of divine guidance. The phrase “before Allah” indicates that the comparison is not based on worldly status, wealth, or power, but on the true value and rank in the sight of the Creator. The emphatic answer immediately follows: “They are not equal!” The inequality is absolute and irreversible. On earth, a believer and a disbeliever may appear similar in their human needs and daily activities, but their paths, their inner realities, and their ultimate destinations have nothing in common. This verse serves both as a comfort to the righteous and a warning to the heedless. It establishes a fundamental principle of divine justice that will be fully unveiled in the Hereafter, and it sets the stage for the detailed descriptions of the two homes that follow.
19 | As for those who believe and do good, they will have the Gardens of ˹Eternal˺ Residence—as an accommodation for what they used to do. | The verse now spells out the reward for the first group. “As for those who believe and do good” links inward conviction with outward righteous action, neither being sufficient alone. Their destination is “the Gardens of Eternal Residence,” a place where they will settle forever, never to be expelled or to face death. The term “Residence” emphasizes safety, permanence, and belonging. Interestingly, the reward is described as “an accommodation,” which translates the Arabic word nuzul. Nuzul originally refers to the hospitality and provision prepared for an honored guest upon arrival. This choice of word transforms the image of Paradise into a generous welcome, as if the believers are arriving as beloved visitors for whom the finest dwelling has been prepared. It is not a cold, transactional entry but a warm reception. The phrase “for what they used to do” reaffirms that this honor is tied to their deeds, yet it is ultimately an act of Allah’s grace, because human actions alone can never equal such an infinite reward. The verse extends the contrast begun earlier, holding up the believers’ home as a place of peace and honor in stark opposition to the home of the rebellious described next.
20 | But as for those who are rebellious, the Fire will be their home. Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it, and will be told, “Taste the Fire’s torment, which you used to deny.” | The surah now presents the corresponding fate of the second group with terrifying clarity. “But as for those who are rebellious,” the same fasiqun mentioned earlier, “the Fire will be their home.” Just as the believers have a residence of eternal welcome, these sinners have a permanent dwelling of blazing fire. The verse then describes a particularly painful aspect of their torment: “Whenever they try to escape from it, they will be forced back into it.” In their desperation, driven by unimaginable suffering, they will attempt to flee, perhaps when the flames surge or when they catch a glimpse of relief. But the angels of punishment will drive them back with iron hooks and harsh rebukes, extinguishing any flicker of hope. To compound the physical agony, they will be told: “Taste the Fire’s torment, which you used to deny.” This verbal reprimand links their current suffering directly to their worldly attitude. They mocked the idea of hellfire and dismissed the warnings, and now they are made to experience the very reality they belittled. The verse completes the parallel of the two homes, ending this section on a note of solemn warning that urges every listener to choose the path of belief.
21 | We will certainly make them taste some of the minor torment ˹in this life˺ before the major torment ˹of the Hereafter˺, so perhaps they will return ˹to the Right Path˺. | After warning the rebellious of the eternal Fire, Allah now reveals a profound aspect of His mercy even within the framework of punishment. "We will certainly make them taste some of the minor torment" refers to afflictions and hardships that strike in this worldly life before the great, overwhelming torment of the Hereafter. This "minor torment" takes many forms: it could be a humiliating defeat in battle as happened at Badr, a severe famine that gripped Mecca for years, illnesses, loss of wealth, or the torment of the grave which is a foretaste of the afterlife. The purpose is not mere vengeance but is made beautifully clear in the next phrase: "so perhaps they will return." The Arabic word la'allahum carries the meaning of hope and expectation. The worldly punishment serves as a divine wake-up call, a merciful jolt designed to shake the heedless soul out of its deep slumber. When a person suffers, the false supports of this world crumble, and they are more likely to turn back to their Lord in humility and repentance. The verse thus frames even calamities as opportunities for correction. The phrase "before the major torment" underscores the urgency: the current life is a limited window of probation. If the minor warning is not heeded, what follows will be infinitely more severe and inescapable. This balance between warning and hope demonstrates that Allah desires reformation, not destruction, and it encourages believers to view their own trials as potential means of purification and return to the right path.
22 | And who does more wrong than the one who is reminded of Allah’s revelations then turns away from them? We will surely inflict punishment upon the wicked. | This verse poses a rhetorical question that identifies the height of human injustice. "And who does more wrong" invites reflection: there is no person more unjust and self-destructive than the one "who is reminded of Allah’s revelations then turns away from them." To be reminded of the verses of the Quran, the signs in creation, or the admonitions of the prophets is to be given the most valuable gift—a clear map to truth and salvation. Yet this person deliberately ignores it, as if the reminder had never reached his ears. He does not even engage, refute, or consider; he simply turns away with disdain, busying himself with worldly distractions. This action is a direct violation of the very purpose of human faculties, especially the intellect which was given to discern truth. The verse then declares the inevitable consequence: "We will surely inflict punishment upon the wicked." Here, "the wicked" translates al-mujrimun, those who are steeped in crime against their own souls through persistent rejection and sin. The punishment is certain and decreed by divine justice, matching the severity of their crime. This verse serves as a powerful reproach to anyone who habitually hears the Quran and the call to faith without allowing it to penetrate their heart. It underscores that the greatest wrong is not against other people but against one's own soul by cutting it off from the source of eternal life, and it leaves no room for excuses on the Day when accounts are settled.
23 | Indeed, We gave the Scripture to Moses—so let there be no doubt ˹O Prophet˺ that you ˹too˺ are receiving revelations—and We made it a guide for the Children of Israel. | In the face of constant rejection and mockery, the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, is comforted with a reference to a previous messenger. "Indeed, We gave the Scripture to Moses" reminds him that divine revelation is an established pattern. The Torah was a comprehensive book sent down to a great prophet, just as the Quran is now being sent down. This historical parallel serves a dual purpose: it strengthens the Prophet’s heart and challenges the disbelievers’ claim that it is strange for God to reveal a book to a man. The parenthetical statement "so let there be no doubt that you too are receiving revelations" is directed at the Prophet and, by extension, at all who hear the Quran. The Arabic construction can also be understood as "so be not in doubt about the meeting with Moses" or "about the reception of the Quran," but the clearest meaning is that just as Moses was truly a recipient of revelation, so is Muhammad. The verse then says, "and We made it a guide for the Children of Israel." The Torah was a source of light and direction for the Israelites, containing laws, wisdom, and prophecies about the final messenger. By mentioning this, Allah hints that the Quran holds an even more universal and complete guidance. The verse weaves together the comfort of prophetic brotherhood with the certainty of the Quran's divine origin, encouraging the believers to stand firm in their conviction despite the noise of the deniers.
24 | We raised from among them leaders, guiding by Our command, when they patiently endured and firmly believed in Our signs. | Continuing the reference to the Children of Israel, this verse explains how they were led after Moses. "We raised from among them leaders" translates the word a'immah, which means spiritual guides and exemplars who lead others to truth by divine appointment. These leaders could refer to the prophets sent among the Israelites after Moses, or to the righteous scholars and devout men who upheld the Torah. Their defining quality was "guiding by Our command," meaning their guidance was not based on personal opinion or worldly interest but was rooted in the divine instructions they received. The verse then reveals the two essential qualities that qualified them for this exalted position: "when they patiently endured and firmly believed in Our signs." Patience, or sabr, was required to face the trials of leadership, the stubbornness of their people, and the hardships of upholding truth in a hostile environment. Their firm belief in the signs of Allah, meaning both the revealed verses and the signs in the cosmos, gave them unshakeable conviction. This verse provides a timeless principle for leadership in faith: it is not inherited or achieved by ambition, but is a divine trust given to those whose hearts are filled with patience and certainty. It was a subtle reminder to the Quraysh that true leaders are not the wealthy chiefs but those who embody these spiritual traits, and it serves as a model for all who seek to guide others toward righteousness.
25 | Indeed, your Lord will decide between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences. | This verse offers a decisive conclusion to the discussion about the People of the Book and the disagreements that arose among them after receiving guidance. "Indeed, your Lord will decide between them" points to a future, definitive judgment. The "differences" include the theological disputes that fractured the Israelites into sects, their arguments over the truth of the Torah, and their conflicting positions regarding the Prophet Muhammad and the Quran. In this world, these disagreements can cause confusion, strife, and even doubt in the hearts of believers who witness the bitter divisions among those who claim to follow earlier scriptures. But the verse assures the Prophet and his followers that these disputes are not permanent and will not remain unresolved. On the Day of Judgment, Allah, the All-Knowing, will render His perfect verdict, making clear who was right and who was wrong, rewarding the faithful and punishing the deviant. This promise of final arbitration brings immense peace to the believer’s soul. It teaches that one should not become overly distressed by the endless arguments of this life, for the ultimate justice lies with the Lord. The verse thus redirects the focus from earthly polemics to the certainty of the Hereafter, and it serves as a warning to those who knowingly distort divine truth, for their judgment is inescapable and will be based on perfect knowledge.
26 | Is it not yet clear to them how many peoples We destroyed before them, whose ruins they still pass by? Surely in this are signs. Will they not then listen? | The surah now appeals directly to the disbelievers’ own experience and observations. "Is it not yet clear to them" is a question filled with wonder at their persistent heedlessness. The evidence is right before their eyes: "how many peoples We destroyed before them." The Arabian Peninsula was littered with the ruins of vanished civilizations—the 'Ad, the Thamud, the people of Lot—whose once-proud dwellings lay desolate along the trade routes the Quraysh traveled regularly to Syria and Yemen. "Whose ruins they still pass by" emphasizes that these are not distant, unfamiliar histories but tangible, visible remnants they encounter in their daily and seasonal journeys. They walk through the empty homes and abandoned cities of those who denied their messengers, seeing the physical proof of divine punishment with their own eyes. "Surely in this are signs," clear indications pointing to the consequences of rejecting truth. The verse concludes, "Will they not then listen?" The call to listen indicates that the stories of the past carry spoken warnings as well; travelers relate these histories, and the ruins themselves seem to speak of the fate of arrogance. The question implies that their failure is not a lack of intellect but a refusal to pay attention and draw the obvious conclusion that the same Lord who destroyed those peoples can just as easily hold them to account.
27 | Do they not see how We drive rain to parched land, producing ˹various˺ crops from which they and their cattle eat? Will they not then see? | Moving from the historical sign of destruction to the natural sign of revival, the verse directs their attention to the earth beneath their feet. "Do they not see how We drive rain to parched land" paints a picture of a barren, lifeless terrain that appears utterly dead, cracked, and devoid of vegetation. The driving of rain is attributed directly to Allah, showing His complete control over the life-giving water. When the rain descends, the "parched land" is transformed, "producing various crops." The diversity of crops—grains, fruits, vegetables—emerges from what was just dry dust, a manifest sign of resurrection. The phrase "from which they and their cattle eat" reminds them that their very sustenance and the survival of their livestock depend on this process, so they cannot pretend to be indifferent to it. The verse then asks, "Will they not then see?" Here the call is to insight and deeper understanding, not just physical sight. The parallel is striking: just as dead earth is revived by water, dead humans will be revived by their Lord for judgment. This is an argument they witness annually, yet they fail to make the connection. The verse exposes the inconsistency of those who believe in the natural cycle of life from dead soil but deny the divine power to resurrect human beings from their graves, making their denial all the more irrational.
28 | They ask ˹mockingly˺, “When is this ˹Day of final˺ Decision, if what you say is true?” | The surah returns to the direct speech of the deniers, capturing their skeptical and impatient tone. "They ask mockingly" reveals the spirit behind the question; it is not a sincere search for knowledge but a challenge dripping with sarcasm. The phrase "Day of Decision" refers to the Day of Judgment, which is described as the Day of Opening or Victory in other parts of the Quran—a day when all matters will be finally decided between truth and falsehood, and between the believers and the disbelievers. Their demand, "When is this Day of Decision, if what you say is true?" is designed to pressure the Prophet into naming a specific date. They believe that the failure to provide a date proves the whole warning is a bluff. This was a common tactic of the Quraysh, who thought that since the punishment had not arrived immediately, it never would. They confused divine forbearance with weakness or falsehood. The question reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the purpose of the warning: the exact timing is deliberately hidden to keep people in a state of readiness and to test their faith. By mocking the unseen, they only compound their guilt, and this verse sets the stage for the sharp reply that follows, exposing their short-sightedness and the futility of their demand.
29 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “On the Day of Decision it will not benefit the disbelievers to believe then, nor will they be delayed ˹from punishment˺.” | In answer to their mocking inquiry, the Prophet is instructed to deliver a response that cuts to the heart of the matter. "Say, O Prophet" signals a direct divine command on how to address the challenge. The response begins by focusing not on the "when" but on the "what" of that Day: "On the Day of Decision it will not benefit the disbelievers to believe then." This reveals the tragic irony of their demand. They ask for the Day as if they would then accept the truth, but when that Day actually arrives, the door of faith will be slammed shut. Belief that comes only after seeing the reality of punishment with one's own eyes is not true faith; it is forced acknowledgment that carries no merit and brings no salvation. The verse adds, "nor will they be delayed from punishment." Their earlier request for the Day to come quickly will be met with a terrifying reality: once it begins, there is no postponement, no second chance, and no respite. The punishment will overtake them immediately. This reply is a powerful psychological reversal. Their mockery about the delay is answered with the warning that the delay is their only window of mercy; once it closes, their regret will be eternal and their wished-for "Decision" will be their ruin. The verse underscores the urgency of seizing the present moment for genuine repentance before it is too late.
30 | So turn away from them, and wait! They too are waiting. | The surah ends with a concise and powerful instruction to the Prophet that captures the entire attitude he must adopt. "So turn away from them" does not mean abandoning his mission or ceasing to convey the message. Rather, it means to withdraw his heart from their insults, their endless arguments, and their mockery. He is to stop allowing their rejection to cause him personal distress, for he has fulfilled his duty of delivering the clear warning. The command is a form of emotional and spiritual detachment from the hostility of the stubborn disbelievers, leaving their affair to Allah. The second part, "and wait!" is a call to patient anticipation of Allah's decree. The Prophet is to wait for the promised victory for the truth or for the divine judgment upon those who reject it. The verse concludes, "They too are waiting." This sets up a powerful contrast. The disbelievers are waiting, hoping for the Prophet's downfall, for the movement to fizzle out, or for some misfortune to befall him. But what they wait for is fundamentally different from what the Prophet waits for. He waits for the fulfillment of Allah's promise of help and justice. This closing verse, like many at the end of Meccan surahs, offers profound comfort. It assures the believers that the final outcome is in Allah's hands, and that the apparent delay is not neglect. The silent wait itself becomes a form of strength, leaving the deniers to their own doom while the faithful rest in the certainty of their Lord’s plan.
