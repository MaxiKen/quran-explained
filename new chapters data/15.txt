1 | Alif-Lãm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran. | The surah opens with the disconnected letters Alif-Lãm-Ra, a combination that appears at the beginning of several Meccan surahs including Yunus, Hud, Yusuf, and Ibrahim. These letters have always been understood by the earliest generations as a deliberate feature of the Quran whose full meaning Allah has kept to Himself. At the same time, they serve an unmistakable rhetorical purpose. By beginning with the very letters that make up everyday Arabic speech, the Quran immediately draws attention to the fact that its miraculous composition comes from these same ordinary building blocks of language, yet no human being can produce anything comparable to it. The letters function almost like an audible signal, alerting the listener that something of immense weight is about to follow. Immediately after these letters, Allah identifies what He is presenting: "These are the verses of the Book; the clear Quran." The word translated as "verses" here is ayat, which carries the meaning of signs, indicators, and unmistakable marks pointing toward a deeper reality. Each verse is not merely a piece of text but a signpost guiding the thoughtful reader toward Allah. When Allah calls it "the Book," He uses the Arabic word kitab, which conveys the sense of something written down, recorded, and established with permanence and authority. A book is complete, structured, and purposeful, and by calling the Quran a Book even in the Meccan period when it was still being revealed, Allah indicates that it possesses an internal coherence and a definitive status from the very beginning. The description of the Quran as "clear" translates the Arabic mubin, a word that comes from a root meaning to make something evident, to separate truth from falsehood, to clarify without ambiguity. The Quran does not speak in riddles meant to confuse sincere seekers. Its fundamental guidance on matters of belief, worship, morality, and the ultimate realities of existence is presented with clarity so that anyone who approaches it honestly can understand what Allah expects of them and why they have been created. This opening verse sets the stage for everything that follows in the surah. It establishes that what is about to be recited is not the speech of a poet or a madman, as the disbelievers would later claim, but a divinely revealed Book whose verses are clear signs for those who reflect. The pairing of the mysterious letters with the affirmation of clarity is itself significant. It suggests that while some aspects of divine wisdom may remain hidden from human beings, the essential message needed for guidance has been made plain and accessible. This opening line thus functions as both a declaration of authority and an invitation to listen with an open heart.
2 | ˹The day will come when˺ the disbelievers will certainly wish they had submitted ˹to Allah˺. | After establishing the nature of the Quran as a clear Book of signs, Allah turns immediately to the consequences of rejecting it. The verse speaks of a future moment when those who disbelieve will wish with intense regret that they had been among those who submitted to Allah. The placement of this warning right after the opening verse is deliberate and powerful. It tells the listener that the Quran is not merely information to be received with indifference. It carries stakes of the highest order. The wish described here is not a passing thought but a deep, consuming regret that overtakes the disbelievers when the reality they denied throughout their lives suddenly confronts them. Scholars explain that this wish will occur at various points during the journey of the Hereafter. It will arise at the moment of death, when the veils are lifted and the dying person sees the angels and understands that the promise of resurrection was true. It will intensify in the grave when the questioning begins. It will reach its peak on the Day of Judgment when the disbelievers see the scale of their deeds, the books of their records, and the destination they have earned. In that moment, the wish to have submitted will be overwhelming, but the time for choosing will have passed. The verse does not specify exactly when this wish occurs, and this ambiguity is part of its rhetorical force. It leaves the warning hanging over the disbelievers as an ever-present threat. At any moment, death can come, and with it the beginning of this regret. The word "submitted" translates the Arabic aslamu, which shares its root with Islam and carries the meaning of surrendering oneself entirely, making peace with Allah by submitting one's will to His. The disbelievers will wish not merely that they had performed certain actions but that they had fundamentally oriented their entire being toward Allah in submission. This connects directly to the opening description of the Quran as clear. The clarity was given. The signs were provided. No one will be able to claim that they did not know or that the message was too obscure to follow. The regret of the disbelievers will be compounded by the memory of having encountered clear truth and having turned away from it. This verse serves as the surah's first direct warning. It addresses the Makkan opponents who were hearing the Quran and responding with mockery and rejection. It tells them, and all who follow in their path, that the confidence and arrogance they display in this world will one day collapse into desperate wishing. The warning is delivered not with harshness alone but with an undertone of urgency that seeks to awaken the listener before the window of opportunity closes forever.
3 | ˹So˺ let them eat and enjoy themselves and be diverted by ˹false˺ hope, for they will soon know. | This verse contains what appears on the surface to be permission but is in reality a severe threat delivered with divine irony. When Allah says "let them eat and enjoy themselves," He is not granting approval or license. He is issuing a warning wrapped in the language of indifference to their ultimate fate. The command "let them" carries the force of telling the Prophet, and by extension the believers, not to be consumed with anxiety over those who persist in disbelief and mockery. Their enjoyment is temporary and their diversion will end. The verb translated as "be diverted" comes from the Arabic root that means to be occupied with something that distracts from a more important matter. False hopes and worldly pursuits have so filled their minds and hearts that they have no space left for serious reflection on the Quran's message. The word translated as "hope" here is amal, which in this context refers specifically to the false hope that there will be no resurrection, no judgment, and no accountability. The disbelievers convince themselves that life is only what they see, that death is the end, and that they can continue in their pleasures without consequence. This false hope acts as a narcotic, numbing the natural human instinct to seek answers about ultimate purpose and destiny. Then comes the chilling conclusion: "for they will soon know." The shortness of this phrase belies its weight. What they will come to know is the truth of everything they denied: the reality of Allah's power, the certainty of resurrection, the accuracy of the warning, and the terrible outcome of their rejection. The word "soon" emphasizes the brevity of worldly life. From the perspective of eternity, the longest human lifespan is nothing more than a fleeting moment. Even if a disbeliever lives for decades in comfort and pleasure, that entire period will seem to have passed in an instant once death arrives. This verse does not call the Prophet to passivity. It is not saying that the believers should cease calling people to the truth. Rather, it is a reassurance to the Prophet's heart. He had been deeply distressed by the rejection of his people, as we learn elsewhere in the Quran. Here Allah tells him that those who insist on disbelief will have their brief period of enjoyment, but the ultimate outcome is already determined. The verse also functions as a psychological challenge to the disbelievers themselves. Hearing that they are being left to their false hopes should disturb any thoughtful person. It forces the question: what if this warning is true? What if my enjoyment is temporary and my hopes are an illusion? For the believer, this verse serves as a reminder to avoid becoming too attached to worldly pleasures. The eating and enjoyment are not condemned in themselves, but making them the ultimate goal of life and being diverted by them from the purpose of creation is the path to ruin.
4 | We have never destroyed a society without a destined term. | This verse establishes a foundational principle of divine justice that runs throughout the Quran. Allah has never destroyed any community or civilization except that there was a specific, predetermined term set for it. The word translated as "society" is qaryah, which literally means a town or settlement but in Quranic usage refers to the people dwelling there, the entire community with its collective character and moral standing. The "destined term" translates kitabun ma'lum, which literally means a known decree or a written prescription. This concept is crucial for understanding how Allah's judgment operates in human history. No nation is destroyed arbitrarily or impulsively. Every community is given a defined period, a span of time during which it has the opportunity to hear the message, to reflect, to repent, and to reform. The destruction only comes when that term has expired and the community has definitively proven its rejection of the truth. This verse directly addresses a potential objection that the disbelievers of Makkah might have raised. They might have said that if the Quran were really from Allah, why were they not being punished immediately for their mockery? Why did the Prophet's warnings not result in instant destruction as had happened to earlier peoples? The answer is that every community has its appointed term, known only to Allah, during which His wisdom allows them to continue. The Makkans were living within their term, and the fact that punishment was delayed did not mean it would never come. This principle also clarifies a common misunderstanding about divine punishment. Some people imagine that Allah's destruction of past nations was a matter of sudden anger or impulsive wrath. The Quran corrects this notion repeatedly. The destruction of the people of Noah, of Aad, of Thamud, of Lot, and of others did not happen until they had been given extensive warning, until messengers had been sent to them with clear proofs, and until they had exhausted their appointed term while persisting in rejection. The verse also teaches that every community, including our own, operates within a timeline that Allah has established. This should inspire neither fatalism nor complacency. Instead, it should remind us that the opportunity to repent and reform is real but limited. The term is known to Allah but hidden from us. No one knows when their individual term, or the term of their community, will expire. The only wise response is to take the warning seriously while the door of opportunity remains open. This verse thus connects logically to the preceding one. The disbelievers are being left to their brief enjoyment, but this does not mean they have escaped accountability. Their term is approaching, and when it arrives, the judgment will be certain and just.
5 | No people can advance their doom, nor can they delay it. | This verse completes the principle established in the previous one by affirming the absolute precision and immovability of the divine decree regarding the appointed term. Once Allah has set a time for a community's destruction or judgment, no one has the power to bring it forward even by a single moment or to push it back by even an instant. The word translated as "doom" is ajalahum, which refers to their appointed term, the specific point in time that Allah has decreed. This is the same term mentioned in the previous verse as the kitabun ma'lum, the known decree. The verse uses two contrasting verbs to express the totality of human powerlessness over this timing. To "advance" means to attempt to make something happen earlier than its decreed time. No matter how urgent the believers' prayer for deliverance or how intense the disbelievers' mockery, the timing remains exactly as Allah has willed. To "delay" means to push something back, to buy more time. No nation that has exhausted its term can negotiate an extension. No amount of worldly power, wealth, or cleverness can add a single hour to the time that has been decreed. This principle operates on multiple levels simultaneously. At the cosmic level, it affirms Allah's complete sovereignty over history and time. The rise and fall of nations, the timing of every major event, and the duration of every civilization are not random occurrences but parts of a precise divine plan. At the level of the community, it teaches that collective behavior determines collective destiny, but the exact timing of consequences remains in Allah's hands alone. At the individual level, every person must grapple with the fact that their own death is fixed at an appointed moment. No one dies before their time, and no one survives beyond it. For the believers in Makkah who were facing persecution, this verse offered reassurance. The delay in punishment for the disbelievers was not a sign of divine indifference or inability. It was simply that their term had not yet arrived. When it did arrive, it would be neither early nor late, and the believers' patience would be vindicated. For the disbelievers, the verse was a sobering reminder that their continued safety was not proof of their rightness. They were being given time, but that time was finite and precisely measured. This verse also carries a profound spiritual lesson for every believer. It teaches us to trust Allah's timing. We often pray for relief, for justice, for change, and we grow impatient when the answer seems delayed. But Allah's clock does not follow human expectations. What He has decreed will come to pass at exactly the right moment, and our role is to remain steadfast in faith while the divine plan unfolds.
6 | They say, “O you to whom the Reminder is revealed! You must be insane!” | After the opening warnings, the surah now begins to recount the specific accusations that the Makkan disbelievers directed at the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The verse quotes their speech directly, preserving for all time the tone and content of their mockery. They address the Prophet with the phrase "O you to whom the Reminder is revealed," and this form of address is itself deeply sarcastic. The word "Reminder" translates adh-dhikr, one of the Quran's own names for itself. A reminder is something that awakens knowledge already present in the human soul, calling to mind the primordial covenant when all human beings testified that Allah is their Lord. The disbelievers use the Prophet's own claim against him. They do not say "the one who claims to receive revelation." They say "the one to whom the Reminder is revealed," mimicking the language of belief while intending utter mockery. The accusation that follows is stark: "You must be insane." The Arabic word used is majnun, which comes from the root meaning to be possessed by jinn, to be mad, to have lost one's rational faculties. This was not a casual insult but a specific strategy of the Makkan leadership. They understood that the Prophet was not a liar in the ordinary sense. His honesty and trustworthiness had been known to them for forty years before his prophethood. They called him al-Amin, the trustworthy one. It was far easier to discredit his message by suggesting that something had happened to his mind, that he had fallen under some supernatural influence that had deranged his thinking. This accusation was particularly painful for the Prophet. He knew himself to be sane, to be in possession of his faculties, to be receiving the most profound truth from the Lord of all worlds. To be called mad was not merely an insult to him personally but an attack on the very nature of the revelation he carried. If the messenger is mad, then the message can be safely dismissed without serious consideration. This same accusation was leveled against prophets throughout history. Noah was called a madman by his people. Moses faced similar charges. The pattern is consistent because the underlying psychology of rejection does not change. When a message challenges deeply held beliefs, lifestyles, and power structures, one of the easiest defenses is to attack the mental stability of the messenger rather than engage with the message itself. This verse reminds believers that accusations of extremism, irrationality, or mental imbalance are a predictable part of the experience of those who carry unpopular truths. The Prophet's response to such accusations, as we will see later in this surah, was not to descend into argumentation but to continue his mission with patience and to seek comfort in worship.
7 | Why do you not bring us the angels, if what you say is true?” | This verse continues the disbelievers' challenge to the Prophet, revealing the next stage of their argumentation. After calling him insane, they demand that he produce angels as proof of his truthfulness. The demand is framed as a logical condition: "if what you say is true," then bring the angels. On the surface, it appears to be a reasonable request. If the Prophet claims to receive revelation from Allah through the angel Gabriel, why not provide visible, public angelic confirmation that would silence all doubt? However, the Quran reveals the disingenuous nature of this demand. The Makkans did not genuinely seek evidence that would lead them to faith. They were setting a condition they believed could not be fulfilled, using it as a pretext to justify their continued rejection. The demand for visible angels reflected a deeper misunderstanding of the nature of faith and the purpose of human existence in this world. The present life is a realm of testing, where belief in the unseen is the foundation of genuine faith. If angels were to appear openly and confirm the truth, the test would effectively be over. The veil between the seen and unseen worlds would be lifted, and belief would become compulsory rather than voluntary. The Quran explains elsewhere that if angels were sent down visibly, the matter would be decided immediately, and no respite would be given to those who then rejected. Moreover, the demand reveals the Makkans' materialist mindset. They wanted sensory, tangible proof of the supernatural realm while simultaneously dismissing the spiritual and rational proofs that the Quran was providing. The Quran itself, in its eloquence, its wisdom, its accurate knowledge of past nations, and its transformation of individual souls and society, was the primary sign. Those who approached it with sincerity found within it ample evidence of its divine origin. Those who approached with the demand for a different kind of proof, one that catered to their own specifications, revealed that their real problem was not lack of evidence but an unwillingness to submit. This verse also highlights an important principle about divine communication. Allah chooses the means by which He guides humanity. He sent human messengers with human language, supported by miracles appropriate to their contexts, but He did not make belief dependent on the constant visible intervention of angels. The wisdom in this is that it preserves human free will and makes the moral and spiritual effort of belief meaningful. The demand for angels was, in essence, a refusal to engage with the evidence already provided and an attempt to dictate terms to the Creator.
8 | We do not send the angels down except for a just cause, and then ˹the end of˺ the disbelievers will not be delayed. | Here Allah provides the definitive response to the disbelievers' demand for angels to be sent down. The verse establishes two crucial principles about angelic descent. First, angels are not sent merely to satisfy human curiosity or to meet the demands of skeptics. They descend only "for a just cause," which translates the Arabic phrase bil-haqq, meaning with truth, with a serious and weighty purpose, with divine justice. Angels are not performers in a cosmic spectacle designed to entertain or convince the obstinate. When angels do descend, it is for matters of immense significance: delivering revelation to prophets, carrying out divine commands, recording human deeds, or executing judgment. The descent of angels is always purposeful and aligned with Allah's wisdom, never a response to human ultimatums. The second principle is even more sobering: when angels do come down in the context of a disbelieving people, it signals that the period of respite has ended and judgment is imminent. The verse states that if angels were sent "then the end of the disbelievers will not be delayed." The word translated as "the end" refers to their destruction, their final judgment. Once the angels appear visibly to execute divine punishment, the window for repentance closes instantly. This is precisely what happened to the people of Lot when the angels came in human form. Lot's people demanded that the visitors be handed over to them, and by sunrise the punishment had arrived. The Makkans, in their ignorance, were asking for the very thing that would seal their doom. They imagined that seeing angels would give them the evidence they needed to believe, but Allah knows human nature better. The same people who rejected the Prophet's message and called him mad would not suddenly become sincere believers if angels appeared. Either they would find new justifications for disbelief, as the following verses suggest, or the appearance of angels would mark the moment when belief no longer benefits because the test has ended. This verse contains an implicit warning to all who demand specific signs from Allah as a condition for their faith. The signs have already been provided in abundance. The Quran, the created universe, the human conscience, and the example of the Prophet are all more than sufficient for sincere seekers. To demand additional signs while neglecting those already given is not a search for truth but a strategy of evasion. The verse also comforts the Prophet by explaining that his inability to produce angels on demand is not a weakness in his message but a reflection of divine wisdom. Allah's ways are not subject to human negotiation.
9 | It is certainly We Who have revealed the Reminder, and it is certainly We Who will preserve it. | This verse stands as one of the most significant divine guarantees in the entire Quran, a promise whose fulfillment has been witnessed across more than fourteen centuries. Allah declares with emphatic certainty that He Himself has revealed the Reminder and He Himself will preserve it. The verse uses multiple layers of emphasis in its Arabic construction. It begins with the emphatic particle inna, meaning certainly or indeed, and couples the pronoun "We" directly with the act of revelation and preservation. The structure leaves no room for doubt or qualification. The "Reminder" here, adh-dhikr, refers to the Quran itself, as established by its usage in verse six where the disbelievers used the same word mockingly. Allah reclaims the word and invests it with His own authority. The preservation promised in this verse is comprehensive. It includes the preservation of the Quran's text, ensuring that not a single letter would be lost, altered, or corrupted over time. It includes the preservation of its language, making the Quran the living standard by which Arabic is measured. It includes the preservation of its meaning through the work of sincere scholars who dedicate their lives to its tafsir. It includes the preservation of its recitation, passed down through an unbroken chain of memorizers from the Prophet's time to the present day. And it includes the preservation of its guidance, ensuring that in every generation, those who seek the truth will find within the Quran a source of light and direction. This promise was particularly significant in the context of the Meccan period. The Muslims were a small, persecuted minority with no political power, no state apparatus, and no means of protecting the Quran through institutional force. Humanly speaking, the likelihood of this book surviving and spreading was minimal. Yet Allah took responsibility for its preservation Himself, without depending on human agencies. Human beings would be the means through which preservation occurred, but the ultimate guarantor is Allah. The fulfillment of this promise is evident. No other scripture from antiquity has been preserved with the same rigor. The Quran exists today exactly as it was recited by the Prophet Muhammad. Millions of human beings have memorized it in its entirety, a phenomenon unique in human history. This verse also serves as a response to those who feared that the message would be lost or corrupted as previous scriptures had been. By stating that He Himself has revealed it and will Himself preserve it, Allah assures the believers that the fate of earlier revelations will not befall the Quran. It is a promise that gives believers confidence in the permanence and reliability of their source of guidance.
10 | Indeed, We sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ among the groups of early peoples, | After reassuring the Prophet about the preservation of the Quran, Allah now turns to comfort him by placing his experience within the broader pattern of prophetic history. The rejection he faces from his own people is not unique. Messengers were sent before him to earlier communities, and they too faced resistance. The word "groups" translates shiya', which refers to factions, sects, or distinct communities. Human history is marked by the recurring cycle of divine guidance followed by human rejection. Every community received its messenger, and every messenger encountered those who opposed the message. By drawing the Prophet's attention to this pattern, Allah provides a form of solace. The loneliness that a messenger can feel when surrounded by rejection is real, but knowing that one stands in a long line of noble predecessors who faced the same struggle makes the burden easier to bear. The Prophet was not being rejected because of any deficiency in him or his message. He was being rejected because the human tendency toward arrogance, attachment to ancestral ways, and resistance to accountability is a recurring feature of history. This verse also contains an implicit argument against the Makkan disbelievers. They were not the first community to receive a prophet, and they could learn from the fate of those who came before. The ruins of earlier civilizations, some of which the Makkans passed by on their trade journeys, stood as silent witnesses to what happens when communities persistently reject their messengers. The verse invites the Makkans to study history with a reflective eye. If they did so, they would see a clear pattern: messengers came with truth, their people rejected them with mockery and demands for impossible signs, and eventually the divine judgment came while the messengers and their sincere followers were saved. This is not merely ancient history but the living sunnah of Allah in His creation, an unchanging law that applies to all communities that follow the same path of rejection. For believers reading this verse, there is a lesson about the importance of learning from history. The Quran consistently presents the stories of past nations not as mere entertainment but as lessons for those who have hearts to understand. The rise and fall of civilizations, the fates of the arrogant and the humble, and the ultimate triumph of truth over falsehood are patterns that repeat because human nature remains constant. By studying these patterns, believers can navigate their own times with wisdom and avoid the mistakes that led others to ruin.
11 | but no messenger ever came to them without being mocked. | This verse completes the historical observation of the previous one by identifying the specific form of rejection that has been universal across all prophetic missions: mockery. Every messenger who came to his people with the truth was met not merely with polite disagreement or intellectual debate but with ridicule, sarcasm, and contempt. The Arabic word used for mockery, yastahzi'un, carries the sense of making someone an object of jest, treating them with derision, and seeking to diminish their standing in the eyes of others through humor and scorn. This is a particularly effective weapon of social pressure. It is easier to laugh at a message than to refute it. It is easier to make the messenger appear ridiculous than to engage seriously with the content of what he says. Mockery serves to create a social barrier around the messenger, making it socially costly for others to take him seriously. Those who might otherwise be inclined to listen are deterred by the fear of being laughed at themselves. The universality of this pattern is striking. Noah was mocked for building an ark on dry land far from any sea. Hud was mocked for threatening his people with divine punishment while they felt secure in their mountain fortresses. Moses was mocked by Pharaoh and his court for his speech impediment and his simple shepherd's staff. Jesus was mocked for his humble origins and his association with the poor and marginalized. And Muhammad, peace be upon them all, was mocked for being an ordinary man who claimed to receive revelation from heaven. Understanding this pattern helps the believer maintain perspective when facing ridicule for their faith. The mockery is not evidence that the message is false. On the contrary, it is exactly what the historical pattern would lead one to expect. The elites, the intellectuals, the powerful, and the comfortable have always found it convenient to dismiss divine messengers through mockery rather than confront the uncomfortable truths they bring. This verse also carries a warning to those who engage in mockery. They think they are demonstrating their superiority and sophistication, but in reality they are placing themselves in the company of those who have been destroyed throughout history. The Quran consistently juxtaposes the mockery of this world with the humiliation that awaits the mockers in the Hereafter. What seems clever and entertaining in the assembly halls of this life will appear as catastrophic folly when the reality of resurrection dawns. The verse thus serves both as comfort for the Prophet and as a subtle but powerful warning to his opponents.
12 | This is how We allow disbelief ˹to steep˺ into the hearts of the wicked. | After describing the universal pattern of mockery, Allah now explains the mechanism behind this persistent rejection. The verse states that this is how Allah causes disbelief to enter and settle into the hearts of those who are criminals or wicked. The phrase "to steep" translates the Arabic verb naslukuhu, which means to cause something to enter, to thread something through, to make something penetrate. The imagery is striking. Disbelief is not simply a surface-level opinion that can be easily changed. Through a process that Allah describes here, it becomes threaded into the very fabric of the heart, woven into the person's inner being, until it becomes part of their identity and worldview. The word translated as "wicked" is al-mujrimin, which refers to those who are guilty of crimes, but in the Quranic context it specifically denotes those who have persistently chosen to violate Allah's commands and transgress the boundaries He has set. These are not innocent seekers who simply have not yet received sufficient evidence. They are people whose moral choices have led them to a state of willful opposition to the truth. This verse addresses a profound question about divine guidance and human free will. Does Allah cause people to disbelieve, and if so, how can they be held accountable? The key to understanding this lies in the description of the recipients as "the wicked." Allah does not arbitrarily cause belief or disbelief in hearts. Rather, when a person repeatedly chooses wrongdoing, rejects truth, and hardens themselves against divine signs, Allah responds by sealing that direction as their settled state. This is not an initial act of divine compulsion but a divine response to sustained human choice. The process is gradual. A person commits a sin and feels remorse. If they repent, the heart remains soft. If they ignore the remorse and continue, the heart becomes slightly harder. Each subsequent sin makes the next one easier. Each rejection of truth makes the next rejection more automatic. Over time, the capacity for sincere reflection diminishes, and disbelief becomes not just an opinion but a settled disposition. The verse also explains why mockery specifically becomes characteristic of those whose hearts have reached this state. Mockery is the defense mechanism of a heart that has invested too much in disbelief to turn back. It is easier to laugh at the truth than to admit that one has been wrong for decades, that one's ancestors were wrong, that one's entire social identity is built on a foundation of falsehood. The mockery is both a symptom of the heart's disease and a means by which the disease deepens.
13 | They would not believe in this ˹Quran˺ despite the ˹many˺ examples of those ˹destroyed˺ before. | This verse continues the explanation of the disbelievers' stubbornness by pointing to the paradoxical nature of their rejection. They have before them the lessons of earlier destroyed nations, yet they still do not believe in the Quran that warns them of a similar fate. The word "examples" translates sunnah, which carries a much richer meaning than simply a past event. The sunnah of Allah refers to His established way of dealing with humanity, the consistent pattern according to which He has caused the arrogant to fall and the humble to rise. These patterns are not merely historical anecdotes. They are living lessons, case studies in the consequences of rejecting divine guidance. The Makkan disbelievers were not ignorant of these examples. Their trade caravans traveled regularly to Syria and Yemen, passing through the ruined dwellings of Thamud, the overturned cities of Lot's people, and other archaeological evidence of destroyed civilizations. The Arabs had oral traditions about Aad and Thamud. They could see with their own eyes the remnants of communities that had been mightier than them in power and more impressive in their construction, yet had been reduced to ruins. The verse expresses a kind of astonishment at the human capacity to ignore evidence that is literally visible along the roadside. How can a people see the ruins of the destroyed and not pause to reflect that the same fate could befall them if they follow the same path? The answer lies in what was described in the previous verse about disbelief becoming threaded into the heart. When the heart is sealed, the eye looks but does not see. The ruins are visible as physical structures, but their significance as divine warnings does not penetrate the consciousness. They are dismissed as accidents of history, natural disasters, or the fortunes of war rather than recognized as the deliberate acts of a just God. This verse also contains an implicit argument for the truth of the Quran. The detailed knowledge of these destroyed nations, their names, their locations, and the specific sins that led to their downfall, was not available in the libraries of Makkah. The Prophet was unlettered and had no access to the scriptures of the Jews and Christians. Yet the Quran speaks of these events with precision and confidence. For those who reflect, this is itself a sign that the Quran is not the product of human research but of divine revelation. The failure to believe despite such evidence is, as the verse indicates, a symptom of a deeper spiritual illness rather than a response to insufficient information.
14 | And even if We opened for them a gate to heaven, through which they continued to ascend, | This verse presents a powerful hypothetical scenario that exposes the true nature of the disbelievers' stubbornness. Allah challenges the assumption that more spectacular signs would produce faith. He proposes an extreme hypothetical: what if a gate to heaven were opened for them, and they were to ascend through it, seeing the wonders of the celestial realm with their own eyes? The imagery is vivid. A gate in the sky, an opening into the unseen world, a direct passage through which human beings could climb and witness the angels, the celestial order, and the majesty of Allah's creation above the earth. This would be the ultimate fulfillment of their demand for visible, undeniable proof. It would far exceed anything they had asked for. They wanted angels to descend; in this hypothetical, Allah gives them something even more spectacular: the ability to ascend themselves and witness the heavenly realm directly. The phrase "continued to ascend" suggests not a momentary glimpse but an ongoing experience. They would have time to observe, to process, to verify what they were seeing. This eliminates the possible objection that a brief vision might be dismissed as a hallucination. Day after day, they would ascend through that gate, exploring the heavens and accumulating evidence of the reality they had denied. The purpose of this hypothetical is not to suggest that such a thing will happen. It is to demonstrate the depths of human obstinacy. Allah is setting up the argument that will be completed in the next verse, showing that the problem with the disbelievers is not a lack of signs but a willful determination to reject. The human heart, when it has committed itself to disbelief, becomes impervious even to the most extraordinary evidence. This is a profound psychological insight that the Quran articulates with remarkable clarity. Evidence alone does not change hearts that have chosen to be closed. The modern world provides many examples of this principle. People witness the fine-tuning of the universe, the complexity of biological systems, the evidence of design in nature, and the historical preservation of the Quran, yet they find ways to explain away every sign. The problem is not in the evidence but in the heart that has already decided it will not submit. The verse also serves as a warning to believers not to become overly focused on trying to prove the truth of Islam through ever more sophisticated arguments. Arguments and evidence have their place, but ultimately guidance is in Allah's hands, and no amount of proof will convince someone whose heart is not open.
15 | still they would say, “Our eyes have truly been dazzled! In fact, we must have been bewitched.” | This verse completes the hypothetical scenario begun in the previous one. Even after ascending through a gate to heaven and witnessing the celestial realm with their own eyes, the disbelievers would still not submit. Instead, they would find a way to explain away the experience. They would claim that their eyes had been dazzled, that they had been overwhelmed by some kind of optical illusion, that what they thought they saw was not real but a trick played upon their perception. The word "dazzled" translates sukkirat, which comes from the same root as intoxication. They would claim that their senses had been clouded, that they were not in their right minds, that the experience was the result of some spell or enchantment that had made them see things that were not really there. The accusation of being "bewitched" is particularly significant. This was one of the very charges the Makkans made against the Prophet himself. They claimed that he was under the effect of magic, that the Quran was a spell that bewitched those who listened to it, separating fathers from sons and husbands from wives. In this hypothetical, they would use the same explanation for the most direct possible evidence of divine reality. They would rather believe that their own senses had been magically tampered with than accept that the Prophet's message was true. This reveals something profound about the psychology of entrenched disbelief. When a person has constructed their entire identity, their social standing, their moral framework, and their sense of meaning around the rejection of a particular truth, they will go to extraordinary lengths to maintain that rejection. The cost of admitting they were wrong is too high. It is easier to believe in a massive cosmic conspiracy or a universal delusion than to accept that one has been fundamentally mistaken about the nature of reality. The verse also demonstrates the spiritual principle that the problem of disbelief is ultimately a moral and spiritual problem, not an intellectual one. The disbelievers are not lacking in evidence or intelligence. They are lacking in the humility to submit. Their pride will not allow them to admit that an unlettered man from among their own people has been chosen by Allah to receive revelation. Their attachment to the ways of their ancestors and to the social structures built on idolatry makes the truth too costly to accept. This has direct relevance for believers who engage in dialogue with those who reject the faith. It reminds us that winning arguments is not the same as opening hearts, and that only Allah can guide whom He wills.
16 | Indeed, We have placed constellations in the sky, and adorned it for all to see. | After exposing the depths of the disbelievers' obstinacy, the surah now shifts attention to the signs of divine power and wisdom that are visible in the created world. The disbelievers may refuse to believe even if they ascended to heaven, but the heavens themselves are filled with signs that testify to the Creator for those who have eyes to see. This verse begins with the affirmation that Allah has placed constellations in the sky. The Arabic word is buruj, which can refer to the constellations, the zodiacal signs, or the great groupings of stars that serve as celestial landmarks. These are not randomly scattered points of light but organized patterns that have guided travelers across deserts and seas for millennia. The placement of these constellations with such precision that they serve as reliable navigational tools points to an intelligence behind their arrangement. The verse then states that Allah has "adorned" the sky. The beauty of the night sky is not merely functional. It is deliberately made beautiful, decorated like a ceiling painted with care and artistry. The stars do not merely provide light or navigational assistance. They inspire awe. They have moved poets, philosophers, and ordinary human beings throughout history to contemplate the vastness of creation and their own small place within it. This aesthetic dimension of creation is itself a sign. Beauty points to a Beautifier. The fact that human beings can perceive and be moved by beauty that serves no immediate survival function suggests that we are meant to look beyond mere utility and recognize the handiwork of the Creator. The phrase "for all to see" emphasizes the universal accessibility of this sign. Unlike the specialized arguments of theologians or the revealed texts that require literacy, the night sky is available to every human being regardless of education, language, or culture. The Bedouin in the desert, the sailor on the sea, and the modern city dweller who escapes light pollution can all look up and see the same stars. This universality is part of divine mercy. No one can claim that they were denied access to the signs of Allah when the sky itself is an open book for all humanity. This verse also serves to humble human pride. The disbelievers of Makkah walked the earth with arrogance, but the night sky puts human pretensions in perspective. The constellations that Allah has placed and adorned have been shining since long before any of us were born and will continue long after we are gone. The appropriate response to contemplating such a sky is not mockery of a prophet but humble recognition of the Creator's majesty.
17 | And We protected it from every accursed devil, | This verse describes a further aspect of the celestial realm. Not only has Allah adorned the sky with constellations, but He has also protected it from every shaytan rajim, every accursed devil. The word "protected" translates hafiznaha, meaning We have guarded it, preserved it, kept it safe. The sky is not an open realm that any being can traverse at will. It is a protected domain with boundaries that certain creatures are not permitted to cross. The "accursed devil" refers to the rebellious jinn, specifically those who attempt to eavesdrop on the heavenly assembly. The curse mentioned here is the state of being expelled from divine mercy, driven away from proximity to Allah. These devils are not cursed arbitrarily but because of their rebellion, their arrogance, and their determination to lead human beings astray. This verse answers a question that was relevant to the Arab context. The pre-Islamic Arabs believed that soothsayers and fortune-tellers received their information from jinn who listened in on heavenly conversations. The Quran affirms that such eavesdropping does occur, but it also makes clear that the heavens are protected. The devils are not free to gather whatever information they want. Their attempts are met with celestial defenses, and the information they sometimes manage to snatch is fragmentary and unreliable. The protection of the sky serves multiple purposes. On one level, it maintains the integrity of the unseen realm. The conversations of the angels, the divine decrees that are issued, and the matters of the higher world are not meant for public broadcast to every spiritual entity that wishes to listen. On another level, it protects human beings from a flood of supernatural information that would overwhelm and confuse them. If devils had unrestricted access to heavenly knowledge, they could use it to deceive humanity on a massive scale, mixing truth with falsehood in ways that would be nearly impossible to untangle. This verse also carries a symbolic meaning about the nature of spiritual reality. The sky represents the higher realm of truth, purity, and divine presence. The devils represent the forces of falsehood, impurity, and rebellion that seek to penetrate and corrupt what is pure. That the sky is protected from them is a sign that evil, no matter how persistent, cannot ultimately breach the boundaries that Allah has established. Truth remains protected even when falsehood makes its attempts to infiltrate.
18 | except the one eavesdropping, who is then pursued by a visible flare. | This verse describes a specific exception to the protection of the heavens mentioned in the previous one. Some devils do attempt to eavesdrop, to steal a hearing of what is being discussed in the heavenly realm. The Arabic word for eavesdropping here is istaraqa, which conveys the sense of stealing something secretly, listening furtively, trying to catch fragments of conversation that one is not meant to hear. This is not open access but a covert and forbidden attempt. The verse then reveals the consequence of such attempts: the eavesdropping devil is pursued by a "visible flare," a shihabun mubin, a clear, bright, pursuing flame. This is commonly understood as a shooting star or meteor, a projectile of fire that targets the intruder and drives it away or destroys it. The flare is described as "visible," meaning that its trajectory and purpose are unmistakable. It is not an ambiguous natural phenomenon that could be interpreted in multiple ways. It is a clear and targeted response to a specific transgression. The pre-Islamic Arabs were familiar with the phenomenon of shooting stars and had various superstitious beliefs about them. The Quran corrects these beliefs by providing the true explanation within the context of divine protection of the heavens. The shooting stars are not random celestial events or omens of earthly happenings. They are part of a cosmic security system designed to repel those who are not permitted access to the higher realm. The information that the devils sometimes manage to snatch before being struck is the source of the fragmentary and mixed predictions that fortune-tellers sometimes offer. A devil may hear a word or a phrase of a divine decree before being driven off, and it then whispers that fragment to its human ally. The fortune-teller mixes that one grain of truth with a hundred lies, and people are impressed by the single accurate detail while ignoring the overwhelming falsehood. The Prophet Muhammad explained this in a hadith, clarifying that the jinn pass the information down through levels, each one adding to the message, so that by the time it reaches human ears it has been contaminated with much falsehood. This verse also carries a broader lesson about the limits of forbidden knowledge. Those who seek to gain access to what Allah has not made accessible to them, whether through illicit spiritual means or through any other prohibited avenue, will find themselves pursued by consequences they cannot escape. The shooting star is a dramatic image of the inescapable divine justice that catches up with transgression.
19 | As for the earth, We spread it out and placed upon it firm mountains, and caused everything to grow there in perfect balance. | After describing the sky and its adornments, the surah now turns attention to the earth, completing the picture of the cosmic signs. The verse presents three distinct aspects of the earth's creation. First, Allah "spread it out." The Arabic word madadnaha conveys the sense of extending, stretching, making something wide and spacious. The earth has been made habitable, with vast expanses that allow human beings and other creatures to move, settle, travel, and find their livelihoods. It is not a cramped or difficult environment but one that has been prepared for ease of living. Second, Allah "placed upon it firm mountains." The mountains are described as rawasi, which comes from a root meaning to be firm, steady, and anchored. The mountains serve as stabilizers for the earth's crust, preventing it from shaking excessively. This is a Quranic theme repeated in several surahs. The mountains are like pegs or stakes driven into the earth, providing geological stability. For the human observer, mountains also serve as landmarks, sources of minerals and water, and regions of unique beauty and ecological diversity. Third, Allah caused everything to grow there "in perfect balance." The phrase "perfect balance" translates mawzun, which comes from the root meaning to weigh, measure, and balance. The vegetation on earth is not random. It follows precise laws, specific proportions, and an ecological equilibrium that sustains life. Every plant species has its role, its season, its nutritional composition, and its place within the web of life. The balance is evident in the water cycle, the nutrient cycle, the pollination systems, and the countless other interconnected processes that make life possible. This verse is a complete refutation of the materialist worldview that sees the earth as a product of random cosmic accidents. The spreading of the earth for habitability, the placement of mountains for stability, and the balanced growth of vegetation for sustenance all point to deliberate design. No one who reflects honestly on these phenomena can attribute them to mere chance. The verse also reminds human beings of their dependence on the earth. We did not create the ground we walk on, the mountains that stabilize it, or the plants that feed us. All of this has been provided by a wise and merciful Creator, and the appropriate response is gratitude, humility, and submission to the One who spread out the earth and brought forth its provisions in perfect measure.
20 | And We made in it means of sustenance for you and others, who you do not provide for. | This verse continues the description of the earth's blessings by highlighting the means of sustenance that Allah has placed within it. The phrase "means of sustenance" translates ma'ayish, which comes from the root meaning to live, and refers to everything necessary for life and livelihood. This includes food, water, air, materials for shelter and clothing, and all the resources that human beings depend on for their existence. The earth has been equipped with everything needed to sustain human life, and this equipment preceded human arrival. The food was growing, the water was flowing, and the minerals were deposited long before human beings appeared on the scene. This indicates that the earth was prepared for us, that we were expected, and that our needs were anticipated by the One who created both us and the earth. The verse then adds a significant qualifier: "for you and others, who you do not provide for." The sustenance in the earth is not exclusively for human beings. There are countless other creatures that share this planet, and many of them are entirely outside human care and provision. Wild animals, birds, insects, marine life, and microorganisms all draw their sustenance from the earth's systems without any human involvement. Human beings do not feed the fish in the depths of the ocean, the birds in their migratory routes, or the countless creatures in remote wilderness areas. Allah provides for them directly. This observation serves to humble human pride. We sometimes imagine that we are the sole providers for ourselves and our dependents, forgetting that the ultimate source of all provision is Allah. A farmer plants seeds but does not create the soil, the sunlight, the rain, or the biological processes that transform the seed into a harvest. A worker earns wages but does not create the economy, the opportunities, or the health and ability that make work possible. The verse also contains an implicit argument against the idolaters who worshipped various deities for provision. If Allah is the One who has placed sustenance in the earth for all creatures, then He alone is the true Provider, and He alone deserves worship. No idol, no saint, and no intermediary can claim to provide for the fish in the sea or the birds in the air. The provision comes from Allah alone. This verse invites the reader to look at the world with eyes of recognition, seeing in every meal, every breath, and every moment of life a sign of the Provider whose generosity sustains all that exists.
21 | There is not any means ˹of sustenance˺ whose reserves We do not hold, only bringing it forth in precise measure. | After describing the sustenance placed in the earth for human beings and countless other creatures, Allah now makes an even more comprehensive statement about His absolute ownership and control over all provision. The verse begins with a sweeping negation: there is not a single thing that serves as a means of sustenance whose treasuries, whose reserves, are not held exclusively by Allah. The word translated as "reserves" is khaza'in, the plural of khizanah, meaning storehouses or treasuries. This is a powerful metaphor. Every resource, every form of provision, every source of life and livelihood exists within the vast storehouses of Allah's power and generosity. The rain that falls, the crops that grow, the minerals beneath the earth, the energy of the sun, the oxygen in the air, the knowledge and skills that enable human productivity, all of these originate from divine treasuries that never diminish and never deplete. This understanding fundamentally reorients the human relationship with provision. We often imagine that our sustenance comes from our jobs, our businesses, our investments, or our social connections. These are merely means, channels through which Allah delivers what He has already decreed. The true source, the reservoir from which all provision flows, is with Allah alone. The verse then adds the crucial qualifier: "only bringing it forth in precise measure." The Arabic word for measure here is qadar, which carries the sense of a determined amount, a precise decree. Allah does not release provision haphazardly or randomly. Everything that reaches any creature has been measured, apportioned, and sent down according to a wisdom that encompasses all of creation. Some receive abundance, some receive scarcity, and both are tests. The one given plenty is tested with gratitude and the responsibility of spending correctly. The one given little is tested with patience and trust. Neither condition is an indication of Allah's pleasure or displeasure by itself. This precise measure also extends to the natural world. The rain falls in specific quantities. The seasons bring their cycles of growth and dormancy in exact patterns. The ecological balance is maintained through countless measured provisions that interact with one another. The verse is both a comfort and a warning. For the believer, it is a comfort to know that one's provision is guaranteed by the One who owns all storehouses. Anxiety about sustenance loses its grip when the heart truly understands that no soul will miss what was decreed for it. For the arrogant, it is a warning that their wealth and resources are not truly theirs. They are merely temporary custodians of what belongs to Allah, and He can withdraw it as easily as He granted it.
22 | We send fertilizing winds, and bring down rain from the sky for you to drink. It is not you who hold its reserves. | This verse draws attention to two specific and intimately connected manifestations of divine provision: the winds and the rain. The winds are described as "fertilizing," translating lawaqih, which literally means impregnating or fecundating. This description is remarkably precise. Winds carry pollen from plant to plant, enabling the reproduction of countless species. They carry moisture-laden clouds across continents. They stir the atmosphere and facilitate the mixing of air masses that produce weather patterns. The modern scientific understanding of wind as a vector for pollination, cloud formation, and atmospheric circulation is encapsulated in this single word. The pre-Islamic Arabs understood the practical importance of winds for their agriculture and their lives, but the Quran elevates this understanding by linking the winds directly to Allah's creative and sustaining action. The winds are not independent natural forces. They are sent by Allah as agents of His mercy, carrying out a fertilizing function that prepares the way for rain. Then comes the rain itself, brought down from the sky for human beings to drink. The phrase "for you to drink" highlights the direct benefit, but the rain's purposes extend far beyond quenching thirst. It sustains rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers. It irrigates crops, supports livestock, and maintains the entire freshwater ecosystem on which human civilization depends. The verse then closes with a statement that echoes the previous verse's theme of divine ownership: "It is not you who hold its reserves." Human beings do not control the storehouses of rain. We cannot summon a storm when we want it or hold back a flood when it threatens. Despite all our technological advances in weather prediction and water management, we remain utterly dependent on the natural cycles that Allah has established and that He alone sustains. This humbling reminder is particularly relevant in an age of technological hubris. We can build dams, desalination plants, and irrigation systems, but we cannot create water from nothing. The reserves are with Allah, and He releases them according to His wisdom and measure. The verse connects seamlessly to the previous one, where Allah stated that all storehouses are with Him. Here He provides a concrete, observable example that every human being can witness. The rain that falls on the just and the unjust alike is not the product of human effort but of divine mercy. The appropriate human response is not pride in our water management systems but humble gratitude to the One who sends the fertilizing winds and brings down the life-giving rain.
23 | Surely it is We Who give life and cause death. And We are the ˹Eternal˺ Successor. | This verse presents a direct and emphatic declaration of Allah's exclusive power over life and death. The Arabic construction uses inna for emphasis, leaving no ambiguity. Life and death are not autonomous processes, not the results of natural selection, not accidents of biology, and not within the domain of any other deity or force. They are the direct and deliberate acts of Allah. Every living thing that comes into existence does so by His creative command. Every living thing that dies does so at His decree. The embryo that forms in the womb, the seed that germinates in the soil, the microbe that divides in the water, all receive life from the same source. And when the appointed term arrives, that life is taken back. Between the giving and the taking, there is no independent power that can add a moment or subtract one. This dual power, life and death, is one of the central proofs of Allah's uniqueness. The idols that the Makkans worshipped could neither create a fly nor prevent its death. The powerful rulers who oppressed the believers could not escape their own mortality. The Prophet's opponents who mocked him would one day face the death they refused to contemplate. The verse functions as both a comfort for the believers and a warning to the rejecters. The believers who feared persecution could take solace in knowing that their lives and the lives of their enemies were both in Allah's hands. The timing of victory and defeat, of life and death, was not determined by human power but by divine wisdom. The verse then concludes with a profound declaration: "And We are the Eternal Successor." The Arabic word is al-Warithun, from the root of inheritance. Allah is the Inheritor, the One who remains after everything else has perished. All human possessions, all kingdoms, all wealth, all relationships, all achievements, will eventually be left behind. Their owners will die, and they will pass to others or crumble into dust. Ultimately, everything returns to Allah, the only Being who is eternal and self-subsisting. This title provides the ultimate perspective on life and death. Human beings spend their lives acquiring, building, and competing for things they will inevitably leave behind. The only thing that endures is what is done sincerely for Allah. Everything else becomes part of what Allah inherits. This understanding transforms the believer's relationship with worldly possessions and ambitions. They are means to serve Allah, not ends in themselves, because ultimately everything goes back to Him.
24 | We certainly know those who have gone before you and those who will come after ˹you˺. | After declaring His power over life and death and His status as the Eternal Inheritor, Allah now affirms His comprehensive knowledge of humanity across all time. The verse states that He knows those who have passed away, the earlier generations, and He equally knows those who are yet to come, the later generations. The Arabic uses the verb form for intensive and complete knowledge. This is not a general awareness but a detailed, specific, and exhaustive knowledge of every individual, their circumstances, their actions, their intentions, and their ultimate fate. The verse can be understood on multiple levels simultaneously. On one level, it addresses the Prophet and his community directly. Allah knows those who believed and passed away before the Prophet's mission, and He knows those who will believe after him until the end of time. The struggle the Prophet was engaged in was not isolated in history. It was part of a continuous chain of faith stretching back to the earliest human beings and forward to the final generation. On another level, the verse speaks of Allah's knowledge of all human beings in relation to the Day of Gathering mentioned in the next verse. When the time comes to assemble all of humanity for judgment, there will be no confusion, no missing records, and no need for investigation. Every soul is already known to Allah, its entire history recorded, its precise place in the sequence of generations established. The verse also provides a subtle but powerful argument against the Makkan disbelievers' mockery. They acted as if they were the arbiters of truth and that the Prophet was a marginal figure who would be forgotten. Allah reminds them that He knows every generation, the ones who came before and the ones who will come after. The arrogant people of Makkah were just one small link in a vast chain of humanity, and their significance was not measured by their wealth or status but by their response to the truth. For the individual believer, this verse offers a sense of significance and accountability. Each human life is known to Allah. No one is lost in the crowd. No one is forgotten by the Creator. The generations that came before us lived their lives and faced their judgment. The generations after us will do the same. Our own lives unfold under the watchful knowledge of the One who knows all who have gone and all who will come. This awareness should inspire both humility and a sense of purpose. We are part of a larger human story, and our chapter matters.
25 | Surely your Lord ˹alone˺ will gather them together ˹for judgment˺. He is truly All-Wise, All-Knowing. | This verse provides the conclusion to the sequence that began with the declaration of Allah's ownership of all sustenance and moved through His power over life and death and His comprehensive knowledge of all generations. Now Allah states that He alone will gather all of humanity together for judgment. The word "gather" translates yahshuruhum, which carries the meaning of assembling, collecting, and bringing together from all directions. This gathering encompasses every human being who has ever lived, from the first to the last, across all times and places. The verse specifies that this gathering is the exclusive act of "your Lord." The disbelievers of Makkah might have imagined that their idols would intercede for them or that they would somehow escape accountability. This verse firmly closes that door. The gathering is Allah's alone, and no one else has any role or authority in it. The two divine names that conclude the verse, al-Hakim and al-Alim, the All-Wise and the All-Knowing, explain why the gathering is both certain and just. Allah's knowledge ensures that nothing is forgotten, no deed is overlooked, and no injustice occurs in the accounting. Every action, every intention, every secret thought is fully known to Him. His wisdom ensures that the gathering serves a perfect purpose. It is not an arbitrary display of power but the necessary culmination of human existence. A world in which good and evil, belief and disbelief, justice and oppression all ended in the same nothingness of death would be a world devoid of ultimate meaning. Divine wisdom requires that there be a day when accounts are settled, when the oppressed receive their justice, and when the distinction between those who lived for Allah and those who lived in rebellion becomes manifest. This verse also connects directly to the preceding verses about life, death, and knowledge. Allah gives life, causes death, knows every generation, and will gather them all. The cycle is complete. Creation, life, death, and resurrection are all in His hands. For the Prophet and the believers facing persecution, this verse was a source of strength. The apparent triumph of the disbelievers was temporary. The final gathering would reveal the true outcome. The mockers who seemed powerful in Makkah would stand before the same Lord as the believers they oppressed, and the judgment would be based on truth, not on worldly status. This perspective transforms the believer's patience from passive endurance into active trust. The delay in justice is not denial. The gathering is certain, and the Judge is All-Wise and All-Knowing.
26 | Indeed, We created man from sounding clay moulded from black mud. | The surah now transitions into the extended narrative of human creation and the rebellion of Iblis, a story that provides essential context for understanding the nature of human existence, the source of evil, and the ongoing struggle between obedience and temptation. The verse begins with the affirmation that Allah created man, using the Arabic word insan, the human being, from a specific material: sounding clay moulded from black mud. The description is remarkably detailed and sensory. The clay is described as "sounding," translating salsal, which indicates dry clay that produces a ringing sound when struck, like pottery that has been fired. It is "moulded," indicating that it was shaped and formed with deliberate artistry. It originates from "black mud," hama'in masnun, which conveys the image of dark, smooth, sticky mud that has been altered over time, perhaps fermented or aged. This origin story carries profound implications. The human being is created from the earth, from the most humble and common of materials. Mud is something people walk on without a second thought. It is the substance of the ground, trodden underfoot. By reminding human beings of this origin, Allah cuts through all arrogance and pretension. The human who struts proudly on the earth, who oppresses others, who considers himself self-sufficient, was created from mud. His body, no matter how strong or beautiful, is ultimately made of the same elements as the soil. At the same time, the careful description of the clay as "sounding" and "moulded" points to the artistry of the Creator. The same substance that is lowly in its raw form becomes a marvel of design in Allah's hands. The human body, with its intricate systems, its delicate balance, and its capacity for thought, creativity, and worship, is a masterpiece crafted from the humblest of origins. This dual message, humility about our origin and wonder at our design, pervades the Quran's treatment of human creation. We are not to worship the mud from which we came, as some materialists effectively do by denying any higher purpose. Nor are we to deny our lowly origin and act as if we are self-existent deities. We are to recognize that a magnificent Creator took humble material and shaped it into a being capable of knowing and worshipping Him. This recognition naturally leads to the response of gratitude and submission. The One who shaped clay into a human being deserves to be worshipped by that human being.
27 | As for the jinn, We created them earlier from smokeless fire. | This verse provides crucial information about another category of conscious beings, the jinn, and it does so by contrasting their creation with that of humanity. The jinn were created earlier, before human beings. The Quran does not specify exactly how much earlier, but the sequence indicates that their existence predates the creation of Adam. This chronological priority is significant for understanding the story of Iblis that follows. Iblis was among the jinn, a being who had worshipped Allah for ages before the creation of Adam, and his long history of worship became the ground for his later arrogance when he was commanded to prostrate to the new creation. The material from which the jinn were created is described as "smokeless fire," translating the Arabic phrase min marijin min nar. The word marij refers to a smokeless, pure flame, a fire that is intense and translucent, without the dark smoke that accompanies ordinary combustion. This is a form of creation entirely different from the clay-based creation of human beings. The jinn, being created from fire, possess qualities associated with that element: they are subtle, invisible to human eyes under normal circumstances, quick, and capable of assuming different forms. However, their fiery origin does not imply superiority. Fire, for all its intensity and brilliance, can be destructive and consuming. The clay of human creation, for all its humility, is the substance from which life springs, the medium in which seeds germinate and grow. This verse is important for correcting common misconceptions. Some people imagine that the jinn are fallen angels or that Iblis was an angel who disobeyed. The Quran is clear that the jinn are a separate creation, distinct from angels. Angels were created from light and are incapable of disobedience. The jinn were created from fire and, like humans, possess free will. Among them are believers and disbelievers, the righteous and the wicked. Iblis was a jinn who had reached a high station through his worship, but his essential nature as a creature of fire with free will set the stage for his eventual rebellion. The mention of the jinn here also expands the scope of the narrative. The struggle between good and evil is not limited to human beings. There is an entire unseen realm of conscious beings facing similar tests of obedience. This awareness broadens the believer's understanding of the cosmos and reinforces the message that the Quran's guidance addresses not just human concerns but the fundamental realities of existence that affect all created beings with free will.
28 | ˹Remember, O Prophet˺ when your Lord said to the angels, “I am going to create a human being from sounding clay moulded from black mud.” | The narrative now moves to the dramatic moment when Allah announced His intention to create a human being to the angels. The instruction to "remember" is directed to the Prophet, and through him to all who hear the Quran, calling them to reflect on this primordial event as if they were witnessing it. The scene takes place in the heavenly realm, before the creation of Adam, when Allah informed the angels of His plan. The description of the human being repeats the exact words from verse twenty-six: "sounding clay moulded from black mud." This repetition is deliberate. The angels are being told that this new creation will be made from humble earthly material. They are being prepared for the fact that this creature, despite its lowly origin, will be honored with a special status, including the command to prostrate that will follow. The angels' response is not recorded here, but elsewhere in the Quran we learn that they expressed concern, asking whether Allah would place on earth a being that would cause corruption and shed blood. Their question was not an objection but an inquiry born from their knowledge of the jinn's previous history on earth, where they had caused great turmoil. The angels, who are beings of pure obedience, could not understand why a creature with free will, capable of disobedience and bloodshed, would be given authority on earth. Allah's response, recorded in Surah Al-Baqarah, was "I know what you do not know," indicating that the wisdom behind human creation transcended the angels' understanding. This background enriches the verse before us. The announcement to the angels was not merely informational. It was the beginning of a divine plan that would unfold across human history, a plan that included prophethood, revelation, guidance, testing, and ultimately the reward of Paradise or the punishment of Hell. The angels were being enlisted as witnesses and, as the next verse will show, as participants in honoring the new creation. The clay of Adam's origin is emphasized because it highlights the contrast between what the human being is made of and what the human being is destined for. From mud comes a creature that will carry the divine spirit, that will be taught the names of all things, that will be the vicegerent of Allah on earth, and that will be honored with the prostration of angels.
29 | So when I have fashioned him and had a spirit of My Own ˹creation˺ breathed into him, fall down in prostration to him.” | This verse contains the divine command that became the test separating the obedient angels from the arrogant Iblis. Allah instructs the angels that once He has completed the fashioning of the human being and breathed into him from His spirit, they are to fall down in prostration. The sequence of actions is significant. First comes "fashioning," the shaping and forming of the physical body from clay. This is the biological creation, the intricate crafting of the human form with its bones, muscles, nerves, and organs. The word "fashioned" translates sawwaituhu, which implies making something straight, balanced, and complete. The human form is not a rough sketch but a perfected design. The second stage is the breathing of the spirit. The phrase "a spirit of My Own" is highly significant. The Arabic is min ruhi, literally "from My spirit." This does not mean that a portion of Allah's essence entered the human being, a concept that would compromise divine transcendence. Rather, it is an honorific attribution. The spirit that was breathed into Adam was a spirit created by Allah, owned by Him, and specially honored by being attributed to Him directly. This breathing of the spirit transformed the clay form into a living, conscious being. It is the divine gift that makes human beings unique among earthly creatures, endowing them with reason, moral awareness, and the capacity to know and worship Allah. Only after this dual creation, physical and spiritual, does the command to prostrate come. The prostration is not worship of the human being. It is prostration to Allah, with the human being as the direction or the occasion of the prostration. It is an act of honoring the new creation and acknowledging the divine wisdom that brought it into existence. The prostration of the angels was an act of obedience to Allah that also demonstrated their acceptance of the human being's honored status. This command became the dividing line. The angels, created from light and inherently obedient, prostrated without hesitation. Iblis, a jinn of fire who had risen to their company through his worship, refused. The command was simple but the test was profound. Would these created beings accept Allah's choice to honor a creature of clay, or would their pride in their own origin, whether light or fire, prevent them from submitting to the divine will?
30 | So the angels prostrated all together— | This verse reports the angels' immediate and complete obedience to the divine command. The prostration was universal among the angels, as indicated by the phrase "all together," translating ajma'un. There was no hesitation, no discussion, no questioning of the wisdom behind the command. The angels, by their very nature, do not disobey Allah. Their existence is defined by perfect submission to whatever Allah orders. The word for prostration here is sajada, the same word used for the act of worship that human beings perform in prayer. This prostration was a physical act of lowering themselves, placing their faces on the ground in humility. For the angels, it was an act of worship directed to Allah, with Adam as the qiblah, the direction of the prostration. This moment is of immense significance in the cosmic drama. It represents the formal recognition by the highest created beings of the special status that Allah had granted to humanity. The angels, who had initially expressed concern about the potential for human corruption, now submitted to Allah's wisdom and honored the new creation without reservation. Their prostration was an acknowledgment that Allah's knowledge and plan were perfect, even if they could not fully comprehend them. The fact that the prostration was collective is also noteworthy. The angels acted as a unified community in their obedience. There was no dissent among them, no faction that held back, no one who suggested that a creature of clay was unworthy of such honor. This unity in obedience stands in sharp contrast to what happens in the next verse with Iblis, whose individual rebellion breaks the harmony of the heavenly assembly. This verse also serves as a model for human believers. The angels are presented as exemplars of immediate, unquestioning obedience to Allah's commands. When a clear divine instruction comes, the proper response is to say "we hear and we obey," not to delay, negotiate, or rationalize a way out. The angels' prostration was a perfect act of worship precisely because it was done exactly as commanded, when commanded, without adding or subtracting anything from the divine order. For the believer reading this verse, the lesson is clear: true submission means doing what Allah asks, even when the wisdom behind it is not immediately apparent.
31 | but not Iblîs, who refused to prostrate with the others. | The harmony of the angelic prostration is shattered by a single exception: Iblis. He refused to join the others in their act of obedience. The Arabic verb aba carries a strong sense of willful refusal, of stubborn rejection. This was not an oversight, a delay, or a misunderstanding. It was a deliberate, conscious choice to disobey a direct divine command. The verse identifies the refuser by name: Iblis. His name is derived from the root meaning to despair or to be confounded, and it is fitting because his rebellion led him to despair of Allah's mercy and to become a source of despair for others. The verse specifies that he refused to prostrate "with the others," emphasizing that his disobedience was not merely a private act but a public separation from the community of the obedient. While the angels acted collectively, Iblis stood apart. This introduces the Quranic theme that evil often begins with an individual choice to separate from the community of faith, to assert one's own judgment over the collective obedience to Allah. The question naturally arises: was Iblis an angel? The Quran answers this explicitly elsewhere. Iblis was from the jinn, as stated in Surah Al-Kahf. He had, through his extensive worship and devotion, been elevated to the company of the angels, but his essential nature as a jinn, a being of fire with free will, remained. When the command to prostrate came, the flaw in his character was exposed. His long history of worship had not purified his heart from pride, and the test brought that hidden pride to the surface. This is a sobering lesson for every believer. Outward devotion, even of the highest degree and longest duration, does not guarantee a sound heart. A person can spend years in worship, prayer, and spiritual discipline, yet harbor within themselves a seed of arrogance that will reveal itself when a particular test comes. Iblis had knowledge, he had experience, he had a history of obedience, but he lacked humility. When Allah honored a creature of clay over him, his pride would not allow him to accept it. The verse marks the moment when the greatest enemy of humanity was created. From this single act of refusal, an entire history of enmity, temptation, and spiritual warfare would unfold. The surah will now explore the dialogue between Allah and Iblis that followed this refusal, revealing the arguments of the arrogant and the justice of the divine response.
32 | Allah asked, “O Iblîs! What is the matter with you that you did not join others in prostration?” | This verse records the divine interrogation of Iblis following his refusal. Allah's question is not a request for information that He does not already possess. Allah's knowledge encompasses all things, including the hidden thoughts and intentions of every creature. The question serves multiple other purposes. It gives Iblis the opportunity to articulate his reason, thereby exposing the true nature of his disobedience and making it a matter of public record in the heavenly assembly. It also demonstrates divine justice. Allah does not punish without first giving the offender the chance to explain himself, even though the explanation, when given, only confirms the guilt. The question is phrased with a tone of rebuke. "What is the matter with you" translates ma laka, an expression used to express disapproval and to demand an account. Allah is calling Iblis to account for his singular act of defiance. The phrase "that you did not join others in prostration" highlights the communal nature of the obedience that Iblis rejected. It was not that he failed to perform an isolated act. He separated himself from the congregation of the obedient. His refusal was a public dissent, a visible rebellion that set him apart from the angels who had all prostrated together. This divine questioning contains an important lesson about how Allah deals with disobedience. The door to repentance is opened even in the moment of questioning. Iblis is being asked to account for himself, and the very asking implies that a different response, an admission of wrong and a plea for forgiveness, is possible. Yet Iblis will not take that path. His response in the next verse will not be repentance but justification. His pride will not allow him to say "I was wrong, forgive me." Instead, he will offer a reason that reveals his contempt for the human creation. This verse also teaches believers about the importance of being able to give an account of one's actions. Every soul will be questioned by Allah about what it did and why. The wise person lives with an awareness of that coming interrogation and ensures that their actions are such that they can be explained with honesty and humility, not with the arrogant self-justification that Iblis will now display.
33 | He replied, “It is not for me to prostrate to a human You created from sounding clay moulded from black mud.” | Iblis's response to Allah's question is recorded here, and it reveals the core of his rebellion. He does not claim that the command was unclear. He does not claim incapacity. He does not ask for forgiveness. Instead, he offers a justification rooted in his perceived superiority over the human being. The essence of his argument is that his own origin, fire, is superior to the human origin, clay, and therefore it is not fitting for a superior being to prostrate to an inferior one. The phrase "it is not for me" expresses a sense of entitlement and pride. Iblis is effectively setting his own standard of what is appropriate, judging the divine command against his personal assessment of relative worth. He acknowledges that Allah created the human from clay, but he fails to acknowledge that it was Allah who commanded the prostration. In his pride, he elevates his own judgment above the clear command of his Lord. The description of the human as created from "sounding clay moulded from black mud" is Iblis's scornful restatement of what Allah Himself had said. He uses the exact words to emphasize the lowliness of the material. His tone is one of contempt. "How can I, a being of pure, smokeless fire, bow down to this creature of dark, stinking mud?" This is the logic of racism, of caste, of every form of prejudice that judges worth by origin rather than by piety. Iblis looked at the outer form and the material composition, and he missed the inner reality. He saw clay, but he did not see the divine spirit that had been breathed into that clay. He saw a body of mud, but he did not see the vicegerent of Allah, the being taught the names of all things, the creature honored above even the angels. This verse exposes the root of all satanic temptation: the refusal to accept Allah's wisdom and the insistence on one's own flawed standards of value. Iblis's reasoning sounds logical to the arrogant ear. Fire is indeed lighter, subtler, and more powerful than clay in certain respects. But the error lies in assuming that material origin determines spiritual worth, and that the creature has the right to question the Creator's assignments of honor. This error has been replicated throughout human history whenever individuals or groups have considered themselves inherently superior to others based on race, lineage, wealth, or any other worldly criterion.
34 | Allah commanded, “Then get out of Paradise, for you are truly cursed.” | Iblis's arrogant justification is met with an immediate and decisive divine judgment. Allah commands him to leave Paradise, the abode of honor and nearness from which he had previously benefited. The command "get out" translates ukhruj, an imperative of expulsion. The honor of being in the company of the angels in the heavenly realm is revoked. Iblis is demoted, cast out from the station he had attained through his long worship. This expulsion is a direct consequence of his pride. He considered himself too elevated to bow to a creature of clay, and now he is being made lower than that creature. The one who refused to lower himself in obedience is now lowered in status, removed from the sacred presence. The reason for the expulsion is then stated clearly: "for you are truly cursed." The word "cursed" translates rajim, which means expelled from divine mercy, driven away, pelted. It is the same word used in verse seventeen to describe the devils from whom the sky is protected. Iblis, who once enjoyed a station of honor, has now become one of the accursed, an object of divine rejection. This transformation from honored worshipper to accursed outcast is one of the most dramatic falls in the history of creation. It demonstrates that past righteousness, no matter how extensive, provides no guarantee of future standing. A single act of arrogant disobedience, unrepented and persisted in, can undo a lifetime of apparent devotion. The test that Iblis faced was not of intellectual understanding but of humble submission. He knew the command was from Allah. He knew prostration was an act of worship due to Allah. But his pride in his own essence prevented him from accepting that Allah could honor a creature of clay above him. This verse is a warning to every believer. The moment we begin to think that our worship, our knowledge, our lineage, or our achievements make us inherently superior to others, we are walking in the footsteps of Iblis. The only proper response to divine command is "we hear and we obey," without adding our own assessment of what is fitting or appropriate. The cursed status of Iblis is not an arbitrary punishment but the natural consequence of separating oneself from divine mercy through arrogance.
35 | And surely upon you is condemnation until the Day of Judgment.” | The judgment upon Iblis is extended and clarified in this verse. The curse is not temporary or probationary. It is a fixed decree that will remain upon him "until the Day of Judgment," meaning the Day of Resurrection, the final reckoning. The word translated as "condemnation" is la'nah, which carries the meaning of being expelled from Allah's mercy, being deprived of all good, and being the object of divine wrath. This is a comprehensive state of ruin. To be deprived of Allah's mercy is to be deprived of hope, of guidance, of peace, and of any ultimate good. The specification of the time period, "until the Day of Judgment," is significant. It establishes that Iblis has a limited period during which his enmity and his tempting activities will be permitted. He is not granted an eternal reprieve. His time is fixed and will end with the judgment of all creation. This means that evil, despite its apparent power and persistence in this world, is not eternal. It has a terminal point. The Day of Judgment will mark the end of Iblis's activities and the beginning of his final punishment. For the believer, this specification of a time limit is a source of hope. The trials and temptations of this world, no matter how intense, have an expiration date. The enemy of humanity has been given time, but that time is finite. Every day that passes brings the end of his reign closer. The struggle against satanic temptation is not an endless war but a battle with a known conclusion. The verse also establishes a fundamental difference between the fate of Iblis and the fate of those humans who follow him. Iblis's condemnation is already decreed. Those humans who repent can still attain Allah's mercy. The door remains open for the descendants of Adam to return, but Iblis himself has locked himself into permanent rebellion. He knows his fate is sealed, and this knowledge fuels his desperate determination to drag as many human beings as possible into the same ruin. This is the psychology of the enemy: having despaired of mercy for himself, he works tirelessly to make others share his despair. The believer's defense is to remember that Allah's mercy is vast and that sincere repentance can overcome even a lifetime of following the accursed one.
36 | Satan appealed, “My Lord! Then delay my end until the Day of their resurrection.” | Despite the severity of the judgment just pronounced upon him, Iblis does not repent. Instead, he makes a request. He addresses Allah as "My Lord," a recognition of Allah's sovereignty that he does not deny even in his rebellion. This is a strange and disturbing feature of satanic psychology. Iblis believes in Allah. He knows Allah's power and lordship. He does not deny the truth of divine existence or attribute partners to Allah. His sin is not atheism but arrogance. He is a theist whose pride makes him the worst of creation. The request is for respite, for his death to be delayed until the Day when human beings are resurrected. He is not asking for forgiveness or a reduction of punishment. He is asking for time to execute a project of revenge. The destruction of his own standing has filled him with malice toward the creature whose creation was the occasion of his fall. He wants to live until the Day of Judgment so that he can devote himself to leading human beings astray. This request reveals the depth of his enmity. He does not see the human being as a fellow creature to be pitied or guided. He sees humanity as the cause of his humiliation, and he seeks to prove, in his twisted reasoning, that these creatures of clay are not worthy of the honor they received. He wants to demonstrate, through the widespread disobedience of humans, that Allah's choice to honor them was misplaced. This is the project that the Quran calls the work of Satan: beautifying evil, whispering temptations, and leading humans step by step toward the same ruin that befell him. Allah's response, which will follow, grants the request but with clear limitations. The granting of this respite is part of the divine wisdom. Human beings are placed on earth to be tested. The presence of a dedicated enemy who works tirelessly to mislead them is part of that test. Will they remember their Lord and resist? Will they follow the guidance of the prophets? Or will they succumb to the whispers of the one who has declared open war on their species? The request also shows that Iblis acknowledges the reality of resurrection. He does not ask to be spared from death entirely but to be given reprieve until the day of resurrection. This confirms that even the chief of the devils knows that the Day of Judgment is real, even as he works to make human beings doubt it.
37 | Allah said, “You will be delayed | Allah's response to Iblis's request is brief but definitive. The request is granted. Iblis will be among those who are given respite, whose death is delayed until the appointed time. The word "delayed" translates from the same root as the word used in Iblis's request, indicating that Allah is granting exactly what was asked: a postponement of his end. However, the simple affirmative "you will be delayed" carries within it layers of meaning that become clear in the following verse. The granting of this request is not an indication of divine approval or a sign that Iblis has any power to negotiate with his Creator. It is rather an element of the divine plan that was already decreed. The test of humanity required the presence of an adversary. The honor that Adam received required a context in which that honor would be challenged and in which human beings would have to choose between following the guidance of Allah and following the temptations of the enemy. Iblis's request was already known to Allah, and the granting of it was already part of the cosmic script. This teaches an important lesson about the relationship between divine decree and creaturely requests. Sometimes a creature asks for something, and Allah grants it, but the granting is not because the creature has any claim or power but because the request aligns with what Allah had already wisely decreed. The permissive will of Allah allows many things that He does not love or command, including the continued existence and activity of Satan. This is part of the mystery of divine wisdom. Evil exists in the world not because Allah is unable to eliminate it but because its temporary existence serves a purpose in the larger scheme of testing and spiritual growth. The believer who struggles against temptation and overcomes it reaches a spiritual station that could not be attained without the struggle. The victim of satanic whispers who repents and returns to Allah experiences the sweetness of divine mercy in a way that the one who never sinned cannot know. This is not to glorify evil but to recognize that Allah's wisdom encompasses even the existence of His enemy. The respite granted to Iblis is ultimately a component of the mercy shown to believing humans, for it is through resisting him that they earn their place in Paradise. The verse, in its stark simplicity, marks the moment when the cosmic battle between Iblis and humanity is formally set in motion.
38 | until the appointed Day.” | The respite granted to Iblis is qualified with a precise time limit: "until the appointed Day." The word "appointed" translates ma'lum, meaning known, specified, determined. The Day referred to here is the Day of Judgment, the Day when all accounts will be settled and the current order of testing will come to an end. This qualification is crucial because it establishes that Iblis's freedom to operate has a definite expiration. He is not immortal. He is not an eternal principle of evil existing in perpetual opposition to a principle of good. He is a created being with a limited span of activity, after which he will face the full consequences of his rebellion. The "appointed Day" is known to Allah but hidden from all creatures, including Iblis. He knows that his time is limited, but he does not know exactly when the limit will be reached. This uncertainty is part of his punishment and also part of what drives his frantic efforts to lead humanity astray. He is working against a deadline he cannot see. The concept of the appointed Day ties this verse back to the earlier verses of the surah that spoke of every community having a destined term. Just as nations have their appointed times, so too does the great enemy of humanity have his appointed end. The precision of divine timing governs all things. Nothing in creation, not even the most rebellious creature, operates outside the boundaries of Allah's predetermined schedule. This knowledge is a source of comfort for believers. The enemy seems powerful, his whispers seem persistent, and his influence appears widespread, but his time is running out. Every day that passes brings closer the moment when his power will be stripped away forever. The believer is engaged in a struggle with an opponent who has already been given his sentence and whose defeat is certain. The only question is whether individual human beings will be among those who overcome his temptations or among those who follow him to his ultimate destination. The appointed Day thus functions as both a warning and an encouragement. For the heedless, it is a warning that the pleasures of sin are temporary and the accounting is certain. For the struggling believer, it is an encouragement that the battle has a fixed endpoint and that perseverance will be rewarded when the Day finally arrives.
39 | Satan responded, “My Lord! For allowing me to stray I will surely tempt them on earth and mislead them all together, | Having received the respite he requested, Iblis now reveals his intentions with stark clarity. He swears an oath of enmity against humanity, declaring the program he will pursue for the remainder of his time. The verse begins with Iblis addressing Allah again as "My Lord," the same recognition of sovereignty that makes his rebellion all the more perverse. He acknowledges that Allah is his Lord even as he declares war on Allah's honored creation. The phrase "For allowing me to stray" is significant and requires careful understanding. Iblis attributes his misguidance to Allah, saying essentially that because Allah decreed or permitted him to go astray, he will respond by leading others astray. This is an attempt to shift blame and to justify his actions by pointing to divine decree. It is the argument of every sinner who says "it was my destiny" as an excuse for their choices. The Quran does not endorse this reasoning. Allah's decree encompasses all things, including Iblis's misguidance, but Iblis chose his path through his own free will and his own arrogance. The divine decree does not negate personal responsibility. Iblis's statement is recorded to expose the psychology of the unrepentant sinner, who blames Allah for his own evil choices. His declared program has two components. First, "I will surely tempt them." The verb used means to beautify, to make attractive, to adorn falsehood so that it appears desirable. Iblis will not force human beings to sin. He will make sin look appealing. He will work on their perceptions, their desires, and their imaginations so that the path of disobedience seems more attractive than the path of obedience. Second, he will "mislead them all together." His ambition is comprehensive. He is not content with leading a few individuals astray. He wants to corrupt the entire human race, to prove that these creatures of clay are unworthy of the honor they received. This is the voice of pure malice, of a being whose only remaining satisfaction is to see others share his ruin. The believer who reads this verse should feel a sense of alertness. The enemy has openly declared his intentions. There is no ambiguity, no hidden agenda. He wants your destruction, and he will use every means at his disposal to achieve it. This knowledge is a defense. When temptation comes, the believer can recognize it as the fulfillment of an ancient threat and respond with the shield of faith.
40 | except Your chosen servants among them.” | After declaring his comprehensive plan to mislead all of humanity, Iblis makes a crucial exception. He acknowledges that there is a category of human beings whom he will not be able to corrupt: Allah's chosen servants. The word "chosen" translates mukhlasin, which can be read with a slightly different vocalization to mean either "chosen" or "sincere" or "purified." Both meanings are complementary. The servants whom Iblis cannot touch are those whom Allah has chosen for His mercy and who have purified their devotion to Him, making it sincere and free from the mixtures of pride and desire that Iblis exploits. This admission by Iblis is of immense significance. The enemy himself testifies that his power has limits. He is not an all-powerful force of evil that no human can resist. There are human beings, fallible creatures of clay, who are beyond his reach. This testimony should fill the believer with hope and determination. If other human beings have attained the status of being protected from Iblis, then it is possible for us as well. The path to becoming among the chosen servants is the path of sincerity, of purifying one's worship for Allah alone, of struggling against the ego and its desires. Iblis's admission also clarifies the nature of his power. He does not control human beings. He whispers, he beautifies falsehood, he presents temptations, but the final decision to sin or to obey rests with the human soul. Iblis cannot breach the fortress of a heart that has sincerely turned to Allah. This is affirmed elsewhere in the Quran when Iblis himself says to the wrongdoers on the Day of Judgment that he had no authority over them except that he called them and they responded. They followed him by their own choice. The exception of the chosen servants is a direct challenge to Iblis's pride. He had declared that he would mislead all humans, but he is forced to admit that there is a group he cannot touch. His project of proving humanity unworthy will fail. The chosen servants will enter Paradise, and their very existence will testify for all eternity that Adam's children, despite their clay origin, were capable of the most sublime devotion and that Allah's honor of them was fully justified.
41 | Allah said, “This is the Way, binding on Me: | After Iblis declared his intention to mislead humanity and admitted his inability to touch the chosen servants, Allah responds with a definitive statement that establishes the unchangeable reality. The verse begins with Allah saying "This is the Way," and the word translated as "Way" is sirat, the straight path, the clear course of truth and justice. It refers to the path of guidance, devotion, and protection that Allah has laid down for His sincere servants. Iblis had just drawn a line between those he could tempt and those he could not, and now Allah confirms that this distinction is indeed a fixed path, a binding principle that governs the entire human struggle. The phrase "binding on Me" is of immense theological significance. Allah is not bound by any external force, but in His infinite wisdom and mercy He has decreed upon Himself certain things. The Quran mentions elsewhere that Allah has prescribed mercy upon Himself. Here He affirms that the protection of His sincere servants from Iblis's authority is something He has committed to, not because He must but because His perfect justice and mercy make it a certainty. The believers can take absolute comfort from this. The enemy who declared war on humanity is already constrained by a divine decree that he cannot overcome. Those who truly seek Allah, who purify their faith and follow the straight path, will find that the path itself is a fortress. The "Way" also implies a journey, a direction of travel. Those who walk this path are moving toward Allah, and as they move, they receive His protection. The image is not of a static state but of an active striving, a conscious walking on the path that Allah has made binding upon Himself to safeguard. This verse thus answers the question that might arise in the believer's heart: can I truly be safe from the whispers of Satan? The answer is yes, by taking the path of sincerity, by walking the straight way that Allah has guaranteed to protect, you will be among those over whom the enemy has no authority. It is a promise from the Lord of all worlds, and His promise is truth.
42 | you will certainly have no authority over My servants, except the deviant who follow you, | Allah continues the declaration that began in the previous verse, now addressing Iblis directly and explicitly limiting his power. The word translated as "authority" is sultan, which in Arabic denotes not just raw power but compelling authority, the kind of dominance that leaves the subject with no ability to resist. Allah tells Iblis that he will not have this kind of authority over "My servants." The possessive "My" is deeply significant. These servants belong to Allah, and their connection to their Lord is the very thing that shields them. It is not their own strength or intelligence that protects them but their relationship of servitude and devotion to the Creator. The verse then makes the crucial exception clear: "except the deviant who follow you." The word for "deviant" is ghawin, which comes from a root meaning to go astray willfully, to err after knowing the truth. These are not people who were simply uninformed or who made honest mistakes. They are those who chose to deviate, who heard the call of truth and turned away, who found the whispers of Iblis more attractive than the guidance of Allah. The authority that Iblis exercises over them is not one of force but of invitation. He calls, and they respond. They follow him, and by following him they place themselves under his influence. The phrase "follow you" indicates a voluntary alignment. No one is dragged into Iblis's camp against their will. The believer who reads this verse should understand that the protection from Satan is not a magical immunity but a spiritual reality tied to sincere devotion. As long as a person remains a true servant of Allah, actively striving to obey and avoiding what He has forbidden, the enemy's weapons are blunted. The danger comes when the servant begins to deviate, when the heart turns away from remembrance of Allah, when sins become habitual and unrepented. It is then that Iblis finds an opening and his authority begins to take hold. The verse also clarifies a common misconception about spiritual warfare. Satan is not an equal opponent to Allah. He has no power over anyone except those who give it to him by their own choices. The ultimate control remains with the human soul and its Creator.
43 | and surely Hell is their destined place, all together. | Having defined the two groups, the sincere servants who are protected and the deviant who follow Iblis, Allah now states the final destination of the followers. Hell is their "destined place," translating the Arabic maw'id, which means an appointed meeting place or a promised rendezvous. The use of this word carries a heavy irony. Iblis and his followers will meet in Hell. The appointment was set long ago, and it will be kept with absolute precision. There will be no missed connections, no excuses, no alternative arrangements. The word also echoes the "appointed Day" mentioned earlier when Iblis was granted respite. That Day is the time of the appointment, and Hell is the place. The verse adds the phrase "all together," ajma'in, which is the same word used in verse thirty to describe the angels prostrating all together. The deliberate parallel is striking. The angels came together in unified obedience to Allah's command, and their reward was eternal nearness to Him. Iblis and his followers will come together in unified punishment. The community of obedience is contrasted with the community of damnation. This also implies that the followers of Iblis, despite their different sins and different levels of worldly status, will share a common fate. The leaders of disbelief and the ordinary people who followed them, the active sinners and the passive accepters, will all find their appointment in Hell. There is a lesson here about the danger of following the crowd. The fact that many people are doing something, that one is part of a large group, provides no safety if that group is headed toward destruction. The only community that matters in the end is the community of the faithful. This verse also serves as a warning that the respite given to Iblis does not mean escape. He was granted time, but the end of that time is a confirmed appointment in Hell. His project of leading humanity astray will not save him from his own fate. On the contrary, every soul he misleads will add to his burden and to the severity of his punishment. The appointment is certain, and it will not be broken.
44 | It has seven gates, to each a group of them is designated. | This verse provides a vivid detail about the structure of Hell, revealing that it is not a single undifferentiated abyss but a realm with seven distinct gates, each gate assigned to a specific category of its inhabitants. The number seven is significant in the Quran and in Arabic usage, often indicating completeness, multiplicity, and a full range. The seven gates suggest that Hell encompasses every type of sin and every level of punishment, with each gate serving as the entrance for a particular group defined by the nature of their wrongdoing. Islamic scholarship has discussed the names and assignments of these gates based on various narrations. Some reports mention that the gates include Jahannam for the sinful among the Muslims, Laza for the Christians, Hutama for the Jews, Sa'ir for the Sabians, Saqar for the Magians, Jahim for the polytheists, and Hawiya for the hypocrites. Other scholars have arranged the categories differently. What matters is the principle that divine justice is precise and proportionate. Punishment is not dispensed in a haphazard manner. Each group enters through the gate that corresponds to its specific type of deviation, and the torment they experience is calibrated to what they earned. The word "designated" translates maqsum, meaning divided, apportioned, assigned. The division is not random but based on a divine decree that takes into account the exact nature of each person's disbelief and sins. This precision demonstrates that Allah's knowledge encompasses every detail of human behavior and that His judgment is perfectly fair. The existence of multiple gates also serves as a rhetorical device to emphasize the vast capacity of Hell. It is not a small prison that might become overcrowded. It has seven great gates, each capable of receiving a massive influx of condemned souls. This was a warning to the Makkan disbelievers who dismissed the Prophet's warnings as fanciful. Hell is real, it is vast, it is organized, and it has a designated entrance for every group that chose to follow the path of Iblis rather than the path of the prophets. The verse also calls to mind the contrast with Paradise, which according to other Quranic verses has eight gates. The gates of Paradise are for the righteous, and the gates of Hell are for the followers of Iblis. The choice of which gate one will eventually enter is being made in this life, with every deed and every decision.
45 | Indeed, the righteous will be amid Gardens and springs. | After the detailed description of Hell's gates and the fate of the deviant, the Quran immediately shifts the focus to the reward of the righteous, maintaining the balance between warning and glad tidings that characterizes the entire surah. The word "righteous" translates muttaqin, those who have taqwa, who are conscious of Allah, who guard themselves against sin, who live with an awareness of divine accountability. These are the same as the "chosen servants" whom Iblis admitted he could not mislead. Their destination is described as "Gardens and springs." The word for Gardens is jannat, which evokes the image of lush, verdant orchards, places of shade, fruit, and beauty that were the ideal of comfort in the Arabian environment. The springs, 'uyun, signify flowing water, a constant, effortless supply of the most precious resource in a desert land. The combination of gardens and springs speaks of perfect provision, a place where every need is met and every sense is delighted. The verse emphasizes their location by saying they are "amid" these blessings, meaning they are surrounded by them, immersed in them, not merely observing from a distance. This is their permanent environment. The contrast with the preceding verses is stark and intentional. The followers of Iblis enter through the seven gates of Hell into a realm of torment, while the righteous enter Gardens with flowing springs. The two destinations could not be more different, and yet the path that leads to each begins in the same world, with choices made in the same human heart. The verse serves as both comfort and motivation. For the believers in Makkah who faced persecution and hardship, the promise of Gardens and springs was a reminder that their current difficulties were temporary and that an eternal reward awaited them. For anyone hesitating between the path of Iblis and the path of righteousness, the imagery of the verse paints a picture of what is at stake. The choice is between the flames and the gardens, between the cursed company of Iblis and the blessed company of the righteous. Every soul is making that choice moment by moment, and the destination is being built from the sum of those choices.
46 | ˹It will be said to them,˺ “Enter in peace and security.” | This verse describes the moment of entry into Paradise, capturing the greeting that the righteous will receive as they cross its threshold. The phrase "It will be said to them" indicates that this is an announcement, a welcome delivered by the angels who guard the gates of Paradise. The words "Enter in peace and security" are rich with meaning. The word "peace" is salam, the greeting of Islam, the word that signifies not merely the absence of conflict but a state of wholeness, well-being, and harmony. The righteous are being invited into a realm where everything is as it should be, where no discord exists, and where their relationship with their Creator, with themselves, and with others is perfectly ordered. The word "security" is aminin, from the same root as iman, faith. It means safety, freedom from fear, the complete absence of any threat or danger. In the earthly life, even the most powerful and wealthy individuals live with some level of anxiety about the future, about their health, about their possessions, about their enemies. In Paradise, that anxiety vanishes forever. The righteous will know with absolute certainty that their bliss is permanent, that no harm can reach them, and that their Lord is pleased with them. This greeting of peace and security is the fulfillment of what the believers experienced in a limited way in this world when they submitted to Allah and found inner peace, but now it is complete and unending. The verse also has a legal dimension. The command "Enter" is a formal admission. The gates of Paradise are opened to them, and they are authorized to take possession of their eternal home. It is the culmination of a life of faith, the moment when hope becomes reality and the promises of Allah are fulfilled in the most direct and personal way imaginable. For the listener who is still in the world of testing, this verse is an invitation to imagine that moment and to let that imagination fuel the determination to be among those who hear these blessed words.
47 | We will remove whatever bitterness they had in their hearts. In a friendly manner, they will be on thrones, facing one another. | This verse describes one of the most profound blessings of Paradise: the purification of the heart from all negative feelings. The word translated as "bitterness" is ghill, which refers to deep-seated resentment, malice, envy, grudges, and ill-will. In this world, even the most righteous people sometimes struggle with these feelings. Relationships between believers can be strained by misunderstandings, by competition, by perceived slights. The hearts of the best among us are not entirely free from the whisperings of ego and the residues of conflict. But in Paradise, Allah will remove all of this. The hearts of the righteous will be cleansed so thoroughly that not a trace of bitterness remains. They will meet one another with pure love, with genuine joy at each other's company, with no memory of any past disagreement that might mar their fellowship. This purification is an essential preparation for the social life of Paradise. The verse goes on to describe that condition: "In a friendly manner, they will be on thrones, facing one another." The word translated as "in a friendly manner" is ikhwanan, which literally means as brothers. The relationship between the inhabitants of Paradise is one of brotherhood, a bond of sincere affection and mutual regard that surpasses even the closest friendships of this world. They are seated on thrones, indicating honor and comfort, and they are facing one another, indicating intimate and open social interaction. No one is sitting in a corner alone. Paradise is a place of joyful community. The image of facing one another also suggests that there is no hierarchy that separates them, no king looking down on subjects, but mutual respect and enjoyment. This verse directly answers the psychology of Iblis. His downfall was caused by the bitterness in his heart, the resentment he felt toward Adam. The righteous are saved from that same poison. Their hearts are made pure by Allah's grace, and they inherit what Iblis lost: eternal fellowship in a state of peace and love.
48 | No fatigue will touch them there, nor will they ever be asked to leave. | This verse adds two further assurances about the nature of life in Paradise. First, "no fatigue will touch them there." The word for fatigue is nasab, which refers to weariness, exhaustion, the draining of energy that accompanies labor and effort in this world. Human life on earth is a constant cycle of exertion and rest. Even the most enjoyable activities eventually tire us. In Paradise, this law is abolished. The blessings are experienced without any depletion of energy. The joy is constant, the delight is unflagging, and the body and soul do not experience the diminishing returns that characterize worldly pleasures. There is no need for sleep to recover, no sense of having had enough, no creeping boredom. This is part of the perfection of the next life. The second assurance is equally profound: "nor will they ever be asked to leave." The Arabic word mukhrajin means expelled, evicted, forced out. This statement carries a deep resonance with the story of Adam. Adam and Eve were placed in the Garden and then, after their slip, were told to descend to the earth. Their stay in Paradise was conditional, and the condition was broken. For the righteous in the eternal Paradise, there is no such condition and no such expulsion. Their residence is permanent, irrevocable, and eternal. The fear of losing what one loves is one of the deepest anxieties of the human heart. In Paradise, that fear is gone. The righteous possess their joy forever. This verse thus closes the description of Paradise with the ultimate security. The peace they entered, the purified hearts they enjoy, the brotherhood they share, all of this is not a temporary state but an everlasting reality. The believer who reads this verse should feel a surge of hope and a renewed commitment to the path that leads to such an abode. The hardships of this world, the fatigue of worship, the pain of resisting temptation, are temporary. The rest, the eternal home, is coming.
49 | Inform My servants ˹O Prophet˺ that I am truly the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, | The surah now pivots from the extended narrative of Iblis and the descriptions of Hell and Paradise to a direct instruction to the Prophet, and through him to all who convey the message. Allah tells him to inform His servants of two fundamental truths. The first is that Allah is truly the All-Forgiving, the Most Merciful. The name Al-Ghafur, the All-Forgiving, means that Allah covers sins, wipes them away, and does not hold them against the one who repents. No sin is too great for His forgiveness if the servant turns back sincerely. The name Ar-Rahim, the Most Merciful, means that His mercy is vast, continuous, and encompasses all things. It is the mercy that manifests as blessings, guidance, and ultimately the admission into Paradise. These two names together convey a message of immense hope. The Prophet is instructed to proclaim this to the servants of Allah, not to hide it, not to make the religion appear as a burden of impossible demands without a path of redemption. The emphasis "truly" translates inna, indicating that this is an established truth, not a speculation or a mere hope. The believers can rely on Allah's forgiveness and mercy with full confidence. This verse also subtly addresses the psychology of Iblis. Iblis despaired of Allah's mercy, and his despair led to his eternal ruin. The believers are called to the opposite response: to recognize the vastness of divine mercy and to run toward it rather than away from it. The verse is a remedy for the despair that Satan tries to instill in the hearts of sinners. No matter what one has done, the door of forgiveness is open. The condition is sincere repentance, a turning back to Allah with a heart that regrets the past and resolves to amend the future. The verse also balances the severe warnings that came before. The seven gates of Hell are real, but so is the mercy that can save a person from entering any of them. The message of Islam is not a message of doom alone but a message of hope grounded in the reality of divine mercy. The Prophet is told to inform, to announce, to make known this joyful truth.
50 | and that My torment is indeed the most painful. | The instruction to the Prophet continues with the second part of the message he must convey. Alongside the announcement of Allah's forgiveness and mercy, he must also inform them that Allah's torment is the most painful. The two truths must be communicated together. A message that only speaks of mercy can lead to complacency and a disregard for divine commands. A message that only speaks of punishment can lead to despair and a sense that striving is futile. The Quranic method is to hold both in balance, presenting the hope of mercy and the warning of punishment side by side so that the believer lives between fear and hope. The word for "torment" is 'adhab, which implies a pain that is not just physical but also psychological and spiritual. The word for "painful" is alim, an adjective of intensity. The verse declares that this painful torment is indeed from Allah, and it is a reality that must not be ignored. The conjunction "and that" ties this verse directly to the previous one. The same Prophet who tells the people "My Lord is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful" must also tell them "and My Lord's torment is the most painful." This is not a contradiction but a complete picture of divine justice. Allah's mercy does not negate His justice, and His justice does not overwhelm His mercy. Both attributes are perfect and operate in full harmony. The sinner who repents finds a Lord who is ready to forgive. The sinner who persists and dies without repentance finds a Lord whose punishment is severe. Neither outcome contradicts the divine nature. The verse also serves as a warning to those who might misinterpret the previous verse as a license to sin. The fact that Allah is forgiving does not mean that one can sin carelessly with the intention of repenting later. The pain of His torment is described as the most painful, and no intelligent person would risk such an outcome by delaying repentance. The balance of these two verses is the essence of prophetic preaching. The prophets brought glad tidings and warnings, and this surah exemplifies that methodology with precision and power.
51 | And inform them ˹O Prophet˺ about Abraham’s guests | After the general proclamation of mercy and punishment, Allah now instructs the Prophet to convey a specific story that illustrates both of these realities in action. He is told to inform the people about the guests of Abraham. The word "guests" translates dayf, which in Arabic can refer to a single guest or a group. Here it refers to the angels who visited Abraham in human form. This story is told in several surahs, each time with different details suited to the context. In this surah, the story will highlight the mercy shown to Abraham through the good news of a son, and the punishment that befell the people of Lot, demonstrating the two messages of verses forty-nine and fifty in a concrete historical event. The instruction to "inform them" places this story within the broader mission of the Prophet. He is not merely recounting ancient tales for entertainment. He is providing evidence from history that Allah's mercy and punishment are real, that they descend upon human beings in this world as well as the next, and that the pattern is consistent. Abraham is chosen as the central figure of this narrative because he is the father of prophets, the friend of Allah, and a model of hospitality, faith, and concern for others. The Makkan disbelievers claimed descent from Abraham through Ishmael and honored him as their forefather. By telling a story in which Abraham receives angels, believes in their message, and shows concern for the fate of a wicked people, the Quran challenges the Makkans to examine whether their own response to divine messengers matches the response of their revered ancestor. Abraham recognized the messengers and treated them with honor. The Makkans mocked their messenger and called him insane. The contrast is deliberate and powerful. The verse sets the stage for a narrative that will reinforce every major theme of the surah so far: the rejection of messengers, the certainty of divine punishment, the protection of the righteous, and the operation of Allah's mercy and justice in human history.
52 | who entered upon him and greeted ˹him with˺, “Peace!” He ˹later˺ said, “Surely we are afraid of you.” | The narrative begins with the angels arriving at Abraham's dwelling and greeting him with the word "Peace," the same greeting that the righteous will receive upon entering Paradise as mentioned earlier in the surah. This greeting of salam is the mark of the believers and of the angels, a sign of goodwill and divine blessing. Abraham responded to their greeting, but his response is not immediately recorded. Instead, the verse moves to a later moment when Abraham, after observing something unusual in his guests' behavior, expresses fear. The phrase "He later said" indicates a shift in the situation. According to the detailed accounts in other surahs, Abraham quickly prepared a roasted calf for his guests, as was the custom of generous hospitality. But when he placed the food before them, he noticed that their hands did not reach toward it. They did not eat. In the ancient Near Eastern culture, refusing to eat the food of one's host was a sign of hostile intent. A guest who would not share a meal was suspected of coming with harmful purposes. Abraham, who had initially assumed they were human travelers, now became alarmed. He said, "Surely we are afraid of you," expressing his sudden fear that these strangers might intend harm to him and his household. The use of "we" indicates that the fear was shared, likely by his wife Sarah as well. This verse captures a very human moment. Abraham, the great prophet and friend of Allah, experiences the natural emotion of fear when faced with an uncertain and potentially dangerous situation. This does not diminish his status but makes him relatable. Even the most faithful servants of Allah can feel fear, and that fear is not a sign of weak faith but of normal human prudence. The verse also builds suspense in the narrative. The reader or listener wonders: who are these guests, why do they not eat, and what will happen next? The angels' response in the following verses will both calm Abraham's fear and reveal the dual purpose of their mission.
53 | They reassured ˹him˺, “Do not be afraid! Surely we give you good news of a knowledgeable son.” | Seeing Abraham's fear, the angels immediately moved to calm him. Their first words were "Do not be afraid," a reassurance that has been given by angels to prophets throughout history. When Gabriel appeared to Mary, he told her not to fear. When the angels visited Lot, they told him not to fear. The presence of angels can be overwhelming, and the circumstances of their visits are often extraordinary, so reassurance is the necessary prelude to communication. After calming his fear, the angels delivered their first piece of news: the glad tidings of a knowledgeable son. The word for "good news" is nubashshiru, which means we bring you tidings that should make you rejoice, news that will bring joy to your heart. The news was that Abraham would have a son, despite his old age and his wife's lifelong barrenness. This was the fulfillment of a deep and long-held longing. Abraham had prayed for a righteous child, and now the answer to that prayer had arrived. The son is described as "knowledgeable," ghulamun 'alim. A ghulam is a boy who has reached the age of youth and vigor, indicating that the son would live to maturity. The word 'alim means knowledgeable, wise, possessing understanding. This points to the future prophethood of Isaac, who would be granted wisdom and knowledge by Allah and would carry the line of prophethood forward. The tidings were not just of a child but of a child with a special destiny. This verse illustrates the first part of the angelic mission: the delivery of mercy and blessing to a beloved prophet. It demonstrates that Allah's mercy is personal and specific. It addresses the deepest hopes of the individual believer. Abraham's hospitality and righteousness are rewarded with news that changes his life and the course of history. The knowledgeable son would become a prophet, an ancestor of many prophets, and a link in the chain that ultimately leads to the final Prophet, Muhammad, peace be upon them all.
54 | He wondered, “Do you give me good news despite my old age? What unlikely news!” | Abraham's response to the glad tidings is recorded in this verse. He expresses wonder and astonishment, not disbelief. The phrase "Do you give me good news despite my old age" reveals the source of his amazement. He is fully aware of the natural impossibility of the situation. He is old, well past the age of fathering children. His wife Sarah is also old and has been barren throughout her life. From every human perspective, the birth of a son is impossible. Abraham acknowledges this reality openly. His question is not a challenge to the angels' truthfulness but an expression of joyful surprise at the unexpected working of divine power. He is essentially saying: how can this be, when all the normal means have ceased? His wonder is the wonder of a person who knows the laws of nature and suddenly sees them suspended by the command of the One who established those laws. The phrase "What unlikely news" translates the Arabic fabima tubashshirun, which carries a tone of amazed inquiry. He is not rejecting the news but asking about its basis. His mind is grappling with the gap between what is naturally possible and what divine power can accomplish. This is a healthy spiritual response. The believer acknowledges the reality of natural causes but also knows that Allah is not limited by them. The verse also highlights Abraham's humility. He does not say "I am a great prophet, of course I deserve such a miracle." He focuses on his own human limitations, his old age, his frailty. This humility is part of what made Abraham the friend of Allah. He did not let his high spiritual station make him forget that he was a mortal human being dependent entirely on divine grace. The verse teaches that it is permissible to express wonder at Allah's blessings and to acknowledge the gap between human capacity and divine power. Such wonder is a form of praise, recognizing that what is impossible for creation is easy for the Creator.
55 | They responded, “We give you good news in all truth, so do not be one of those who despair.” | The angels respond to Abraham's wonder with a firm affirmation. They tell him that they bring the good news "in all truth," bil-haqq, meaning with absolute certainty, grounded in reality, and issued from the decree of Allah. This is not a guess, a hope, or a metaphor. It is a fact that will come to pass as surely as they are standing before him. The reassurance is necessary because the news is so extraordinary that a human heart might waver between hope and doubt. By anchoring the promise in truth, the angels eliminate the space for doubt. Then they add a gentle caution: "so do not be one of those who despair." The word for "despair" is qanitin, which means those who have lost hope, who have given up on the possibility of good. The angels are telling Abraham that his expression of wonder could, if carried too far, border on despair of Allah's mercy. But they quickly clarify that they are not accusing him of despair. They are warning him away from it. This nuance is important. Even a prophet is reminded to guard against the subtle entry of hopelessness into the heart. If a man of Abraham's stature needs such a reminder, then ordinary believers should be all the more vigilant. This verse connects directly to the earlier declaration that Allah is the All-Forgiving, Most Merciful, and that His mercy must never be doubted. The angels are embodying that message. They bring a miracle to an old man and tell him not to lose hope. The verse teaches that despair of Allah's mercy is a dangerous state. It is, as the next verse will explicitly state, the characteristic of the misguided. The believer must always maintain hope, even when the situation appears impossible. The power of Allah is not constrained by human calculations. The child that was promised to Abraham would indeed be born, and from that child would come a great nation. This was the truth that the angels delivered, and Abraham's heart was being prepared to receive it fully.
56 | He exclaimed, “Who would despair of the mercy of their Lord except the misguided?” | Abraham's response to the angels' gentle admonition is swift and emphatic. He declares that no one would ever despair of the mercy of their Lord except those who are misguided. In saying this, Abraham clarifies that his earlier expression of wonder was not despair but simple human amazement at the extraordinary power of Allah. He distances himself completely from any suggestion of hopelessness. The word "misguided" translates dallun, which means those who are lost, who have strayed from the right path, who are in a state of error. To despair of Allah's mercy is, in Abraham's understanding, a hallmark of being spiritually lost. The person who truly knows Allah knows that His mercy is vast, that His power is unlimited, and that He can create whatever He wills regardless of natural impossibilities. Despair is therefore not a minor emotional state but a sign of a deeper problem in one's knowledge of Allah. This verse has resonated throughout Islamic spirituality. It is cited as a proof that a believer must never lose hope, no matter how great the sin or how difficult the circumstance. If an elderly, childless couple can be given a son, then any problem can be solved, any sin can be forgiven, and any prayer can be answered in the way Allah sees best. The statement also contains a subtle warning. By saying "who would despair," Abraham implies that no reasonable person who understands Allah's attributes would ever fall into despair. Therefore, if one finds oneself despairing, one should recognize that as a symptom of a misunderstanding that needs to be corrected. The cure is to learn about Allah's names and attributes, to reflect on His mercy, and to remember the stories of those who received His blessings after long waiting and against all odds. This verse also ties back to Iblis's despair. Iblis despaired of mercy and became the archetype of the misguided. Abraham, by contrast, is the archetype of the hopeful believer who trusts his Lord completely.
57 | He ˹then˺ added, “What is your mission, O messenger-angels?” | Having received the glad tidings and affirmed his unwavering hope in Allah's mercy, Abraham now turns his attention to the broader purpose of the angelic visit. He addresses them as "messenger-angels," recognizing their true nature. By this point, the combination of their supernatural greeting, their refusal to eat, and the extraordinary news they delivered has made it clear that these are no ordinary travelers. They are messengers sent by Allah, and messengers come with specific tasks. Abraham's question reflects his spiritual insight and his concern for the affairs of Allah's creation. He understands that the angels who brought him good news are also carrying out a larger mission, and he wants to know what it is. This is characteristic of Abraham, who is portrayed throughout the Quran as someone deeply concerned with justice and the fate of others. He argued with the angels about the people of Lot, seeking to avert punishment if possible. His question here is the prelude to that intercession. The phrase "O messenger-angels" translates ayyuha al-mursalun, which is a respectful and formal address. Abraham treats the angels with the honor due to their station as envoys of the Almighty. This verse also serves a narrative function. It moves the story forward from the private good news given to Abraham to the public mission of punishment that the angels were primarily sent to execute. The good news of a son was a secondary purpose, a mercy granted along the way. The primary mission was the destruction of the people of Lot. By having Abraham ask the question, the Quran draws the listener's attention to this shift in focus. The angels are about to reveal the terrible purpose of their journey, and Abraham's question sets the stage for that revelation. For the believer, there is a lesson in Abraham's attentiveness. He did not become so absorbed in his own joy that he forgot to ask about the larger purposes of Allah. Personal blessings should not make one indifferent to the state of the world and the unfolding of divine justice.
58 | They replied, “We have actually been sent to a wicked people.” | The angels answer Abraham's question with a direct and sobering statement. They have been sent to a "wicked people." The word used is qawmin mujrimin, a people characterized by crime and wrongdoing. The same word mujrimin was used earlier in the surah, in verse twelve, to describe those into whose hearts disbelief is threaded. It refers to those who have gone beyond the bounds of decency and morality, who have made sin their habit and identity, and who have rejected the warnings of the prophet sent to them. This people is the nation of Lot, who lived in the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah near the Dead Sea. The angels do not immediately name the people, but Abraham, who knows the region and knows his nephew Lot, understands exactly who is being referred to. The angels' statement is deliberately blunt. They do not offer a detailed list of the people's sins at this moment. They simply characterize them as wicked, a sufficient description given the gravity of what is about to befall them. The word "actually" translates inna, providing emphasis. There is no ambiguity about their mission. They have been sent with a specific command from Allah, and that command is to execute punishment. This verse illustrates the second part of the message the Prophet was told to convey: that Allah's torment is indeed the most painful. The angels who brought mercy to Abraham in the form of a promised son are the same angels who will bring destruction to the people of Lot. Mercy and punishment are two sides of the same divine justice. The believers are protected and blessed, while the wicked are held accountable. Abraham, a prophet who knows both the mercy and the justice of Allah, now faces the tension between his joy at the good news and his concern for Lot and his family. The verse sets up that tension, which will unfold in the following verses as Abraham seeks to understand the scope of the coming punishment.
59 | As for the family of Lot, we will certainly deliver them all, | The angels now provide Abraham with the crucial exception to the destruction they are about to unleash. The family of Lot will be saved. The word "deliver" translates la-munajjuhum, which means to rescue, to save, to bring safely out of danger. The emphasis in the Arabic construction is strong, using the lam of emphasis and the pronoun hum, meaning all of them. The angels want Abraham to know that his nephew Lot and those who believe with him will not be touched by the punishment. This is a direct expression of divine mercy. Even in the midst of a terrible judgment, Allah distinguishes between the wicked and the righteous. The destruction is not indiscriminate. It targets the criminals and spares the believers. Abraham's concern for Lot is addressed before he even voices it. The angels know that Abraham's heart will immediately turn to the fate of his relative, and they provide the reassurance without being asked. This demonstrates the care with which Allah treats His prophets. Abraham is not left in suspense. He is told clearly that the righteous will be saved. The phrase "the family of Lot" includes his believing household members, those who accepted his message and followed his guidance. According to the Quranic account in other surahs, the number of believers in Lot's community was very small. Some reports say it was only his daughters and perhaps a few others. But however small the group, they are known to Allah and they will be delivered. This verse reinforces the principle that Allah does not punish a people without saving the believers among them. The presence of a small righteous minority can delay punishment, but when the time comes, that minority is extracted before the destruction descends. The verse also serves as a comfort to the Prophet Muhammad and the believers in Makkah. They were a small minority surrounded by a hostile majority. The story of Lot reminds them that numbers do not determine outcome. The few who believe are the ones who are saved, while the many who reject are the ones who perish.
60 | except his wife. We have determined that she will be one of the doomed. | The comprehensive deliverance promised for Lot's family has a single, painful exception: his wife. The angels state clearly that she is excluded from the rescue and will be among those who are destroyed. The word "doomed" translates al-ghabirin, which literally means those who remain behind, those who are left in the destruction, those who do not go forward with the saved group. Lot's wife is decreed to be among them. This exception is significant for several reasons. Lot's wife was not merely a passive bystander. According to the Quranic account in Surah At-Tahrim, she betrayed her husband, not in a marital sense but in matters of faith and community. She sympathized with the wicked people of the city. She is said to have informed them when guests arrived at Lot's house, acting as a spy and an accomplice to their evil intentions. Her heart was with the criminals, not with the prophet. The fact that she was the wife of a prophet did not save her. The Quran is emphatic on this point across multiple surahs. On the Day of Judgment, no familial relationship will benefit anyone unless it is accompanied by true faith. The wife of Noah and the wife of Lot are both cited as examples of disbelievers who were married to righteous men but perished with their people. This is a sobering warning against the assumption that one can ride to salvation on the piety of one's family. Every soul stands alone before Allah. The phrase "We have determined" translates qaddarna, which means We have decreed, We have ordained. The fate of Lot's wife was not an accident or an oversight. It was a specific divine decree based on her own choices. She had made herself one of the criminals, and she would share their destiny. This verse completes the dual lesson of the angelic mission. Abraham receives mercy and good news, while the wicked are warned of punishment, and even a prophet's wife is not exempt if she aligns herself with evil. The surah continues to weave its themes of personal responsibility, divine justice, and the uncompromising nature of true faith.
61 | So when the messengers came to the family of Lot, | The narrative now moves from Abraham's encounter with the angels to their arrival at the dwelling of Lot. The transition is marked by the word "so," indicating the direct consequence of the mission they disclosed to Abraham. The angels, having delivered the glad tidings to Abraham and calmed his concern for Lot's believing family, depart and reach the cities of the plain. The phrase "the family of Lot" here refers to Lot and his household, the small band of believers residing in Sodom. Lot, like Abraham, was a prophet and a man of immense hospitality and moral courage. He had been sent to a people whose wickedness had reached a level unprecedented in human history, as the Quran mentions elsewhere that they committed abominations no other nation had practiced before them. The angels arrived in the form of handsome young men, a form that would immediately test the depravity of the city's inhabitants. Lot, unaware of their true identity, saw them as human travelers in need of shelter and protection. The arrival of these guests at Lot's home is the spark that ignites the final confrontation between the prophet and his people, a confrontation that will end with the salvation of the believers and the total destruction of the transgressors. This verse also serves as a narrative bridge, linking the mercy shown to Abraham with the justice about to descend on Sodom. The same angels who brought laughter to an elderly couple now bring terror to a corrupt society. It demonstrates that the divine messengers execute Allah's will in all its dimensions, carrying both reward and punishment to their respective destinations without partiality or hesitation.
62 | he said, “You are surely an unfamiliar people!” | When Lot saw the angels, he immediately recognized that these were strangers. The phrase "unfamiliar people" translates qawmun munkarun, which can mean people who are unknown, whose appearance or manner is unfamiliar, or people who bring with them a sense of something out of the ordinary. Lot had never seen these men before, and in a city as dangerous as Sodom, the arrival of unknown, strikingly handsome strangers was a cause for immediate alarm. Lot's statement is not a greeting of welcome but an expression of anxiety. He knew his people. He knew what they would do if they discovered the presence of these guests. His heart immediately became heavy with the burden of protecting them from the inevitable assault. This reaction reveals Lot's deep sense of responsibility and his understanding of the moral depravity that surrounded him. He was a stranger to his own people in terms of his righteousness, and now strangers had arrived who would be vulnerable to that people's evil. The tension in this verse is palpable. Lot is caught between his duty of hospitality, a sacred obligation in the ancient Near East and in the Abrahamic tradition, and his knowledge that extending hospitality to these guests would put them in mortal danger. He does not yet know that these are angels who need no protection. He sees them as human beings, and his prophetic conscience cannot bear the thought of handing them over or failing to shield them. This verse captures the psychological pressure under which Lot lived daily. He was a righteous man in a city that had normalized the worst of sins, and each new arrival of innocent travelers brought a fresh crisis. The verse sets the stage for the confrontation that follows and highlights the courage of Lot, who despite his fear, chose to protect his guests rather than surrender them to the mob.
63 | They responded, “We have come to you with that ˹torment˺ which they have doubted. | The angels, perceiving Lot's distress, immediately reveal the true nature of their visit. They have not come as ordinary travelers in need of protection. They have come bearing the very torment that the people of Sodom had persistently doubted and mocked. Lot had warned his people for years about the consequences of their sins. He had called them to repentance, to decency, to the natural order of marriage that Allah had established. They responded with ridicule, with threats of exile, and with the chilling demand that he hand over his guests for their gratification. They did not believe that any punishment would ever come. The angels now declare that what was doubted has arrived. The word "torment" here translates 'adhab, the same word used throughout the surah for divine punishment. The people doubted its reality, treating Lot's warnings as the rantings of a madman or a self-righteous moralist. The angels' statement is direct and without euphemism. They do not negotiate. They do not offer a final chance for repentance. The time for repentance had passed. The appointment that Abraham had been told about in verse fifty-eight has now reached its hour. This verse is a chilling example of how divine punishment can arrive when it is least expected and when the opportunity for turning back has been exhausted. For the believers reading this, it is a reminder that the warnings of the prophets are not empty threats. They are news of a reality that will unfold on a timeline known only to Allah. The mockery of the disbelievers does not delay the decree. It only makes the arrival of the punishment more devastating because it finds them utterly unprepared.
64 | We come to you with the truth, and we are certainly truthful. | The angels reinforce their previous statement by emphasizing the absolute certainty and truthfulness of their mission. The phrase "with the truth" translates bil-haqq, which means with the established reality, with the decree that cannot be altered, with the just and inevitable outcome. This is the same phrase they used with Abraham when they told him they brought good news in truth. The angels are not speaking from their own authority. They are conveying a divine decree, a truth that is as fixed as the laws of existence. Then they add, "and we are certainly truthful." This second affirmation addresses any remaining human hesitation in Lot's heart. Lot might have thought that the situation, though terrible, could still be resolved, that perhaps the people might be given more time, that his years of struggle might yet bear fruit. The angels make it clear that the matter is settled. They are truthful in what they say, and what they say is that the destruction is now imminent. The repetition of the concept of truth underscores the Quranic theme that Allah's promises and threats are always true. The disbelievers may live as if the Day of Judgment and divine punishment are fables, but the angels who execute Allah's commands know otherwise. This verse also serves a subtle function in the narrative: it distinguishes Lot's reaction from that of the disbelievers. Lot believes the angels. He does not argue, does not mock, does not demand additional signs. He accepts the truth when it is presented to him. This is the mark of a prophet and a believer, immediate recognition of and submission to the truth, even when it brings news of a catastrophic upheaval. The angels' truthfulness is a guarantee that the believers will be saved and the wicked will be destroyed, a guarantee that will be fulfilled to the letter in the verses that follow.
65 | So travel with your family in the dark of night, and follow ˹closely˺ behind them. Do not let any of you look back, and go where you are commanded.” | The angels now give Lot precise instructions for his escape. The plan is detailed and urgent. First, they are to travel "in the dark of night," under the cover of darkness. This timing is for their safety, to avoid detection by the people of the city, and also because the destruction is scheduled for the early morning, as verse sixty-six will reveal. Night is the time when the city sleeps and when movement is least likely to be noticed. Second, Lot is instructed to "follow closely behind them." He is to be the last in the group, ensuring that everyone under his care proceeds ahead of him. This is the posture of a shepherd who watches over his flock, making sure that no one falls behind or turns back. It reflects Lot's pastoral responsibility and his love for his family. Third, the command is given: "Do not let any of you look back." The prohibition against looking back is not merely a practical instruction to keep moving forward. It carries a deep symbolic meaning. Looking back would indicate attachment to the place of sin, lingering doubt, or a desire for what was being left behind. The destruction that was about to befall Sodom was so catastrophic that even witnessing it could be harmful, and the angels wanted the believers to be fully oriented toward their destination, not toward the past. This same command was given to Lot's wife, and she disobeyed. She looked back, and she was consumed by the punishment. Fourth, they are to "go where you are commanded," a direction that would lead them to safety, away from the blast zone. The destination was specified by Allah and communicated through the angels. This verse is a masterclass in obedience. Salvation requires following divine instructions precisely, without modification, delay, or personal rationalization. The believers were saved because they did exactly as they were told, when they were told, in the manner they were told.
66 | We revealed to him this decree: “Those ˹sinners˺ will be uprooted in the morning.” | Alongside the practical instructions for escape, Allah also revealed to Lot the fate of the people he was leaving behind. The verse uses the word "revealed" translating qadayna, which means We decreed, We communicated as a definitive judgment. The decree is stark: the sinners will be uprooted in the morning. The word "uprooted" translates maqtu', which literally means cut off, severed at the root. The image is of a plant that is completely removed from the ground, leaving nothing behind. The people of Sodom, with all their numbers, their buildings, their wealth, and their power, would be cut off from the land of the living as if they had never existed. The phrase "in the morning" specifies the exact time of the destruction. The night was for the escape of the believers. The dawn would bring the end for the transgressors. This timing is precise and meaningful. Morning is the time when the world awakens to a new day, when people typically feel safe, when the darkness of night gives way to light. But for the people of Sodom, that particular morning would not bring light and safety. It would bring a blast that would end everything. The revelation of this decree to Lot served multiple purposes. It reinforced the urgency of the night escape. It gave Lot the knowledge that his long struggle was about to be resolved. And it was a mercy to him, confirming that the justice he had called for during years of patient preaching was now arriving. For the believers of Makkah and all later generations, this verse is a reminder that Allah's decrees are precise and unalterable. The destruction of a people is not a random event but a scheduled occurrence, timed to the exact hour according to divine wisdom. The morning came, and the decree was fulfilled.
67 | And there came the men of the city, rejoicing. | This verse describes the arrival of the mob at Lot's house, but with a profoundly disturbing detail: they came rejoicing. The Arabic word yastabshirun means they were delighted, exultant, celebrating in advance. They had heard about the handsome guests, and they were arriving not with shame or hesitation but with excitement and glee. This is the depth of their moral corruption. Sin had become so normalized, so celebrated, that the prospect of committing a heinous crime was a source of joy. They did not skulk in the shadows or feel any sense of wrongdoing. They rushed to Lot's door openly, as if to a festival. This detail is significant for understanding why the punishment was so severe. When a society reaches the point where it not only commits evil but celebrates it, when the collective conscience is so dead that atrocity is a cause for rejoicing, that society has passed the point of no return. The men of the city were not unaware of Lot's warnings. They had heard him preach, forbid their behavior, and call them to decency. But their response was not merely to ignore him. They actively sought to humiliate him and to violate the guests he was protecting. The rejoicing indicates that they saw this as an opportunity to assert their power and to mock the prophet in his own home. This verse also evokes the earlier verse of the surah where Allah said the disbelievers would enjoy themselves for a while and then come to know. The men of Sodom were rejoicing in their final moments of respite, unaware that the morning would bring their annihilation. The contrast is stark and intentional. Their joy is the prelude to their ruin. The verse serves as a powerful warning that moral complacency and the celebration of sin are signs of a society heading toward its doom.
68 | Lot pleaded, “Indeed, these are my guests, so do not embarrass me.” | As the mob clamored at his door, Lot stepped out to confront them with words of desperate appeal. He began by asserting his right to protect those under his roof: "Indeed, these are my guests." In the culture of the ancient Near East, the obligation to protect a guest was sacred. A host was expected to defend his visitors with his life if necessary. Lot invoked this principle, hoping that even these hardened criminals would respect the age-old custom of hospitality. The word "guests" translates dayf, which also means visitors, those who have placed themselves under one's protection. By emphasizing that they are his guests, Lot was making a moral claim on his people, appealing to whatever remnant of decency might still exist in their hearts. He then said, "so do not embarrass me." The word for embarrass is tafdahun, which means to disgrace, to shame, to bring scandal upon someone. Lot was not merely asking them to refrain from a crime. He was asking them not to bring shame upon him in his own home, not to violate his honor as a host. This shows that Lot framed his plea in terms that his society could understand. He appealed to their concepts of honor and shame, even though those concepts had been twisted and perverted. This verse reveals the painful position of a prophet who lives among a people whose moral compass is broken. Lot had to argue on their terms, using their language, while knowing that the deeper issue was their rebellion against Allah. His plea is both brave and tragic. He is one man standing against a mob, using words against brute force, honor against depravity. Yet, as the next verses will show, his words fell on deaf ears. The mob had come with a specific intention, and no appeal to hospitality, honor, or decency would deter them.
69 | Fear Allah and do not humiliate me.” | Lot intensifies his appeal by shifting from the social argument of honor to the religious argument of fearing Allah. He commands them to fear Allah, to have taqwa, to be conscious of the divine power that watches over all actions and will hold every soul accountable. This is the fundamental call of every prophet: to reconnect human beings with their Creator and to instill in them a sense of reverent awe that restrains them from evil. Lot had been delivering this message for years, and now, in this final confrontation, he repeats it one last time. He then adds a personal dimension: "and do not humiliate me." The word for humiliate is tukhzun, which means to disgrace, to bring low, to cause shame. Lot is speaking as a prophet who has been sent to these people, and their actions reflect on him. If they succeed in violating his guests, it would be a profound humiliation for Lot, a public demonstration that his message had no effect and that his authority as a prophet was disregarded. This personal appeal adds emotional weight to his plea. He is not just a preacher delivering an abstract sermon. He is a human being, standing in front of his own home, facing the imminent violation of his protected guests, and begging his people to spare him this degradation. The combination of the religious command "Fear Allah" and the personal plea "do not humiliate me" is a powerful rhetorical strategy. Lot is using every possible argument, from the highest spiritual principle to the most personal emotional appeal, to turn the mob away from their evil intent. Yet the mob's response, recorded in the next verse, will show that neither the fear of Allah nor the bonds of human decency held any sway over them. They had sunk below the reach of both divine warning and human compassion.
70 | They responded, “Have we not forbidden you from protecting anyone?” | The mob's answer to Lot's desperate pleas is chilling in its arrogance. They do not engage with his arguments. They do not deny that his guests deserve protection. Instead, they throw back at him their own previous threats. They remind him that they had already forbidden him from protecting anyone, from offering shelter, from interfering with their desires. The word "protecting" translates from the root meaning to shelter, to guard, to give refuge. The people of Sodom had established a society in which the fundamental human act of offering protection to a vulnerable stranger was outlawed. They had inverted the moral order so completely that hospitality was a crime and predation was the norm. Lot had presumably been warned before, perhaps many times, that he must not interfere with their activities. They had told him to stop his preaching, to stop his moralizing, and above all to stop providing refuge to travelers. Now they were reminding him of that prohibition, with the clear implication that if he persisted in protecting these guests, he would face the consequences. This verse reveals the psychology of a society in the final stages of moral collapse. It is not enough for the corrupt to practice their corruption. They must also silence and punish anyone who dares to stand against them. Lot's righteousness was an offense to them. His very existence as a man who said "no" to their evil was a rebuke, and they sought to eliminate that rebuke by forbidding him from acting on his conscience. This dynamic repeats throughout history. When evil becomes dominant, it does not tolerate goodness. It seeks to make virtue illegal and vice mandatory. The people of Sodom are the archetype of this tyrannical inversion of values, and their response to Lot is the classic argument of the oppressor: we have forbidden you from doing good, so comply or suffer.
71 | He said, “O my people! Here are my daughters ˹so marry them˺ if you wish to do so.” | In a final attempt to divert the mob from their evil intent, Lot offers an alternative that appeals to the natural and lawful order. He points them toward his daughters, meaning the women of his community or his own daughters as some commentators explain, and suggests that they marry them instead of pursuing their illicit desires. The phrase "if you wish to do so" indicates that Lot was presenting a lawful outlet for their desires, the institution of marriage between men and women as ordained by Allah. This was not a casual offer but a prophetic plea pointing to the path of purity. Lot's offer has been the subject of much discussion in tafsir. By "my daughters," Lot was referring to the women of his nation, as a prophet is a father to his people, or to his own daughters, whom he offered in lawful marriage as a means of reforming their behavior. This was not an offer to commit sin but an invitation to the natural, divinely sanctioned relationship. The mob, however, had no interest in marriage. Their depravity had gone so far that what is lawful held no appeal for them. They wanted what was forbidden. This is a common feature of unchecked lust. The lawful no longer satisfies because the thrill of transgression has become an addiction. Lot's offer was an act of desperate love for his people. He wanted to save them from the punishment he knew was coming. He was willing to bind his own family to them in marriage if that would turn them from their path. But the mob's refusal, implicit in what follows, shows that they were beyond rehabilitation. This verse also demonstrates that the messengers of Allah exhaust every possible means of reform before punishment descends. Lot did not give up on his people even when they were at his door demanding his guests. He sought a last-minute opening, a final chance for them to choose the lawful over the forbidden. Their rejection of even this sealed their fate.
72 | By your life ˹O Prophet˺, they certainly wandered blindly, intoxicated ˹by lust˺. | This verse contains a unique and powerful oath. Allah swears by the life of the Prophet Muhammad, a distinction not given to any other messenger in the Quran. The phrase "By your life" is la'amruka, an oath by the Prophet's life and duration of his existence. This is an extraordinary honor, indicating the elevated status of the Prophet in the sight of Allah. Allah could swear by any of His creation, but choosing to swear by the life of Muhammad is a declaration of his immense worth and the special position he holds. The oath is made to emphasize the truth of what follows: that the people of Lot were wandering blindly, intoxicated by their lust. The word "wandered blindly" translates la-fi sakratihim ya'mahun. Sakrah means drunkenness or intoxication, but here it refers to the stupor of unrestrained desire, a state in which reason and moral judgment are completely suspended. The word ya'mahun means they were wandering, stumbling, moving without direction or purpose. This is a precise psychological description of what happens when a society gives itself over to lust. Reason becomes clouded. The ability to distinguish right from wrong fades. People stumble through life chasing desires that never satisfy, losing all sense of higher purpose. The oath by the Prophet's life also serves to console him. In the midst of the rejection he faced in Makkah, Allah swears by his very existence, affirming his truthfulness and the certainty of the divine narratives he conveys. The story of Lot is not a fable. It is a historical reality, as true as the life of the Prophet himself. The verse also serves as a warning to the Makkans and to all who would follow the path of Sodom. Unchecked lust leads to a blindness that makes destruction seem impossible until it arrives. The people of Sodom were staggering in their intoxication, unable to see the divine judgment that was already at their doorstep.
73 | So the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them at sunrise. | The consequence of the people's moral blindness arrives with devastating precision. The word "blast" translates sayhah, which means a mighty shout, a thunderous sound, an explosive cry. This was the divine punishment that came suddenly and overwhelmingly. Some commentators say it was the terrifying scream of the angel Gabriel, a sound so powerful that it shattered their hearts and leveled their structures. The timing is specified as sunrise, the moment when light begins to spread across the land. The people who had spent the night in their intoxication, who had rejoiced at the prospect of violating Lot's guests, were now facing the dawn of their annihilation. The choice of sunrise is deeply symbolic. Light normally represents hope, guidance, and a new beginning. For the people of Sodom, the sunrise brought none of these. It brought the blinding light of divine wrath. The punishment began at the time when they might have expected the day to proceed as usual. This sudden reversal is a common feature of Quranic narratives of destruction. The punishment comes when the disbelievers feel most secure, when they least expect it, when they are going about their ordinary business. The blast that overtook them did not give them time to flee, to repent, or even to understand what was happening. It was swift, final, and absolute. This verse brings the narrative of Lot's people to its climax. The long period of warning, the years of patient preaching, the final night of escape for the believers, all led to this single moment at sunrise when the city and its inhabitants were wiped from the earth. The verse is a stark reminder that divine punishment, when it comes, is not a gradual process but a decisive act that leaves no room for second chances. The morning that the sinners had greeted with rejoicing became the morning of their utter ruin.
74 | And We turned the cities ˹of Sodom and Gomorrah˺ upside down and rained upon them stones of baked clay. | The destruction of Lot's people is described in even more graphic detail in this verse. Not only were they struck by a blast, but their entire physical environment was transformed into an instrument of their punishment. Allah turned the cities upside down. The phrase "turned upside down" indicates a complete geological inversion. What was high became low, and what was low became high. The region where these cities once stood, near the Dead Sea, is characterized by unusual geological features consistent with a massive upheaval. The destruction was not a random earthquake but a deliberate act of divine justice that literally flipped the landscape. In addition to the inversion, Allah "rained upon them stones of baked clay." The stones are described as being of sijjil, baked clay or stones marked with specific designations. These stones fell from the sky like a deadly hail, targeting the criminals individually even as their cities were being overturned. The combination of these two forms of punishment, from below and from above, ensured that no one would survive. The earth rose against them, and the sky fell upon them. The four elements of nature, which normally sustain human life, became the agents of their destruction. The stones of baked clay are particularly significant. The people of Sodom had prided themselves on their sophistication, their urban civilization, their material achievements. They were destroyed by something as simple as hardened clay, a material that is formed from earth and fire, reminiscent of the very elements of creation that Allah mentioned in the verses about the creation of humanity and jinn. This is a profound demonstration that Allah can use any means to execute His will. The most advanced civilization is helpless against the simplest elements when Allah commands those elements to serve as soldiers of His justice.
75 | Surely in this are signs for those who contemplate. | After recounting the destruction, Allah draws the listener's attention to the lessons embedded in the narrative. The word "signs" translates ayat, the same word used to describe the verses of the Quran itself. The historical event and the Quranic account of it are both signs, pointing toward deeper truths about Allah, about human nature, and about the consequences of moral choices. The phrase "for those who contemplate" translates lil-mutawassimin, which is a particularly rich Arabic term. It comes from the root meaning to observe carefully, to scrutinize, to read the signs and draw conclusions. It refers to people who do not merely glance at events but study them, probe their meaning, and allow the lessons to penetrate their hearts. This verse is a call to intelligent reflection. The ruins of Sodom and Gomorrah are not merely archaeological curiosities. They are messages from the past, reminders written in stone and ash of what happens when a society defies its Creator and normalizes abomination. Those who look at such sites with a contemplative eye will see not just ruins but warnings. The verse implies that the signs are there, but they benefit only those who have cultivated the capacity to read them. A person who approaches the world with a closed heart or a dismissive attitude will see nothing but rocks and dirt. The person who approaches with faith and reflection will see the fingerprints of divine justice. This principle applies to all of the destroyed nations mentioned in the Quran. Their stories are preserved not for entertainment but for edification. Every believer is called to be among the mutawassimin, those who contemplate and draw the right conclusions. The sign is present. The question is whether we have the eyes to see it and the wisdom to apply its lesson to our own lives and societies.
76 | Their ruins still lie along a known route. | This verse adds a specific historical and geographical detail to reinforce the reality of the narrative. The ruins of Lot's people were not lost to history or buried in some inaccessible location. They lay along a "known route," a path that the Arabs of the Prophet's time traveled regularly. The trade caravans of the Quraysh passed through the region of the Dead Sea on their journeys to Syria. They could see with their own eyes the desolate landscape, the strange rock formations, and the evidence of a once-thriving civilization that had been obliterated. The ruins were not hidden. They were visible, accessible, and unmistakable. This detail serves as a powerful argument against those who dismissed the Prophet's warnings as myths or fables. Allah is essentially saying: go and look for yourselves. The evidence is along the road you travel twice a year. You do not need to undertake an archaeological expedition. The ruins are right there, in plain sight, along the well-known route that your grandfathers and great-grandfathers used. This appeal to empirical observation is consistent with the Quran's method of engaging the human intellect. Faith is not a blind leap. It is supported by evidence that can be seen, examined, and verified. The ruins of Sodom and Gomorrah are a standing testimony that the Quran's account is true. The verse also carries a lesson about the impermanence of worldly power. The people of Lot were prosperous, settled, and secure. Their cities were major urban centers. Today, those cities are a wasteland, a cautionary tale for travelers who pass by and wonder what happened. The known route that once facilitated their trade now facilitates the contemplation of their ruin. This reversal of fortune is a recurring pattern, and those who travel the roads of the world with open eyes can see similar lessons everywhere. The verse is an invitation to learn from history, to recognize that the fate of past nations is not random but the operation of unchanging divine laws.
77 | Surely in this is a sign for those who believe. | This verse echoes verse seventy-five but with a subtle and important difference. Whereas the previous verse said the signs are for "those who contemplate," this verse says the sign is for "those who believe," al-mu'minin. The two descriptions are complementary. Contemplation is the process, and belief is the outcome. Those who contemplate the ruins with sincerity and intellectual honesty will arrive at belief. The sign is present for all to see, but only those who have the quality of iman, of faith, will fully benefit from it. For the believers, the destruction of Lot's people is not just an ancient story. It is a confirmation of everything the Quran teaches about Allah's justice, about the reality of prophethood, and about the consequences of rejecting divine guidance. It strengthens their faith because it shows that the warnings they believe in are not abstract theories but verified historical facts. The verse also makes a distinction between different levels of engagement with the signs. The contemplative person sees the sign and begins to think differently. The believer sees the sign and feels their faith deepen and their resolve strengthen. The ultimate goal of the Quranic narratives is to produce and nurture iman, and this verse explicitly states that the story of Lot is a sign that serves exactly that purpose. For the believers in Makkah who were suffering under the persecution of the Quraysh, this verse was a source of immense comfort. It reminded them that the forces of evil, no matter how dominant they appear, have an expiration date. The people of Lot seemed invincible, their society seemed permanent, their rejection of the prophet seemed secure. But the morning came, and they were no more. The believers who trust in Allah and follow His guidance will be saved, just as Lot and his family were saved. The sign is for them, to reassure their hearts and to anchor their faith in the certainty of divine justice.
78 | And the residents of the Forest were truly wrongdoers, | The surah now moves to another example of a destroyed people, the residents of the Forest. The Arabic word is al-Aykah, which refers to a thicket, a dense cluster of trees, a forested area. These were the people of the prophet Shu'ayb, who is mentioned in several surahs. They lived in the region of Madyan, near the Gulf of Aqaba, in a fertile and well-watered area that supported extensive vegetation, hence the name. They were known for their commercial activities and their dishonest business practices, particularly their habit of giving short measure and cheating their customers in trade. The verse describes them as "truly wrongdoers," emphasizing the depth and persistence of their injustice. The word for wrongdoers is zalimun, which comes from the root meaning to put something in the wrong place, to transgress, to violate rights. This description encompasses all their crimes: economic exploitation, rejection of their prophet, and worship of false gods. The story of Shu'ayb is recounted in detail in Surah Ash-Shu'ara and other surahs. He called his people to worship Allah alone and to deal justly in their business transactions. He warned them not to shortchange people, not to spread corruption, and not to threaten those who believed. They responded with mockery and arrogance, telling Shu'ayb that if not for his family's status they would have stoned him. They challenged him to bring down the punishment he was warning about. This verse condenses their entire history into a single identifying phrase. They were the people of the forested region, and their defining characteristic was wrongdoing. The mention of them here, immediately after the story of Lot, reinforces the pattern of surahs like this one that recount multiple destroyed nations in rapid succession. Each people had its prophet, its specific sins, and its specific form of destruction, but the underlying pattern is the same: rejection of divine guidance leads to ruin.
79 | so We inflicted punishment upon them. The ruins of both nations still lie on a well-known road. | Allah states that He inflicted punishment upon the people of the Forest, just as He did upon the people of Lot. The specific form of punishment given to Shu'ayb's people is described elsewhere in the Quran. They were seized by a terrible earthquake or a blast that left them lying dead in their homes, faces down. Some reports also mention a cloud of fire that rained down upon them after they had experienced an intense heat wave. The punishment was sudden, comprehensive, and final, leaving their thriving commercial centers in ruins. The verse then draws an interesting geographical parallel. Just as the ruins of Lot's people lie along a known route, so do the ruins of both nations. The phrase "both nations" refers to the people of Lot and the people of Shu'ayb. Both sets of ruins were visible to the Arab travelers of the time. The route to Syria passed by the Dead Sea region where Sodom and Gomorrah once stood. The route to Yemen or within the Hijaz passed through or near the old territories of Madyan. The Quraysh, in their winter and summer journeys, would have been familiar with both sites. This geographical detail is a powerful argument from divine mercy. Allah did not only send prophets with warnings. He left behind physical evidence of the truth of those warnings. The ruins are like open books, written not in words but in stones and sand, telling the story of what happens when human arrogance meets divine justice. The "well-known road" indicates that these are not remote or hidden locations that require special effort to find. They are along the paths that people travel regularly. The travelers can see them, reflect on them, and draw the correct conclusions, if they have the will to do so. This accessibility of the evidence removes any excuse. No one can claim that the warnings were delivered in secret or that the evidence of past punishments was unavailable. The ruins are there, on the well-known road, a silent but eloquent testimony to the truth of the Quranic message.
80 | Indeed, the residents of the Stone Valley also denied the messengers. | The surah now introduces the third and final example in this sequence: the residents of the Stone Valley, known as the people of al-Hijr. This is the community from which the surah takes its name. They were the people of Thamud, the nation to whom the prophet Salih was sent. They lived in a region of northwestern Arabia characterized by massive rock formations, and they were famous for their skill in carving elaborate homes and palaces directly into the mountainsides. The phrase "denied the messengers" indicates their rejection of Salih, and by extension the entire concept of prophethood and divine message. The denial of one messenger is equivalent to the denial of all, because the core message is the same. The people of Thamud were successors to the civilization of Aad, and they inherited great power, wealth, and technological skill. Their arrogance led them to believe they were invincible. They demanded that Salih bring them a miraculous sign, and Allah sent them a she-camel as a test. They were commanded to allow the camel to drink from the well on specific days and to share the water. Instead, they hamstrung and killed the camel, openly defying the prophet and challenging Allah to bring the punishment they had been warned about. The term "Stone Valley" is a direct reference to the physical environment they inhabited and the very mountains they carved with such pride. Their skill in stonework gave them a false sense of security. They thought that their carved homes, their fortresses in the rock, would protect them from any calamity. The following verses will describe their destruction and the futility of their defenses. By beginning with "Indeed," the verse signals that this is an established fact, a well-known piece of history that the Arabs were familiar with. The ruins of Thamud were located along the trade route to Syria, and the Quraysh passed by them regularly. This verse sets up the final historical example in the surah, which will be developed in the next verses and which serves to drive home the warning to the Makkan disbelievers that their own rejection of the messenger places them in the same category as those who came before and who were destroyed.
81 | We gave them Our signs, but they turned away from them. | The people of Thamud were not left without guidance. Allah gave them His signs, clear and unmistakable proofs of the truth of Salih's prophethood. The most famous of these signs was the she-camel, a miraculous camel that emerged from the rock as a direct response to their demand for proof. This was not an ordinary camel. It was enormous, unique, and clearly supernatural in origin. The sign was meant to test their obedience: they were instructed to allow it to drink freely on alternate days and to share the water with it. The presence of this camel among them was a living miracle, a daily reminder of Allah's power and of the truth of the message. Yet despite the clarity of the sign, they turned away from it. The phrase "turned away" translates a'radu, which means they deliberately turned their backs, they averted their faces, they refused to engage. This was not passive neglect but active rejection. They saw the sign, acknowledged it for what it was, and then chose to defy it. The ultimate expression of their turning away was the hamstringing of the camel, a brutal act of defiance in which they killed the very sign that had been sent as a mercy and a test. This pattern of receiving signs and then rejecting them is the common thread that runs through all the destroyed nations mentioned in the surah. The people of Noah had signs but mocked. The people of Lot had a prophet living among them but preferred their desires. The people of Shu'ayb had clear warnings but continued their fraud. And the people of Thamud had the she-camel but slaughtered it. In every case, the problem was not lack of evidence. The problem was a willful decision to ignore what the evidence pointed toward. For the Quraysh hearing this, the implication was unmistakable. They too had been given signs, the Quran itself being the greatest among them. The question was whether they would follow the path of those who turned away and were destroyed or the path of those who accepted and were saved. The verse is a warning that the mere presence of signs does not guarantee salvation. What matters is the response.
82 | They carved their homes in the mountains, feeling secure. | The people of Thamud are described as having carved their homes directly into the mountains. This was a remarkable feat of engineering and craftsmanship. The rocky cliffs of their homeland were transformed into elaborate dwellings, palaces, and halls. The Quran mentions this skill in several surahs, noting that they hewed out the mountains with skill and pride. The phrase "feeling secure" translates farihin, which carries the sense of exultant, rejoicing, delighting in their own achievement. They felt invincible within their rock-hewn fortresses. The mountains that surrounded them seemed to be their ultimate protection. They had carved their homes deep into the stone, and they believed that nothing could touch them there. No flood could sweep them away, no army could easily breach their defenses, and no force of nature, they imagined, could harm a people so deeply embedded in the earth's own bones. This false sense of security is a recurring human folly. Material achievements create an illusion of invulnerability. The people of Thamud looked at their carved homes and saw strength, permanence, and mastery over nature. They did not see that the same mountains that provided their shelter were creations of Allah and could become instruments of His punishment if He willed. Their security was an illusion, but it was a powerful one. It allowed them to dismiss Salih's warnings with contempt. What could a prophet do to them when they were protected by the very mountains? This attitude is mirrored in every age. Civilizations that achieve great technological and architectural feats often develop a sense of self-sufficiency that blinds them to their dependence on the Creator. The skyscrapers of modern cities are the carved mountains of our time, and the same false security can take root in the hearts of those who build and inhabit them. The verse exposes the psychological trap of equating material achievement with existential safety. The people of Thamud felt secure, but their security was the calm before the storm.
83 | But the ˹mighty˺ blast overtook them in the morning, | The illusion of security was shattered with devastating suddenness. The blast, the sayhah, the same word used for the destruction of Lot's people, came upon them in the morning. The timing is again specified. The morning is the time of awakening, the beginning of a new day, the moment when people rise with a sense of hope and expectation. For the people of Thamud, that particular morning brought not hope but annihilation. The blast was mighty, an overwhelming sound that rent the air and shook the mountains they had so proudly carved. Some narrations describe it as a terrifying thunderclap or a shriek that caused their hearts to burst within their chests. The punishment was not a gradual process. It was a single, instantaneous event that left no time for escape or regret. The morning that they had greeted so many times with thoughts of their daily affairs became the morning of their end. The contrast between their carved homes and the blast is significant. Their homes were their pride, their protection, their achievement. But the blast did not need to break down their doors or scale their walls. It came as sound, penetrating every crevice, reaching every inhabitant simultaneously. The mountain fortresses were useless against a punishment that came from the sky. This demonstrates the utter helplessness of human constructions before divine power. No wall, no bunker, no technological shield can protect against the decree of Allah when it arrives. The verse also echoes the destruction of Lot's people, who were also overtaken by a blast at sunrise. The symmetry is deliberate. Both nations felt secure, both mocked their prophets, both faced a sudden blast, and both were reduced to ruins. The morning light, which reveals and illuminates, became for them the light of their extinction. For the believer, this is a reminder that the time of reckoning can come at any moment, and the only true security is in Allah.
84 | and all they achieved was of no help to them. | The final verdict on the people of Thamud is pronounced in this verse. Everything they had achieved, their wealth, their technology, their carved palaces, their military power, their social organization, proved to be of absolutely no avail when the punishment came. The phrase "all they achieved" translates ma kanu yaksibun, which means what they used to earn, acquire, and accomplish. This encompasses their entire civilization, everything they had built and gathered over generations. The word for "no help" is yughni, meaning to suffice, to avail, to provide protection or benefit. Their achievements availed them nothing. The elaborate homes became their tombs. The wealth they had accumulated remained buried with them. The power they had wielded evaporated in a single morning. This is one of the most sobering verses in the Quran for anyone who places their trust in worldly accomplishments. The people of Thamud were not primitive. They were advanced, skilled, and prosperous. They had mastered the art of architecture to a degree that impressed later generations. Yet none of it mattered when the divine decree arrived. The lesson is not that material achievement is bad in itself. It is that material achievement, when it becomes a source of pride and a substitute for faith in Allah, is a false refuge. The only thing that truly helps in the moment of ultimate need is a sound relationship with the Creator. The verse also serves to demolish the argument that worldly success is a sign of divine favor. The people of Thamud were successful by every material measure, and yet they were destroyed because of their rebellion. Conversely, Salih and the believers with him were a small, marginalized group with no impressive achievements to their name, and they were saved. The criteria of success in Allah's sight are not the same as the criteria of success in the eyes of the world. This verse calls the believer to evaluate life from the perspective of eternity, recognizing that the achievements that truly avail are those done with faith and for the sake of Allah.
85 | We have not created the heavens and the earth and everything in between except for a purpose. And the Hour is certain to come, so forgive graciously. | After the succession of historical examples, the surah turns to a sweeping declaration about the meaning of creation and the attitude the Prophet should adopt. Allah states that He did not create the heavens, the earth, and all that exists between them except with truth, with a purpose, for a reason. The word "purpose" translates bil-haqq, which means with truth, in justice, for a serious and meaningful end. The universe is not a product of random chance or meaningless chaos. It is a deliberate creation, designed with wisdom, and it is moving toward a definite conclusion. This affirmation directly addresses the worldview of the disbelievers who lived as if life had no ultimate purpose and no final accountability. The second part of the verse states that the Hour, the Day of Resurrection, is certainly coming. There is no doubt about its arrival. The certainty of the Hour is the logical consequence of the purposeful creation of the universe. A universe created for a purpose must have a final resolution, a day when the moral accounts are settled and the purpose is fully realized. Then comes a sudden and beautiful shift: "so forgive graciously." The Prophet is instructed to forgive. The word "graciously" translates al-safh al-jamil, which means a beautiful overlooking, a forgiveness that is not grudging or accompanied by reminders of the offense, but generous, complete, and dignified. This is a remarkable instruction. After recounting the destruction of nations who mocked their prophets, after affirming the certainty of judgment, Allah tells the Prophet to adopt a posture of gracious forgiveness toward his opponents. This is not a contradiction. The punishment of the disbelievers is in Allah's hands and will occur in His time. The Prophet's role is to convey the message with patience and to respond to personal attacks with forgiveness. This combination of firm belief in divine justice and personal magnanimity is the hallmark of prophetic character.
86 | Surely your Lord is the Master Creator, All-Knowing. | This verse provides the foundation for the instruction to forgive that was given in the previous verse. The Prophet can forgive graciously because his Lord is the Master Creator, the One who originates and brings into being, and the All-Knowing, the One whose knowledge encompasses everything. The word "Master Creator" translates al-Khallaq, an intensive form meaning the One who creates repeatedly, expertly, and in abundance. Every human being, including every enemy of the Prophet, is a creation of Allah. The Prophet's opponents were not outside Allah's creative power or knowledge. The One who created them knows their intentions, their actions, and the exact justice they deserve. Because Allah is the Creator of all, and because He knows all, the Prophet does not need to take personal revenge or to be consumed by anger against those who mock him. Their case is known to Allah, and their judgment is in His hands. The verse also implies that the Prophet's role as a messenger is part of Allah's creative plan. He was chosen, prepared, and sent by the same Master Creator who made the mountains the people of Thamud carved, the heavens adorned with constellations, and the earth spread out for habitation. This connection between creation and prophethood reinforces the unity of the Quranic worldview. The same Allah who creates is the Allah who guides, judges, and forgives. For the believer, this verse is a source of immense comfort. When one is wronged, the natural human impulse is to seek revenge or to dwell on the injustice. But the knowledge that the Creator of all things is fully aware of what happened, and that His judgment is perfect, liberates the heart from the burden of resentment. Forgiveness becomes possible not because the wrong was insignificant but because the case has been entrusted to the most competent Judge.
87 | We have certainly granted you the seven often-repeated verses and the great Quran. | This verse contains a direct address to the Prophet Muhammad, announcing a gift of immense magnitude. Allah declares that He has granted him the "seven often-repeated verses" and the "great Quran." The seven often-repeated verses are universally understood by the companions and the majority of scholars to refer to Surah Al-Fatihah, the Opening, which consists of seven verses and is repeated by every Muslim in every unit of prayer multiple times a day. It is the most recited chapter of the Quran, the essential prayer without which the ritual prayer is incomplete. Al-Fatihah is a direct conversation between the servant and the Lord, a praise of Allah, a declaration of exclusive worship, a plea for guidance, and a summary of the entire religious worldview. To grant the Prophet this surah is to grant him and his followers a gift that renews itself with every recitation. Then Allah adds "and the great Quran," indicating that the entire Quran, in all its majesty and completeness, has been granted to the Prophet. The adjective "great" translates 'azim, meaning magnificent, tremendous, of immense weight and significance. The Quran is not merely a book. It is a revelation that dwarfs all other gifts. The pairing of the seven often-repeated verses with the great Quran is a way of highlighting both the part and the whole. Al-Fatihah is the essence, and the Quran is the full elaboration. This verse was a powerful consolation to the Prophet in the face of the mockery and rejection he was experiencing. The disbelievers might have offered him wealth, status, or power to abandon his mission, as the Quraysh once proposed. But what Allah had given him was greater than anything the world could offer. The seven oft-repeated verses and the great Quran are gifts that connect the Prophet to Allah, that guide humanity, and that will remain until the end of time. No worldly treasure can compare.
88 | Do not let your eyes crave the ˹fleeting˺ pleasures We have provided for some of the disbelievers, nor grieve for them. And be gracious to the believers. | After the magnificent declaration of what Allah has granted the Prophet, comes an instruction about how to relate to the world and its people. The Prophet is told not to stretch his eyes toward the fleeting pleasures that Allah has given to some of the disbelievers. The phrase "do not let your eyes crave" translates la tamuddanna 'aynayk, which means do not look longingly, do not gaze with desire at what others have been given. The "fleeting pleasures" are the enjoyments of this world: wealth, status, luxury, power. The disbelievers of Makkah had these in abundance. The Prophet and his followers, by contrast, lived in simplicity and often in hardship. The instruction is a reminder that what Allah has given to the disbelievers is temporary, a brief enjoyment that will end with their death and be followed by an eternity of regret. What Allah has given to the Prophet, the Quran and the spiritual station of prophethood, is of infinitely greater value and permanent duration. The Prophet is also told not to grieve for the disbelievers. The word "grieve" translates tahzan, meaning a deep and persistent sorrow. The Prophet felt a profound sadness at the refusal of his people to believe. He loved them and wanted their salvation. This instruction does not mean he should stop caring but that he should not allow their rejection to consume him with grief. Their fate is their own choice, and he has fulfilled his duty by delivering the message. Then comes a positive instruction: "be gracious to the believers." The word "gracious" is khafid janahaka, literally lower your wing, an image of a bird lowering its wing to shelter and protect its young. The Prophet is to be gentle, humble, and compassionate toward those who have believed. He is to extend to the believers the very mercy and care that he is instructed to embody. This verse outlines a complete emotional and social posture for the Prophet and, by extension, for all believers: detachment from the allure of worldly possessions, release from the grief of rejection, and active, tender care for the community of faith.
89 | And say, “I am truly sent with a clear warning”— | The Prophet is instructed to make a straightforward declaration of his mission. He is to say that he is a warner, sent by Allah with a clear and unambiguous warning. The word "truly" emphasizes the certainty and reality of this claim. He is not a poet, a soothsayer, or a madman as his opponents alleged. He is a legitimate warner in the tradition of all the warners who came before him. The "clear warning" translates nadhirun mubin, meaning a warner whose warning is evident, free from ambiguity, and supported by proofs. The clarity of the warning refers to the content of the message itself. The Quran does not present a confusing or esoteric doctrine. It states plainly that there is one God, that He created humanity for worship, that this life is a test, that death is not the end, and that judgment and an eternal afterlife are certain. The Prophet's role is to deliver this warning with clarity so that no one can claim ignorance. This verse also connects back to the very beginning of the surah, which described the Quran as a clear Book. The Prophet's mission and the Book he carries share the same quality of unmistakable clarity. The declaration "I am truly sent with a clear warning" is both a statement of identity and a line of demarcation. It separates the Prophet from his opponents and places him squarely in the line of the messengers whose stories were just recounted. The warning is not a threat born of personal anger but a message of mercy, because to be warned is to be given a chance to change course before it is too late. The Prophet announces his role so that those who hear can respond appropriately, either by heeding the warning and being saved or by rejecting it and bearing the consequences.
90 | ˹a warning˺ similar to what We sent to those who divided ˹the Scriptures˺, | The warning that the Prophet is delivering is likened to the warning that Allah previously sent to those who divided the Scriptures. The phrase "those who divided" refers to the People of the Book, the Jews and Christians, who received divine revelations but then fragmented them. They divided the Scriptures in multiple ways. Some accepted parts and rejected others, believing in some prophets and disbelieving in others, adopting some laws and discarding others. Some divided themselves into sects, each claiming exclusive possession of the truth while condemning the others. Some divided the text itself, interpreting it in conflicting ways to suit their desires and interests. The comparison is significant for the Makkan context. The Quraysh were now receiving a Scripture, the Quran, and they were in danger of repeating the mistake of the earlier communities. Some of them accepted the moral teachings they liked but rejected the doctrine of resurrection. Some acknowledged the eloquence of the Quran but refused to submit to its authority. Some divided the Quran into fragments, calling it poetry, magic, or ancient fables. By comparing the Prophet's warning to the warning sent to the dividers of the previous Scriptures, Allah is cautioning the new recipients of revelation not to follow the same path of fragmentation and selective acceptance. The verse also serves as a unifying theme across the Abrahamic faiths. The same essential warning was delivered to all, but human beings have a tendency to divide and distort what was revealed as a unified truth. The Prophet is sent to restore that unity and to deliver the warning in its final and most complete form. Those who respond by dividing it will meet the same fate as those who came before.
91 | who ˹now˺ accept parts of the Quran, rejecting others. | This verse identifies a specific group among the contemporaries of the Prophet who were mirroring the behavior of those who divided the previous Scriptures. Some people were adopting a selective approach to the Quran, accepting parts that suited their inclinations and rejecting others. The Makkan polytheists might have appreciated the Quran's eloquence, its moral exhortations, and its social teachings, but they rejected its core doctrines of monotheism and resurrection. Some Jews and Christians in the region acknowledged the truth of certain passages but denied the prophethood of Muhammad altogether. This selective acceptance is not true faith. True faith requires accepting the entirety of what Allah has revealed, without picking and choosing. The Quran condemns this attitude elsewhere, asking whether people believe in part of the Scripture and disbelieve in part, and stating that the recompense for such behavior is disgrace in this world and severe punishment in the Hereafter. The psychology behind selective acceptance is the desire to make religion conform to one's own preferences rather than to submit one's preferences to religion. It is a form of intellectual and spiritual arrogance, a refusal to surrender fully to the divine authority. The verse warns that those who adopt this approach are essentially following the path of the previous nations who divided their Scriptures and were destroyed. They may think they are being sophisticated or moderate, but they are in fact drifting toward the same ruin. For the believer, the verse is a call to holistic acceptance of the Quran. One must embrace its entire message, even the parts that challenge personal inclinations, cultural norms, or societal pressures. The Quran is an indivisible whole, and the attempt to divide it is a sign of a diseased heart. The clear warning of the Prophet is directed at those who are tempted to divide, and it calls them to a complete and undivided submission.
92 | So by your Lord! We will certainly question them all | The surah now reaches a powerful climax of warning. Allah swears by His Lordship over the Prophet, "by your Lord," an oath of immense gravity. The use of the intimate "your Lord" addresses the Prophet directly and emphasizes the special relationship between Allah and His messenger. By swearing this oath, Allah affirms with absolute certainty that He will question all of them, every single one of those who divided the Scriptures and those who accept parts of the Quran and reject others. The questioning will encompass everything they did, every belief they held, every word they spoke, and every choice they made. No one will escape the interrogation. The rich and the poor, the leaders and the followers, the mockers and the silent accepters, all will stand before their Lord and give an account. The oath serves to remove any doubt about the reality of the coming judgment. The Makkans who mocked the Prophet and divided the Quran might have thought they could escape accountability through death. This verse assures them that death is not an escape but a transition to a realm where questioning is certain. The swearing by Allah's Lordship is particularly appropriate because the role of the Lord includes judgment. The One who creates, sustains, and gives laws has the right to hold His servants accountable for how they used the gifts they were given. The verse is both a warning to the disbelievers and a comfort to the Prophet and the believers. The injustices they suffered, the mockery they endured, and the rejection they faced will all be brought to account. No wrong will be overlooked, and no oppressor will go unquestioned.
93 | about what they used to do. | The subject of the divine questioning is specified in this brief but comprehensive verse. They will be questioned "about what they used to do." The phrase is deceptively simple but all-encompassing. Every action, every word, every intention, every secret thought, every public declaration, every deed done in the darkness of night and in the light of day falls under "what they used to do." The scope of the questioning includes their mockery of the Prophet, their division of the Quran, their worship of idols, their oppression of the believers, their rejection of the truth, and their leading of others astray. But it also extends to all the ordinary moments of their lives, the opportunities they had to do good, the time they wasted, the blessings they took for granted, and the choices they made when the truth was presented to them. The verse implies that human beings will not be questioned only about major dramatic events but about the totality of their conduct. The habitual nature of "what they used to do" indicates that patterns of behavior, the routines and habits that define a life, are precisely what will be scrutinized. A single isolated sin is different from a lifelong habit of sin. The consistent rejection of truth, the persistent mockery, the ingrained arrogance, these patterns are what seal a person's fate. The verse also serves as a reminder to the believers to be conscious of their daily actions. Every moment is being recorded, and every deed contributes to the record that will be presented on the Day of Judgment. The questioning will not be a mere formality. It will be a detailed, precise, and unescapable examination of a life lived. The only way to prepare for that questioning is to live with constant awareness that every action is significant and will one day be brought to light.
94 | So proclaim what you have been commanded, and turn away from the polytheists. | After the powerful oaths and warnings, the surah returns to direct instruction for the Prophet. He is commanded to proclaim openly and boldly what he has been ordered to proclaim. The word "proclaim" translates isda', which literally means to split open, to declare loudly and clearly, to make a decisive statement that cuts through the noise. The message is not to be whispered or kept hidden. It is to be proclaimed publicly, regardless of the consequences. The command to proclaim is coupled with the command to "turn away from the polytheists." This turning away is not a physical abandonment of the duty of conveying the message. It is an emotional and psychological detachment from their mockery and rejection. The Prophet is to deliver the message with clarity and then leave the results to Allah, not allowing the rejection of the polytheists to deter him or consume him. This dual instruction captures the balance that the surah has been teaching throughout: firm proclamation combined with personal detachment. The truth must be declared, but the hearts of the recipients are in Allah's hands. The Prophet's responsibility is to deliver, not to convert. By turning away from the polytheists, he protects his own soul from the corrosive effects of constant rejection and frees himself to focus on those who are receptive. The verse also marks a transitional moment in the Meccan period. The early phase of quiet, private teaching was giving way to a phase of open, public proclamation. The command to "proclaim what you have been commanded" is a signal that the time for discretion has passed and the time for bold confrontation of falsehood has arrived. This required immense courage, and the following verse provides the assurance that Allah will be sufficient for the Prophet in the face of opposition.
95 | Surely We will be sufficient for you against the mockers, | Allah now gives the Prophet a direct promise of protection and sufficiency. The word "sufficient" translates kafaynaka, meaning We will take care of you, We will defend you, We will be enough for you against whatever the mockers plan. The mockers were specific individuals among the Quraysh leadership who made it their mission to ridicule the Prophet, to discredit his message through satire and insult, and to turn public opinion against him. They were influential, wealthy, and persistent. Their mockery was a form of psychological warfare designed to break the Prophet's spirit and to discourage others from listening to him. This verse assures the Prophet that he does not need to worry about them. Allah will handle them directly. The sufficiency of Allah means that no human plot, no matter how clever or well-resourced, can succeed against someone whom Allah protects. This promise was fulfilled during the Prophet's life. Several of the most notorious mockers met untimely deaths or were humiliated in ways that demonstrated divine intervention. Their plans to stop the message failed, and the very mockery they used as a weapon became evidence of their own bankruptcy when the truth prevailed. The verse also teaches believers that the appropriate response to mockery is not to descend to the level of the mockers or to be consumed by anxiety. The response is to trust in Allah's sufficiency and to continue the mission with confidence. The mockers may have their day, but their day is short. The One who is sufficient for the Prophet is sufficient for every believer who faces ridicule for their faith. This promise does not mean that believers will never be harmed. It means that the ultimate outcome is secure, and the mockers will not have the last word. The history of Islam testifies to this. The mockers of Makkah are forgotten or remembered only as cautionary examples, while the Prophet they mocked is loved and honored by billions.
96 | who set up ˹other˺ gods with Allah. They will soon come to know. | This verse identifies the mockers more precisely as those who set up other gods alongside Allah. Their mockery of the Prophet was rooted in their polytheism, their worship of idols that they associated with Allah. The act of setting up gods with Allah is the fundamental crime of shirk, the association of partners with the Creator. This is the root sin from which their mockery, their rejection, and their persecution of the believers all stemmed. By identifying them in this way, the verse makes clear that the conflict is not personal or political but theological. It is a conflict between monotheism and polytheism, between submission to Allah and the worship of created things. The verse then adds a chilling warning: "They will soon come to know." The word "soon" conveys imminence and certainty. The knowledge they will come to is the knowledge of the truth they rejected, the reality of the punishment they mocked, and the error of the path they chose. This knowledge will come to them in this world or the next, but come it will. Some commentators note that this "coming to know" was partially fulfilled during the Prophet's lifetime when the most prominent mockers were struck down, and when Mecca was eventually conquered and the idols were smashed. But the full knowledge will come on the Day of Judgment when the veil is lifted and the reality of their shirk becomes agonizingly clear. The phrase "they will soon come to know" echoes the earlier verse of the surah where the disbelievers were told to eat and enjoy themselves, for they will soon know. The repetition reinforces the theme that the period of respite is limited and the day of realization is approaching. For the believer, this verse is a comfort. Those who mock the faith and set up false gods may appear to be winning in the short term, but the outcome is already determined. They will know, and their knowledge will be a source of eternal regret.
97 | We certainly know that your heart is truly distressed by what they say. | This verse is an extraordinary moment of divine empathy and comfort. Allah acknowledges the emotional pain that the Prophet was experiencing. The word "distressed" translates yadiqu, which literally means to become narrow, constricted, tight. The Prophet's heart, that noble vessel that carried the revelation, was under immense pressure from the words of the mockers. Their insults, their accusations of madness and sorcery, their mockery of the message he held most dear, all of this weighed heavily upon him. Allah knows this. The verse does not rebuke the Prophet for feeling distressed. It validates his pain. The Prophet was not a stoic philosopher unaffected by human emotion. He was a man with a sensitive heart, and the cruelty of his people hurt him deeply. By stating "We certainly know," Allah assures the Prophet that he is not alone in his suffering. His Lord is fully aware of every pang in his chest, every moment of sadness, every sleepless night caused by the rejection of his people. This divine awareness is itself a comfort. To be understood by the Highest is a balm for the wounded heart. The verse also has a profound lesson for all believers who face similar distress because of their faith. It is permissible to feel hurt. The heart is not expected to be stone. The prophets themselves felt pain and sorrow. The remedy is not to deny the pain but to bring it to Allah, who already knows it and who responds with guidance and comfort. The following verses will provide the prescription for this heart distress, showing that the cure lies in worship, glorification, and constant devotion. The acknowledgment of the pain is the first step toward healing. The Prophet's heart was distressed, but the next verses point the way to relief.
98 | So glorify the praises of your Lord and be one of those who ˹always˺ pray, | The divine prescription for the distressed heart is now given. The Prophet is instructed to glorify the praises of his Lord. The word "glorify" is sabbih, which means to declare Allah's perfection, to exalt Him above any flaw or deficiency, to affirm that He is free from all that the mockers attribute to Him. The phrase "the praises of your Lord" is bi-hamdi rabbik, combining glorification with praise. To praise Allah is to acknowledge His perfect attributes, His mercy, His power, His wisdom, His justice. When the heart is heavy with the insults of creation, the antidote is to turn to the praise of the Creator. By focusing on Allah's perfection, the sting of human imperfection is diminished. The Prophet is also commanded to "be one of those who always pray," specifically those who prostrate. The word for those who pray is al-sajidin, the prostrators. Prostration is the highest form of physical submission, the moment when the servant places the most honored part of the body, the face, on the ground in humility before the Lord. It is in prostration that the believer is closest to Allah. The instruction to be among the prostrators is a call to make prayer a consistent, defining practice. The rhythm of daily prayer, with its cycles of standing, bowing, and prostrating, is the spiritual sustenance that replenishes the soul and eases the heart. When the Prophet was distressed, he would turn to prayer and say, "Comfort us with it, O Bilal," calling for the call to prayer. Prayer was not a burden but a relief, a conversation with the Beloved that washed away the pain inflicted by the words of the enemies. This verse teaches every believer the practical response to spiritual distress. Do not dwell on the words of the mockers. Turn to Allah, glorify Him, praise Him, and prostrate. In that act of worship, the constriction of the heart will give way to the expansiveness of divine presence.
99 | and worship your Lord until the inevitable comes your way. | The surah concludes with an instruction that encapsulates the entire purpose of human existence. The Prophet is told to worship his Lord until the "inevitable" comes to him. The word "inevitable" translates al-yaqin, which means certainty. In the context of this verse, the majority of scholars understand it to mean death. Death is the one absolute certainty of human life. Everything else is subject to doubt, delay, or change, but death is the inevitable appointment that no soul can escape. The instruction is to continue worshiping until that moment arrives. Worship is not a phase of life, not a temporary practice, not something to be done only when one is young or old or in difficulty or in ease. Worship is the constant, the permanent occupation of the believer until the final breath. The use of "your Lord" for the final time in the surah reinforces the intimate and personal nature of this worship. The Prophet is not being asked to worship an abstract concept but to worship his Lord, the One who knows his distress, who has granted him the seven oft-repeated verses and the great Quran, who has promised to be sufficient for him against the mockers. This worship is the fitting response to all that Allah has done and all that Allah is. The verse also contains an implicit warning. The "inevitable" will come to everyone, including those who mock and reject. For the believer, the arrival of certainty is a transition to eternal bliss. For the disbeliever, it is the beginning of the punishment they were warned about. The surah that began with the clear Book and the warning of the disbelievers' regret ends with the command to worship until certainty arrives. The entire message is thus framed by the reality of the Quran and the certainty of the end. Life is a brief period between the revelation of guidance and the arrival of death. The only sensible response is to spend that brief period in worship of the Lord who created, guided, and will judge. This is the path of the righteous, the path of Abraham, Lot, Shu'ayb, and Salih, and the path of the final Prophet and all who follow him until the end of time.
