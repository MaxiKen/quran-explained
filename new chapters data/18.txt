1 | All praise is for Allah Who has revealed the Book to His servant, allowing no crookedness in it, | The surah opens with the fundamental declaration that all praise belongs to Allah alone. The phrase “All praise is for Allah” is not merely a statement of gratitude but an acknowledgment that every perfection, every blessing, and every instance of beauty ultimately comes from Him. This praise is tied directly to a monumental act of mercy: “Who has revealed the Book to His servant.” The Book is the Quran, and calling the Prophet “His servant” is a profound honour. It shows that the highest station a human can reach is servitude to God, distancing the Prophet from any claim of divinity or sonship. Then the verse describes a core quality of this revelation: “allowing no crookedness in it.” The Arabic term translated as crookedness conveys the idea of deviation, distortion, or any unevenness. The Quran has no contradictions in its narratives, no errors in its guidance, and no injustice in its rulings. It is a straight path, entirely reliable. This foundation is essential for everything that follows in the surah. The stories of the cave, the two gardens, Moses, and Dhul-Qarnain all serve as illustrations of the Quran’s uprightness, showing that its principles hold true across time and circumstance. The Prophet ﷺ described the Quran as a rope extended from heaven to earth; holding fast to it protects a person from going astray. This opening verse, therefore, prepares the heart to receive the entire surah with trust. It also serves as a subtle but powerful response to those who accused the Prophet of inventing the Quran or claimed it contained fables of the ancients. A book from the All-Wise cannot contain crookedness, and the one who brought it is nothing but a faithful servant. As we move forward, the next verse clarifies the purpose for which this perfectly straight Book was sent down, explaining its function as both a warning and a glad tiding.
2 | ˹making it˺ perfectly upright, to warn ˹the disbelievers˺ of a severe torment from Him; to give good news to the believers—who do good—that they will have a fine reward, | After stating that the Book has no crookedness, the verse adds “making it perfectly upright.” The Arabic word qayyiman intensifies the idea of straightness, meaning that the Quran stands firm in its truth, establishes justice, and watches over the affairs of creation in the best way. Its uprightness is not passive; it actively corrects falsehood and guides to what is most sound. The revelation then serves two primary purposes. First, “to warn the disbelievers of a severe torment from Him.” The warning is immediate and severe, meant to shake those who persist in rejecting faith after the truth has become clear. The torment is described as ba’san shadeedan, a punishment that is mighty and crushing, coming from God Himself. This is not a light caution but a dire alert that should cause a person to reconsider their path. Second, the Quran gives “good news to the believers—who do good—that they will have a fine reward.” Notice that belief is coupled with righteous deeds; faith must manifest in action. The reward is husnan, something excellent and beautiful, far beyond mere recompense. It includes the gardens of Paradise and the pleasure of Allah. This pairing of warning and glad tidings runs throughout the surah: the youths in the cave are rewarded with mercy, while the owner of the two gardens faces ruin. The verse also shows the perfect balance of the Quran; it does not only frighten, nor does it only promise ease without accountability. By clearly dividing humanity into those who reject and those who believe and act righteously, the verse sets the stage for the tests and outcomes that follow. The listener is immediately invited to examine which group they belong to and what their own standing is before their Lord.
3 | in which they will remain forever; | This short verse completes the description of the reward mentioned at the end of the previous verse. The “fine reward” that the believers will have is not temporary or fleeting; it is an eternal state “in which they will remain forever.” The Arabic khālidīna conveys absolute permanence, without any interruption, decline, or fear of it ever ending. This simple statement corrects a deep human tendency to measure all good things by the limited span of worldly life. In this world, every joy eventually fades, every comfort is mixed with hardship, and death terminates every pleasure. The life to come is utterly different. By linking eternal dwelling to the reward, Allah reassures the believers that their efforts and patience are not in vain. For a small group of young men who would soon abandon the comforts of their city and sleep in a cave for centuries, the promise of unending reward would have been a tremendous source of strength. Similarly, for the Prophet ﷺ and his companions facing persecution in Mecca, the knowledge that a moment’s hardship leads to everlasting bliss makes the heart firm. The verse also underscores the seriousness of rejecting faith, because just as the reward is forever, so is the punishment for those who earn it. The surah will later describe the opposite fate of the disbelievers in detail, but here the emphasis remains on hope. The brevity of the verse, placed right after the warning and good news, serves as a powerful pause, allowing the listener to reflect on the weight of eternity. It teaches that our ultimate concern should not be the passing adornments of this world but the permanent residence we are building through our faith and deeds.
4 | and to warn those who claim, “Allah has offspring.” | The Quran now specifies a particularly grievous form of disbelief that the Book came to warn against: “those who claim, ‘Allah has offspring.’” This includes the pagan Arabs who said the angels were daughters of Allah, the Christians who called Jesus the son of God, and certain Jewish groups who made similar claims. The warning is severe because this claim strikes at the very heart of God’s oneness and self-sufficiency. To attribute a child to the Creator implies need, division, and resemblance to creation. Allah is As-Samad, the Eternal Refuge who neither begets nor is begotten. The verse appears here in the early part of the surah because the stories that follow demonstrate God’s absolute power and independence. The people of the cave were revived after centuries without any assistance; the man with two gardens learned that wealth and offspring cannot protect from divine decree; Moses was taught that divine knowledge is beyond human reasoning; and Dhul-Qarnain’s vast kingdom was a gift from God, not an inheritance. All these narratives refute the idea that Allah needs a helper or a successor. The warning is directed at a specific false belief that was prevalent in the society of revelation, yet it remains relevant wherever people elevate created beings to divine status. The fact that the warning is repeated in a separate verse after the general warning shows the gravity of this particular misconception. It teaches the believer never to compromise on the purity of Tawhid and to recognize that the most dangerous falsehood is that which touches the essence of God. This verse flows naturally into the next, where the utter groundlessness of such a claim is exposed.
5 | They have no knowledge of this, nor did their forefathers. What a terrible claim that comes out of their mouths! They say nothing but lies. | Here Allah exposes the total lack of foundation for the claim that He has offspring. “They have no knowledge of this” means that neither the ones making the claim nor their ancestors had any divine revelation, rational proof, or sensory evidence to support it. It is a belief inherited blindly, rooted in conjecture and cultural custom rather than truth. The phrase “nor did their forefathers” shows that the mere fact that a belief is old does not make it true; falsehood remains falsehood regardless of how long people have been repeating it. Then the verse intensifies the condemnation: “What a terrible claim that comes out of their mouths!” The word used, kalimatun, indicates a statement, and describing it as terrible or grievous (kaburat) highlights its enormity. It is a monstrous thing to utter against the Majesty of God. Finally, the verdict is delivered: “They say nothing but lies.” This is a direct, unambiguous rejection of the assertion. The verse teaches a critical principle: in matters of belief, one must rely on knowledge and revelation, not on inherited tradition or speculation. It also shows that speech about God must be guarded with the utmost care, because a false word about Him is among the greatest of injustices. As the surah progresses, we see the youths in the cave standing firm against the baseless beliefs of their people, saying, “Why do they not produce a clear proof of them?” Their challenge echoes the same Quranic logic: if a deity is worshipped, there must be evidence. This verse, therefore, lays the groundwork for the courage of conviction that will be praised in the coming story.
6 | Now, perhaps you ˹O Prophet˺ will grieve yourself to death over their denial, if they ˹continue to˺ disbelieve in this message. | The address now shifts directly to the Prophet ﷺ, offering him profound consolation. The verse begins with the emphatic “Now, perhaps” (fa-la‘allaka), indicating a deep concern. The Prophet is told that he might “grieve yourself to death” (bākhi‘un nafsaka) over their turning away. The imagery is of someone exhausting their soul to the point of destruction through sorrow. This reveals the immense mercy and care the Prophet had for his people; he desperately wished for their guidance, and their rejection weighed heavily on his heart. Allah gently restrains him from this extreme grief, reminding him that his duty is to convey the message, not to compel acceptance. The phrase “if they continue to disbelieve in this message” connects the grief to the specific revelation of the Quran. The Prophet had brought them the truth, yet they persisted in denial, mocking it and calling it sorcery. This verse serves as a divine intervention to preserve the Prophet’s wellbeing and to set the limits of his responsibility. The same theme is echoed in other surahs, such as “You are not, by the grace of your Lord, a madman” and “Do not let your soul be expended in regrets over them.” This moment also prepares for the first story of the surah. In the very next verses, Allah will show that the Prophet is not the first to face rejection. The youths in the cave were also rejected by their society, yet they trusted Allah and He took care of them in a way no one could have imagined. The message is clear: do not let the denial of people break your spirit, for the outcome is in God’s hands. The earth’s adornments that captivate the disbelievers will all be turned to dust, but those who hold fast to faith will be preserved and honoured.
7 | We have indeed made whatever is on earth as an adornment for it, in order to test which of them is best in deeds. | After consoling the Prophet, the Quran explains the very purpose of the worldly attractions that preoccupy the disbelievers. Allah declares, “We have indeed made whatever is on earth as an adornment for it.” The mountains, rivers, gardens, wealth, and power that people chase are a zinah, a beautification that makes the earth appealing. This does not mean the world is evil in itself; rather, its beauty is a deliberate part of the divine plan. The second half of the verse reveals why: “in order to test which of them is best in deeds.” The adornment is the material of the test. Every beautiful thing a person encounters is an opportunity—will they use it in obedience and gratitude, or will they be distracted from their Lord and transgress? The focus is on “best in deeds” (ahsanu ‘amalan), not most in deeds. Quality matters more than quantity; a small action done with sincerity, according to the prophetic guidance, and with a heart attached to Allah is better than mountains of deeds performed for show or in ignorance. This verse sets the philosophical foundation for the entire surah. The youths in the cave turned away from the adornments of their city to preserve their faith. The owner of the two gardens became arrogant and was destroyed by his attachment to his beautiful property. Wealth and children are later described as the adornment of this life. Thus, the verse teaches the believer to engage with the world not as an ultimate goal but as a test ground, constantly asking oneself: is my response to this blessing making me among those who excel in deeds? It reframes every worldly experience as a divine assessment, urging mindfulness and constant self-evaluation.
8 | And We will certainly reduce whatever is on it to barren ground. | To reinforce the temporary nature of the world’s adornments, Allah immediately follows with a powerful image of finality: “And We will certainly reduce whatever is on it to barren ground.” The word sa‘īdan means dust or level ground, and juruzan describes land that is barren, stripped of all vegetation and life. Everything that dazzles the eye—the lush gardens, towering buildings, and glittering treasures—will one day be utterly wiped away, leaving a flat, lifeless plain. This is not only a description of the Day of Resurrection, when the earth will be changed to a different earth, but also a general truth about the fate of all worldly things. Civilizations rise and fall, gardens wither, and even the most magnificent structures crumble to dust. By juxtaposing this verse with the previous one, the Quran delivers a balanced worldview: the world is a place of testing, not permanence; we are to use its adornments for good, but never to anchor our hearts in them. For the Prophet ﷺ, this was a further consolation. The splendor and power of the Quraysh chiefs who opposed him would inevitably vanish. For the believer today, it is a reminder that the ultimate reality is not what we see but what God has promised. The verse also serves as a natural bridge to the story of the people of the cave. They abandoned the fleeting adornments of their society, took refuge in a cave, and were miraculously preserved while their civilization’s riches turned to dust. Their sleep and awakening became a living sign that what matters is not the accumulation of worldly beauty but the state of one’s faith. Thus, the surah moves from the grand principle of the earth’s impermanence to the specific, wondrous sign that illustrates it.
9 | Have you ˹O Prophet˺ thought that the people of the cave and the plaque were ˹the only˺ wonders of Our signs? | With this verse, the surah transitions into the first major story. The interrogative form, “Have you thought,” is a rhetorical question directed at the Prophet ﷺ, and by extension at all who hear the message. The question implies: do you consider the story of the people of the cave and the plaque (Ar-Raqim) to be the only strange and marvellous sign from God? The context of revelation clarifies this. The Quraysh, prompted by Jewish scholars, asked the Prophet about the youths who disappeared, about a mighty traveller (Dhul-Qarnain), and about the spirit. They intended to test his prophethood. When the answer came in this surah, Allah did not simply narrate the story; He prefaced it by alerting the listener that the wonders of God’s power are far greater than this single incident. The “plaque” has been interpreted by some as the inscription or tablet that bore the names of the youths, and by others as the name of the mountain, valley, or even the dog. The exact meaning is left ambiguous, which itself teaches that the precise historical details known only to God are not the primary focus; the lesson of faith and resurrection is. This verse also corrects a potential human tendency to become overly fascinated with miraculous stories while neglecting the countless signs surrounding us every day. The sun that inclined away from the cave, the preservation of the youths’ bodies, their dog’s watch—these are all wonders, but even the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of night and day are greater signs for those who reflect. By posing this question, Allah humbles the listener and prepares the mind to receive the narrative not as mere entertainment but as a profound testimony to His power, knowledge, and mercy.
10 | ˹Remember˺ when those youths took refuge in the cave, and said, “Our Lord! Grant us mercy from Yourself and guide us rightly through our ordeal.” | The story begins with an act of faith and a heartfelt supplication. “When those youths took refuge in the cave” indicates that they were a group of young men who fled from their people to protect their religion. Their youth is emphasized because young people are often under immense pressure to conform, yet their hearts were filled with faith. They did not have political power or social influence; all they could do was remove themselves from a corrupt environment and throw themselves upon God’s care. Their prayer, “Our Lord! Grant us mercy from Yourself,” is a humble plea for a special, direct mercy that no human can provide—a mercy that would cover their weaknesses, forgive their sins, and shield them from harm. Then they added, “and guide us rightly through our ordeal.” The word amr is translated as ordeal or affair, encompassing their entire predicament. They asked not only for escape from danger but for right guidance in handling their situation in a way pleasing to God. This is a timeless model for believers facing trials: flee from sin if necessary, turn to the Creator with complete dependence, and ask for mercy coupled with guidance. There is no trace of arrogance or self-reliance in their words, only pure trust. The prayer was answered in a manner beyond their imagination; God not only protected them but made their story an enduring sign until the end of time. This verse teaches that when one’s environment threatens faith, physical separation might be required, but that separation must be accompanied by a spiritual turning to Allah. Their collective unity in supplication also highlights the strength found in a community of sincere believers, a lesson the early Muslims in Mecca would have deeply internalized.
11 | So We caused them to fall into a dead sleep in the cave for many years, | The response to their sincere prayer was nothing short of miraculous. Allah says, “So We caused them to fall into a dead sleep.” The Arabic verb darabnā ‘alā ādhānihim literally means “We struck upon their ears,” an expression indicating a deep, impenetrable sleep in which noise does not disturb. This was no ordinary slumber but a divinely induced state of unconsciousness that preserved their bodies from decay and their minds from the agony of waiting. The phrase “for many years” introduces the element of time, which will be detailed later as three hundred and nine years. The fact that they remained asleep for centuries without food, water, or movement is a clear sign of God’s power over life and death. It serves as a proof of resurrection, which was one of the central points of dispute with the Meccan pagans. If God can make a small group sleep for three centuries and then wake them up with no harm, He can certainly resurrect all of humanity after death. The verse also subtly highlights the theme of divine protection. While the world outside the cave went on, dynasties changed, and the people who threatened them perished, the youths were kept in a state of perfect safety. God’s mercy enveloped them so completely that time itself was suspended for their benefit. For the Prophet ﷺ and the believers who were enduring the persecution of Quraysh, this was a powerful reassurance that God’s care can manifest in ways that defy all ordinary laws. When one places trust in Allah, He may not remove the difficulty immediately, but He can alter the very fabric of reality to grant salvation and make the believers’ story a beacon of hope.
12 | then We raised them so We may show which of the two groups would make a better estimation of the length of their stay. | After their long sleep, Allah awakened them. The purpose stated here is profound: “so We may show which of the two groups would make a better estimation of the length of their stay.” The “two groups” are understood by many commentators to refer to differing factions among the people of the city concerning the resurrection and the soul. Some might have been believers who trusted in God’s knowledge, while others were skeptics who speculated. By causing the youths to wake up and begin discussing among themselves how long they had remained—some saying a day or part of a day—God manifested that human perception of time is utterly limited and unreliable in the face of divine decree. The true duration could only be known by Allah. The test was not merely about guessing a number; it was about recognizing the limits of human knowledge and the necessity of referring ultimate matters to the All-Knowing. This connects directly to the core theme of the Quran: only God possesses the knowledge of the unseen. The verse also serves as a subtle rebuke to those who would later argue about the exact number of the sleepers or the length of their stay without certain knowledge. By embedding this purpose within the narrative, the Quran teaches believers how to approach stories of the unseen: seek the lesson in faith, not the superfluous historical detail. For the Prophet’s contemporaries who had asked about the cave people, this verse was an indication that the true miracle was not just the sleep itself but the entire event as a sign pointing to resurrection and the vastness of God’s knowledge. It gently directs the inquirer away from trivial disputes and toward the core spiritual truths.
13 | We relate to you ˹O Prophet˺ their story in truth. They were youths who truly believed in their Lord, and We increased them in guidance. | Here the Quran emphasizes the authenticity and purpose of the narrative: “We relate to you their story in truth.” It is not a legend, myth, or fable, but al-haqq, the unerring truth. This assertion guarantees that the account is free from the distortions that had crept into earlier scriptures and folk traditions. The verse then highlights the defining quality of the cave’s inhabitants: “They were youths who truly believed in their Lord.” Their youth is mentioned again because it magnifies their virtue; despite the passions and social pressures that often sway the young, they held firmly to genuine faith. Their belief was not superficial or inherited, but a deep conviction in the Oneness of God. The next phrase, “and We increased them in guidance,” reveals a crucial spiritual principle. When a person takes a sincere step toward faith, God responds by granting them more guidance, clarity, and strength. This is an increase in the light that illuminates their path. The youths did not merely receive a one-time gift of faith; as they stood firm, God continually enhanced their insight and steadfastness. For the companions of the Prophet ﷺ, many of whom were young, this verse was a powerful motivator. It showed that youth is not an obstacle to piety; rather, young hearts, when filled with true belief, become worthy of divine honour. The increase in guidance also explains how they were able to make the courageous stand described in the next verse. This dynamic relationship—human effort met with divine increase—is a recurring theme. It reassures every believer that the struggle to maintain faith in a hostile environment is never in vain; God sees it and will open doors of guidance that were previously unimaginable.
14 | And We strengthened their hearts when they stood up and declared, “Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth. We will never call upon any god besides Him, or we would truly be uttering an outrageous lie.” | The verse describes the peak moment of their courage. “We strengthened their hearts” (rabatnā ‘alā qulūbihim) gives a vivid image of God tying and fortifying their hearts so that fear would not shake them. This inner binding made their resolve unbreakable. The strengthening came “when they stood up,” indicating a public confrontation. They did not keep their faith hidden in fear, but rose before their people—likely a tyrannical ruler and idolatrous society—and delivered a clear, uncompromising declaration of Tawhid. Their words, “Our Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth,” established that God’s lordship encompasses everything, leaving no room for any other deity. They followed this with a solemn vow: “We will never call upon any god besides Him.” The oath “never” (lan nad‘uwa) signifies an absolute and permanent rejection of all false gods. Their reasoning is then stated: if they were to do so, “we would truly be uttering an outrageous lie.” They described shirk (associating partners with God) not merely as a mistake but as an outrageous, extravagant falsehood (shataṭan). This kind of clear moral language shows that they understood the gravity of polytheism; it is the most extreme departure from truth. This verse provides a timeless model for speaking truth to power. The strength did not come from their own bravery alone but was a direct gift from God. It also demonstrates that the core of the struggle between faith and disbelief has always been Tawhid. The youth were not engaged in a political dispute but a fundamental theological stand. The verse seamlessly bridges to the next, where they turn to one another and analyze the intellectual bankruptcy of their people’s idolatry, showing that their stand was both spiritual and rational.
15 | ˹Then they said to one another,˺ “These people of ours have taken gods besides Him. Why do they not produce a clear proof of them? Who then does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah?” | After their public declaration, the youths turned to each other and engaged in mutual reinforcement of their faith. They observed, “These people of ours have taken gods besides Him,” acknowledging the reality of their society’s deviation. Their analysis is strikingly rational: “Why do they not produce a clear proof of them?” They demanded evidence. They knew that belief must rest on a foundation of certainty, not blind imitation. Since no idol can speak, create, or reveal guidance, there can never be a clear proof for worshipping anything other than the Creator. This intellectual challenge is a hallmark of Quranic logic. The believers did not merely say, “We feel our faith is true”; they pointed out the epistemological void in idolatry. They then posed a rhetorical question: “Who then does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah?” Attributing partners to God is a fabrication, an invention of the greatest injustice. This question solidifies their moral clarity and further strengthens their resolve. For the early Muslims in Mecca, this verse was a mirror of their own situation. They too were surrounded by idols that their forefathers worshipped without proof, and they too faced persecution for challenging this tradition. The verse teaches that part of preserving faith is to analyze the falsehood around you and to remind one another of its groundlessness. It also highlights the importance of righteous companionship. These youths did not isolate themselves in despair; they spoke to one another, reasoned together, and arrived at the conclusion that their withdrawal from the society was necessary. This inner dialogue prepared them for the practical step of taking refuge in the cave, which is described in the following verse.
16 | Since you have distanced yourselves from them and what they worship besides Allah, take refuge in the cave. Your Lord will extend His mercy to you and accommodate you in your ordeal. | Having recognized the danger to their faith, the youths resolved to act. The verse opens with the premise, “Since you have distanced yourselves from them and what they worship besides Allah.” This separation was both physical and spiritual; they had already rejected the false gods and the community that clung to them. The instruction that followed—possibly spoken by one of them to the group—was to “take refuge in the cave.” The cave was not a place of luxury but of isolation and hardship. Yet they chose it over the comfort of a society built on falsehood. This act of fleeing with one’s faith is known as hijrah, and it has been the response of believers throughout history when faced with overwhelming corruption. Immediately, the statement of trust follows: “Your Lord will extend His mercy to you and accommodate you in your ordeal.” They expected that God would spread out His mercy over them, like a protective covering, and would make their difficult situation easy and manageable. The word translated as “accommodate” (yuhayyi’) also implies preparing and arranging matters for comfort. Even in a dark cave, divine care can create a safe and peaceful haven. This verse had direct relevance for the Prophet ﷺ and his companions, who would soon undertake their own hijrah to Medina. It taught them that when one leaves something for the sake of Allah, He replaces it with something far better. The cave, which might seem like a place of fear, became a sanctuary of miracles. The lesson is timeless: when believers are pressured to compromise their faith, the solution is not to surrender but to seek a space where they can worship God freely, trusting fully in His providence. The subsequent verses then describe how God’s care manifested in the most miraculous physical details.
17 | And you would have seen the sun, as it rose, inclining away from their cave to the right, and as it set, declining away from them to the left, while they lay in its open space. That is one of the signs of Allah. Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. But whoever He leaves to stray, you will never find for them a guiding mentor. | The Quran now paints a scene of extraordinary divine care. “And you would have seen the sun, as it rose, inclining away from their cave to the right, and as it set, declining away from them to the left.” The sun’s movement was regulated so that its direct, scorching rays did not fall upon the sleepers, which could have harmed their bodies, while still allowing enough gentle light and air to reach them. The phrase “while they lay in its open space” indicates they were situated in a spacious part of the cave where the sun’s path could be managed by God’s command. This is explicitly called “one of the signs of Allah.” It demonstrates that even celestial bodies are subservient to God’s will in caring for His righteous servants. The verse then shifts to a fundamental principle: “Whoever Allah guides is truly guided. But whoever He leaves to stray, you will never find for them a guiding mentor.” The miraculous protection of the youths is linked to guidance. God guided them to faith, to the cave, and in their physical preservation. True guidance is a gift that leads to safety in this world and the next. Conversely, those whom God leaves astray because of their own persistent rejection have no guide; no human effort can bring them to the path if God does not will it. This interjection serves as a powerful reminder to the Prophet and the believers that their mission is to deliver the message, but the outcome is in God’s hands. The Quraysh who saw the sun rise and set every day failed to see the sign of the Creator in it, while the youths in the cave experienced a special solar sign because of their faith. The verse connects physical miracles with spiritual insight, teaching that the universe itself can become a book of signs for those whom God guides.
18 | And you would have thought they were awake, though they were asleep. We turned them over, to the right and left, while their dog stretched his forelegs at the entrance. Had you looked at them, you would have certainly fled away from them, filled with horror. | The description of the sleepers’ state continues with further miraculous details. “And you would have thought they were awake, though they were asleep.” Their eyes were open, and their appearance was so lifelike that an observer would assume they were conscious. This feature was part of God’s plan to protect them; if someone had chanced upon the cave, they would not see corpses but a group of vigilant-looking young men, which might deter intruders. “We turned them over, to the right and left” illustrates that Allah Himself took care of their physical well-being, rotating their bodies to prevent decay and bedsores, a medical reality only fully appreciated much later. The mention of “their dog stretched his forelegs at the entrance” has captured the imagination of readers for centuries. The dog, having faithfully followed them, was granted a share in this miracle. It lay at the cave’s threshold with its legs extended, appearing to guard them. Despite the long years, neither the youths nor the dog deteriorated. The final statement, “Had you looked at them, you would have certainly fled away from them, filled with horror,” adds a layer of awe. God cast upon them an aura of reverence and fear so that anyone who might discover them would be overwhelmed with terror and turn back, thus preserving their secret for the appointed time. This fear was a divine safeguard, not a sign of weakness in them. The entire scene demonstrates the completeness of God’s protection. It also reinforces the theme that the unseen arrangements of Allah for His servants are far more meticulous and powerful than any human security plan. For the believers in Mecca under threat, this was a deeply comforting image of how God can hide and protect His people in plain sight.
19 | And so We awakened them so that they might question one another. One of them exclaimed, “How long have you remained ˹asleep˺?” Some replied, “Perhaps a day, or part of a day.” They said ˹to one another˺, “Your Lord knows best how long you have remained. So send one of you with these silver coins of yours to the city, and let him find which food is the purest, and then bring you provisions from it. Let him be ˹exceptionally˺ cautious, and do not let him give you away. | After the long period of sleep, God awakened them at the precise moment when their story was meant to become a sign for the people. “And so We awakened them so that they might question one another.” Their first natural reaction was confusion about the time. “One of them exclaimed, ‘How long have you remained asleep?’” They felt they had slept for a short while. Their responses, “Perhaps a day, or part of a day,” show they had no perception of the centuries that had passed. Their uncertainty immediately led them to the correct spiritual attitude: “Your Lord knows best how long you have remained.” They deferred the precise knowledge to God. Then, shifting to practical matters, they said, “So send one of you with these silver coins of yours to the city.” The coins, called wariq, were presumably silver dirhams that were no longer in circulation, a detail that would later alert the townspeople. Their instructions were careful: “let him find which food is the purest,” meaning the most lawful and wholesome provision, and “let him be exceptionally cautious, and do not let him give you away.” They feared discovery because they still thought the old persecution was ongoing. Their caution, politeness, and concern for halal sustenance even in a state of need illustrate deep God-consciousness. They chose the purest food, not just any food, highlighting the importance of lawful earnings. They also strategized to avoid detection, showing that reliance on God does not negate taking careful worldly measures. This verse teaches the balance between trust in divine knowledge and responsible human action. Their awakening also began to fulfill God’s purpose of making the event a clear proof of resurrection for those who would witness it.
20 | For, indeed, if they find out about you, they will stone you ˹to death˺, or force you back into their faith, and then you will never succeed.” | The youths continue their cautious instructions by explaining the dire consequences of being discovered. “For, indeed, if they find out about you, they will stone you to death.” Stoning was a brutal form of execution, and it highlights the violent intolerance of the society they had fled. The alternative threat was equally severe: “or force you back into their faith.” Compulsion in religion, threatening a person’s very faith and eternal salvation, was the ultimate danger. The third part of the warning, “and then you will never succeed,” refers to the absolute spiritual failure that would follow if they were forced back into disbelief. In their view, returning to falsehood after having embraced the truth would mean permanent loss, with no hope of true success in this life or the next. This verse captures the extreme test of faith they were undergoing. The threat to life was real, but the threat to their afterlife was even more terrifying to them. Their primary concern was not physical death but spiritual death. This scale of priorities is a lesson for all believers. The statement also reflects the Quranic teaching that apostasy under coercion that negates the heart’s true conviction is a matter God judges with mercy, but here the youths were determined not to take that path voluntarily. Their conversation shows a community of believers who valued their faith above all else and were willing to take careful, calculated risks to preserve it. For the early Muslims in Mecca, who faced similar threats of torture and forced recantation, this verse was a mirror of their own daily struggle, reassuring them that their caution and reliance on God were exactly the pattern of the righteous before them.
21 | That is how We caused them to be discovered so that their people might know that Allah’s promise ˹of resurrection˺ is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour. When the people disputed with each other about the case of the youth ˹after their death˺, some proposed, “Build a structure around them. Their Lord knows best about them.” Those who prevailed in the matter said, “We will surely build a place of worship over them.” | The long, protective sleep finally gave way to a divinely orchestrated discovery. “That is how We caused them to be discovered” signals that the moment their presence became known was not an accident but part of the grand plan. The purpose was “so that their people might know that Allah’s promise of resurrection is true and that there is no doubt about the Hour.” The people of the city had been arguing about whether the dead would be raised. Witnessing these sleepers wake after centuries was a tangible, irrefutable sign that God can bring the dead back to life. It settled the debate not through philosophical argument but through a miracle standing before their eyes. After the discovery, “the people disputed with each other about the case of the youth after their death.” Once the youths had completed their divine mission and passed away for the final time, the townsfolk differed on how to honour them. Some, likely people of faith, proposed, “Build a structure around them.” This idea was to erect a wall or a monument that would preserve their memory as a sign. They wisely added, “Their Lord knows best about them,” a phrase filled with humility, acknowledging that the true sanctity of the sleepers was known fully only to God. However, “those who prevailed in the matter” were not the humble believers. They were the influential, the people of power and authority. They declared, “We will surely build a place of worship over them.” This shift from a simple memorial to a masjid was a dangerous deviation. The Prophet ﷺ warned that such practices, turning graves of the righteous into places of worship, lead to veneration of humans rather than God and were a root of shirk among previous nations. The verse gently critiques the prevailing group’s solution, teaching the believers that even well-intentioned honour can cross the limits set by God. For us, it is a caution that reverence for saints must never evolve into worship of them or their relics. The story thus closes with a powerful lesson on the corruptibility of human remembrance when it detaches from the pure worship of the One who alone knows the unseen.
22 | Some will say, “They were three, their dog was the fourth,” while others will say, “They were five, their dog was the sixth,” ˹only˺ guessing blindly. And others will say, “They were seven and their dog was the eighth.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “My Lord knows best their ˹exact˺ number. Only a few people know as well.” So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those ˹who debate˺ about them. | Having narrated the core of the story, the Quran addresses the inevitable human curiosity about its trivial details. The verse presents the speculations that people would engage in regarding the number of the sleepers. “Some will say, ‘They were three, their dog was the fourth,’ while others will say, ‘They were five, their dog was the sixth,’ only guessing blindly.” The phrase “guessing blindly” (rajman bil-ghayb) dismisses these claims as conjecture without evidence, akin to throwing stones into the dark. A third opinion is mentioned: “And others will say, ‘They were seven and their dog was the eighth.’” The Quran does not explicitly endorse any of these numbers, though the way the third opinion is presented without the negative qualifier has led many scholars to consider it the most plausible. However, the verse immediately commands the Prophet to respond in a way that redirects the focus: “My Lord knows best their exact number. Only a few people know as well.” This teaches that while some learned individuals might have access to certain knowledge through previous scriptures, the precise number is ultimately a matter of the unseen known fully only to God. The instruction that follows is direct and practical: “So do not argue about them except with sure knowledge, nor consult any of those who debate about them.” The Prophet and the believers are told not to enter into futile arguments with the People of the Book or others over such details. The only legitimate discussion is that which rests on clear revelation, and even that should not become a source of division. This verse is a profound lesson in epistemic humility. When God has chosen to leave a matter ambiguous, it is because the exact figure adds nothing to one’s faith or salvation. The real miracle is the resurrection sign, not the head count. By forbidding consultation with those who merely debate for sport, the Quran guards the community against losing sight of the spiritual substance of narratives in a sea of speculative disputes.
23 | And never say of anything, “I will definitely do this tomorrow,” | This verse begins a crucial lesson in etiquette directly tied to the story of the cave. When the Prophet ﷺ was asked about the sleepers, he had said, “I will answer you tomorrow,” hoping for revelation to come. However, the revelation was delayed, causing him distress and giving the Quraysh an opportunity to mock him. After fifteen days, the surah was revealed with this corrective. The command “And never say of anything, ‘I will definitely do this tomorrow,’” is absolute. A person should never speak about a future action with unqualified certainty, because the guarantee of life, ability, and circumstances belongs to God alone. Tomorrow is entirely in the unseen; a thousand obstacles can prevent the intended deed. By stating it as a definitive, a person implicitly assumes a power they do not possess. The wording “I will definitely do” (innī fā‘ilun) reflects a firm resolution, but that resolution must be tempered by acknowledging God’s will. This is not a discouragement from planning or having determination; the Quran itself instructs believers to be prepared and to strive. Rather, it is a spiritual discipline that keeps the heart attached to the Almighty at every moment. It reminds the believer that even the simplest intention—to wake up, to work, to meet someone—is only fulfilled by divine permission. The lesson from the Prophet’s experience is that his momentary omission of the phrase was a teaching moment for the entire Ummah. The Prophet, despite his status, was not exempted from this divine courtesy. The verse immediately ties to the next, which provides the necessary exception to the rule, showing that the solution is not to avoid planning but to always link the plan to the will of God.
24 | without adding, “if Allah so wills!” But if you forget, then remember your Lord, and say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.” | The direct continuation from the previous verse completes the command. The phrase “without adding, ‘if Allah so wills!’” is the exception that must accompany every assertion about the future. “In shā’ Allāh” is not a mere ritual phrase; it is a verbal acknowledgment of God’s absolute sovereignty over time and events. By saying it, the believer hands over the outcome to God’s wisdom, expressing that the planned action will only occur if it aligns with His will. Then the verse mercifully addresses human weakness: “But if you forget, then remember your Lord.” The nature of human beings is to forget, and even the Prophet momentarily forgot. The remedy is to say the phrase the moment one remembers, even if the intended time has passed. Remembering God in this context means filling the gap left by forgetfulness with His remembrance. Moreover, the person is taught to say, “I trust my Lord will guide me to what is more right than this.” This is a beautiful expression of hope. When a believer forgets to say “in shā’ Allāh” and perhaps misses the opportunity or the outcome differs from what they planned, they should not despair. Instead, they should express confidence that God will guide them to something closer to guidance and truth than what they originally intended. This phrase transforms a simple omission into an opportunity for deeper trust. The entire episode demonstrates how the story of the cave was not just a narrative but a direct source of practical guidance for the Prophet and his community. The connection between the delayed revelation and these verses teaches that every hardship in the mission had a wisdom behind it, and the etiquette taught here has become a hallmark of Muslim speech until the end of time.
25 | They had remained in their cave for three hundred years, adding nine. | Here the Quran provides one of the clear pieces of information regarding the people of the cave, revealing the duration of their stay. “They had remained in their cave for three hundred years.” This is the length of their sleep according to the solar calendar. The phrase “adding nine” provides the count according to the lunar calendar, as the difference between three hundred solar years and three hundred lunar years is roughly nine years. By stating both, the Quran speaks precisely to both reckonings, demonstrating its divine origin and removing any potential source of contradiction. The detail is not presented as a point for debate but as a factual statement from the One who possesses complete knowledge. This stands in contrast to the earlier instruction not to argue about their number; here the duration is clarified because it serves a purpose. Three hundred and nine years is a span that stretches far beyond the limits of ordinary human life, making the preservation and awakening of the youths an undeniable sign of power over time and decay. It is long enough that civilizations had changed, their people had died, and their language and currency had become archaic, yet short enough that the cave itself could still be identified. The number also shows the meticulous care with which God preserves the details that truly matter for the lesson of resurrection. The verse leads into the next one where the Prophet is instructed to attribute ultimate knowledge to God, reminding the listener that even while giving the number, the Quran maintains the principle that only God knows the full unseen reality behind every detail. The figure of three hundred and nine years thus functions both as a concrete historical fact and as a springboard for reflecting on the relativity of time and the reality of the afterlife.
26 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah knows best how long they stayed. With Him ˹alone˺ is ˹the knowledge of˺ the unseen of the heavens and the earth. How perfectly He hears and sees! They have no guardian besides Him, and He shares His command with none.” | After providing the duration, the Quran immediately instructs the Prophet to declare that ultimate and complete knowledge of the matter rests with God alone. “Allah knows best how long they stayed” is not a contradiction to the previous verse but a nuancing of the epistemology. The three hundred and nine years is the reality that God has revealed and is true, but the exact conditions of their sleep, the precise state of their souls, and all the hidden aspects of their preservation are part of God’s exclusive knowledge. The verse expands this to a universal principle: “With Him alone is the knowledge of the unseen of the heavens and the earth.” The entire realm of the ghayb, whether the secrets of the cosmos, the future, or the inner realities of hearts, belongs to Allah without any partner. The two attributes “How perfectly He hears and sees!” emphasize that nothing escapes His perception. He hears every whisper and sees every hidden state, including the condition of the sleepers through the ages. Then comes a profound declaration of God’s sole authority and care: “They have no guardian besides Him.” The people of the cave had no human protector; God was their walī, and the same is true for all creation. The verse ends with a monumental statement: “and He shares His command with none.” No one, not a prophet, an angel, or any saint, has a share in God’s dominion or decision-making. This is a direct refutation of any belief that ascribes divine helpers or intercessors who can act independently. It reinforces the pure Tawhid that the surah champions. For the Prophet, this instruction was both a shield against the mockery of the Quraysh and a consolidation of his own reliance on God. For the believer, it is a reminder that the only true source of knowledge about the unseen is divine revelation, and all other speculations must yield to what God has chosen to disclose.
27 | Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord. None can change His Words, nor can you find any refuge besides Him. | With the story of the cave concluded, the address returns directly to the Prophet and, through him, to every believer. The command “Recite what has been revealed to you from the Book of your Lord” is an instruction to hold fast to the Quran, to convey it, to live by it, and to make it the centre of one’s being. The phrase “from the Book of your Lord” personalizes the relationship; it is a message from the Most High to His servant. The recitation is not a mechanical reading but an act of engagement, contemplation, and submission. This command was particularly crucial in Mecca where the opposition was intense, and the temptation to compromise or soften the message might arise. The verse immediately provides the reason for this steadfastness: “None can change His Words.” The Words of God are final, protected, and immutable. No human authority, no shifting cultural norms, and no power in the universe can alter what God has decreed and revealed. This is a source of immense comfort and confidence. The truth the Prophet delivered was not subject to revision by the disbelievers, no matter how much they wished to change it. The second part of the assurance is deeply personal: “nor can you find any refuge besides Him.” When one stands firm on the truth and faces the resulting hostility, there is no other sanctuary to run to except God. All human allies, fortresses, and strategies are ultimately powerless. True protection lies only with the One who revealed the Book. This verse serves as the thematic anchor for the following instructions about keeping company with the righteous and warning the heedless. It establishes that the believer’s mission is to recite and uphold the unchanging truth, and their sole reliance is on God, who is the only real guardian and protector.
28 | And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure. Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life. And do not obey those whose hearts We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follow ˹only˺ their desires and whose state is ˹total˺ loss. | This verse provides a social and spiritual directive that follows logically from the command to recite the Book. The Prophet is told, “And patiently stick with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening, seeking His pleasure.” The “those” refers to the poor and humble believers who were mocked by the Quraysh elite. The chiefs of Mecca had demanded that the Prophet drive away these “lowly” companions if he wanted the nobles to sit with him. This verse was a decisive rejection of that demand. The phrase “patiently stick with” (wa-ṣbir nafsaka) implies restraining oneself and enduring difficulty in keeping their company. Their quality is that they invoke God at the beginning and the end of the day, with pure intention, “seeking His pleasure” alone. Their value is not in wealth or social rank but in their sincere devotion. Then the instruction: “Do not let your eyes look beyond them, desiring the luxuries of this worldly life.” The Prophet is told not to divert his gaze away from these sincere believers towards the rich and powerful, hoping to attract them through compromise. The adornment of the world that the disbelievers enjoy should never be the object of longing. Finally, the warning: “And do not obey those whose hearts We have made heedless of Our remembrance, who follow only their desires and whose state is total loss.” God had sealed the hearts of certain leaders due to their persistent arrogance, making them heedless. Their lives are governed by whims, and their ultimate end is bankruptcy. This verse establishes a timeless principle of community: the worth of a person is measured by their faith and devotion, not by their wealth or status. Believers are to prioritize the company of the sincere, even if society considers them insignificant, and to resist the temptation to flatter the powerful for worldly gain.
29 | And say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “˹This is˺ the truth from your Lord. Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve.” Surely We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire whose walls will ˹completely˺ surround them. When they cry for aid, they will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn ˹their˺ faces. What a horrible drink! And what a terrible place to rest! | Having instructed the Prophet to stick with the believers and not obey the heedless, the Quran now teaches him to declare the truth with uncompromising clarity. “This is the truth from your Lord” anchors the message in divine authority. It is not the Prophet’s opinion but an absolute reality from the Creator. The statement that follows, “Whoever wills let them believe, and whoever wills let them disbelieve,” is not an endorsement of disbelief but a declaration of human responsibility and freedom of choice in light of the clear truth that has been presented. After the warning has been delivered, people are free to choose their path, but they must bear the consequences. The verse immediately details the consequence for those who choose wrong: “Surely We have prepared for the wrongdoers a Fire whose walls will completely surround them.” The imagery is that of being trapped, with no escape. The walls of Hell enclose them from all sides, indicating utter helplessness. Their torment is intensified by the description of the only relief offered: “When they cry for aid, they will be aided with water like molten metal, which will burn their faces.” The word “muhl” describes a substance like the dregs of oil or molten copper—scalding, thick, and foul. Far from quenching thirst, it destroys the face as it is brought near. The verse ends with two exclamations: “What a horrible drink! And what a terrible place to rest!” The exclamation form draws the listener into the horror of the scene. This is the reality that awaits those who treat the truth as a matter of indifference. The stark, vivid description is meant to shake the heart and serve as a powerful deterrent, fully consistent with the Quran’s role as a warner and bringer of glad tidings.
30 | As for those who believe and do good, We certainly never deny the reward of those who are best in deeds. | After depicting the terrifying end of the wrongdoers, the Quran immediately balances the warning with a promise of perfect justice and mercy for the believers. “As for those who believe and do good” combines faith and righteous action, which are inseparable in the Quranic vision of salvation. The verse declares, “We certainly never deny the reward of those who are best in deeds.” The Arabic construction “innā lā nuḍī‘u” uses an emphatic negation to stress that God will absolutely never let the reward of the doers of good go to waste. Not an atom’s weight of their righteous effort will be lost. The phrase “those who are best in deeds” echoes the earlier verse about testing which of us is best in deeds. The reward is not merely for the quantity of action but for its quality—the sincerity, the adherence to prophetic guidance, and the excellence in execution. This verse is a profound source of hope. A believer might see the worldly prosperity of heedless people and feel that their own struggles in faith are unrewarded. But God affirms that the accounting is exact and the compensation unfailing. This assurance was critical for the early Muslims in Mecca, who had sacrificed social standing, family ties, and material comfort for their faith. It reminded them that God sees, records, and will recompense every hardship endured for His sake. The verse also establishes a principle of divine fairness: God does not wrong anyone. The disbelievers earned their punishment through their own choices, and the believers will receive their full reward without reduction. It sets the stage for the detailed description of Paradise in the next verse, where the “fine reward” mentioned earlier in the surah begins to be painted in vivid colours.
31 | It is they who will have the Gardens of Eternity, with rivers flowing under their feet. There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade, reclining there on ˹canopied˺ couches. What a marvellous reward! And what a fabulous place to rest! | The reward for those who believe and do good is now described in a series of beautiful images. “It is they who will have the Gardens of Eternity,” the phrase “Gardens of Eternity” (jannātu ‘adnin) implies a permanent, unchanging paradise, a dwelling that never decays. The imagery of “rivers flowing under their feet” creates a sense of continuous, effortless abundance. The water is not distant but flowing beneath them, easily accessible, providing coolness and life. Then, the personal adornment of the believers is described: “There they will be adorned with bracelets of gold, and wear green garments of fine silk and rich brocade.” Gold bracelets were a mark of royalty in ancient times, and silk was the fabric of kings, forbidden for men in this world as a test of restraint, but granted freely in the next. The colour green is associated with freshness, life, and tranquility. The mention of “rich brocade” (istabraq) indicates thick, luxurious silk, emphasizing the tangible, sensory beauty of the reward. Their posture is that of complete peace and honour: “reclining there on canopied couches.” Reclining is the posture of those who are safe, satisfied, and free from worry. The canopied couches provide shade and privacy. The verse then closes with two exclamations of praise: “What a marvellous reward! And what a fabulous place to rest!” The word “marvellous” (ni‘ma) and “fabulous” (ḥasunat) invite the reader to reflect on the perfection of this final resting place, which is a rest not of sleep but of uninterrupted bliss. This description of Paradise stands in direct contrast to the horrible drink and terrible resting place of Hell. The structure of the surah continually places these opposites side by side, reinforcing the urgency of making the right choice. The reward is not abstract; it is a real, physical, and eternal honour from the Most Generous.
32 | Give them ˹O Prophet˺ an example of two men. To ˹the disbelieving˺ one We gave two gardens of grapevines, which We surrounded with palm trees and placed ˹various˺ crops in between. | The surah now transitions into the second major story, that of the owner of the two gardens, by instructing the Prophet to “Give them an example.” This parable is designed to illustrate the dangers of arrogance, attachment to worldly wealth, and denial of the afterlife. “To the disbelieving one We gave two gardens of grapevines.” The description immediately highlights the extraordinary wealth granted to one of the two men. Gardens of grapevines were highly prized, representing luxury, shade, and delicious fruit. His property was not just a simple plot but two entire gardens, indicating double the bounty. Then, “which We surrounded with palm trees” adds another layer of value; date palms are tall, stately trees that provide protection, wood, and a different kind of sustenance. “And placed various crops in between” shows that his land was intensively cultivated, with grains and other plants filling every available space between the vines and palms, ensuring maximum yield and constant harvest. The detail of the description is important. It shows that the man’s wealth was vast and his property self-sustaining. The source of this wealth is clearly attributed to God: “We gave.” The man did not create these gardens; they were a divine grant, a test in disguise. The story is set in motion without yet naming the second man in detail. The listener is drawn into the scene, picturing a man walking through his magnificent property, his heart swelling with pride. The stage is set for the dialogue that will reveal the spiritual state of the wealthy man and the contrasting wisdom of his companion. The lesson for the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their trade and orchards, is obvious. They were being shown a mirror of their own potential fate if they did not recognize the true Giver of their blessings.
33 | Each garden yielded ˹all˺ its produce, never falling short. And We caused a river to flow between them. | The description of the gardens continues with further emphasis on their perfection and self-sufficiency. “Each garden yielded all its produce, never falling short.” The Arabic verb “ātā” conveys that the gardens gave their fruit and crops completely, without any failure, blight, or deficiency. There was no bad season, no failed harvest. This kind of consistent, flawless productivity is every cultivator’s dream. The phrase “never falling short” indicates that the bounty was always at its peak, a state of perpetual abundance. This detail is crucial because it removes any potential justification the man might have had for his arrogance. His success was not due to his own skill or hard work overcoming natural difficulties; the gardens themselves were miraculously bountiful, a gift from God that required minimal anxiety. Then, “And We caused a river to flow between them” provides the final touch. A river flowing between the two gardens ensured constant irrigation, independence from rainfall, and enhanced beauty. The water was a lifeline, literally and aesthetically, weaving the two gardens together into a seamless paradise. All of these blessings were arranged by God, yet they would become the very source of the man’s spiritual ruin because of his response to them. The verse teaches that great worldly success is not necessarily a sign of God’s pleasure; it is often a test. The richer and more trouble-free a person’s life becomes, the greater the danger of heedlessness and self-deification. For the early Muslims who owned little, this was a consolation: the splendour of the disbelievers was not to be envied, because it was the material of a severe examination. The stage is now fully set for the wealthy man to enter his garden and reveal the disease in his heart.
34 | And he had other resources ˹as well˺. So he boasted to a ˹poor˺ companion of his, while conversing with him, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in manpower.” | The Quran adds that the man’s assets were not limited to the gardens. “And he had other resources as well.” The Arabic “thamara” can be understood as other fruits, profits, or forms of wealth such as gold, silver, servants, and livestock. His wealth was diversified and overflowing. This abundance became the fuel for his arrogance. “So he boasted to a poor companion of his.” The companion is commonly understood to be a believing man of modest means. The wealthy man, in the course of normal conversation, turned the interaction into a display of superiority. His words, “I am greater than you in wealth and superior in manpower,” reveal the core of his worldview. He measured worth exclusively by material possessions and social power. Wealth for him was a sign of personal greatness; manpower, meaning his family, servants, and retinue, signified his influence and invincibility. He completely ignored the spiritual reality that the poor companion might be far greater in the sight of God. The sin was not in possessing wealth but in the boastful, contemptuous attitude it fostered. This verse is a vivid depiction of the seduction of dunya (the worldly life). The wealthy man saw his gardens, his resources, and his people, and concluded that he was inherently superior. He forgot that all of it was a trust from God that could be taken away at any moment. For the Prophet’s community, this scene was a warning against looking at the rich with envy, and for the wealthy, it was a caution against the pride that leads to a fall. The dialogue is now underway, and the wealthy man’s following words, which he speaks as he enters his garden, will expose an even deeper layer of spiritual sickness.
35 | And he entered his property, while wronging his soul, saying, “I do not think this will ever perish, | The scene becomes more intimate and charged with spiritual significance. “And he entered his property, while wronging his soul.” The verse does not say he entered with his companion, but rather that he entered, and that his act of entering was a wrong against himself. The wrongdoing (ẓulm) was not a physical action but a state of heart: his pride, his denial of the transience of the world, and his attribution of his wealth to himself rather than to God. “Saying, ‘I do not think this will ever perish.’” The statement reveals a profound delusion. Standing amid the lush, perfectly yielding gardens, with the river flowing before him, he was so mesmerized by the apparent permanence of his riches that he denied the possibility of their extinction. He projected his present comfort into an indefinite future, forgetting that time, disaster, or divine decree could obliterate it all in a moment. This mindset is the essence of the worldly delusion: mistaking the temporary for the eternal. It echoes the earlier verse where Allah said He would reduce the earth’s adornments to barren ground. The man’s error was not merely a lack of intelligence but a moral and spiritual blindness. His heart had become so attached to his property that he could not conceive of life without it. This attitude directly contradicts the Quranic teaching that everything on earth is subject to decay and that only God remains. The verse sets up the dramatic irony of the story; the listener knows that this beautiful garden will soon be reduced to ruins. It is a timeless warning against the false sense of security that wealth can create, a security that prevents a person from preparing for the inevitable Day when all that they owned will be dust.
36 | nor do I think the Hour will ˹ever˺ come. And if in fact I am returned to my Lord, I will definitely get a far better outcome than ˹all˺ this.” | The man’s delusion deepens. From denying the destruction of his property, he moves to denying the ultimate reality: “nor do I think the Hour will ever come.” He not only doubts but actively disbelieves in the Resurrection. This reveals the link between materialism and denial of the afterlife. When a person’s heart is entirely filled with the enjoyment of this world, the idea of a divine judgment becomes an uncomfortable intrusion. Denying it is a way to justify the unchecked pursuit of pleasure. He then adds a hypothetical, arrogant statement: “And if in fact I am returned to my Lord, I will definitely get a far better outcome than all this.” His reasoning is corrupt. He assumes that his wealth and ease in this world are a sign of God’s favour, so if there is an afterlife, the same favour should logically continue. He measures the unseen by his limited, material experience. This is a classic example of a person who views worldly prosperity as evidence of righteousness, a fallacy the Quran repeatedly refutes. God gives the world to those He loves and those He does not, but the Hereafter is only for the pious. The man’s statement is loaded with presumption. He does not say, “I hope for God’s mercy”; he declares with certainty that he will get a better return. This reflects a transactional relationship with God, as if God owed him something. The verse exposes the depths of spiritual ignorance that wealth can breed. It is a direct warning to those who, like the Quraysh, boasted of their riches and assumed that their worldly superiority would translate into eternal favour. The stage is now set for the powerful rebuttal from his believing companion, who will dismantle these illusions with words of faith and clarity.
37 | His ˹believing˺ companion replied, while conversing with him, “Do you disbelieve in the One Who created you from dust, then ˹developed you˺ from a sperm-drop, then formed you into a man? | The focus shifts to the other man, the believer, who now speaks. “His believing companion replied, while conversing with him.” The dialogue continues as a natural conversation between two people, not a hostile debate. The believer’s approach is instructive; he does not start by attacking the man’s wealth or pride, but goes directly to the foundation of faith. “Do you disbelieve in the One Who created you from dust?” The question is designed to provoke reflection. The man is asked whether he can truly reject the Creator who fashioned his very being. The stages of creation are laid out: “from dust” refers to the origin of Adam, but also symbolizes the earthly components of the human body. “Then developed you from a sperm-drop” highlights the humble, almost insignificant beginning of every human life. “Then formed you into a man” celebrates the miraculous result: a balanced, intelligent, complete human being. The argument is that the One who initiated this journey from lifeless dust to a fully formed man is fully capable of resurrecting after death. Denying the Hour is illogical for someone who has witnessed the miracle of his own creation. This approach shifts the wealthy man’s gaze from his gardens to his own soul. The believer is not merely defending a doctrine; he is inviting his companion to recognize the signs of God within himself. This verse teaches an important method of da‘wah: start with what is undeniable, such as the wonder of human creation, and then lead from there to the truths the person is denying. The believer’s speech is filled with a tone of concern and rational persuasion, setting a model for how to address those afflicted with arrogance.
38 | But as for me: He is Allah, my Lord, and I will never associate anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺. | The believer continues his testimony, shifting from addressing the companion’s disbelief to declaring his own faith. “But as for me: He is Allah, my Lord.” The use of “but as for me” draws a clear line of distinction. The wealthy man had expressed doubt about the Hour and made arrogant claims, but the believer takes a stand on a completely different foundation. He affirms that the Creator he just described is specifically Allah, and that Allah is “my Lord.” This personal relationship of lordship means he accepts God’s guidance, submits to His commands, and trusts in His wisdom. He then makes the core declaration of Tawhid: “and I will never associate anyone with my Lord in worship.” The word “never” (lā ushriku) is absolute, a lifelong commitment to pure monotheism. This is a deliberate contrast to the wealthy man’s implicit shirk—associating his own effort and wealth as partners in his success. The believer’s statement is not just a creed but a shield. Because he has made Allah his sole Lord, he is protected from the delusions of wealth. He recognizes that his poor condition does not diminish his Lord’s greatness, and the other man’s rich gardens do not indicate a flaw in God’s justice. This verse teaches the power of a clear, personal testimony. In the face of a world that measures people by their possessions, the believer redefines honour through faith. His words are calm, firm, and unshaken by the wealth displayed before him. This is the second time in the surah that a believer makes such a declaration, echoing the words of the youths in the cave who said, “We will never call upon any god besides Him.” The consistent thread of Tawhid is the sword of the believers in every age, cutting through the glittering illusions of power and status.
39 | If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed! There is no power except with Allah!’ Even though you see me inferior to you in wealth and offspring, | The believer now gently corrects his companion, teaching him the words he should have uttered when he entered his magnificent garden. “If only you had said, upon entering your property, ‘This is what Allah has willed! There is no power except with Allah!’” The phrase “mā shā’ Allāh” is an attribution of all that exists to the will of God. By saying it, a person acknowledges that the beauty and productivity of the garden is not his own doing but the result of God’s will. The second phrase, “lā quwwata illā billāh,” is a negation of any independent power or strength. It is a confession that the garden’s maintenance, protection, and continued existence depend entirely on God’s power, not human effort. Together, these phrases form a complete antidote to arrogance. The believer is not scolding the wealthy man for his words but expressing a profound wish that he had possessed this spiritual awareness. He then makes a striking admission: “Even though you see me inferior to you in wealth and offspring.” The believer is fully aware of his own worldly status. He does not deny that he has less money and fewer children, two of the primary measures of status in society. Yet he does not allow this disparity to affect his faith or his sense of self-worth. He understands that value is not measured by these metrics. This is a powerful lesson in self-confidence rooted in faith. The believer is not bitter about his poverty, nor is he impressed by the other’s riches. He stands on the truth and offers sincere advice. The verse shows that a believer can acknowledge material inferiority without feeling diminished, because their real treasure lies in their relationship with God. It is this unshakable inner strength that enables him to deliver the warning in the next verse with clarity and courage.
40 | perhaps my Lord will grant me ˹something˺ better than your garden, and send down upon your garden a thunderbolt from the sky, turning it into a barren waste. | The believer continues his speech with a statement of hope for himself and a warning for his companion, both anchored in the power of God. “Perhaps my Lord will grant me something better than your garden.” The word “perhaps” (‘asā) expresses hopeful expectation, not doubt in God’s ability. He trusts that the God who gave his companion the gardens is able to give him, the believer, something even better—whether in this world or, more importantly, in the Hereafter. This shows that he does not covet the specific garden but desires what is with God. His hope is not empty optimism but confidence in divine generosity. Then he delivers the warning: “and send down upon your garden a thunderbolt from the sky, turning it into a barren waste.” A “ḥusbān” is a calamity from heaven, like a destructive hail, fire, or lightning, that can obliterate vegetation completely. The resulting “barren waste” (ṣa‘īdan zalaqan) is a ground so smooth and bare that nothing can grow, a stark contrast to the luxurious gardens. The believer is not cursing his companion out of malice; he is warning him of the possible consequence of his ingratitude and disbelief. He knows that God is capable of removing the blessing as easily as He gave it. This warning is the culmination of the believer’s sincere counsel. He has reminded the rich man of his creation, declared his own faith, and now points to the reality of divine power over the very garden the man thinks will never perish. The stage is set for the next section, where exactly what the believer warned of comes to pass, and the wealthy man’s arrogant words are answered by the swift decree of God.
41 | Or its water may sink ˹into the earth˺, and then you will never be able to seek it out.” | The believing companion continues his warning by presenting another dimension of vulnerability that the wealthy man had overlooked. “Or its water may sink into the earth.” The river that flowed between the two gardens was the source of life for all that vegetation. Without water, the gardens would be nothing. The believer points out that this water is not an independent resource but a gift that God can withdraw at any moment. He imagines a scenario where the water disappears deep underground, beyond the reach of any digging or machinery. The phrase “and then you will never be able to seek it out” emphasizes the absolute helplessness that would follow. The verb “tabtaghiya” suggests searching with effort and determination, but the outcome would be total failure. No human power, no amount of wealth, and no manpower could retrieve what God had caused to vanish. This warning is especially powerful because it moves the threat from a dramatic thunderbolt from the sky to a quiet, hidden disappearance beneath the earth. The man’s pride was in what was visible and measurable—the grapes, the palms, the crops, the flowing river. But the true source of it all was invisible, resting in God’s command. If the water simply sank away, the beauty would wither silently, without a sound, and the proud owner would be left helplessly wringing his hands. The believer is teaching a fundamental lesson: all means of sustenance are held by God’s hand. The water we rely on, the air we breathe, the health we enjoy—all are divine trusts that can be recalled. The wealthy man had spoken as if his property was eternal and his wealth guaranteed, but the believer dismantles that illusion by reminding him of the hidden, fragile threads that hold worldly success together. The warning is not given with spite but with sincere concern, hoping that the man will awaken to reality before it is too late.
42 | And so all his produce was ˹totally˺ ruined, so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it, while it had collapsed on its trellises. He cried, “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord ˹in worship˺!” | The warning of the believing companion was not mere speculation. “And so all his produce was totally ruined” signals the swift execution of divine decree. The word “uḥīṭa” conveys the sense of being completely encircled and destroyed. Everything he owned perished. His fruits, his crops, his vines—all gone. The reaction is painted in a vivid, poignant image: “so he started to wring his hands for all he had spent on it.” Wringing one’s hands is a universal gesture of extreme regret, grief, and helplessness. He was physically expressing the pain of seeing his investment and effort vanish. The phrase “for all he had spent on it” highlights his attachment to the material cost—the money, labour, and time he had poured into the gardens. His heart was entirely bound to these expenditures. The scene is made more devastating by the detail “while it had collapsed on its trellises.” The garden structures, the wooden frameworks that once supported the vines, had fallen, and the vines themselves lay in ruin. What was once a picture of order and beauty was now a scene of wreckage. Then comes his cry of realization: “Alas! I wish I had never associated anyone with my Lord in worship!” The Arabic “yā laytanī” is a lament of irreversible loss. In the moment of disaster, he finally understood the root of his error. He recognized that his boastful attitude, his reliance on his own power, and his denial of the afterlife were forms of associating partners with God. He had made his wealth a partner in his sense of security, his manpower a partner in his strength, and his own self a partner in control. His regret, however, came too late. This verse is a sobering illustration that the veils of pride often fall only when the punishment arrives, and then repentance is no longer accepted. It is a call to repent while the opportunity of life remains.
43 | And he had no manpower to help him against Allah, nor could he ˹even˺ help himself. | The verse zeroes in on the complete and utter isolation of the wealthy man in the face of divine decree. “And he had no manpower to help him against Allah.” Earlier, he had boasted of being superior in manpower, meaning his retinue of family, servants, and followers. He thought that his numbers made him strong and secure. But when the calamity struck, none of them were of any use. No army of servants could restore the ruined gardens. No family could reverse what God had decreed. The word “fi’ah” means a group or faction prepared to assist, but against the will of God, all such groups dissolve into nothingness. The second part intensifies the helplessness: “nor could he even help himself.” The Arabic “wa mā kāna muntaṣiran” implies that he could not defend or rescue himself. There was no inner resource, no clever strategy, no personal strength that could save him. The very self that he had placed at the centre of his existence was powerless. This is the inevitable endpoint of every arrogance that fails to recognize the sovereignty of God. The verse functions as a universal principle: when God’s punishment or a trial arrives, the props that a person relied on—wealth, social connections, intelligence, status—all crumble. This reality was not only a lesson for the disbeliever in the parable but a warning to the Quraysh and all who read the Quran. The early Muslims, who faced the might and wealth of the Quraysh, were reminded that those who appeared invincible could be reduced to absolute weakness in a moment. It also serves as a personal reflection for every believer: do I place my trust in my own resources, or in the One who controls all resources? The collapse of the man’s false sense of security is now complete, setting the stage for the declaration of true support in the following verse.
44 | At this time, support comes ˹only˺ from Allah—the True ˹Lord˺. He is best in reward and best in outcome. | From the ruins of the disbeliever’s false hopes, the Quran draws the ultimate lesson. “At this time, support comes only from Allah.” The phrase “at this time” (hunālika) refers to the moment of judgment, either in this life when calamity strikes, or on the Day of Resurrection, or both. When all else fails, the only real protection and aid is with God. The word “al-walāyah” implies loyalty, support, and guardianship. It belongs exclusively to Allah, “the True Lord.” His attribute of al-Ḥaqq, the True, contrasts with all false supports. Wealth, manpower, and personal abilities are fleeting falsehoods; God alone is the permanent Reality whose help truly matters. The verse then adds a beautiful conclusion: “He is best in reward and best in outcome.” God’s reward for the believers is far superior to anything the world can offer. The gardens of Paradise eclipse any earthly garden. His outcome, meaning the final consequence for those who rely on Him, is the best possible ending. Even for the wealthy man in the parable, the best outcome would have been to turn to God, but he lost it through his own choice. This verse is a direct echo of the earlier description of the believers’ reward and a reaffirmation of the surah’s central theme: reliance on God alone. For the Prophet’s companions who had left their wealth and homes for the sake of faith, this was a powerful reassurance. They might have lost worldly support, but they had gained the support of the True Lord. The lesson extends to all believers: in moments of crisis, when human support vanishes, one must turn to Allah with full certainty that His guardianship is real, His reward is beyond imagination, and His planned outcome is always the best, even if immediate appearances suggest otherwise.
45 | And give them a parable of this worldly life. ˹It is˺ like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky. Then they ˹soon˺ turn into chaff scattered by the wind. And Allah is fully capable of ˹doing˺ all things. | The story of the two gardens naturally leads into a broader, universal parable about the nature of worldly life itself. The Prophet is instructed, “And give them a parable of this worldly life.” The parable serves to cement the lesson in the listener’s mind through a scene they witness every season. “It is like the plants of the earth, thriving when sustained by the rain We send down from the sky.” Rain falls, and the earth bursts into life. Vegetation becomes dense, green, and vibrant, looking magnificent and permanent. This stage corresponds to youth, health, wealth, and worldly prosperity when a person feels they are at their peak. Then comes the inevitable turn: “Then they soon turn into chaff scattered by the wind.” The lush plants dry out, become yellow and brittle, and are broken into pieces. The wind blows, and the fragments scatter, leaving the ground bare as if nothing was ever there. The word “hushīman” describes dry, broken stubble, and “tadhrūhu ar-riyāḥ” paints the picture of the wind dispersing it without any power to resist. This is the lifecycle of every worldly beauty and achievement. The phrase “And Allah is fully capable of doing all things” serves as both a comfort and a warning. He has the power to give and to take away, to bring life and to cause death, to create and to resurrect. This parable distills the entire message of the surah into a single, memorable image. The cave youths, the wealthy man’s gardens, and all human civilizations follow this pattern. The believer who internalizes this parable holds the world with an open hand, enjoying God’s blessings without being fooled by their apparent permanence, always preparing for the eternal life to come.
46 | Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life, but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope. | Having given the parable of vegetation, the Quran now specifies two of the most potent attractions of worldly life: “Wealth and children are the adornment of this worldly life.” Wealth and offspring are specifically highlighted because they are the primary measures of status and success in almost every society. The wealthy man in the parable had both, and they were the source of his boast. The word “zinah” means adornment or ornament; it is something beautiful on the surface but not the substance. An adornment can be taken away, and it does not define the true worth of the thing it decorates. This reframes the entire human pursuit of these things. They are not evil in themselves, but they are tests, decorations that can distract from the real purpose of life. Then the verse provides the alternative: “but the everlasting good deeds are far better with your Lord in reward and in hope.” Al-bāqiyāt aṣ-ṣāliḥāt, the enduring righteous deeds, are acts of worship, charity, prayer, remembrance of God, and good character that outlast death. Their reward is “khayrun ‘inda rabbika,” better in the sight of your Lord in terms of recompense. And they are “khayrun amalan,” better as a source of hope. A person can place their ultimate hope in their deeds done for God, whereas hope placed in wealth and children is often disappointed. This verse does not call for the abandonment of family or wealth; it calls for a correct ordering of the heart. The believer may enjoy the adornment of the world, but their true investment and hope lie in the deeds that will endure beyond the grave. This is the balanced Quranic worldview that prevents both materialistic obsession and escapist monasticism.
47 | ˹Beware of˺ the Day We will blow the mountains away, and you will see the earth laid bare. And We will gather all ˹humankind˺, leaving none behind. | The surah now turns to a vivid scene from the Day of Judgment, connecting the themes of the transient world and eternal reward. The verse begins with an implicit warning: “Beware of the Day We will blow the mountains away.” The mountains, symbols of stability, permanence, and immense weight, will be reduced to dust and blown away. This image is meant to shake the listener’s confidence in the material world. If mountains can be annihilated, then what of the gardens and palaces that people boast of? Then, “you will see the earth laid bare.” The earth will become a flat, empty plain without any of its landmarks, vegetation, or structures. Everything that adorned it will be gone, echoing the earlier verse about reducing the earth’s adornments to barren ground. “And We will gather all humankind, leaving none behind.” The gathering is universal and complete. Every human being who ever lived will be resurrected and assembled. No one will be forgotten, missed, or able to hide. The Arabic “fa-lam nughādir minhum aḥadan” emphasizes absolute inclusivity. This verse is a direct rebuttal to the wealthy man’s doubt about the Hour. The scene transitions seamlessly from the destruction of the world to the presentation before God, forcing the listener to visualize their own standing on that plain. The mountains that seem so permanent are mere illusions; the only reality that endures is God and His promise. For the believer, this verse inspires a profound sense of accountability and a reordering of priorities, knowing that the world they see will one day be utterly transformed.
48 | They will be presented before your Lord in rows, ˹and the deniers will be told,˺ “You have surely returned to Us ˹all alone˺ as We created you the first time, although you ˹always˺ claimed that We would never appoint a time for your return.” | The scene on the Day of Judgment continues with the image of humanity standing before God. “They will be presented before your Lord in rows.” The word “ṣaffan” indicates that people will be lined up, orderly and exposed, with no one able to hide behind another. There is no hierarchy, no wealth, no followers to protect a person. Each stands as an individual before the Creator. The address that follows is directed at those who denied the resurrection: “You have surely returned to Us all alone as We created you the first time.” The phrase “all alone” (furādā) hammers home the reality of individual accountability. They come back stripped of wealth, family, and all the worldly things they relied upon, just as they were born naked and empty-handed. This is the ultimate refutation of their arrogance. Then the statement adds a sharp note of rebuke: “although you always claimed that We would never appoint a time for your return.” Their lifelong mockery of the Hour is thrown back at them. The word “za‘amtum” implies a claim that was always false. Standing in that majestic and terrifying presence, they face the undeniable truth of what they used to ridicule. This verse serves as a powerful reminder that the denials and arguments we make in this life will be answered by the sheer reality of the Hereafter. For the Prophet’s opponents who scoffed at the idea of bones being reassembled, this was a direct warning. The imagery is designed to make the listener reflect: on that day, there will be no companion, no wealth, and no argument, only the deeds one has sent forward.
49 | And the record ˹of deeds˺ will be laid ˹open˺, and you will see the wicked in fear of what is ˹written˺ in it. They will cry, “Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?” They will find whatever they did present ˹before them˺. And your Lord will never wrong anyone. | After the presentation in rows, the deeds are brought forth. “And the record of deeds will be laid open.” The book containing every action, word, and intention is placed before each person, completely accessible. The focus then turns to “the wicked” (al-mujrimīn), the criminals who rejected faith. They are described as “in fear of what is written in it.” The Arabic “mushfiqīn” conveys a trembling, anxious fear. They do not want to look, yet they cannot look away. Upon seeing the exhaustive record, they cry out in shock and horror: “Woe to us! What kind of record is this that does not leave any sin, small or large, unlisted?” The exclamation “yā waylatanā” is an expression of utter despair. They are stunned by the meticulous precision of the record. No minor sin (ṣaghīrah) is overlooked, and no major sin (kabīrah) is left out. Everything is counted, everything is present. This directly counters the human tendency to dismiss small sins or to assume that some deeds will be forgotten. Then the verse states: “They will find whatever they did present before them.” There is no escape; their entire life’s work is laid out in front of their eyes. The verse ends with a declaration of perfect divine justice: “And your Lord will never wrong anyone.” The punishment they face is not an act of injustice but the direct consequence of their own actions. God’s record is accurate, and His judgment is fair. This verse is a profound meditation on the all-encompassing nature of divine surveillance. Every thoughtless word, every hidden deed, every forgotten sin is recorded. For the believer, it instills a state of muraqabah—a constant awareness that nothing escapes God’s knowledge, which leads to carefulness in conduct and swift repentance when one falls short.
50 | And ˹remember˺ when We said to the angels, “Prostrate before Adam,” so they all did—but not Iblîs, who was one of the jinn, but he rebelled against the command of his Lord. Would you then take him and his descendants as patrons instead of Me, although they are your enemy? What an evil alternative for the wrongdoers ˹to choose˺! | The surah now shifts to the primordial story of Iblis, connecting his rebellion to the arrogance displayed in the previous parables. “And remember when We said to the angels, ‘Prostrate before Adam.’” This prostration was an act of honour and greeting for the first human, commanded by God. All the angels obeyed immediately, but “not Iblis.” The verse clarifies a critical point: Iblis “was one of the jinn.” He was not an angel but a jinn who, through his devotion, had been elevated to the company of angels. However, his inner nature, made of smokeless fire, was different. His refusal is described as “he rebelled against the command of his Lord.” The word “fasaqa” means he stepped outside obedience with defiance and arrogance. The question then is posed to humanity: “Would you then take him and his descendants as patrons instead of Me, although they are your enemy?” It is the height of irrationality and ingratitude to abandon the worship of the loving Creator and instead follow a sworn enemy whose only goal is human destruction. Iblis is not a neutral figure; he is an active enemy. Taking him and his offspring as awliyā’ means obeying his whispers and making him a partner in allegiance. The verse calls this “an evil alternative for the wrongdoers to choose.” It is a bad exchange (badal), trading the mercy of God for the enmity of Satan. This verse establishes the cosmic backdrop of the human struggle: there is a clear enemy dedicated to leading people astray, and following him is a willful act of self-harm. The stories of the cave youths and the believing companion demonstrate resistance to Satan, while the arrogant wealthy man exemplifies falling into his trap. The connection reminds the believer that the battle against arrogance and materialism is part of this larger spiritual war.
51 | I never called them to witness the creation of the heavens and the earth or ˹even˺ their own creation, nor would I take the misleaders as helpers. | This verse continues the theme of Iblis and his offspring by underlining their complete lack of authority or partnership with God in the act of creation. Allah declares, “I never called them to witness the creation of the heavens and the earth or even their own creation.” The creation of the cosmos and of their own selves was a matter of God’s exclusive will. Neither Iblis nor any other being was present as an observer, advisor, or assistant. They were themselves created, so how could they have witnessed their own coming into existence? This rhetorical point demolishes any basis for taking them as associates. If they had no role in the grandest acts of creation, what power or authority could they possibly have to merit worship or obedience? Then the verse concludes: “nor would I take the misleaders as helpers.” The word “muḍillīn” refers to those who mislead others. God does not need the help of those who lead astray. His plan is carried out without their support, and He does not depend on any creature. This is a direct refutation of the idea that Satan or his followers have any hidden power or that they are a necessary counterpart to divine guidance. Everything occurs by God’s will and power alone. This verse reinforces the absolute sovereignty of God and the utter helplessness of all false objects of worship. For the believer, it is a powerful reminder that the spiritual forces of misguidance have no independent power. They cannot create, they cannot harm without God’s permission, and they are certainly not partners with the Almighty. All reliance should be placed solely on the One who created the heavens and the earth without any need for witnesses or helpers.
52 | And ˹beware of˺ the Day He will say, “Call upon those you claimed were My associate-gods.” So they will call them, but will receive no response. And We will make them ˹all˺ share in the same doom. | The scene returns to the Day of Judgment with a specific episode of humiliation for the polytheists. “And beware of the Day He will say, ‘Call upon those you claimed were My associate-gods.’” The command is a form of rebuke. God, in His knowledge, knows that these supposed partners are false, but He instructs the disbelievers to call upon them in their moment of need, just as they used to do in the world. The polytheists, in desperation, will turn to the idols, saints, or beings they worshipped and call out for help. “But will receive no response.” The Arabic “fa-da‘awhum fa-lam yastajībū lahum” conveys the absolute silence. There is no answer, no aid, no intercession without God’s permission. The idols are lifeless objects, and even those worshipped among the jinn or righteous humans will disown their worshippers. The verse then states: “And We will make them all share in the same doom.” The word “mawbiqan” can mean a barrier of destruction, a shared place of ruin, or a valley in Hell. The worshippers and their false gods will be thrown together into the same punishment. For example, the stone idols will be heated and pressed against their worshippers, adding to the torment. The false gods become a cause of additional suffering, not rescue. This scene is a powerful deterrent against associating anything with God. It exposes the bankruptcy of shirk on the day when it matters most. The warning “beware of” reminds the listener that this Day is certain and one must prepare for it by purifying their faith now. It connects back to the earlier warnings and the parable of the gardens, showing the eternal consequences of what may seem like minor compromises in this life.
53 | The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it, and will find no way to avoid it. | The punishment intensifies. “The wicked will see the Fire and realize that they are bound to fall into it.” The verb “ra’ā” indicates seeing with their own eyes, not just knowing theoretically. The reality of Hell will be directly in their sight. The phrase “they are bound to fall into it” (annahum muwāqi‘ūhā) means they have a firm conviction that there is no escape and that they are inevitably heading into it. This is the moment of dreadful certainty before the actual entry. The psychological torment of seeing the destination and knowing it is inescapable is a punishment in itself. Then, “and will find no way to avoid it.” The word “maṣrifan” means a place to turn away, a detour, or an escape route. They will desperately look for any exit, any way to divert from this fate, but none will exist. The Fire will surround them just as the walls of the Fire surrounded the wrongdoers described earlier. This verse represents the culmination of a life spent in heedlessness. Every warning they ignored, every opportunity for repentance they rejected, now stands before them as an unyielding reality. For the believer, the vividness of this scene serves as a powerful motivator to seek God’s protection from the Fire and to live a life of obedience and repentance. It is an act of divine mercy that the Quran paints such images so that we might avoid them while there is still time. The verse also completes the picture of the judgment scenes: the gathering, the presentation of the books, the confrontation with false gods, and now the sight of the final abode, all leading to an unchangeable fate.
54 | We have surely set forth in this Quran every ˹kind of˺ lesson for people, but humankind is the most argumentative of all beings. | The Quran now reflects on its own method, having just presented parables, narratives, and warnings. “We have surely set forth in this Quran every kind of lesson for people.” The word “ṣarrafnā” implies variety, repetition, and careful arrangement. The Quran contains stories, laws, parables, threats, promises, and logical arguments, all designed to reach every type of heart and mind. No essential guidance is omitted. This rich tapestry of lessons is a mercy, providing multiple avenues for people to understand and return to their Lord. Yet, despite this comprehensive presentation, there is a tragic human response: “but humankind is the most argumentative of all beings.” The word “jadal” refers to argumentation, disputation, and quarrelling. Instead of receiving the lessons with humility and acting upon them, many people use their intellect to argue against the truth, raise objections, and seek loopholes. This was evident in the Quraysh who questioned the Prophet about the cave sleepers and other matters not to learn but to test and dispute. It is also a general characteristic of those whose hearts are not receptive. They will argue about trivial details, like the number of the sleepers, while ignoring the profound lesson of resurrection. The verse serves as a mirror for every reader: when we engage with the Quran, is our approach one of seeking guidance with an open heart, or one of debating and finding excuses? The contrast between God’s exhaustive guidance and human disputatiousness highlights the depth of divine mercy and the tragedy of human stubbornness. The following verses will illustrate the consequences of this argumentative attitude and clarify the role of messengers in the face of it.
55 | And nothing prevents people from believing when guidance comes to them and from seeking their Lord’s forgiveness except ˹their demand˺ to meet the same fate of earlier deniers or that the torment would confront them face to face. | The verse diagnoses the real barrier to faith. “And nothing prevents people from believing when guidance comes to them and from seeking their Lord’s forgiveness…” The Quran and the Prophet have brought clear guidance, and the door to forgiveness through repentance is wide open. There is no intellectual or practical obstacle on God’s side. So what is it that stops them? The answer is a single, stubborn condition: “except their demand to meet the same fate of earlier deniers or that the torment would confront them face to face.” The disbelievers were not asking for more evidence; they were essentially demanding that God bring upon them a visible, overwhelming punishment like the flood of Noah, the winds of ‘Ad, or the thunderbolt of Thamud, or that they should see the torment of the Hereafter directly in this life. They said things like, “O Allah, if this is the truth, then rain down stones upon us!” They were mocking and challenging, not sincerely seeking. But when such punishment arrives, it is too late to believe; it is the end. The verse exposes the irrationality of this stance. It is like a person who refuses to believe a warning about a collapsing building unless the roof falls on his head. True guidance requires acting on the unseen with faith, not demanding to see the punishment first. By linking this attitude to the fate of earlier deniers, the Quran reminds the Quraysh and all such people that history is filled with examples of nations that made the same demand and were destroyed when the punishment came. This verse is a warning that God’s mercy is available now through faith and seeking forgiveness, but persisting in the demand for punishment will only bring about that very punishment, leaving no room for return.
56 | We do not send the messengers except as deliverers of good news and warners. But the disbelievers argue in falsehood, ˹hoping˺ to discredit the truth with it, and make a mockery of My revelations and warnings. | This verse clarifies the mission of the messengers in light of the previous argument about punishment. “We do not send the messengers except as deliverers of good news and warners.” The role of the Prophet is not to bring the punishment on demand. He is a mubashshir (bearer of glad tidings) for those who believe, and a nadhīr (warner) for those who reject. His purpose is to convey, not to compel. The authority to send punishment belongs solely to God, according to His wisdom and timeline. This is a consolation for the Prophet, who was grieved by their rejection and their demands for immediate torment. Then the verse describes the disbelievers’ reaction: “But the disbelievers argue in falsehood, hoping to discredit the truth with it.” Instead of accepting the good news and heeding the warning, they engage in debate using false arguments (bāṭil). Their goal is not to find the truth but to refute and overcome it (li-yud’ḥiḍū bihi al-ḥaqq). They twist words, raise irrelevant questions, and employ sophistry. They also “make a mockery of My revelations and warnings.” They turn the signs of God and the warnings of punishment into objects of ridicule, laughing at them instead of trembling before them. This behaviour is the height of spiritual corruption. The verse highlights the patience required of the messenger; his duty is simply to continue delivering the message with the qualities of a bearer of glad tidings and a warner, leaving the outcome to God. For the believer, this verse teaches that the truth will always face arguments based on falsehood, and the proper response is steadfastness, not despair. It also underscores the seriousness of mocking divine signs, a sin that deeply hardens the heart.
57 | And who does more wrong than those who, when reminded of their Lord’s revelations, turn away from them and forget what their own hands have done? We have certainly cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend this ˹Quran˺—and deafness in their ears. And if you ˹O Prophet˺ invite them to ˹true˺ guidance, they will never be ˹rightly˺ guided. | The verse describes the most severe type of wrongdoing. “And who does more wrong than those who, when reminded of their Lord’s revelations, turn away from them and forget what their own hands have done?” The rhetorical question implies that no one is more unjust. They are reminded of the truth, but they actively turn away (a‘raḍa). Their turning away is not passive ignorance but deliberate avoidance. Moreover, they “forget what their own hands have done.” They suppress the memory of their own sins, making them unwilling to face the need for repentance. Because of this persistent rejection and heedlessness, a divine consequence follows: “We have certainly cast veils over their hearts—leaving them unable to comprehend this Quran—and deafness in their ears.” The veils (akinnah) on the heart prevent intellectual and spiritual understanding. The deafness (waqran) in the ears blocks the ability to hear it with benefit. This is not an arbitrary punishment but the natural outcome of their repeated choice to turn away. When a person constantly ignores the truth, their spiritual faculties atrophy and become sealed. Then the final, sobering judgment: “And if you invite them to true guidance, they will never be rightly guided.” This is not a statement that God’s mercy is limited, but that for those who have reached this stage of sealed hearts, no external effort will change them. The Prophet’s role is to convey, but guidance belongs to God. This verse is both a warning about the danger of persistent sin and a comfort for the Prophet that their rejection is not his failure. It closes the passage on the deniers with a note of finality, before transitioning to God’s mercy and the next story.
58 | Your Lord is the All-Forgiving, Full of Mercy. If He were to seize them ˹immediately˺ for what they commit, He would have certainly hastened their punishment. But they have an appointed time, from which they will find no refuge. | Immediately after the severe warning about sealed hearts, the Quran balances the discourse with a reminder of God’s attributes of mercy. “Your Lord is the All-Forgiving, Full of Mercy.” The words “Al-Ghafūr” and “Dhū ar-Raḥmah” emphasize that forgiveness and mercy are central to His dealing with creation. The verse then presents a profound explanation for why the disbelievers are not immediately destroyed despite their crimes: “If He were to seize them immediately for what they commit, He would have certainly hastened their punishment.” If God were to exact instant retribution for every sin, no human being would remain on the earth. The fact that they are given respite, enjoy provisions, and have time to reflect is a direct manifestation of His mercy. However, this respite should not be mistaken for approval or weakness. “But they have an appointed time, from which they will find no refuge.” There is an “ajal,” a fixed term decreed by God for individuals and nations. When that term arrives, there is no escape, no shelter, and no delay. The mercy of God offers a window of opportunity for repentance, but once it closes, justice prevails. This verse provides a complete framework for understanding the delay of punishment in this life. It is an act of grace, giving people chance after chance to turn back. The Prophet is being taught to view the persistence of the disbelievers through this lens: God is giving them time, so he should not grieve himself to death over their current state. For the believer, it is a call to never despair of God’s mercy for oneself, while also never feeling secure from His plan. The appointed time is certain, and preparation must happen before it arrives.
59 | Those ˹are the˺ societies We destroyed when they persisted in wrong, and We had set a time for their destruction. | The verse points to history as evidence for the principle just stated. “Those are the societies We destroyed when they persisted in wrong.” The word “tilka al-qurā” refers to the well-known towns and nations of the past—‘Ad, Thamud, the people of Lot, and others—whose ruins were known to the Arabs along their trade routes. These societies were not destroyed arbitrarily. The phrase “when they persisted in wrong” (lammā ẓalamū) indicates that the punishment came as a result of their continuous injustice and oppression, despite warnings. The root ẓulm includes disbelief, idolatry, and moral corruption. They crossed the limits and refused to reform. Then the verse adds, “and We had set a time for their destruction.” The destruction had an appointed term (maw‘idan). It was not an impulsive act; God had predetermined the moment when their respite would end and their punishment would begin. This reinforces the message that the current respite given to the Meccan disbelievers is part of the same divine pattern. The history they see around them is a direct warning. The verse also serves as a comfort to the believers who might wonder why the tyrants seem to get away with their injustice. The pattern is clear: the term is fixed, and when it comes, the destruction is complete. The next section of the surah will seamlessly transition into the story of Moses and Al-Khaḍir, which further explores the theme of divine wisdom and the hidden timing of events. The destruction of the unjust societies and the appointed times all operate under a knowledge that human beings may not immediately grasp, a perfect introduction to the lessons Moses will learn about patience with the unseen wisdom of God.
60 | And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his young assistant, “I will never give up until I reach the junction of the two seas, even if I travel for ages.” | The surah now begins the third major story, that of Moses and the righteous servant of God, traditionally known as Al-Khaḍir. The verse opens, “And remember when Moses said to his young assistant.” The young assistant, identified in hadith as Yusha‘ ibn Nūn (Joshua), was in the service of the great prophet Moses. Moses makes a solemn declaration of intent: “I will never give up until I reach the junction of the two seas, even if I travel for ages.” The “junction of the two seas” (majma‘ al-baḥrayn) is the location where Moses was divinely informed he would meet a servant of God who possessed knowledge different from his own. The determination in Moses’ words is striking: “I will never give up” (lā abraḥu) means he will not cease his journey. The phrase “even if I travel for ages” (aw amḍiya ḥuquban) indicates that he was prepared to go for a very long time, possibly years or decades, without stopping. This shows the immense zeal of Moses for knowledge and his readiness to endure hardship in its pursuit. The background to this journey is narrated in Sahih Bukhari and Muslim: Moses was once asked who was the most knowledgeable person, and he replied, “I am.” God then revealed to him that there was a servant at the junction of the two seas who had knowledge that Moses did not possess. Moses, with the humility of a great prophet, immediately set out to find him. This story introduces the theme of knowledge, its levels, and the need for patience when encountering things whose wisdom is not immediately apparent. The journey is a masterclass in the adab (etiquette) of seeking knowledge, and it begins with Moses’ sincere resolve and willingness to undertake great effort.
61 | But when they ˹finally˺ reached the point where the seas met, they forgot their ˹salted˺ fish, and it made its way into the sea, slipping away ˹wondrously˺. | The journey that Moses undertook with his young assistant finally reaches its first milestone. “But when they finally reached the point where the seas met” refers to the exact location God had described, where the two bodies of water converge. Commentators have discussed where this might be—some suggest it is where the Red Sea meets the Indian Ocean, others say it is where the sea of Moses’ time met a river, but the precise geography is not the Quran’s concern. The focus is on what happened there as a sign. “They forgot their salted fish” tells us that they had carried a fish, likely salted and dried, as their travel provision. The forgetting was not a random lapse but part of the divine arrangement to mark the spot where the knowledgeable servant would be found. In a well-known hadith narrated by Ubayy ibn Ka‘b, the Prophet ﷺ explained that the fish was dead and placed in a basket. When they reached the appointed place, the fish came to life, sprang out of the basket, and “made its way into the sea, slipping away wondrously.” The Arabic “fa-ttakhadha sabīlahu fī al-baḥri ‘ajaban” captures the miraculous nature of the event. The fish’s path into the water became a tunnel or a passage, and the water around it stood still like an arch. For Yusha‘, this was an astonishing sight, but he was distracted and forgot to mention it. The forgetting itself was a test and a means to make them retrace their steps. This verse teaches that even seemingly small lapses of memory can be part of a larger divine plan to guide a person to a deeper truth. It also shows that God’s signs can appear in the most ordinary things—a fish, a rock, a moment of rest—but recognizing them requires attentiveness. The servant of God was near, and the sign was meant to pinpoint the location, yet human forgetfulness nearly caused them to pass it by. The story subtly reminds the believer that spiritual milestones are often preceded by tests of alertness and that nothing in the journey of seeking knowledge happens without divine orchestration.
62 | When they had passed further, he said to his assistant, “Bring us our meal! We have certainly been exhausted by today’s journey.” | After leaving the junction of the two seas, Moses and Yusha‘ continued walking. “When they had passed further” indicates they travelled beyond the appointed spot without realizing they had already reached it. Human determination, even that of a great prophet, can sometimes overshoot the mark when divine guidance has not yet fully clarified the location. Moses then turned to his assistant and said, “Bring us our meal! We have certainly been exhausted by today’s journey.” The request for food was natural; they had been travelling with great resolve, and the physical demands of the journey had caught up with them. The word “laqad laqīnā” emphasizes that they had encountered severe fatigue and toil on this particular stage of their travel. This moment of physical weakness and hunger became the occasion for remembering the forgotten fish. It is a profound lesson that our bodily needs, which we often see as distractions from spiritual pursuits, can actually serve as the trigger for remembering divine signs. The hunger of Moses opened the door to the recollection of the miracle. The verse also highlights the humaneness of the prophets. Moses was not a superhuman being immune to tiredness or hunger. He felt the strain of the journey just as any of us would, yet his quest for knowledge did not stop. He sought to replenish his strength so he could continue. For the seeker of knowledge today, this is a reminder that taking care of one’s physical needs is part of the journey, not a betrayal of it. The request for the meal sets the stage for Yusha‘s response, which will bring the forgotten miracle back to memory and redirect their path precisely to where God intended them to be.
63 | He replied, “Do you remember when we rested by the rock? ˹That is when˺ I forgot the fish. None made me forget to mention this except Satan. And the fish made its way into the sea miraculously.” | Yusha‘’s response immediately clarifies the mystery. “He replied, ‘Do you remember when we rested by the rock?’” The rock was a specific landmark near the junction of the two seas. It was at that place, while they paused to rest, that the miracle occurred. Yusha‘ then confesses, “That is when I forgot the fish.” His forgetting was not mere negligence; it was an oversight that had profound consequences. He then provides a striking theological explanation: “None made me forget to mention this except Satan.” This statement teaches an important aspect of Islamic belief. Forgetfulness in matters of duty and obedience can be from Satan, who seeks to distract humans from recognizing God’s signs. Yusha‘ does not absolve himself entirely but attributes the cause to Satanic influence, showing an awareness of the spiritual battle behind even small lapses. However, he also describes the miracle with wonder: “And the fish made its way into the sea miraculously.” The word “‘ajaban” conveys his astonishment. The fish coming to life and taking a path through the sea was a clear, unmistakable sign that they had been at the appointed place. His description confirms that what he witnessed was no ordinary event but a tangible act of divine power. This verse demonstrates the importance of honest reporting in the student-teacher relationship. Yusha‘ could have hidden his forgetfulness out of embarrassment, but he truthfully recounted what happened, including the miracle. His honesty provided Moses with the exact information needed to recognize that this was the sign they were seeking. It also shows that even the closest companions of prophets can be momentarily distracted by Satan, but that does not diminish their overall virtue. The sincere admission of a mistake and the accurate reporting of a sign pave the way for the next phase of the journey.
64 | Moses responded, “That is ˹exactly˺ what we were looking for.” So they returned, retracing their footsteps. | Upon hearing Yusha‘s account, Moses immediately recognized the sign. “Moses responded, ‘That is exactly what we were looking for.’” The Arabic “dhālika mā kunnā nabghi” expresses a moment of clarity and confirmation. The miraculous revival of the fish was the precise marker God had provided. There was no hesitation, no doubt. Moses connected the sign with the goal, demonstrating the keen spiritual insight of a prophet. He understood that the God who brought the fish to life would also guide him to the servant of knowledge. The response also shows Moses’ humility. Despite his own immense status as a prophet who spoke directly with God, he eagerly sought a teacher from whom he could learn. The phrase “what we were looking for” places Moses and his assistant together as joint seekers, showing the collegial nature of the quest. Then they took action: “So they returned, retracing their footsteps.” The word “fa-irtaddā” means they turned back, carefully following the same path they had just walked. They did not bemoan the wasted steps or complain about the extra effort. The joy of finding the sign made the return journey light. This teaches a profound lesson: when a person realizes they have passed by a place of spiritual benefit or a source of knowledge, they should be willing to go back, even if it means effort and humbling themselves. The world often tells us to always move forward, but the path to divine wisdom sometimes requires a return—a retracing of steps, a revisiting of a moment where God’s sign was present but overlooked. The journey of seeking knowledge is not linear; it includes detours, forgetfulness, and corrections, all of which are part of the training. Their return to the rock leads directly to the encounter that will change Moses’ understanding of knowledge itself.
65 | There they found a servant of Ours, to whom We had granted mercy from Us and enlightened with knowledge of Our Own. | They arrived at the rock and found the one they had travelled so far to meet. “There they found a servant of Ours” introduces the figure without naming him, though authentic hadith identify him as Al-Khaḍir. The description “servant of Ours” (‘abdan min ‘ibādinā) is the highest honour; it places him in the category of devoted worshippers of God. The phrase immediately dispels any idea that this person was an angel or a prophet in the conventional legislative sense, as the majority of scholars hold that he was a righteous servant given special knowledge. The verse then describes two qualities: “to whom We had granted mercy from Us.” The mercy is a direct gift from God’s own presence, implying a special intimacy and divine care. This mercy enveloped him inwardly and outwardly, making him a recipient of God’s compassion and blessings. The second quality is “and enlightened with knowledge of Our Own.” The word “‘ilman min ladunnā” is profoundly significant. It is “ladunnī” knowledge, knowledge directly from God, not acquired through study or human instruction. It is an inner illumination regarding the hidden realities of events, the secrets of divine decree, and the wisdom behind occurrences. This type of knowledge is different from the knowledge of law and prophecy that Moses had. Moses was given the Torah, a clear shariah for his people, while Al-Khaḍir was given insight into the unseen layers of events. The combination of mercy and knowledge made him the perfect teacher for the specific lesson Moses needed: that not everything that appears bad is truly evil in God’s plan, and that human intellect, even prophetic intellect, has limits. This verse elevates the station of sacred knowledge and reminds believers that God bestows His gifts upon whom He wills, opening unseen doors of understanding to His sincere servants.
66 | Moses said to him, “May I follow you, provided that you teach me some of the right guidance you have been taught?” | The encounter begins with Moses displaying exemplary etiquette. “Moses said to him, ‘May I follow you?’” The form is a polite, humble request. Moses, the great prophet to whom God spoke directly, asks for permission to be a follower. He does not demand or assume authority. This is the first lesson for every student of knowledge: approach the teacher with humility, even if the student holds high worldly or religious status. The phrase “provided that you teach me some of the right guidance you have been taught” clarifies the purpose of the following. Moses is specific: he wants to learn from the “rushd,” the sound guidance and wisdom that Al-Khaḍir has been taught. He acknowledges that the knowledge Al-Khaḍir possesses is a gift from God, not his own invention. Moses uses the word “mimmā ‘ullimta” (from what you have been taught), showing respect for both the teacher and the ultimate source of knowledge. This request also indicates Moses’ intellectual humility. He already possessed the Torah and was the leader of the Israelites, but he recognized that God’s knowledge is vast and that there were areas of understanding he had not been given. The verse is a direct refutation of the arrogance that sometimes afflicts learned people. No matter how much one knows, there is always someone who knows something different, and the path of wisdom is to seek it with an open heart. The seeker is not diminished by following; he is elevated. Moses’ words became a timeless model for the teacher-student relationship, demonstrating that the pursuit of knowledge is an act of worship that requires patience, humility, and a sincere intention to follow the truth wherever it leads.
67 | He said, “You certainly cannot be patient ˹enough˺ with me. | Al-Khaḍir’s response is direct and realistic. “He said, ‘You certainly cannot be patient enough with me.’” The Arabic “innaka lan tastaṭī‘a ma‘iya ṣabran” uses the emphatic particle “lan” to indicate that Moses will not be able to endure what he will witness. This is not a rejection but a statement of fact based on Al-Khaḍir’s divine knowledge. He knows the nature of the events that will unfold and the natural human reaction to apparent injustice, harm, and irrationality. Moses, whose mission was to establish justice, uphold the law, and command good and forbid evil, would inevitably be shocked by actions that seemed to contradict all these principles. The patience required is not ordinary patience in the face of hardship, but a specific endurance in the face of cognitive dissonance—seeing things that make no sense according to conventional morality and law. Al-Khaḍir is essentially preparing Moses for a test of his ability to suspend judgment. This verse teaches a crucial principle: there are levels of knowledge and wisdom that require an entirely different kind of patience. A person may be patient with physical pain or personal insult, but being patient with intellectual confusion, with seeing an apparently evil act that one cannot yet justify, is far more difficult. The teacher’s role here is to set clear expectations and warn the student of the challenge ahead. This is a sign of Al-Khaḍir’s own wisdom and mercy; he is not setting Moses up for failure without warning. The verse also implies that the journey ahead will be psychologically and spiritually demanding, preparing the listener for the three shocking events to come. It is a reminder that true learning often involves the painful process of unlearning one’s initial assumptions.
68 | And how can you be patient with what is beyond your ˹realm of˺ knowledge?” | Al-Khaḍir elaborates on his warning with a rhetorical question. “And how can you be patient with what is beyond your realm of knowledge?” The phrase “mā lam tuḥiṭ bihi khubran” literally means “that which you have not encompassed in knowledge.” The word “khabar” implies deep, comprehensive understanding. Al-Khaḍir is saying that the events Moses will witness are outside the scope of his prophetic knowledge and his human experience. Moses’ knowledge, vast as it was, was primarily the outer, legal, and revealed knowledge for guiding a community. Al-Khaḍir’s knowledge is the inner knowledge of hidden realities and divine decrees. Without that deeper context, the actions will appear completely wrong. The question underscores a fundamental epistemological point: a person cannot have full patience with something they do not understand. Impatience arises when the mind cannot make sense of what it sees. If a doctor performs a painful surgery on a child, the child screams and resists because they lack the knowledge that the pain is healing. The parent who understands the surgeon’s intent can remain patient. Moses will be like the child who sees only the surface pain, and Al-Khaḍir will be like the surgeon who sees the cure. This verse teaches humility about the limits of one’s own knowledge. In life, believers often encounter situations that seem utterly unfair or senseless. This verse reminds them that they may be in a position similar to Moses, lacking the complete picture that God possesses. The proper response is not to rush to judgment or to lose faith, but to trust that the One who sees the full picture has a wisdom that will eventually be revealed or rewarded. This is a profound comfort for those going through inexplicable trials, and a caution against condemning what one does not fully understand.
69 | Moses assured ˹him˺, “You will find me patient, Allah willing, and I will not disobey any of your orders.” | Moses, eager to begin his learning, responds with a sincere promise. “Moses assured him, ‘You will find me patient, Allah willing.’” His words are full of resolve. He does not say, “I will definitely be patient,” relying solely on his own strength. Instead, he attaches his promise to the will of God: “Allah willing” (in shā’ Allāh). This is the very etiquette taught earlier in the surah when Allah commanded the Prophet never to say of anything, “I will definitely do it tomorrow,” without adding “if Allah wills.” Moses, the prophet, demonstrates this lesson perfectly. He knows that patience is a virtue granted by God, not merely a product of human willpower. By saying “Allah willing,” he places his trust in God and seeks divine assistance for the challenge ahead. He then adds, “and I will not disobey any of your orders.” This is a comprehensive commitment to the role of a student. He pledges full compliance with the teacher’s instructions, recognizing that the path of learning requires following, not questioning. This is the second time in the surah that a believer makes a solemn declaration of intent to follow guidance. The youths in the cave said, “We will never call upon any god besides Him,” and Moses says, “I will not disobey any of your orders.” Both statements reflect a total submission of the self for the sake of truth. Moses’ promise sets the contract between him and Al-Khaḍir. However, the story will reveal that the reality of witnessing injustice is far more emotionally overwhelming than the abstract idea of being patient. Moses’ sincere intention is clear, and God knows his truthfulness, yet the human element of immediate moral reaction will prove difficult to suppress. This teaches us that good intentions must be accompanied by divine help, and even the best of us can be caught off guard by the intensity of a test.
70 | He responded, “Then if you follow me, do not question me about anything until I ˹myself˺ clarify it for you.” | Al-Khaḍir accepts Moses’ pledge but lays down a strict condition. “He responded, ‘Then if you follow me, do not question me about anything until I myself clarify it for you.’” The condition is absolute: no questions, no objections, no expressions of disapproval, regardless of what Moses sees. The phrase “until I myself clarify it for you” (ḥattā uḥditha laka minhu dhikran) places the entire initiative for explanation in the teacher’s hands. Al-Khaḍir retains the right to explain events only at the time and in the manner he chooses. This condition is not harshness; it is the necessary pedagogical framework for the type of knowledge being transmitted. The lesson Moses is about to learn cannot be absorbed if he interrupts with his own moral reasoning after each event. He must first witness the totality of the actions, feel the weight of the perplexity, and then receive the explanation that ties everything together. This condition is also a test of Moses’ sincerity and his earlier promise. True following means trusting the guide, even when the path seems to lead into darkness. For the student of sacred knowledge, this verse teaches the importance of discipline in the learning process. There are stages in understanding where questioning prematurely can block the deeper insight. The teacher knows the sequence of unveiling, and the student must trust that sequence. This does not promote blind following in all matters; rather, it establishes a specific contract for a specific journey. Al-Khaḍir is not asking Moses to accept falsehood, but to delay his judgment until the hidden wisdom is revealed. With the terms set and accepted, the two set out together, and the astonishing events begin to unfold.
71 | So they set out, but after they had boarded a ship, the man made a hole in it. Moses protested, “Have you done this to drown its people? You have certainly done a terrible thing!” | The journey of learning begins. “So they set out” marks the commencement of their travels together. “But after they had boarded a ship, the man made a hole in it.” The incident happens swiftly. They were welcomed aboard a vessel that was presumably carrying innocent people. Al-Khaḍir’s action was shocking: he deliberately damaged the ship by making a hole in its hull. From any conventional perspective, this was an act of sabotage that endangered the lives of everyone on board. Moses, witnessing this, could not contain his reaction. “Moses protested, ‘Have you done this to drown its people? You have certainly done a terrible thing!’” The Arabic “kharqahā li-tughriqa ahlahā” directly links the hole to the potential drowning. Moses’ mind, trained in justice and the protection of life, immediately saw the apparent crime. His exclamation “laqad ji’ta shay’an imran” uses the word “imr,” meaning a grave, horrifying, and monstrous deed. Moses broke his silence, forgetting the condition he had accepted, because the urgency of the situation overwhelmed him. His protest was not out of arrogance but out of sincere concern for the innocent. This first test targeted Moses’ deep-seated sense of justice. It reveals that even a prophet, when faced with what seems to be clear-cut evil, finds it extremely difficult to remain silent. The verse captures the raw human struggle between a commitment to spiritual discipline and the instinctive moral outrage at visible harm. It also sets up the pattern for the next two events, where each apparent transgression will escalate in severity, testing Moses’ patience to its absolute limit.
72 | He replied, “Did I not say that you cannot have patience with me?” | Al-Khaḍir’s response is not an explanation but a calm reminder of the contract. “He replied, ‘Did I not say that you cannot have patience with me?’” The question is gentle but firm. It points Moses back to his own limitations, which were forewarned. Al-Khaḍir does not show anger or frustration; he simply reiterates the reality he had stated at the outset. This is the role of a wise teacher when the student stumbles: not to humiliate or dismiss, but to remind the student of the agreed-upon terms and the challenge they are facing. The reminder serves multiple purposes. It re-establishes the teacher’s authority without harshness. It helps Moses become conscious of his reaction and recognize that his impatience is exactly what was predicted. This self-awareness is the first step toward growth. The question also implicitly invites Moses to reflect: “If you could not bear this first act, how will you bear what is to come?” The nature of the events will only get more difficult. Al-Khaḍir’s calmness in the face of Moses’ outburst teaches that the bearer of hidden wisdom is not disturbed by the surface judgments of others because they know the deeper reality. For the student of knowledge, this verse is a lesson in receiving correction. When a teacher points out a mistake, the proper response is not defensiveness but acknowledgment. Moses’ next words will demonstrate exactly that. The exchange also deepens the listener’s curiosity: what possible justification could exist for endangering a ship full of people? The answer will come only at the end of the journey, teaching the believer that some divine actions only make sense when the complete picture is revealed.
73 | Moses pleaded, “Excuse me for forgetting, and do not be hard on me.” | Moses’ reaction to the reminder is a model of humility and accountability. “Moses pleaded, ‘Excuse me for forgetting, and do not be hard on me.’” He does not argue, justify his outburst, or claim that the situation was so extreme that his protest was warranted. Instead, he immediately attributes his lapse to forgetfulness. “Lā tu’ākhidhnī” means “Do not take me to task” or “Do not hold me accountable.” He admits that he forgot the condition he had sworn to uphold. The Arabic “nisyān” is the same word used for Yusha‘ forgetting the fish. The parallel is striking: just as Yusha‘ forgot the miracle and was excused, Moses now asks for excusal for his own forgetfulness. This teaches a profound interconnectedness in the story. Both the assistant and the great prophet experienced moments of human weakness, and the proper response was mutual gentleness. Moses then adds, “and do not be hard on me” (wa lā turhiqnī min amrī ‘usran). He is asking Al-Khaḍir not to make the journey overly difficult for him, not to impose a harsh penalty, and to continue to treat him with kindness despite his mistake. The word “‘usran” implies hardship and constriction; Moses is asking for spaciousness and ease in the teacher-student relationship. This is a beautiful example of how a student should recover after a misstep: acknowledge the error without making excuses, attribute it to human frailty, and ask for continued mercy and guidance. Al-Khaḍir’s acceptance of this plea demonstrates the importance of mercy in teaching. The journey will continue, and Moses will be given another chance. This verse instills hope that mistakes on the path of learning do not mean the end of the road, provided the heart remains sincere and humble.
74 | So they proceeded until they came across a boy, and the man killed him. Moses protested, “Have you killed an innocent soul, who killed no one? You have certainly done a horrible thing.” | The journey continues, and the second test is far more severe than the first. “So they proceeded until they came across a boy, and the man killed him.” Without any preamble or explanation, Al-Khaḍir took the life of a young boy. In the scale of moral atrocities, the deliberate killing of a child is among the most heinous. For Moses, this was beyond comprehension. The verse states his protest in powerful terms: “Have you killed an innocent soul, who killed no one?” The Arabic “nafsan zakiyyatan” describes a soul that is pure and innocent, completely free of any crime that could justify execution. Moses emphasizes that this boy had not taken another life, so there was no retribution due. His shock is captured in the second exclamation: “You have certainly done a horrible thing!” The word “nukran” indicates something far more severe than the “imran” he used for the ship incident. It means a deed that is utterly evil, detestable, and unacceptable by every moral standard. This time, Moses’ protest was not about property damage but about a human life. The intensity of his reaction reflects the depth of his commitment to the sanctity of life, which is a core principle of the Mosaic law. From his perspective, the teacher had crossed a red line that no wisdom could justify. The listener, too, is brought to the edge of moral tolerance. How could a merciful servant of God commit such an act? The question hangs in the air, heavy and unresolved. This verse confronts the believer with the most difficult aspect of the story: the acceptance that God’s wisdom can encompass actions that, on the surface, appear absolutely evil. It forces a deep reflection on the limits of human judgment when it lacks knowledge of the unseen.
75 | He answered, “Did I not tell you that you cannot have patience with me?” | Once again, Al-Khaḍir responds with the same calm reminder, repeating exactly the words he used after the first incident. “He answered, ‘Did I not tell you that you cannot have patience with me?’” There is no immediate explanation, no soothing of Moses’ horror. The repetition of the identical phrase is itself a lesson. It reinforces the consistency of the condition. Al-Khaḍir is not reacting emotionally to Moses’ outburst; he is simply holding up the mirror of their original agreement. The calmness in the face of such a severe accusation is extraordinary. It indicates that Al-Khaḍir’s knowledge of the hidden reality made him impervious to the surface judgment. He knew that what he had done was, in its ultimate reality, a mercy and a protection, even though it appeared as the most abominable crime. This repetition also highlights the persistence of human nature. Moses had already stumbled once and been reminded, yet he stumbled again because the test was exponentially harder. The teacher’s patience is remarkable; he does not abandon the student after repeated failure. He simply asks the question again, leaving the door open for Moses to reflect on his own inability to remain silent. The verse subtly teaches that spiritual growth often involves repeated stumbles in the face of escalating trials. The fact that the test became harder after the first failure shows that God was training Moses through these experiences. For the believer, this verse is a comfort: when we fail a test, God may give us another, more difficult one, not to break us, but to build our reliance on Him and to teach us that true patience is a gift that must be sought anew every moment.
76 | Moses replied, “If I ever question you about anything after this, then do not keep me in your company, for by then I would have given you enough of an excuse.” | Moses, now deeply aware of his repeated failure, makes a solemn and final pledge. “Moses replied, ‘If I ever question you about anything after this, then do not keep me in your company.’” He sets a penalty for himself: expulsion from the teacher’s company. This is a serious self-imposed condition because the entire purpose of the journey was to stay with Al-Khaḍir and learn. He is essentially saying, “If I fail again, I forfeit the privilege of your companionship.” Then he adds, “for by then I would have given you enough of an excuse.” The Arabic “balaghta min ladunnī ‘udhran” means that he would have exhausted all valid excuses, and Al-Khaḍir would be fully justified in parting ways with him. Moses acknowledges that two warnings are more than sufficient. A third violation would prove that he cannot abide by the necessary discipline. This is a moment of profound self-awareness and humility. Moses does not promise that he will definitely be patient; he has learned that his own strength is insufficient. Instead, he sets a boundary for the relationship, recognizing that there must be a limit to the teacher’s forbearance. This is a mature understanding of the student-teacher dynamic. A student who repeatedly breaks the rules eventually makes the teaching relationship untenable, regardless of the teacher’s mercy. Moses’ words also reflect the Islamic principle of ‘udhr—giving an excuse and acknowledging when one has been given enough chances. The verse sets the stage for the third and final incident. The listener knows that if Moses breaks his silence again, the journey will end. The tension is palpable, and the next event will bring the story to its climax, forcing Moses to choose between his deep-seated moral instincts and the commitment he has just made.
77 | So they moved on until they came to the people of a town. They asked them for food, but the people refused to give them hospitality. There they found a wall ready to collapse, so the man set it right. Moses protested, “If you wanted, you could have demanded a fee for this.” | The final leg of the journey brings them to a town. “So they moved on until they came to the people of a town.” They were tired and hungry after their travels, so they “asked them for food, but the people refused to give them hospitality.” The Arabic “fa-abau an yuḍayyifūhumā” shows that the townspeople actively refused to host or feed these weary strangers. This was a gross violation of the ancient Near Eastern codes of hospitality, which were sacred duties. Al-Khaḍir and Moses were treated with stinginess and coldness. Yet, as they walked through this unwelcoming town, “there they found a wall ready to collapse.” The wall was tilted and on the verge of falling, posing a danger. Instead of leaving it or demanding compensation, “the man set it right.” Al-Khaḍir, without being asked and without any payment, undertook the laborious task of rebuilding the wall and making it straight and secure. This act of unsolicited kindness towards a people who had just mistreated them was the final puzzle for Moses. The contrast between the town’s inhospitality and Al-Khaḍir’s generous service was too much. “Moses protested, ‘If you wanted, you could have demanded a fee for this.’” The Arabic “la-ittakhadhta ‘alayhi ajran” suggests that this service should not have been free. They were in need of food and money, the people were undeserving, and at the very least, Al-Khaḍir should have asked for payment. Moses’ protest this time was not about a shocking crime but about a missed practical opportunity. He saw it as a lack of wisdom in dealing with unkind people. This third test was of a different nature: not an apparent evil, but an apparent failure to secure one’s due rights. It tested Moses’ sense of fairness and practical judgment. The tension is now at its peak, because this protest, though milder, triggers the consequence Moses himself had set.
78 | He replied, “This is the parting of our ways. I will explain to you what you could not bear patiently.” | With Moses’ third question, the condition he himself set is invoked. “He replied, ‘This is the parting of our ways.’” Al-Khaḍir confirms that the companionship has reached its end. The word “firāq” means separation, and it is delivered with calm finality. There is no anger, no rebuke, just a statement of fact. Moses had said that if he questioned anything after this, Al-Khaḍir would have the right to separate from him, and now that moment has arrived. However, Al-Khaḍir does not leave Moses in confusion. He immediately adds, “I will explain to you what you could not bear patiently.” The separation is not a punishment; it is the natural conclusion of the teaching phase. The events are complete, and now the time for explanation has come. Al-Khaḍir had promised that he would provide the clarification, and he honours that promise. The phrase “what you could not bear patiently” (mā lam tastaṭi‘ ‘alayhi ṣabran) reiterates the central theme of the entire encounter. Moses could not endure the sight of these actions because the inner wisdom was hidden from him. The explanation that follows will reveal the mercy, justice, and deep foresight behind each act. This verse teaches that sometimes in the journey of knowledge, the teacher must part ways with the student when the designed curriculum is complete, and the parting is not a failure but a graduation. The student is now ready to hear the interpretation that makes sense of the painful process. For Moses, the three events formed a transformative education, and the separation was necessary for him to reflect on what he had learned without the immediate pressure of further tests. The listener now prepares for the unveiling of the hidden wisdom, which has been eagerly anticipated through the entire narrative.
79 | “As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people, working at sea. So I intended to damage it, for there was a ˹tyrant˺ king ahead of them who seizes every ˹good˺ ship by force. | The explanation begins with the first incident. “As for the ship, it belonged to some poor people, working at sea.” The Arabic “masākīn” emphasizes that the owners were needy, earning their livelihood through hard labour on the water. This immediately changes the moral calculus. Al-Khaḍir’s action was not against the poor but was, in reality, for their protection. The verse reveals a hidden threat: “for there was a tyrant king ahead of them who seizes every good ship by force.” A tyrannical ruler was in pursuit, confiscating every serviceable vessel to add to his fleet or wealth. If the ship had been sound and intact, it would have been seized, and the poor family would have lost their only means of survival entirely. By making a hole that rendered the ship temporarily defective, Al-Khaḍir made it undesirable to the tyrant, who would bypass a damaged vessel. The small damage was a calculated act of mercy that prevented a total and permanent loss. The word “kāna warā’ahum” indicates the king was behind them, coming in their direction, so the danger was imminent. This explanation unveils a profound principle: sometimes a partial harm is necessary to avert a complete catastrophe. The surface appearance was an act of destruction; the hidden reality was an act of preservation. For Moses, who had seen the act as a terrible crime, this revelation showed how limited human judgment can be. The lesson for the believer is that when God allows harm or difficulty to touch a person, it may be diverting a far greater disaster that is unseen. The poor sailors never knew the tyrant was coming, but God’s servant, guided by divine knowledge, acted to save them. This teaches trust in God’s hidden care, even when events are painful or perplexing.
80 | “And as for the boy, his parents were ˹true˺ believers, and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief. | Al-Khaḍir now explains the most shocking act, the killing of the boy. “And as for the boy, his parents were true believers.” The parents are described as mu’minayn, true and faithful believers. This detail is crucial because it establishes that the act was not against a disbelieving family but was directly connected to preserving the faith of righteous parents. The verse then reveals the hidden knowledge that justified the act: “and we feared that he would pressure them into defiance and disbelief.” The Arabic “fa-khashīnā” means “we feared” or “we knew with apprehension.” The “we” here is understood by scholars to refer to Al-Khaḍir speaking on behalf of himself and the knowledge God granted him, or it is a style of speech. The fear was not about a possibility but about a known future reality disclosed by God. The boy, if he had lived to adulthood, would have become a source of immense grief, rebellion, and tyranny (ṭughyān) for his parents, and would have caused them to deviate into disbelief (kufr). The word “yurhiqahumā” means to overwhelm and overburden them with such pressure that their faith would be shaken. This was not a punishment for any sin the boy had committed yet, but a divine intervention to spare the parents a spiritual catastrophe and to spare the boy from accumulating sins that would lead him to Hell. The knowledge Al-Khaḍir possessed was a direct revelation of the boy’s future, and the action was a command from God specific to that case. This explanation highlights that God’s mercy can take forms that the human mind, without that knowledge, can never fathom. It also teaches that the death of a child, while a profound test, can sometimes be a hidden mercy preventing greater tragedies. The verse completes the justification for the most difficult event, leaving Moses and the listener in awe of the vastness of divine wisdom.
81 | So we hoped that their Lord would give them another, more virtuous and caring in his place. | Having explained the reason for the boy’s death, Al-Khaḍir now reveals the divine compensation that followed. “So we hoped that their Lord would give them another, more virtuous and caring in his place.” The word “hoped” (aradnā) here conveys a confident expectation based on divine knowledge, not a wish that might not come true. The boy who was taken away would be replaced by a child far better in two specific qualities: “zakāh” (purity, virtue, righteousness) and “ruḥman” (mercy, compassion, dutifulness). The replacement child would be more spiritually pure and more tender-hearted towards his parents. Some commentators mention that God granted them a daughter who later married a prophet, or a righteous son who brought them great joy. The exact identity is not as important as the principle: God does not take something away from His believing servants except to replace it with something better, or to avert a greater harm. The parents, who were true believers, would have been devastated by the loss of their child, but they did not know the future that awaited them. Al-Khaḍir’s action, carried out by God’s command, was an act of mercy that encompassed the boy (who died innocent and would enter Paradise), the parents (whose faith was preserved and who would receive a better child), and society (which was spared a future tyrant). This explanation reveals a profound layer of divine generosity. The calamity of losing a child is among the hardest tests for any human being. Yet this verse teaches that if the believer remains patient and trusting, God may grant a replacement that brings even greater goodness. It also demonstrates that God’s knowledge of the future is absolute, and His decisions are based on a complete picture that we lack. For Moses, this explanation transformed his horror into awe. The lesson for every believer is to hold on to hope even in the darkest losses, trusting that the Lord who took away is also the Lord who gives, and His giving is always better.
82 | “And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city, and under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them, and their father had been a righteous man. So your Lord willed that these children should come of age and retrieve their treasure, as a mercy from your Lord. I did not do it ˹all˺ on my own. This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.” | The final explanation concerns the wall that Al-Khaḍir rebuilt without asking for payment. “And as for the wall, it belonged to two orphan boys in the city.” The wall was on the verge of collapse, and if it had fallen, what lay beneath it would have been exposed and lost. “And under the wall was a treasure that belonged to them.” The treasure (kanz) was a hidden store of wealth—some say gold, silver, or valuable knowledge inscribed on tablets—left for the orphans by their father. The father is described as “a righteous man” (ṣāliḥan). His righteousness was the reason God extended His care to his children even after his death. This is a powerful demonstration of how the piety of a parent can bring divine protection upon their offspring. The verse continues: “So your Lord willed that these children should come of age and retrieve their treasure.” The wall was rebuilt so that it would remain standing until the boys reached maturity (bulūgh ashuddahumā) and could claim their inheritance safely. Had the wall collapsed, the townspeople, who were inhospitable and likely dishonest, might have taken the treasure. The act of rebuilding was a pure act of mercy, preserving what rightfully belonged to the vulnerable orphans. Then Al-Khaḍir adds a crucial disclaimer: “I did not do it all on my own.” The Arabic “wa mā fa‘altuhu ‘an amrī” means “I did not do it on my own authority or according to my own judgment.” All three actions were carried out under direct divine command and guidance. This assures Moses that no true servant of God would commit such acts without God’s explicit instruction. Finally, Al-Khaḍir concludes: “This is the explanation of what you could not bear patiently.” The ta’wīl (interpretation) is now complete. Moses has received the inner meaning of the outward events that had tested his patience to the breaking point. The story ends with this profound summation, teaching that behind every divine decree or action of God’s righteous servants lies a hidden wisdom that may only become clear when the full picture is unveiled. The believer is left with a renewed sense of humility, trust, and submission to the infinite knowledge of God.
83 | They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ about Ⱬul-Qarnain. Say, “I will relate to you something of his narrative.” | The surah now transitions to the fourth and final major story, that of Dhul-Qarnain. This story is a direct response to the questions posed by the Quraysh, as mentioned in the introduction to the surah. “They ask you about Dhul-Qarnain.” The name means “the possessor of the two horns,” and scholars have debated his identity. The Quran does not specify his name or era, and the purpose is not historical identification but the lessons his story imparts. The instruction to the Prophet is clear: “Say, ‘I will relate to you something of his narrative.’” The phrase “something of” (minhu dhikran) indicates that the Quran will give a portion of his story—a meaningful, instructive reminder—not an exhaustive historical account. This mirrors the teaching from the story of the cave, where Allah instructed not to argue about trivial details. The focus is on the wisdom and signs within the narrative. Dhul-Qarnain is presented as a righteous ruler given immense power and knowledge, and his journeys illustrate how he used these gifts in the service of God and justice. The question of the Quraysh was meant to test the Prophet’s knowledge of hidden histories, and the divine answer came with the truth. By prefacing the story in this way, the Quran sets the expectation that what follows is not mythology or legend but revealed truth that carries guidance for those who reflect. The story will demonstrate the proper use of worldly power, the importance of establishing justice, and the reality of cosmic threats such as Gog and Magog, all culminating in a reminder of the Day of Judgment. The narrative begins with a declaration of God’s role in establishing Dhul-Qarnain’s authority, ensuring that all credit is attributed to the Almighty from the very start.
84 | Surely We established him in the land, and gave him the means to all things. | The foundation of Dhul-Qarnain’s story is laid with the declaration of divine empowerment. “Surely We established him in the land.” The Arabic “makkannā lahu” means that God granted him firm authority, stability, and control over vast territories. His power was not self-made but a direct gift from God. This establishment encompassed political might, military strength, and the submission of various peoples. It is a reminder that all earthly kingdoms rise and fall by the will of God, and a righteous ruler recognizes that his authority is a trust, not a personal possession. The verse then adds, “and gave him the means to all things.” The word “asbāb” (means, ways, resources) is profound. God provided Dhul-Qarnain with the knowledge, tools, and pathways to achieve whatever he needed for his missions. This could include scientific knowledge, military strategy, the ability to travel great distances, and the wisdom to govern diverse populations. He was given access to the causes of success, but he recognized that those causes were created and granted by God, not by his own independent power. This contrasts sharply with the arrogance of the owner of the two gardens earlier in the surah, who saw his wealth as his own doing. Dhul-Qarnain used his means in obedience to God, becoming a model of the grateful and just ruler. The verse sets up the epic journeys that will follow. Having been granted power and the means to accomplish his goals, Dhul-Qarnain embarks on three great expeditions to the west, the east, and a mountainous region. Each journey will reveal a different aspect of his leadership: justice, fairness, and protective service. The believer learns that power and resources are a divine test, and the righteous response is to use them for the benefit of humanity and the establishment of God’s justice.
85 | So he travelled a course, | The concise statement “So he travelled a course” (fa-atba‘a sababan) opens the first of Dhul-Qarnain’s three expeditions. The phrase literally means “he followed a way” or “he pursued a path.” It indicates a purposeful, determined journey using the means and resources God had given him. The sabab here is the route or the practical means by which he traversed the earth. This is not a casual wandering but a deliberate mission. As a ruler with great power, he did not sit idle in his palace; he went out to see the state of the world, to administer justice, and to encounter different peoples. The verse is a lesson in the responsibility that comes with power. Leadership is not about luxury but about service and action. Dhul-Qarnain’s journey to the west will bring him to the place where the sun sets, a location where he encounters a people and is faced with a moral decision. The next verses will elaborate on that encounter. The brevity of this verse serves as a narrative pause, building anticipation. It also underscores that his journeys were facilitated by the means God gave him; he followed the path that God opened for him. In a spiritual sense, every believer is on a journey using the sabab that God has provided—whether it is knowledge, health, or opportunity—and must use those means to reach righteous ends. The verse transitions naturally into the description of his first destination, where the physical signs of the sun’s setting become a backdrop for a profound test of justice and governance.
86 | until he reached the setting ˹point˺ of the sun, which appeared to him to be setting in a spring of murky water, where he found some people. We said, “O Ⱬul-Qarnain! Either punish them or treat them kindly.” | Dhul-Qarnain’s first journey brings him to the westernmost point of his travels. “Until he reached the setting point of the sun.” He went as far west as he could, to the edge of the land known to him. “Which appeared to him to be setting in a spring of murky water.” This is a description from the viewer’s perspective. As the sun descended on the horizon, it looked as if it was sinking into a dark, muddy body of water—likely a vast ocean or a large lake with dark sediment. This is not a literal claim about the sun’s movement; it is a phenomenological description of what a human observer sees, much like we say “the sun rose over the mountains.” The Quran speaks to human perception while conveying a deeper truth about the limits of Dhul-Qarnain’s reach. At this location, “he found some people.” The text does not specify their nature, but they were a community or nation living near that western shore. Then comes the divine test: “We said, ‘O Dhul-Qarnain! Either punish them or treat them kindly.’” God gave him authority over them and presented him with a choice. The Arabic “immā an tu‘adhdhiba wa immā an tattakhidha fīhim ḥusnan” means he could either inflict punishment or adopt a course of kindness and goodness towards them. This divine communication could have been through inspiration, a prophet of his time, or a direct revelation. The key point is that his power was not unchecked; he was given a moral framework by God. He had the freedom to choose, but the choice itself was a test. This mirrors the situation of every person in authority: they can use their power for oppression or for benevolence. The next verse will reveal Dhul-Qarnain’s just response, which balances mercy and accountability in a manner that provides a timeless model for governance.
87 | He responded, “Whoever does wrong will be punished by us, then will be returned to their Lord, Who will punish them with a horrible torment. | Dhul-Qarnain makes his decision, and it reflects a profound balance of worldly justice and eschatological awareness. “He responded, ‘Whoever does wrong will be punished by us.’” The wrongdoers (ẓalamū) among the people he found would face immediate, appropriate punishment from his authority. He does not specify the nature of the wrong, but it can be understood as oppression, corruption, or denial of truth. The punishment is “by us,” meaning his worldly administration will carry out justice. However, he does not stop there. He adds, “then will be returned to their Lord, Who will punish them with a horrible torment.” Dhul-Qarnain immediately connects his worldly punishment to the ultimate punishment of the Hereafter. This serves two purposes. First, it warns the wrongdoers that even if they escape or outsmart human justice, they cannot escape God. Second, it demonstrates his own humility; he recognizes that his punishment is limited and that the real, absolute justice belongs to God. The “horrible torment” (nukran) is the same word Moses used to describe the killing of the boy, meaning something utterly severe and detestable. Dhul-Qarnain’s justice is not only about maintaining order in this world but also about reminding people of their ultimate accountability. This is a complete model of leadership: a leader must uphold the law and punish wrongdoing, but must never let people forget that the true judgment lies with God. The worldly punishment is a means of deterrence and reformation, but it pales in comparison to what awaits the unrepentant sinner in the afterlife. The verse teaches that a just ruler combines firmness with spiritual insight, governing not only bodies but also reminding souls of their eternal destination.
88 | As for those who believe and do good, they will have the finest reward, and we will assign them easy commands.” | Dhul-Qarnain now turns to the other side of his decree. “As for those who believe and do good, they will have the finest reward.” He specifies two conditions: faith (īmān) and righteous deeds (‘amal ṣāliḥ). The reward for such people is “al-ḥusnā,” the best and most beautiful recompense. This primarily refers to the reward in the Hereafter, Paradise and God’s pleasure, as he is speaking from a prophetic perspective. He does not claim to be the source of this reward; rather, he acknowledges that God will grant it. Then he adds, “and we will assign them easy commands.” The Arabic “sa-naqūlu lahu min amrinā yusran” means that his worldly administration will deal with them gently, with light and easy instructions. The believers will not be burdened with harsh taxes, oppressive laws, or difficult demands. They will experience ease and kindness from the state. This is a remarkable principle of governance: the state should be a source of relief for the righteous, not a source of hardship. The burden of strictness falls on the wrongdoers, while the law-abiding, faithful citizens enjoy liberty and ease. Dhul-Qarnain’s policy reflects the Quranic maxim that “with hardship comes ease” and that God desires ease for His servants. It also indicates that a righteous government recognizes virtue and rewards it by minimizing unnecessary intrusion into the lives of good people. This stands in stark contrast to tyrannies that oppress the righteous and favour the corrupt. For the believer today, this verse provides a model for what to expect and advocate for in just governance: protection of faith, encouragement of good deeds, and a system where the righteous can live with ease and dignity. It also serves as a personal reminder that those who believe and do good can expect the finest reward from God, no matter how difficult their worldly situation may be.
89 | Then he travelled a ˹different˺ course | With the western expedition concluded and justice established, Dhul-Qarnain sets out again. “Then he travelled a different course” (thumma atba‘a sababan). The use of “thumma” (then) indicates a sequence in time, and “different course” shows that he now pursued a new direction using the means God had given him. The previous course was westward; this one will take him eastward. The repetition of the phrase “atba‘a sababan” from verse 85 creates a rhythmic structure in the narrative, tying his journeys together under the same principle: he follows the paths that God has enabled. There is no resting on his laurels after the first achievement. The righteous ruler does not become complacent; he continues his mission to reach other lands and other peoples. This verse emphasizes sustained effort in the service of God. The means (asbāb) are not exhausted; God’s provision of resources continues as long as the servant continues to strive. The brevity of the verse again serves as a narrative bridge, carrying the listener from the setting sun to the rising sun, from the western horizon to the eastern horizon. It also suggests that the world is vast and full of different communities, each requiring the attention of just leadership. For the individual believer, the lesson is to keep moving forward in the journey of life, using the means God has given for good, and not to stop at a single success. Every new horizon presents a new test and a new opportunity to serve.
90 | until he reached the rising ˹point˺ of the sun. He found it rising on a people for whom We had provided no shelter from it. | Dhul-Qarnain’s second journey brings him to the opposite end of the known world. “Until he reached the rising point of the sun.” This is the easternmost limit of his travels, parallel to the westernmost point of the first journey. “He found it rising on a people for whom We had provided no shelter from it.” The description paints a picture of a people living in an open land with no natural cover, no mountains, no trees, and perhaps no buildings to shield them from the direct, scorching rays of the sun. When the sun rose, it beat down upon them without any barrier. Some commentators suggest they lived in a region near the equator or in a flat desert where they had not constructed dwellings. Others say they were a primitive people who simply endured the sun’s heat. The Quranic focus is not on their ethnicity or exact location but on their condition of vulnerability. The phrase “We had provided no shelter from it” indicates that God, in His wisdom, had left them without conventional means of protection. This could be a test, or it could be that they lived in such a way that they did not seek shelter. Unlike the first people, who were immediately given a choice of punishment or kindness, the verse here does not record any specific interaction or decree. The next verse says, “So it was. And We truly had full knowledge of him.” The absence of a detailed judgment might imply that these people were simple and not in need of the same form of governance, or that Dhul-Qarnain’s engagement with them was different. The image of the exposed people under the blazing sun is a powerful reminder of the diversity of human conditions and the fact that God sees all. Dhul-Qarnain’s journey to them demonstrates the comprehensive reach of his mission, leaving no corner of the world unvisited.
91 | So it was. And We truly had full knowledge of him. | This brief verse closes the account of the eastern journey with a statement of divine affirmation and comprehensive knowledge. “So it was” (kadhālika) indicates that the matter of those people was as described, and Dhul-Qarnain dealt with them appropriately according to the wisdom and justice he had demonstrated before. The specifics of his interaction are left unmentioned, allowing the focus to remain on the broader lessons. The second part of the verse, “And We truly had full knowledge of him,” is profoundly significant. The Arabic “qad aḥaṭnā bimā ladayhi khubran” means God encompassed everything concerning Dhul-Qarnain—his actions, his intentions, his resources, and his inner state—with complete, thorough knowledge. The word “khubr” implies deep, detailed awareness of the hidden and the apparent. This statement serves multiple purposes. It assures the Prophet and the believers that Dhul-Qarnain’s story is accurate and divinely witnessed. It also emphasizes that Dhul-Qarnain’s righteousness and power were constantly under God’s watch. He did not act in secrecy; God knew everything about him. This is both a commendation and a reminder. For the powerful, it is a warning that God monitors how they use their authority. For the righteous, it is a comfort that their deeds are never lost or forgotten. The verse also bridges to the third journey. By affirming God’s comprehensive knowledge, it reassures the listener that the upcoming encounter with Gog and Magog, and the construction of the barrier, were all under divine supervision. The phrase echoes the earlier theme of the surah: God’s knowledge encompasses all things, while human knowledge is limited. Moses could not encompass the wisdom of Al-Khaḍir, but God encompasses all. Dhul-Qarnain’s vast empire is but a small part of God’s all-encompassing knowledge.
92 | Then he travelled a ˹third˺ course | The narrative advances to the final and most famous expedition. “Then he travelled a third course.” The structure is identical to verses 85 and 89, reinforcing the pattern of his journeys. This third course takes him in a direction different from west and east, likely northward, to a region between two mountain barriers. The repetition of “thumma atba‘a sababan” unifies the three journeys as a continuous, lifelong mission of service. By now, the listener understands that Dhul-Qarnain is a ruler who does not rest; he constantly seeks out new frontiers to establish justice and help those in need. The third journey will bring him to a people who are linguistically isolated and suffering from the raids of Gog and Magog. This expedition is the climax of his story, where his power and resources are used for the ultimate act of protective public service: building a massive barrier to contain a corrupting force. The lesson here is that true leadership involves going to the farthest reaches, not just the comfortable centres of power. Dhul-Qarnain could have stayed in his palace and sent generals, but he himself travelled, observed, and acted. This hands-on approach is a mark of a sincere servant of God. The three journeys together represent the comprehensive use of means: dealing with diverse peoples, applying justice, showing kindness, and solving large-scale problems. The verse transitions the narrative to the detailed account of the barrier, which will occupy the next several verses, providing a vivid picture of human cooperation and divine assistance in the face of existential threat.
93 | until he reached ˹a pass˺ between two mountains. He found in front of them a people who could hardly understand ˹his˺ language. | The third journey reaches its destination. “Until he reached a pass between two mountains.” The Arabic “bayna as-saddayn” refers to a narrow passage or a valley enclosed by two towering mountain barriers. This geographical feature was strategic; it was the only accessible opening between the two sides. “He found in front of them a people who could hardly understand his language.” The people living there were isolated, and their speech was so different that communication was extremely difficult. The phrase “lā yakādūna yafqahūna qawlan” indicates they could barely comprehend what was being said to them. Despite this language barrier, they managed to convey their distress. This detail highlights the universality of Dhul-Qarnain’s mission; not even linguistic obstacles stopped him from understanding their needs. It also shows the diversity of God’s creation, with peoples separated by geography and language. The fact that they could still communicate enough to make their plea demonstrates a shared human experience of suffering and the desire for protection. For a ruler with interpreters and resources, the language barrier was surmountable. This verse teaches the importance of reaching out to marginalized communities, even when communication is difficult. The isolated people had no one else to turn to, and Dhul-Qarnain did not dismiss them because they spoke a different tongue. The next verse will reveal their heartfelt plea, giving voice to the fear and desperation of those who live on the edge of chaos. The scene is set for the request that will lead to one of the most monumental construction projects in history.
94 | They pleaded, “O Ⱬul-Qarnain! Surely Gog and Magog are spreading corruption throughout the land. Should we pay you tribute, provided that you build a wall between us and them?” | The isolated people finally voice their distress. “They pleaded, ‘O Dhul-Qarnain! Surely Gog and Magog are spreading corruption throughout the land.’” Gog and Magog (Ya’jūj and Ma’jūj) are described in Islamic tradition as two wild, destructive tribes or nations. They would pour through the mountain pass and wreak havoc, killing, plundering, and spreading fasād (corruption) across the land. The word “mufsidūn” is used throughout the Quran to describe those who ruin the peace and order of society. The people were living in constant terror of these raids. Their plea to Dhul-Qarnain is a cry for help from a powerful, just ruler. Then they make a concrete offer: “Should we pay you tribute, provided that you build a wall between us and them?” The word “kharjan” refers to a payment, a tax or tribute, that they were willing to gather and give him in exchange for his service. They recognized that such a massive undertaking would require resources and labour, and they offered to compensate him. This shows their sincerity and desperation. They were not demanding free aid; they were willing to sacrifice their wealth for security. The offer also reflects a form of social contract: the ruler provides protection, and the people provide resources. However, Dhul-Qarnain’s response in the next verse will elevate the interaction from a mere transactional arrangement to a mission of higher purpose. His reply will demonstrate that his motivation is not wealth but the fulfillment of his duty to God and to humanity. The presence of Gog and Magog in the narrative also connects to eschatological signs, as their eventual release is one of the major signs of the approaching Hour, a theme the surah will return to.
95 | He responded, “What my Lord has provided for me is far better. But assist me with resources, and I will build a barrier between you and them. | Dhul-Qarnain’s reply reveals the purity of his intention. “He responded, ‘What my Lord has provided for me is far better.’” He immediately refuses any payment. The phrase “mā makkannī fīhi rabbī khayr” means that the power, wealth, and means God had already given him were far superior to any tribute these people could offer. He had no need of their money; he had been enriched by God with something much greater. This is the response of a true servant who sees worldly wealth as trivial compared to the honour of serving God and helping His creation. His refusal of payment transforms the act of building the barrier from a commercial contract into an act of pure charity and divine service. Yet he does not simply send them away and work alone. He says, “But assist me with resources, and I will build a barrier between you and them.” The word “quwwatin” can mean physical strength, manpower, or material resources like tools and labour. He asked them to contribute their effort and whatever local materials they could provide. This is a profound lesson in community empowerment. While the powerful can provide leadership and overarching resources, the affected community must also participate in their own protection. He did not foster dependency; he invited collaboration. The barrier (radm) he proposed was a solid, massive structure, different from a simple wall. The stage is set for one of the most detailed descriptions of an engineering project in the Quran. The interaction teaches that leaders should serve without greed, that the rich should see their wealth as a trust from God, and that solving large-scale problems requires a partnership between those in authority and the people themselves.
96 | “Bring me blocks of iron!” Then, when he had filled up ˹the gap˺ between the two mountains, he ordered, “Blow!” When the iron became red hot, he said, “Bring me molten copper to pour over it.” | Dhul-Qarnain now directs the actual construction of the barrier with precise, step-by-step instructions. “Bring me blocks of iron!” The word “zubar al-ḥadīd” refers to large pieces or ingots of iron. These were the primary structural material. They were brought and placed into the gap between the two mountains. “Then, when he had filled up the gap between the two mountains” describes the first phase: stacking the iron blocks until they reached the level of the mountain sides, completely sealing the pass. This required immense quantities of iron and organized labour. The phrase “sāwā bayna aṣ-ṣadafayn” indicates he levelled the iron with the sloping sides of the mountains, creating a uniform, solid base. Then he ordered, “Blow!” He commanded the workers to use large bellows to blow air into the fuel, creating a massive fire that heated the iron blocks until they glowed red hot. The iron became like a single fused mass, increasing its strength and durability. Then came the second phase: “Bring me molten copper to pour over it.” The word “qiṭran” can mean molten copper, brass, or a mixture of metals. This molten metal was poured over the red-hot iron, filling all the gaps and crevices, binding the entire structure together into an impenetrable, smooth-faced barrier. The combination of iron for bulk and copper as a bonding agent created a metallurgical marvel that could not be scaled or breached. This detailed description highlights the knowledge and means God gave Dhul-Qarnain. It was a practical application of physics and engineering, carried out with divine facilitation. The verse teaches that faith and practical skill go hand in hand. Dhul-Qarnain did not simply pray for protection; he used the material means God provided and executed a sophisticated project, all while acknowledging that the true power came from God.
97 | And so the enemies could neither scale nor tunnel through it. | The result of the massive construction is stated simply and definitively. “And so the enemies could neither scale nor tunnel through it.” The barrier was completely effective. The Arabic “mā as-ṭā‘ū” means they were totally unable to climb over it. The height, likely levelled with the mountain peaks and coated with smooth molten metal, offered no handholds or footholds. The phrase “wa mā as-ṭā‘ū lahu naqban” means they could not make a hole or tunnel through it. The iron core fused with copper was too thick and solid to be pierced by any tools available to Gog and Magog. This state of impenetrability would last until the appointed time decreed by God. The verse highlights the perfection of the work done with God’s guidance. It provided the afflicted people with absolute security, freeing them from the terror they had lived under. The verse is also a testament to the fact that when humans use God-given means with sincerity and skill, the results can be extraordinarily effective. However, the next verse will immediately temper any pride in this achievement by attributing the barrier’s success to God’s mercy and declaring its ultimate impermanence. This teaches a vital lesson: even the greatest human accomplishments are temporary and depend on God’s will for their continuation. The barrier stood not because of the genius of its engineering alone, but because God willed it to remain. The day will come when God’s promise will cause it to be levelled, reminding humanity that all fortresses and protections of this world are ultimately transient.
98 | He declared, “This is a mercy from my Lord. But when the promise of my Lord comes to pass, He will level it to the ground. And my Lord’s promise is ever true.” | Dhul-Qarnain, standing before the completed barrier, does not take credit. “He declared, ‘This is a mercy from my Lord.’” The Arabic “hādhā raḥmatun min rabbī” attributes the success of the entire project to God’s mercy. It was God who gave him the knowledge, the resources, and the cooperation of the people, and it was God who guided the process to completion. This is the peak of humility. A lesser ruler would have named the barrier after himself or boasted of his engineering prowess. Dhul-Qarnain sees only God’s mercy in it. Then he adds the prophetic insight: “But when the promise of my Lord comes to pass, He will level it to the ground.” He acknowledges that this barrier, mighty as it is, is not eternal. There is an appointed time in God’s knowledge when it will be destroyed. The promise of God refers to the approach of the Day of Judgment, when the barrier will be flattened, and Gog and Magog will surge forth, as described in Surah Al-Anbiya and in the next verse of this surah. This statement serves as a reminder of the end of all things. He concludes, “And my Lord’s promise is ever true.” God’s words and decrees are unerring and will inevitably come to pass. This verse beautifully concludes Dhul-Qarnain’s story with a perfect balance of gratitude, humility, and eschatological awareness. The barrier is a mercy in the present, but the future belongs to God’s ultimate plan. For the believer, it is a reminder that all human protections—health, wealth, security—are mercies from God, but they will all cease. The only permanent reality is God and His true promise. This directly connects back to the earlier themes of the surah: the fleeting nature of the world and the certainty of the Hereafter.
99 | On that Day, We will let them surge ˹like waves˺ over one another. Later, the Trumpet will be blown, and We will gather all ˹people˺ together. | The narrative expands from the barrier’s destruction to the cosmic events of the end times. “On that Day, We will let them surge like waves over one another.” When the appointed time comes and the barrier is levelled, Gog and Magog will pour out in a massive, chaotic wave, overwhelming the land. The word “yamūju” evokes the image of surging, tumultuous waves in a stormy sea, indicating their immense numbers and the disruption they will cause. This is a major sign of the approaching Hour. Then, “Later, the Trumpet will be blown, and We will gather all people together.” The Trumpet (aṣ-ṣūr) is the horn blown by the angel Israfil, signaling the end of all life and the resurrection. The blowing of the Trumpet marks the transition from the worldly chaos to the final gathering. All of humanity, from the first to the last, will be assembled before God for judgment. The sequence is clear: the release of Gog and Magog is a prelude to the end, and their destruction or the subsequent events lead directly to the universal resurrection. This verse connects Dhul-Qarnain’s historical act of building the barrier to the ultimate future of humanity. It reminds the listener that the story is not just about a past king but about the unfolding of God’s plan through time. The barrier that seemed so permanent was only a temporary mercy, and the real security lies in preparing for the Day when all will be gathered. The verse serves as a powerful transition into the concluding passages of the surah, which will focus on the state of the disbelievers and believers on that Day.
100 | On that Day We will display Hell clearly for the disbelievers, | The gathering leads directly to the presentation of the final destination. “On that Day We will display Hell clearly for the disbelievers.” The Arabic “‘araḍnā jahannama” means Hell will be brought forth and made plainly visible. It will not be a distant rumor or an abstract concept; it will be a tangible, terrifying reality right before their eyes. The disbelievers will see the Fire they were warned about and denied. This public display is part of the ultimate humiliation and terror. The verse specifies that it is for “the disbelievers” (lil-kāfirīn), those who rejected faith. This sight is a punishment in itself, the overwhelming horror of seeing their eternal abode before entering it. It echoes verse 53 earlier in the surah, where the wicked see the Fire and realize they are bound to fall into it. The connection reinforces the surah’s consistent warning. The display of Hell serves as the ultimate validation of the truth of the messengers’ warnings. The next verse will provide the reason for their punishment, describing the specific nature of their disbelief and heedlessness. The mention of Hell being displayed clearly (ḍalālan) also indicates that there will be no more veils, no more ambiguity; the truth will stand manifest, and the deniers will face the consequences of their rejection. This verse is a sobering conclusion to the Dhul-Qarnain narrative, transitioning the listener from the stories of worldly power and mercy to the inescapable reality of divine judgment that awaits every soul.
101 | those who turned a blind eye to My Reminder and could not stand listening ˹to it˺. | This verse provides the defining characteristic of the disbelievers for whom Hell will be displayed. They are described as “those who turned a blind eye to My Reminder.” The Arabic expression “kānat a‘yunuhum fī ghiṭā’in ‘an dhikrī” paints a vivid picture: their eyes were covered with a veil that prevented them from seeing the truth of God’s reminder. The “Reminder” refers to the Quran, the signs in creation, and the guidance brought by the prophets. This was not a case of innocent ignorance; they actively placed a covering over their spiritual sight. They chose not to look, not to reflect, and not to recognize the clear signs that surrounded them. The second part intensifies their rejection: “and could not stand listening to it.” The Arabic “wa kānū lā yastaṭī‘ūna sam‘an” implies they were unable to bear hearing the revelation. The sound of the Quran, the call to faith, was so disturbing to their ingrained arrogance and lifestyle that they could not endure it. They would plug their ears, make noise to drown it out, or leave the gathering. This describes a willful, aggressive aversion. Together, the blind eye and the deaf ear form a complete rejection of the truth, sealing their fate. The verse follows directly from the display of Hell, answering the unspoken question of why they deserve such a fate. It also echoes the earlier verse about the veils cast over hearts and deafness in ears as a consequence of persistent turning away. This is a warning to every listener: the doors of guidance are open, but if one repeatedly shuts their eyes and blocks their ears, the ability to perceive truth may be taken away. The verse underscores the importance of approaching the Quran with humility and openness, actively seeking to see and hear what God has revealed, rather than avoiding it out of fear that it will disturb one’s current way of life.
102 | Do the disbelievers think they can ˹simply˺ take My servants as lords instead of Me? We have surely prepared Hell as an accommodation for the disbelievers. | This verse forcefully refutes the notion of taking created beings as objects of worship or as independent intercessors. “Do the disbelievers think they can simply take My servants as lords instead of Me?” The rhetorical question expresses astonishment and condemnation. The word “awliyā’” translated as “lords” can also mean patrons, protectors, or allies. The “servants” here include angels, prophets like Jesus, saints, or any righteous beings whom people elevate to divine status. It is the height of irrationality to abandon the worship of the all-powerful Creator and instead turn to servants who themselves are dependent on God. The verse implies that these servants would never consent to such worship; they are themselves devoted to God. This directly connects to the earlier rejection of the claim that Allah has offspring and the story of Satan’s rebellion, where taking him and his descendants as patrons was condemned. The second part of the verse delivers the consequence: “We have surely prepared Hell as an accommodation for the disbelievers.” The word “nuzul” originally means a lodging or a welcome gift prepared for a guest. Here it is used with bitter irony; the only welcome awaiting those who take false lords is the Fire of Hell, prepared and ready. The verse dismisses any hope that such intermediaries could benefit them. In the authentic hadith about intercession, the Prophet ﷺ affirmed that intercession is only by God’s permission and for those with whom He is pleased; no one can act as an independent lord. This verse reinforces the core Islamic principle of Tawhid al-Uluhiyyah, the oneness of worship. It teaches that any devotion, supplication, or ultimate reliance directed to other than God is not merely a mistake but the very definition of disbelief that leads to Hell, a prepared abode that matches the enormity of the crime.
103 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds? | This verse introduces one of the most thought-provoking concepts in the Quran with a rhetorical question. The Prophet is commanded to say, “Shall we inform you of who will lose the most deeds?” The Arabic “al-akhsarīna a‘mālan” refers to those in the greatest state of loss regarding their actions. The question is designed to capture attention and stir curiosity. Every human being naturally works and strives, assuming that their effort will yield some benefit. The notion that there are people whose entire life’s work will result in utter loss is alarming. The question sets up the detailed answer in the following verse. The use of “shall we inform you” makes the listener lean in. It is an offer of crucial knowledge that can prevent a catastrophe. The theme connects back to the opening of the surah where Allah stated that He made the earth’s adornments as a test to see who is best in deeds. Here the focus shifts to those who fail that test most spectacularly. The loss is not a small deduction but a complete nullification of deeds. The question also prepares the ground for understanding that outward success and visible productivity are not the criteria; the inner reality of faith and intention determines the value of deeds. For the companions and all believers, this verse prompts a continuous self-assessment: am I among those who will see their efforts turn to dust, or among those whose deeds are heavy on the scales? It is a sobering invitation to re-evaluate the direction of one’s life and the sincerity of one’s heart before the day when deeds are weighed.
104 | ˹They are˺ those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life, while they think they are doing good! | This verse delivers the shocking answer to the question posed in the previous verse. “They are those whose efforts are in vain in this worldly life.” The word “sa‘y” refers to earnest striving, effort, and hard work. These people are not lazy; they are active, industrious, and perhaps even accomplished by worldly standards. Yet their entire effort goes to waste (ḍalāl) in the life of this world, meaning it yields no benefit for their Hereafter. The reason is then given with piercing irony: “while they think they are doing good!” Their perception of their own actions is completely inverted. They believe they are performing righteous, beneficial deeds, but in God’s sight, those deeds are worthless. This can apply to several categories. It includes disbelievers who perform humanitarian works but reject faith, because without the foundation of belief in God, deeds lack the soul that gives them eternal value. It also includes hypocrites who perform outward acts of worship for show rather than for God. The Prophet ﷺ warned in a famous hadith that the first people to be judged on the Day of Resurrection will be a martyr, a scholar, and a generous person, all of whom will be dragged to Hell because they performed their deeds to be seen and praised by people, not sincerely for God’s sake. This verse is a profound caution against self-deception. It teaches that a person can be completely deluded about their spiritual state. The remedy is constant self-examination, purifying one’s intentions, and measuring deeds against the revealed guidance rather than personal opinion or social approval. The verse also offers a lens through which to understand the earlier stories: the owner of the two gardens thought his wealth was his great achievement, but it was his ruin; the disbelievers thought their worldly power was a sign of truth, but it was a deception.
105 | It is they who reject the signs of their Lord and their meeting with Him, rendering their deeds void, so We will not give their deeds any weight on Judgment Day. | This verse provides the root cause behind the devastating loss described previously. “It is they who reject the signs of their Lord and their meeting with Him.” The two fatal mistakes are identified: denial of God’s revealed signs and denial of the ultimate encounter with Him in the Hereafter. Rejecting the signs means refusing to accept the evidence of God’s oneness, power, and guidance, whether those signs are in the Quran or in the universe. Denying the meeting with God means disbelieving in the resurrection and judgment. These two rejections work together to strip deeds of all value. Without faith in God and the Last Day, a deed is like a body without a soul; it may appear alive but it is dead in terms of eternal consequence. The phrase “rendering their deeds void” means their rejection corrupts and nullifies whatever good they might have done. Then comes the imagery of the Day of Judgment: “so We will not give their deeds any weight on Judgment Day.” The Arabic “nuqīmu lahum yawma al-qiyāmati waznan” means God will not set up any scales to weigh their deeds because there is nothing of substance to weigh. Their deeds are light, insubstantial, like scattered dust. The image of the scales is central to the Quranic depiction of judgment, where good and bad deeds are weighed with absolute justice. For these people, the scale will be empty of any good that matters. This verse reinforces the absolute necessity of correct belief. It warns that good works, no matter how numerous or impressive, cannot compensate for a lack of faith. The belief must be sound, rooted in acceptance of God’s signs and the reality of the return to Him. For the believer, this is a call to hold fast to faith and to renew it constantly, ensuring that every action is built upon a foundation that will bear weight on the most critical day.
106 | That is their reward: Hell, for their disbelief and mockery of My signs and messengers. | The verse pronounces the fitting recompense. “That is their reward: Hell.” The word “dhālikum” points to the great loss and the lack of weight for their deeds, and sums it up as the punishment of Hell. The term “jazā’uhum” means their recompense, indicating perfect justice. They earned this outcome through their own choices. The two reasons are then explicitly stated: “for their disbelief and mockery of My signs and messengers.” Disbelief (kufr) is their covering up of the truth, and mockery (sukhriyyah) is their active ridicule and belittling of the revelations and the prophets who brought them. The combination of disbelief and mockery shows a deep-seated arrogance and disdain. It is not a passive rejection but an aggressive hostility. Earlier in the surah, verse 56 described how the disbelievers argue in falsehood to discredit the truth and make a mockery of God’s revelations. This verse confirms that such mockery is not a minor offense but a major cause of damnation. The mockery may have been a tool to suppress the truth and silence the believers, but on the Day of Judgment, it will be met with the severest punishment. The mention of messengers alongside signs emphasizes that rejecting the human carriers of the message is equivalent to rejecting the message itself. The verse serves as a stark warning against taking the signs of God lightly. It also provides a measure of comfort to the believers who were mocked for their faith: the mockers will face the ultimate humiliation, while the believers will be honoured. It completes the description of the wrongdoers’ fate, preparing the heart for the immediate contrast with the reward of the believers in the next verse.
107 | Indeed, those who believe and do good will have the Gardens of Paradise as an accommodation, | After the terrifying portrait of the losers, the Quran immediately shifts to the beautiful reward of the faithful, maintaining the surah’s balanced structure. “Indeed, those who believe and do good” repeats the central Quranic pairing of inner faith and outward righteous action. Belief is the foundation, and good deeds are its necessary expression. The word “al-firdaus” translated as “Paradise” specifically refers to the highest and most elevated part of Jannah. The Prophet ﷺ said, “When you ask Allah, ask Him for al-Firdaus, for it is the middle of Paradise and the highest part of Paradise.” This promise thus points to the best of the best. The verse states that they will have it “as an accommodation.” The word “nuzul” is used, which we saw earlier in the context of Hell for the disbelievers. Using the same word for Paradise creates a powerful contrast. For the disbelievers, Hell is their prepared welcome; for the believers, the Gardens of Firdaus are their prepared welcome. An “accommodation” suggests a place of rest, hospitality, and honour prepared in advance for honoured guests. It conveys the idea that Paradise is not merely a reward but a gift of divine hospitality, where every comfort is arranged. This connects to the earlier description in verse 31 of the Gardens of Eternity, with flowing rivers, gold bracelets, green silk garments, and canopied couches. The believer is not just escaping punishment but being ushered into a state of everlasting honour and delight. This verse is a source of immense hope, reminding the struggling believer that their efforts are seen and their final destination is a place prepared by the Most Generous Host. The contrast with the previous verses reinforces the gravity of the choice facing every human being.
108 | where they will be forever, never desiring anywhere else. | This verse adds two essential qualities to the reward of Paradise. “Where they will be forever” (khālidīna fīhā) provides the guarantee of eternity. The fear of loss, death, or eviction that accompanies every worldly pleasure is entirely absent. This eternal nature was first mentioned in the surah’s third verse regarding the believers’ reward, and it is repeated here to cement the point. Every moment of joy in this world is tainted by the awareness that it will end. In Paradise, the joy is absolute because it is unending. The second quality is even more profound: “never desiring anywhere else.” The Arabic “lā yabghūna ‘anhā ḥiwalan” means they will not seek to transfer or be removed from it, nor will they ever desire a change or a substitute. This indicates complete and utter contentment. The human soul in this world is fickle; even in the best situations, it longs for something different, something more. In Paradise, the soul is perfectly satisfied. What God has prepared is exactly and infinitely what the soul desires. There is no boredom, no restlessness, no wish for anything different. The word “ḥiwal” suggests a turning away or a shift; the inhabitants will never imagine a better state, because no better state exists. This verse answers a deep-seated human fear: what if Paradise eventually becomes monotonous? The Quran assures us that it is a dynamic, ever-renewing, and perfectly satisfying existence. This completion of contentment is a gift from God, the ultimate peace of heart. It connects to the story of the cave youths, who were given peace and provision beyond imagination, and to the believing companion who hoped for something better than the earthly garden. The final home far surpasses any worldly abode, and its inhabitants will dwell there in eternal, undisturbed bliss.
109 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If the ocean were ink for ˹writing˺ the Words of my Lord, it would certainly run out before the Words of my Lord were finished, even if We refilled it with its equal.” | This verse unveils the limitless nature of God’s knowledge and speech. The Prophet is instructed to say, “If the ocean were ink for writing the Words of my Lord…” The image is vast and majestic: the entire ocean, an unimaginably large body of water, is turned into ink. All of it is used to write the Words of God—His decrees, His knowledge, His wisdom, His commands, and the revelations that proceed from Him. The verse then makes a staggering claim: “it would certainly run out before the Words of my Lord were finished.” The ocean, despite its immensity, would be exhausted while God’s Words would still remain unwritten. And even if another ocean like it were brought as a refill, and then another, the same depletion would occur. This is a declaration of the infinite nature of divine knowledge. The context of revelation sheds light on this verse. The Jews told the Meccans that they had been given abundant wisdom, citing a statement from their scriptures. This verse was revealed to demonstrate that all the knowledge given to humanity, including the Torah and the Quran, is but a drop from the endless ocean of God’s Words. The verse connects profoundly with the surah’s themes. The cave sleepers’ duration, the hidden wisdom of Al-Khaḍir, the unseen realms known only to God—all are part of these infinite Words. The finite human mind cannot encompass God’s knowledge. This realization fosters humility. It also comforts the believer that God’s power and wisdom are inexhaustible; He never runs out of means to create, provide, and solve problems. The verse echoes the earlier command to say “Allah knows best” and the affirmation that the unseen belongs to God. It is an invitation to trust the all-knowing Lord, whose wisdom far exceeds any human comprehension.
110 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I am only a man like you, ˹but˺ it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God. So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.” | The surah concludes with a final, comprehensive declaration of the core of the prophetic message. The Prophet is commanded to say, “I am only a man like you.” This is a profound statement of humility and a rejection of any claim to divinity or superhuman status. The Prophet is a mortal, sharing the same human nature as those he addresses, subject to the same needs and limitations. This counters the exaggeration of prophets into gods, as happened with Jesus, and reinforces that the Prophet is a servant, as stated in the surah’s opening verse. Yet there is a crucial distinction: “but it has been revealed to me that your God is only One God.” The conjunction “but” (illā) introduces the unique factor—divine revelation. His humanity is shared; his reception of revelation is what sets him apart as a messenger. The content of that revelation is the absolute oneness of God, Tawhid, which is the central theme of the entire surah. Then, the verse delivers the final, practical prescription: “So whoever hopes for the meeting with their Lord, let them do good deeds and associate none in the worship of their Lord.” This sentence encapsulates the path to salvation. “Hopes for the meeting” means believing in the resurrection and desiring God’s reward with a combination of hope, fear, and love. The response to this hope is twofold: perform righteous deeds (‘amal ṣāliḥ) and maintain pure Tawhid by not associating any partner in the worship of the Lord. These two elements—correct action and pure faith—have been the consistent criteria for success throughout the surah, from the people of the cave to the believing companion to the righteous ruler Dhul-Qarnain. The verse ends with the emphatic prohibition of shirk, closing the surah as it began, with the declaration of praise for God who sent down the Book without crookedness, guiding to a straight path of pure, unadulterated worship and righteous living.
