1 | ˹The time of˺ people’s judgment has drawn near, yet they are heedlessly turning away. | The opening of this surah strikes with urgency. The phrase “the time of people’s judgment has drawn near” refers to the Day of Resurrection and the final accounting before Allah. It is described as near because whatever is coming is certainly close from the perspective of divine decree, and human life is fleeting. Despite this nearness, the verse laments that people are heedlessly turning away. This heedlessness is not a lack of information; it is a deliberate indifference, a turning aside from the reminders all around them. The Meccan polytheists to whom the Prophet was sent were deeply engrossed in their trade, their idols, and their social status. When the Prophet warned them of a coming judgment, they shrugged it off as remote or impossible. The Quran uses this tone of urgency to shake the listener awake. This verse echoes the opening of Surah An-Nahl and Surah Al-Qamar, where the nearness of the Hour is also proclaimed. The wisdom behind emphasizing nearness is to remove any sense that one can postpone repentance indefinitely. A person who believes that judgment is distant will delay preparation; one who feels it is near will hasten to correct his course. The concept of “people” here is universal, encompassing all of humanity, because every soul will stand before its Lord. The heedlessness described is a disease of the heart: outwardly people see the signs, but inwardly they refuse to let the truth penetrate their consciousness. The lesson is that believers must not fall into this pattern. While living in the world, one must remain aware that the ultimate judgment is ever-approaching. The verse thus sets the stage for the entire surah, which presents evidence, stories of past nations, and the certainty of resurrection, all aimed at curing this heedlessness and calling people to wakefulness before it is too late. 
2 | Whatever new reminder comes to them from their Lord, they only listen to it jokingly, | This verse describes the disbelievers’ reaction to the Quran. “Whatever new reminder comes to them from their Lord” refers to the successive revelations of verses and surahs that the Prophet Muhammad recited to them. Each new portion of the Quran was a fresh reminder, a renewed call to believe in one God, to fear the Day of Judgment, and to live righteously. Yet their response was not reflection but mockery. They “only listen to it jokingly,” meaning they approached the divine words as entertainment, making fun of the message and its messenger. In the gatherings of Quraysh, they would nudge one another and laugh, treating the profound truths as if they were nothing more than amusing tales. This attitude is a defense mechanism against the power of the truth. When a person’s heart is attached to worldly pleasures and false beliefs, sincere engagement with revelation becomes threatening; mockery is a way to dismiss without thinking. It is also a sign of arrogance, because it implies that the listener considers himself above the reminder. The verse highlights a tragic reality: the very words meant to give them life and guidance were turned into material for jest. For the believers, this verse serves as a warning: never allow familiarity with the Quran to breed a casual, joking attitude toward its verses. Every reminder from Allah should be received with attention and humility. It also comforted the Prophet, letting him know that the mockery he faced was not new; it was a pattern of those who reject faith. So the verse lays open the psychology of rejection, showing that the barrier is not intellectual but attitudinal, rooted in a heart that prefers amusement over contemplation. 
3 | with their hearts ˹totally˺ distracted. The evildoers would converse secretly, ˹saying,˺ “Is this ˹one˺ not human like yourselves? Would you fall for ˹this˺ witchcraft, even though you can ˹clearly˺ see?” | The first part of the verse, “with their hearts totally distracted,” uncovers the inner condition that feeds their mockery. Their laughter is not innocent; it comes from hearts that are distracted from their true purpose. The heart, in Quranic language, is the center of understanding and decision. When it becomes distracted by worldly ambitions, grudges, and vain pursuits, it cannot focus on the truth even when it hears it clearly. The verse then reveals their secret conversations. The evildoers among Quraysh would confer in hushed tones, away from the Prophet and the believers. They said to one another, “Is this one not human like yourselves?” This was their chief objection: a messenger should not be a mere man. They expected an angel or a supernatural being. Their statement “Would you fall for this witchcraft, even though you can clearly see?” shows they accused the Quran of being sorcery that captivated minds, making rational people accept something absurd. They appealed to common sense, as if the truth were obvious falsehood. This secret counsel reveals the conspiratorial nature of the opposition; they sought to reinforce one another’s rejection and prevent individuals from seriously considering the message. The verse exposes the tactic of attacking the messenger’s humanity, a theme that will be answered in the following verses by affirming that all messengers were human. The lesson is that when hearts are distracted, the mind finds rationalizations to reject even the clearest signs. It teaches believers to guard their hearts against the distractions that lead to such tragic misjudgments. 
4 | The Prophet responded, “My Lord ˹fully˺ knows every word spoken in the heavens and the earth. For He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” | In response to their secret accusations and mockery, the Prophet is instructed to turn to Allah. He declares that his Lord fully knows every word spoken in the heavens and the earth. This statement accomplishes several things at once. First, it reassures the Prophet that none of their plots or whispers are hidden from Allah; He hears them all and will hold them to account. Second, it is a subtle warning to the disbelievers that their private conversations are not really private; the One who revealed the Quran hears them. Third, it reframes the entire dispute. Their argument was that a mere human could not be a true messenger, but the Prophet redirects attention to the divine source: if Allah, the All-Hearing and All-Knowing, has chosen him and hears all, then the truth of his message is not dependent on his human nature. The two divine names at the end, “All-Hearing, All-Knowing,” are perfectly suited. Allah hears every utterance, so He heard their secret speech, and He knows their hidden intentions, so their inner distraction is not concealed from Him. This verse teaches believers a profound lesson: when confronted with mockery and secret plots, do not become consumed by them or try to refute every whisper. Instead, entrust the matter to Allah, who is fully aware. It also builds the Quranic theme that Allah’s knowledge encompasses all things, a source of comfort for the oppressed and a terror for the oppressors who think they can hide their schemes. 
5 | Yet they say, “This ˹Quran˺ is a set of confused dreams! No, he has fabricated it! No, he must be a poet! So let him bring us a ˹tangible˺ sign like those ˹prophets˺ sent before.” | The disbelievers’ position is presented as inconsistent and desperate. First, they call the Quran “a set of confused dreams,” suggesting it is the rambling of a man who sees jumbled visions in sleep. Then, they change their accusation: “No, he has fabricated it,” claiming he deliberately invented it. Then again, “No, he must be a poet,” accusing him of being a talented wordsmith whose words enchant but are not divine truth. This wavering between contradictory labels shows their confusion and refusal to settle on a reasoned critique. They are throwing out any accusation that might stick, a sign that they have no solid basis for rejection. Their demand follows: “So let him bring us a tangible sign like those prophets sent before.” They wanted a visible, physical miracle, something like the staff of Moses or the she-camel of Salih. In their minds, a book of recited verses was insufficient. They failed to see that the Quran itself is the greatest and most enduring sign. The verse captures a classic pattern of disbelief: when people are determined not to believe, they set conditions and move the goalposts endlessly. If a sign were sent, they would likely find another excuse. The Prophet’s response to such demands is not recorded in this verse, but the surah will later emphasize that the destruction of past nations came after they rejected clear signs, and yet their belief at the point of punishment was not accepted. This teaches that signs are given according to divine wisdom, not human demands, and that the Quran’s internal miracle is sufficient for those who sincerely reflect. 
6 | Not a ˹single˺ society We destroyed before them ever believed ˹after receiving the signs˺. Will these ˹pagans˺ then believe? | This verse provides a historical argument against the demand for a tangible sign. Allah points out that every society destroyed for its disbelief was given signs, but those signs did not produce faith. The nations of Noah, Hud, Salih, Lot, and others all received clear miracles tailored to their peoples, yet they rejected them and were annihilated. The question “Will these pagans then believe?” is rhetorical; the implication is that even if they were given the specific sign they demand, their hearts are no different from those of the destroyed nations. The verse serves as a warning to the Quraysh that demanding signs while entertaining mockery and secret counsel is a path to destruction, not to guidance. It also teaches a deep principle about the nature of faith. Miracles can compel outward acknowledgment, but they rarely produce the heartfelt, voluntary submission that Allah values. The real obstacle is not lack of evidence but pride, distraction, and love of the status quo. The Quran frequently emphasizes that the natural world, human conscience, and the revealed Book are all-sufficient signs for those who truly want guidance. By linking the Meccans to the destroyed societies, the verse is both a threat and an invitation: a threat that they are following the same doomed pattern, and an invitation to break that pattern by believing without waiting for a catastrophic sign. It also comforts the Prophet by showing that the rejection he faces is not a reflection on his message but a repetition of an age-old refusal. 
7 | We did not send ˹messengers˺ before you ˹O Prophet˺ except mere men inspired by Us. If you ˹polytheists˺ do not know ˹this already˺, then ask those who have knowledge ˹of the Scriptures˺. | Now the surah directly addresses the objection raised in verse 3, that a messenger should not be a mere human. Allah states a universal principle: every messenger sent before Muhammad was a man, not an angel. They ate, walked in the markets, married, and lived human lives. The only distinction was that they received divine inspiration. By affirming this, the Quran dismantles the argument that a human cannot be a messenger. The polytheists are then told, “If you do not know this already, then ask those who have knowledge of the Scriptures.” This refers to the Jews and Christians among the People of the Book, who knew that their own prophets—Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and others—were human beings. The Quraysh had commercial and social contact with Jewish communities in Arabia, and they respected the knowledge of the Scriptures. The verse urges them to verify this fact if they doubt it. This is a powerful logical move: it appeals to an authority the Meccans themselves recognized, showing that their own objection was baseless even by the standards of existing religious traditions. It also establishes an important Quranic method: using the witness of previous scriptures to confirm the truth. For believers, this verse reinforces the beauty of the prophetic model. A human messenger can be imitated; an angel could not be. The fact that the prophets were human means their lives are practical examples for us. The verse also hints that the real issue is not the messenger’s humanity but the disbelievers’ arrogance; they think themselves too great to follow someone like them, not realizing that divine choice elevates a man above all worldly rank. 
8 | We did not give those messengers ˹supernatural˺ bodies that did not need food, nor were they immortal. | Continuing the argument of the previous verse, Allah emphasizes the complete humanity of the messengers. They were not given bodies that could dispense with food, meaning they experienced hunger, thirst, and the need for sustenance. Nor were they immortal; they lived their appointed terms and faced death. This further dismantles any expectation that a prophet should be a supernatural being. The mention of food is significant because eating is a fundamentally human need that underscores dependency on the Creator. By highlighting that messengers ate food, the Quran is pointing to their humility and their oneness with the people they were sent to guide. Had they been angels who needed no food, they would have been distant from human experience. This verse also serves as a rebuttal to those who later exaggerated the status of prophets, such as Christians who elevated Jesus to divinity. A being who eats cannot be divine, for the divine is self-sufficient. The verse’s mention of mortality is equally important. The Prophet Muhammad is reminded, and through him all believers, that every human will die. This anticipates later verses in the surah that address the Prophet’s own mortality. The fact that messengers died means their mission was not about their personal permanence but about the eternal message they left behind. Thus, the verse contains a lesson about humility, the nature of prophethood, and the proper focus on the message rather than the messenger’s person. 
9 | Then We fulfilled Our promise to them, saving them along with whoever We willed and destroying the transgressors. | This verse shifts from the humanity of the messengers to the divine action that accompanied them. Allah states that He fulfilled His promise to those messengers: He saved them and the believers who followed them, and He destroyed the transgressors who rejected them. This historical pattern is a core theme in the Quran. The promise was always that the messengers and their followers would ultimately be delivered, while those who persisted in disbelief and oppression would face destruction. By mentioning this, Allah is comforting the Prophet Muhammad, assuring him that the same pattern will hold. The Meccans who mock and plot should not think they are beyond this law. The phrase “along with whoever We willed” indicates that divine salvation is selective, based on faith and righteousness, not on tribal or familial ties. It also emphasizes that deliverance is by Allah’s will, not by the messenger’s power. This verse bridges the general principle about messengers with the specific historical examples that will soon follow in the surah. It sets up an expectation: just as Allah was true to His word with previous nations, He will be true with this one. For believers, it is a source of immense hope. When oppression seems overwhelming and the truth appears weak, they can recall that the arc of history, under divine control, bends towards the salvation of the righteous and the downfall of the obstinate transgressors. The verse also serves as a warning to those who hear the message but choose to mock; they place themselves on the wrong side of an unbreakable divine promise. 
10 | We have surely revealed to you a Book, in which there is glory for you. Will you not then understand? | The addressee here is the entire community, or specifically the Quraysh. Allah declares that He has revealed a Book in which there is their glory, their honor, and their remembrance. This verse is a direct appeal to the Meccans to recognize the immense value of what has been sent to them. The Quran, revealed in their own language through one of their own, is not a source of shame or a cause for division; it is the very thing that could elevate them among the nations. The word “glory” translates “dhikr,” which also means reminder and renown. So the Book is simultaneously a reminder of their duties and a source of lasting honor if they accept it. The rhetorical question, “Will you not then understand?” is a call to think deeply. It suggests that their rejection is born of a failure to truly grasp the gift they have been given. Historically, the Quraysh took great pride in their lineage and their custodianship of the Kaaba. The verse implies that the Quran is a far greater source of honor than any of those worldly distinctions. This appeal to honor is a wise pedagogical approach; instead of only threatening, the Quran offers a positive motivation. The verse also connects to the earlier mention of the destroyed societies; those societies lost their chance, but the Quraysh still have the opportunity to embrace the Book and attain glory in this life and the next. The failure to understand is a recurring Quranic theme, pointing to a deliberate closing of the mind. Thus, the verse is a passionate invitation to reconsider, to see the Quran not as confused dreams or poetry, but as the very thing that could give their lives meaning and their community true greatness. 
11 | ˹Imagine˺ how many societies of wrongdoers We have destroyed, raising up other people after them! | The surah now begins to illustrate the pattern mentioned in verse 9 with a vivid call to reflect on history. The command “Imagine” or literally “And how many...” invites the listener to mentally survey the ruined civilizations of the past. Allah destroyed many societies of wrongdoers, meaning those who persisted in oppression, injustice, and rejection of the truth. The destruction was total, but Allah then raised up other people after them. This teaches that no nation is indispensable; if one generation fails its moral test, others will inherit the earth. The Quraysh passed by the ruins of the ‘Ad, Thamud, and the people of Lot during their trade journeys. The verse taps into that direct experience; they could see the physical evidence of past destructions. The aim is to instill a sense of vulnerability. The Meccans may feel secure in their wealth and power, but the same earth that cradles their cities once cradled those who are now only a memory. By mentioning that other people were raised up, the verse also hints at a divine plan of continuous replacement, which is both a comfort to the oppressed (new, better communities will come) and a threat to the oppressors (they can be easily replaced). The lesson for believers is to always remember that the rise and fall of civilizations is in Allah’s hands, governed by moral laws. No amount of material advancement can protect a society that becomes a “society of wrongdoers.” The verse also sets the stage for the more detailed description of the panic that seizes the wrongdoers when the punishment arrives, which follows in the next verses. 
12 | When the wrongdoers sensed ˹the arrival of˺ Our torment, they started to run away from their cities. | The narrative zooms in on the moment destruction approaches a wrongdoing community. “When the wrongdoers sensed Our torment” describes that terrifying instant when the signs of imminent divine punishment become undeniable. Their immediate reaction is not repentance, but flight. They start to run away from their cities, as if physical escape could outrun divine decree. This is a deeply human picture: even the most arrogant oppressors, when confronted with overwhelming catastrophe, instinctively flee. The verse uses this image to expose the futility of trying to evade Allah’s judgment. Their cities, which were the seats of their power, luxury, and sinful enjoyment, suddenly become places of terror that they desperately want to leave behind. Historically, this could refer to various destroyed nations. Some scholars link it to the people of Lot, or to other nations upon whom a sudden storm or earthquake descended. The point is universal. It also shows the psychology of the wrongdoer: they recognize the punishment as coming from Allah, but instead of turning to Him in humility, they attempt to escape it. Their flight is a continuation of their rebellion, not a genuine turning back. This verse serves as a warning that when Allah’s punishment arrives, there is no refuge. It also comforts believers that the apparent power of tyrants is an illusion; the same people who strut arrogantly will one day flee like frightened animals, unable to save themselves. The vividness of the imagery is meant to awaken the heedless hearts mentioned in verse 1, painting a scene they should dread and seek to avoid through faith and righteous deeds. 
13 | ˹They were told,˺ “Do not run away! Return to your luxuries and your homes, so you may be questioned ˹about your fate˺.” | As the wrongdoers flee, they are met with a sarcastic divine command, either from angels or as a statement of their inevitable fate. They are told not to run away, but to return to their luxuries and their homes. The word “luxuries” translates a term that conveys opulence, ease, and the material blessings in which they took pride. The command to return is not a genuine offer of safety but a bitter irony. They are being reminded of what they are losing and of the life they valued more than the truth. The phrase “so you may be questioned about your fate” implies that they will be held to account for how they used those blessings. Some commentators interpret the questioning as a literal interrogation about their deeds, while others see it as a rhetorical statement meaning “you will face what you deserve.” In either case, the verse underscores that their luxuries and homes, which they thought were signs of divine favor or their own cleverness, are actually a test, and now the test is over. The scene is stark: the very things they ran from their cities to save their lives are now the things to which they are mockingly told to return, because there is no escape anyway. This verse is a profound meditation on the attachment to worldly comforts. Those comforts can blind people to their spiritual state. When the moment of reckoning comes, these comforts provide no refuge and become part of the punishment through regret. The lesson is to hold worldly blessings lightly, enjoy them with gratitude, but never let them become the reason to reject truth, for they are fleeting and will be a witness against those who misused them. 
14 | They cried, “Woe to us! We have surely been wrongdoers.” | At last, the wrongdoers confess. When all escape routes are cut off and punishment is upon them, they cry out “Woe to us!” — an expression of profound grief, regret, and despair. Their admission, “We have surely been wrongdoers,” is a clear acknowledgment of their guilt. It is a belated recognition of what the messengers had been telling them all along. This confession is significant because it demonstrates that the truth was never hidden from them; they had always known, deep down, that they were on the wrong path. But now the confession is of no avail. The door of repentance has closed with the arrival of the decisive punishment. This pattern is repeated throughout the Quran: Pharaoh’s confession as he drowned, the cries of other nations as destruction enveloped them. The verse teaches a crucial lesson about the window of opportunity. Sincere repentance is accepted as long as the sun has not risen from the west and the death rattle has not reached the throat. Once the punishment is witnessed and certain, faith born of compulsion is not true faith. The verse also contains a warning for those who procrastinate in turning to Allah, thinking they will repent later. Later may not come, or it may come at a time when repentance is no longer accepted. For believers, hearing this cry of “Woe to us!” should motivate them to examine their own lives now, to admit their wrongdoings while they still have time to correct them. The confession, though futile for those who utter it, serves as a testimony against them and a lesson for all who hear the Quran. 
15 | They kept repeating their cry until We mowed them down, ˹leaving them˺ lifeless. | This verse completes the picture of destruction with a terrifying image. The wrongdoers’ cry of “Woe to us!” was not a single utterance; it became their constant refrain, their only remaining speech, as the punishment raged around them. They kept repeating their cry of regret, but it did not halt the divine judgment. The phrase “until We mowed them down” uses the imagery of harvesting. Just as a farmer cuts down crops with a sickle, leaving them lying flat and lifeless on the ground, so Allah’s punishment cut them down. The word “lifeless” emphasizes that they were left utterly still, extinguished, a people who once swaggered with pride now reduced to silent corpses. The image is stark and designed to strike fear into the heart of anyone who hears it. There is a clear cause-and-effect logic: their persistent wrongdoings and mockery of the messengers led to persistent regret and then total annihilation. The verse also conveys the finality of divine judgment when it arrives; there is no reprieve, no second chance. The repeated cries highlight the depth of their anguish, yet the heavens were deaf to them because they had closed their own ears to the cries of the messengers during their lifetimes. The lesson here is about the justice of Allah. He does not wrong anyone; people wrong themselves, and their own tongues will testify against them. This conclusion of the scene of destruction serves as a powerful bridge to the next part of the surah, which will refute the idea that creation is purposeless and will establish the truth of resurrection and judgment. The vividness of the destruction imagery is meant to shake the listener from the heedlessness mentioned in the very first verse. 
16 | We did not create the heavens and the earth and everything in between for sport. | Having warned of the destruction of wrongdoers, the surah now turns to a fundamental argument for the reality of judgment. It declares that Allah did not create the massive, intricate universe “for sport.” The heavens, the earth, and all that exists between them were not brought into being as a meaningless game or a passing amusement. This is a direct response to those who think that life ends with death and that there is no ultimate purpose or accountability. Creation, with its order, beauty, and complexity, points to a Creator with serious purpose. The word “sport” translates “la‘ib,” which conveys play, amusement, something done without serious intent. To suggest that the universe is the product of divine sport is to insult the wisdom of the Creator. The verse argues from the nature of creation to the nature of its end. Because creation is purposeful, there must be a final reckoning where the purposes of moral choice are realized. The heavens and the earth are not a stage for empty entertainment; they are a testing ground. This verse also refutes the materialist worldview that sees the cosmos as a random accident without inherent meaning. The Quran consistently appeals to the natural world as a sign, and here it uses the very grandeur of the universe to argue that the Creator does not engage in frivolity. The lesson is that human life, as part of this creation, is likewise purposeful. We were not created for sport, so our lives should not be lived in vain pursuits. This verse sets the foundation for the next verses, which will elaborate on what divine purpose is not and what it is. 
17 | Had We intended to take ˹some˺ amusement, We could have found it in Our presence, if that had been Our Will. | This verse continues the argument of the previous one by posing a hypothetical scenario. If Allah had wanted to take amusement, He would not have needed to create the universe to do so. He could have found it “in Our presence,” meaning within the divine realm, without the elaborate process of creation, revelation, and judgment. The statement implies that Allah is entirely self-sufficient; any notion of amusement is inherently unworthy of His majesty. The hypothetical is framed to show the absurdity of thinking that creation is a divine pastime. The phrase “if that had been Our Will” further emphasizes that Allah does not will such frivolity. The verse is also a subtle rebuttal of those who believed that angels were daughters of God or that God had a family, ideas that are explicitly refuted later in the surah. It anticipates the statement that all beings in the heavens and the earth belong to Allah and worship Him. By asserting that amusement was not the purpose and that any supposed amusement would not require this grand creation, the verse reinforces the concept that the universe is created in truth and for a serious purpose. The logical flow is impeccable: if you claim creation is a game, you imply a need or a whim in the Creator, but the Creator is above such things. The lesson for believers is to align their own understanding of life with this divine purpose. If Allah does not create for amusement, then our lives should not be lived for amusement either. The world is a place of trial and meaningful action, not a playground. This prepares the mind for the next verse, which describes what Allah actually does with truth and falsehood. 
18 | In fact, We hurl the truth against falsehood, leaving it crushed, and it quickly vanishes. And woe be to you for what you claim! | Instead of amusement, the real divine action is described in powerful combat imagery: Allah hurls the truth against falsehood. The truth, meaning divine revelation, sound belief, and righteous reality, is like a solid projectile. Falsehood, with all its illusions and baseless claims, is the target. The result is that falsehood is crushed, its structure shattered, and it quickly vanishes. This metaphor explains the dynamic of history and the cosmic struggle between right and wrong. While falsehood may temporarily appear strong and widespread, a single strike of truth from Allah causes it to crumble, just as a glass idol shatters when struck by a hammer. The phrase “And woe be to you for what you claim” is a direct warning to the polytheists who make false claims about Allah, including the ideas that He has offspring or partners, or that creation is purposeless. Their claims are part of the falsehood that will be destroyed. The verse offers immense hope to the believers: the truth they hold may seem weak in the face of worldly powers, but it carries within it the force of divine reality. When Allah wills, He hurls it, and falsehood cannot stand. This also explains why the Prophet and his followers should not be disheartened by mockery; the battle is not theirs but Allah’s, and the outcome is certain. On a personal level, every believer can apply this verse by “hurling” the truth of the Quran against the false ideas that whisper in their own hearts, shattering doubts and vain desires through firm faith. 
19 | To Him belong all those in the heavens and the earth. And those nearest to Him are not too proud to worship Him, nor do they tire. | This verse transitions from the destruction of falsehood to the universal reality of divine ownership and worship. Everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah; this ownership is absolute. Among those who belong to Him are the angels, described here as “those nearest to Him.” Despite their elevated status and closeness to the divine throne, they are not too proud to worship Allah. Unlike the arrogant polytheists who disdained following a human messenger and refused to bow to the Creator, the angels, who are far superior in power and purity, continuously serve Allah without any sense of pride or fatigue. The phrase “nor do they tire” is crucial. It shows that worship is not a burden they wish to escape; their devotion is constant and joyful. This is a sharp contrast to the Meccan elite, who considered it beneath their dignity to worship one God and who were weary of the call to prayer and devotion. The verse implicitly asks: if the noblest creatures serve Allah tirelessly, who are you to refuse? It also refutes any idea that angels are partners or offspring of God, as they are themselves servants. This description of angelic worship prepares the way for the next verse, which emphasizes their continuous glorification. For the believer, this verse provides a model of humble servitude. The noblest beings are the most humble before their Lord. Pride is the attribute of the disbeliever, while humility and tireless devotion mark those truly close to Allah. The verse invites us to examine our own worship: do we perform it out of love and reverence, without weariness? 
20 | They glorify ˹Him˺ day and night, never wavering. | Completing the description of the angels, this verse emphasizes the constancy of their glorification. They glorify Allah day and night, meaning perpetually, without interruption. The phrase “never wavering” translates a word that indicates no fatigue, no slackening, no loss of energy. Their glorification is perfect and continuous. This glorification is both their primary activity and their natural state of being. The verse functions on multiple levels. First, it glorifies Allah by describing the worship of the most honored beings. Second, it serves as a model for believers, who are called to remember Allah often, standing, sitting, and lying on their sides. While human beings cannot match the angels’ ceaseless glorification due to the need for sleep and daily duties, the ideal is to fill one’s life with the remembrance of Allah so that it becomes a constant thread. Third, it contrasts the angels’ unwavering devotion with the disbelievers’ heedlessness and the believers’ occasional lapses. The angels’ glorification is not only verbal; it is an expression of their submission to Allah’s command. The verse thus forms a beautiful conclusion to this passage, which began with the heedlessness of humans turning away from judgment, passed through the arguments for purpose and the smashing of falsehood, and arrives at the picture of universal, tireless worship. It reminds us that the entire cosmos, except for defiant humans and jinn, is in a state of submission and glorification. The only ones out of tune with this cosmic symphony are those who refuse to worship their Lord. This verse inspires a sense of awe and a desire to join that great company of worshippers, however imperfectly.
21 | Or have they taken gods from the earth, who can raise the dead? | After establishing the absolute ownership and constant worship of all beings in the heavens and the earth, the surah now confronts the pagans with a simple, devastating question: have they taken gods from the earth who can raise the dead? The phrase “from the earth” is significant because it points to the lowly origin of their idols, fashioned from stone, wood, or metal dug out of the ground. These gods are earthbound, created by human hands, utterly powerless. The challenge “who can raise the dead?” strikes at the heart of the pagan worldview. The Quraysh did not believe in resurrection; they considered it a myth. Yet, if their gods were truly divine, they should possess the power over life and death, the most fundamental divine attribute. The question is rhetorical: the answer is obviously that none of them can. The idols are lifeless, and they cannot grant life to anyone. The verse forces the listener to think about the logical requirement of divinity: a true God must be able to originate life and restore it. This is the core argument for the oneness of Allah. If the idols cannot raise the dead, they cannot judge, reward, or punish; they are worthless. The question also prepares the mind for the later discussions in the surah about resurrection. It highlights the absurdity of worshipping objects that are themselves dug from the ground and will one day be buried again, alongside those who worshipped them. The only One worthy of worship is the One who will raise all of humanity from their graves on the Day of Judgment. This verse is a call to reason, asking the pagans to assess their gods by a clear, rational standard of divine capability. 
22 | Had there been other gods besides Allah in the heavens or the earth, both ˹realms˺ would have surely been corrupted. So Glorified is Allah, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim. | This verse presents one of the Quran’s most powerful rational arguments for monotheism, known as the argument from mutual hindrance or cosmic order. The logic is precise: if there were multiple gods in the heavens or the earth, each possessing independent will and authority, the universe would fall into chaos and corruption. Rival wills would clash; one might want the sun to rise while another wanted night to continue, leading to the collapse of the orderly system we observe. The perfect, harmonious functioning of the cosmos is a testament to a single, uncontested Will. The verse declares that both realms would have become corrupted, meaning utterly spoiled and ruined. This refutes any polytheistic notion of a pantheon managing different departments of the universe. The conclusion “So Glorified is Allah, Lord of the Throne, far above what they claim” exalts Allah above all such false attributions. The Throne represents His supreme authority over all creation. The mention of the Throne, a symbol of majestic dominion, reinforces that He alone governs everything. This argument resonated deeply with the Meccans, who appreciated the order of the seasons and the stars due to their trade journeys. The verse appeals to that shared observation: the world is not chaotic, therefore its Governor must be One. The lesson for believers is that the unity of Allah is not just a dogma but an observable truth reflected in the harmony of nature. It also teaches that wherever there are multiple ultimate authorities, corruption inevitably follows, a principle that applies even in human governance. The grandeur of the verse leads the heart to declare Allah’s transcendence over the limitations of polytheistic imagination. 
23 | He cannot be questioned about what He does, but they will ˹all˺ be questioned. | This verse establishes the absolute sovereignty of Allah and the accountability of human beings. “He cannot be questioned about what He does” means that His actions are not subject to the scrutiny or judgment of any creature. Allah does what He wills, and His wisdom is so complete that there is no room for anyone to demand an explanation. This is not an arbitrary assertion; it flows from His perfect knowledge, justice, and mercy. A human judge can be questioned and his rulings appealed because he is fallible. But the One whose decisions stem from infinite wisdom and whose actions are inherently good and just is beyond question. In contrast, “they will all be questioned” — every human being will be brought to account for their deeds. This double declaration brings immense comfort and solemn warning. It comforts the believers that the injustices they suffer are under Allah’s supreme wisdom, and He will not be questioned about why He allowed them, though He will hold the oppressors accountable. It warns the transgressors that while they now question the Prophet and demand signs, one day they will be the ones interrogated. The verse also answers those who ask why certain divine commands are given; the proper stance is submission, recognizing our limited intellect compared to divine wisdom. However, this does not discourage asking for understanding; it simply defines the proper relationship of humble submission. The verse powerfully shifts the perspective: instead of humanity putting Allah in the dock, it is humanity that will stand in the dock. This teaching nurtures a profound sense of humility and responsibility before the Divine. 
24 | Or have they taken other gods besides Him? Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof. Here is ˹the Quran,˺ the Reminder for those with me; along with ˹earlier Scriptures,˺ the Reminder for those before me.” But most of them do not know the truth, so they turn away. | The questioning continues, challenging the polytheists to provide evidence for their worship of other gods. The command to the Prophet is clear: demand proof. Religion is not a matter of blind following; it must be built on clear evidence. The Prophet is told to say, “Show me your proof.” This is a rational challenge, demonstrating that Islam does not fear scrutiny. Then the verse immediately presents the proof for monotheism: “Here is the Quran, the Reminder for those with me; along with earlier Scriptures, the Reminder for those before me.” The Quran, and the Torah, Psalms, and Gospel in their original forms, all testify to the absolute oneness of Allah. The consistent message of all prophets is the same: there is no god but Allah. So the proof is historical and scriptural. If the pagans claim divine sanction for their idols, let them produce any scripture or revelation that commands it. They cannot, because no true revelation ever sanctioned polytheism. The phrase “But most of them do not know the truth, so they turn away” explains their rejection: it is not based on a careful examination of evidence but on ignorance and a stubborn desire to avoid the truth. This verse is a masterclass in how to engage with false beliefs: ask for evidence, and present the clear evidence of revelation. It also underscores the unity of the divine message, a powerful link between the Muslim community and the earlier communities of faith. The believers hold the Reminder, and the earlier scriptures, although corrupted in their present form, originally contained the same Reminder. This is a call to the People of the Book to recognize Islam and to the pagans to realize that their practices have no scriptural basis. 
25 | We never sent a messenger before you ˹O Prophet˺ without revealing to him: “There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Me, so worship Me ˹alone˺.” | This verse declares the universal and unchanging core of all prophetic messages. From the first messenger to the last, the fundamental call has always been the same: pure monotheism. The phrase “We never sent a messenger before you” emphasizes that this is not an innovation brought by Prophet Muhammad but the eternal truth. The revealed message is summarized in the twin declaration: there is no god worthy of worship except Allah, and therefore worship must be directed to Him alone. The word “worship” includes not only ritual acts like prayer and sacrifice but also ultimate love, fear, hope, and obedience. The verse refutes any notion that different religions have fundamentally different goals or that idolatry was ever permitted. All prophets—Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and others—taught their people to abandon false gods and submit to the one true God. The historical deviations into polytheism and trinitarianism were later corruptions. This is a deeply reassuring verse for believers: the path they are on is the same path walked by all the prophets. It also silences the Meccan pagans who claimed that their idol worship was an inherited tradition. The verse tells them that no messenger ever sanctioned such a tradition. It places the Prophet Muhammad within a grand brotherhood of monotheistic preachers, strengthening his resolve. The lesson for all humanity is that salvation lies in the pure worship of the one Creator, a message that remains as urgent today as it was in any age. It strips away all theological complexities and returns to the simple, pristine truth that resonates with the natural disposition of every human soul. 
26 | And they say, “The Most Compassionate has offspring!” Glory be to Him! In fact, those ˹angels˺ are only ˹His˺ honoured servants, | The surah now addresses a specific and grievous false claim: the assertion that Allah has offspring. Some Arab pagans claimed the angels were daughters of God, while Christians spoke of Jesus as the Son of God, and certain Jewish groups had similar notions about Ezra. The verse uses the divine name “The Most Compassionate” (Ar-Rahman) to highlight the absurdity of such a claim; the One whose mercy encompasses all is far above needing a child. The immediate response is “Glory be to Him!” — a declaration of His perfection and transcendence. The verse then corrects the misconception: those they call offspring, specifically the angels, are nothing more than honoured servants of Allah. They are not divine relatives; they are creations, elevated in rank but still subservient. This correction serves multiple purposes. It refutes the pagan Arab belief, it challenges the Christian doctrine of divine sonship, and it places the angels in their proper theological context as noble servants, not objects of worship. The word “honoured” indicates their high status, but it is the status of a servant, not a child. This teaching liberates the human mind from the myth of a divine family, which was often used to justify idol worship by claiming that these lesser deities could intercede. The verse begins a passage that describes the true nature of the angels, their complete submission and their fear of Allah, utterly dismantling the idea that they could be independent divine beings. 
27 | who do not speak until He has spoken, ˹only˺ acting at His command. | Continuing the description of the angels, this verse emphasizes their absolute obedience. They do not speak until He has spoken, meaning they never initiate an action or utterance without divine permission. This is a direct contrast to the human perception of powerful beings who act independently. The angels, despite their immense power and high rank, are utterly bound by the command of Allah. They do not intercede on their own, they do not offer unsolicited advice, and they do not alter the decree. This point is crucial for dismantling pagan worship; if the angels themselves cannot speak or act without Allah’s leave, how could they possibly answer prayers or provide help to those who worship them? The second part, “only acting at His command,” reinforces the first: their entire activity is reactive to divine will. This also serves as a model for believers. The noblest creatures are those who submit their will perfectly to Allah. The angels’ state of constant readiness and obedience is the ideal that the believers strive for when they say “we hear and we obey.” The verse also prepares for the following clarification that the angels intercede only with Allah’s permission, not as an independent right. In the context of the surah, this is a further proof of pure monotheism; the entire heavenly realm operates under a single, unchallenged command. The description of the angels in these verses serves as a mirror for humanity, showing what true servitude looks like and exposing the foolishness of elevating any servant, no matter how exalted, to the status of the divine. 
28 | He ˹fully˺ knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them. They do not intercede except for whom He approves, and they tremble in awe of Him. | This verse continues to outline the relationship between Allah and the angels, emphasizing divine knowledge and control. “He fully knows what is ahead of them and what is behind them” signifies that Allah’s knowledge encompasses every aspect of the angels’ existence: their future, their past, their deeds, and their inner states. Nothing about them is hidden. Therefore, they are not autonomous beings who might surprise Allah or act outside His knowledge. The verse then states a crucial theological principle regarding intercession: “They do not intercede except for whom He approves.” The pagan Arabs believed their idols and angels would intercede for them with Allah, pleading on their behalf. This verse refutes that idea by declaring that any intercession by the angels is strictly conditional on Allah’s approval. They do not intercede out of their own love for someone or because of offerings made to them; they intercede only for those whom Allah is already pleased with. This nullifies the core of pagan hope in intercession. The final clause, “and they tremble in awe of Him,” portrays the angels’ profound reverence. Despite their nearness and honor, they are overwhelmed by fear of Allah’s majesty. This trembling indicates that they fully grasp His greatness and their own insignificance, far from the proud independence that the pagans attributed to their gods. The verse thus teaches believers to rely directly on Allah’s mercy and to seek His approval directly, because even the angels can only help those whom Allah has already chosen to help. 
29 | Whoever of them were to say, “I am a god besides Him,” they would be rewarded with Hell by Us. This is how We reward the wrongdoers. | This verse concludes the passage about the angels with a severe warning, applicable to any exalted being. Even if an angel, despite their nearness, were to claim divinity and say “I am a god besides Him,” the punishment would be swift and certain: Hell. This hypothetical scenario serves to underscore the absolute oneness of Allah. No rank, no nearness, no past obedience exempts any creature from the consequences of the greatest sin: associating partners with Allah. The angels are presented as the ultimate test case; if the most honored servants would be cast into Hell for such a claim, then what about mere human beings who make such claims or worship others alongside Allah? The verse also implicitly refutes the Christian belief in the divinity of Jesus and the pagan worship of angels. It declares that any creature who crosses the line into claiming godhood will be dealt with as a wrongdoer. The phrase “This is how We reward the wrongdoers” makes clear that the punishment is not arbitrary; it is the just recompense for the ultimate wrongdoing. This verse provides a powerful moral lesson: humility and acknowledgment of one’s servant status are fundamental to righteousness. The moment a creation, no matter how great, oversteps its bounds and asserts independence or divinity, it brings upon itself utter ruin. It reinforces the theme that all honor belongs to Allah, and any honour granted to a servant is a test, not a license to be worshipped. The believers are reminded to be steadfast in directing all worship, in all its forms, to the One who alone deserves it. 
30 | Do the disbelievers not realize that the heavens and earth were ˹once˺ one mass then We split them apart? And We created from water every living thing. Will they not then believe? | The surah now shifts to the signs in the universe that testify to Allah’s power and oneness. The verse calls the disbelievers to reflect on the origin of the cosmos. The phrase “the heavens and earth were once one mass then We split them apart” describes a primordial unity followed by a separation that formed the structured universe. This has often been noted by commentators as harmonizing with the modern concept of the Big Bang, but the Quran’s primary purpose is not science; it is to demonstrate the creative power that brought the cosmos into being from nothing, which is the same power that can resurrect the dead. The second sign is “We created from water every living thing.” Water is the essential component of all known life. This observation was as evident to the desert Arab as it is to the modern biologist, though the Quran states it as a fact pointing to a Creator. The question “Will they not then believe?” is a direct appeal to the mind. The signs are so clear that denial is unreasonable. The verse links the macrocosm (the universe) with the microcosm (living organisms), both demonstrating a single intelligent design. This is a powerful argument against atheism and polytheism, for the unity and coherence of creation point to a single Creator. The lesson is that reflection on the world around us should naturally lead to faith. The verse also provides a perfect example of how the Quran uses observable phenomena to guide to spiritual truths, making it a timeless reminder that science and faith, when properly understood, are not in conflict but are complementary paths to recognizing the Creator. 
31 | And We have placed firm mountains upon the earth so it does not shake with them, and made in it broad pathways so they may find their way. | Continuing the signs, the verse moves from the cosmic to the terrestrial. Mountains are described as having been placed firmly upon the earth to prevent it from shaking with its inhabitants. The Quran uses the metaphor of pegs or stabilizers for mountains, suggesting that they play a role in the geological stability of the earth’s crust, a notion that resonates with modern understanding of isostasy and tectonic plates, though the simple meaning is that mountains make the earth stable and habitable rather than a constantly quaking surface. This is a sign of Allah’s care for humanity, making the world a secure dwelling. The second part mentions the “broad pathways” made in the earth, referring to mountain passes, valleys, and natural routes that allow people to travel and find their way. For the Meccans, who embarked on long trade journeys through mountainous terrain, this was a direct and personal sign of divine facilitation. Without such pathways, travel and commerce would be extremely difficult. The verse invites gratitude and reflection: the earth is not a chaotic or hostile place but has been carefully prepared for human life and civilization. This provision is from the same Creator who split the heavens and the earth. Together, these physical signs form a coherent picture of a world designed with purpose, which logically points to a Designer who will also bring about a Day of Judgment to complete the purpose of creation. The practical lesson is to travel the earth with a conscious eye, seeing in its features the generosity and wisdom of Allah, and to use those pathways to spread goodness and truth. 
32 | And We have made the sky a well-protected canopy, still they turn away from its signs. | The verse ascends to the sky, describing it as a “well-protected canopy.” The sky, or the atmosphere and the heavens beyond, is a protective ceiling over the earth. It shields life from harmful cosmic radiation, from the vacuum of space, and from many celestial hazards. The word “canopy” conveys the image of a sheltering roof. This protective function is a sign of Allah’s mercy, as without it, life on earth would be impossible. The verse then laments that despite this clear sign, the disbelievers still turn away. They look at the sky every day, experience its benefit, but refuse to see the Giver behind the gift. Their turning away is not due to lack of evidence but to a willful aversion. The phrase “its signs” refers to the signs present within the sky: the sun, the moon, the stars, the orderly movement of celestial bodies, and the protective nature itself. The verse connects the physical sign with the spiritual blindness of the disbelievers, creating a poignant contrast. For believers, this verse is an invitation to look up at the sky with renewed faith and gratitude, recognizing it as more than just a physical phenomenon; it is an ayah, a sign pointing to the Creator. It also warns against the danger of taking the sky for granted and missing the message it carries. The same sky that protects can also become a means of destruction when the heavens are rolled up on the Day of Judgment, a theme that the surah will later address. The sky, then, is both a present mercy and a reminder of the ultimate return. 
33 | And He is the One Who created the day and the night, the sun and the moon—each travelling in an orbit. | This verse continues the cosmic signs by focusing on the most immediate and observable phenomena: the alternation of day and night and the celestial bodies. Allah is the one who created the day and the night, bringing them in a precise, regular cycle that governs all life. The day is for activity and seeking provision; the night is for rest and tranquility. This alternation is not haphazard but is a deliberate, merciful design. The verse then mentions the sun and the moon, describing each as “travelling in an orbit.” The Arabic word “falak” denotes a rounded course or orbit. This description, revealed in the 7th century, accurately conveys the motion of celestial bodies in set paths. The sun orbits the center of the galaxy, and the moon orbits the earth, all in precise, predictable trajectories. This orderliness speaks of a Lawgiver who established the physical laws. For the Meccans, the sun and moon were objects of awe and sometimes worship; the Quran demystifies them by describing them as created entities moving in assigned orbits, entirely under Allah’s command. This undermines any worship of celestial bodies and redirects awe toward their Maker. The verse is a powerful call to Tawheed: the One who creates and sustains these great lights is the only One worthy of worship. It also invites reflection on the precision of the universe, which science has only increasingly uncovered. The harmony of day and night and the motion of the sun and moon are signs that the Creator is All-Wise and that He has set a term for everything, just as He has set a term for human life. This naturally transitions to the next verse about mortality. 
34 | We have not granted immortality to any human before you ˹O Prophet˺: so if you die, will they live forever? | This verse directly addresses the Prophet Muhammad and, through him, all humanity, about the reality of death. The pagan opponents often waited for the Prophet to die, thinking his message would then fade away. They also found the idea of resurrection strange. The verse declares a universal law: no human being before Muhammad was granted immortality. Every prophet, every king, every mighty person eventually died. So the question “if you die, will they live forever?” is a powerful rhetorical blow. It confronts the pagans with their own mortality. They may rejoice at the thought of the Prophet’s death, but they too will die. The message he brought is not dependent on his physical permanence; it is the truth from the Eternal God. This verse also comforted the Prophet, reminding him that death is the natural end for all mortals and that his role was to deliver the message, not to live forever. The verse implicitly refutes any tendency to worship the Prophet or to consider him divine, as his mortality is shared by all. The lesson for believers is to detach their faith from personalities and attach it to the eternal truth. When a righteous leader or scholar passes away, the community should not despair, because the source of the message is the Ever-Living. The verse also serves as a memento mori, a reminder of death that should inspire immediate preparation for the afterlife. It prepares the heart for the next verse, which universalizes the statement of mortality to every soul. 
35 | Every soul will taste death. And We test you ˹O humanity˺ with good and evil as a trial, then to Us you will ˹all˺ be returned. | This verse is one of the most profound declarations about human existence in the Quran. “Every soul will taste death” is absolute and all-encompassing. No one, no matter how powerful, rich, or pious, can escape this cup. The word “taste” suggests that death is an experience that everyone will fully undergo, passing through the gate from this world to the next. The second sentence, “And We test you with good and evil as a trial,” explains the nature of this life. Life is not a random series of events but a carefully designed test. The “good” includes health, wealth, success, and all blessings, which test whether a person will be grateful and use them righteously. The “evil” includes poverty, illness, loss, and hardship, which test patience and faith. Neither condition is a sign of divine pleasure or wrath in itself; both are tools of trial. The phrase “then to Us you will all be returned” reveals the destination. Life’s test ends with a return to Allah for judgment. This verse encapsulates the entire Islamic worldview: life, death, trial, and return. It is a call to live consciously, always mindful that every situation is a test. The verse also provides immense comfort. For the one suffering, it reminds that the trial is temporary and will be compensated. For the one enjoying ease, it warns that the blessing is a trust to be accounted for. This explains why the Prophet and the believers face hardship while the disbelievers may enjoy luxury; it is all part of the trial, and the ultimate outcome will be determined in the Hereafter. The verse thus serves as a spiritual compass for navigating all of life’s circumstances. 
36 | When the disbelievers see you ˹O Prophet˺, they only make fun of you, ˹saying,˺ “Is this the one who speaks ˹ill˺ of your gods?” while they disbelieve at the mention of the Most Compassionate. | The surah returns to describing the contemporary reaction of the Meccan disbelievers to the Prophet. They do not meet him with respect or serious debate; they only make fun of him. The mockery is captured in their words: “Is this the one who speaks ill of your gods?” They would point him out to one another, ridiculing the man who dared to criticize their cherished idols. The phrase “speaks ill of your gods” reveals their primary irritation: the Prophet’s uncompromising monotheism threatened their ancestral religion and their social and economic structure built around idol worship. The verse then exposes a deep inconsistency in their attitude. They scoff at the Prophet for challenging their gods, yet they themselves disbelieve at the mention of the Most Compassionate. When they hear the name Ar-Rahman, the uniquely merciful God, they react with rejection and aversion. In fact, according to historical reports, the Quraysh even disliked the name “Ar-Rahman” and preferred the names of their idols. This contrast shows that their anger is not really about protecting sacred things; it is about clinging to their own false deities while rejecting the true God whose mercy encompasses all. Their mockery is thus a symptom of a deeper spiritual illness. This verse comforted the Prophet, showing him that the mockery was rooted in their own blindness, not in any deficiency in his message. For believers, it is a lesson that being ridiculed for speaking the truth is a prophetic legacy, and one must endure it with patience, knowing that the rejecters are only harming themselves by shutting their ears to the name of the Most Compassionate. 
37 | Humankind is made of haste. I will soon show you My signs, so do not ask Me to hasten them. | This verse diagnoses a fundamental human trait: haste. Humankind is made of haste, meaning impatience and a desire for immediate results are woven into human nature. The pagans, out of this haste, demanded that the punishment they were warned about be brought immediately. They would say, “When is this promise, if you are truthful?” The verse addresses this directly. Allah declares, “I will soon show you My signs,” meaning the signs of His power and the truth of the message will become manifest in due time, whether in this world through victory or punishment, or in the Hereafter. The command “so do not ask Me to hasten them” is a rebuke. The timing of divine signs is based on absolute wisdom, not human impatience. Rushing divine decree is a sign of a lack of trust and understanding. The verse also contains a profound psychological insight. Haste leads people to demand proof on their own terms, and when it doesn’t come immediately, they reject the message altogether. But divine wisdom often unfolds gradually, allowing time for hearts to repent and for the test to complete its course. The believers are taught to be patient and to trust that Allah’s timing is perfect. The history of the prophets is filled with examples where victory or punishment came after a long period of waiting, and when it came, it was all the more decisive. The verse thus counsels a calm, long-term view of the struggle between truth and falsehood, rooted in the knowledge that Allah’s signs will definitely appear, but according to His schedule, not ours. 
38 | They ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?” | The verse now quotes the exact question the disbelievers would pose to the Prophet and his followers. Their question, “When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?” is a challenge wrapped in sarcasm. They did not believe in the Day of Judgment or the punishment; they considered it a fairy tale. By asking for a specific date, they sought to embarrass the believers and prove that the warning was empty. This demand for a precise timetable is a classic tactic of those who reject unseen realities. They treat the delay as proof of falsehood, not realizing that the delay itself is a mercy. The verse presents the question without an immediate answer in this same verse, allowing the following verses to address it in depth. The question reflects the materialist mindset that only values the immediate and tangible. It also reveals a deep misunderstanding of the nature of divine threats. Allah’s warnings are not like human threats that lose credibility if not carried out swiftly. The delay is part of the divine plan, allowing people to repent and making the eventual punishment, if it comes, an undeniable manifestation of justice. For the believers, hearing this question posed so openly in the Quran validated their own experience of being heckled by the pagans. It reminded them that they were not alone in facing such taunts; the scripture itself recorded and answered them. The verse captures the tension of the Meccan period and sets up the powerful and frightening response in the next two verses. 
39 | If only the disbelievers knew that a time will come when they will not be able to keep the Fire off their faces or backs, nor will they be helped. | This verse begins the response to the previous question by painting a terrifying scene of the Hereafter. It expresses a wish or a lament: “If only the disbelievers knew…” This is not merely a wish for their knowledge in this world, but an expression of how their certainty will come too late. The time in question is the Day of Judgment, when they will face the Fire of Hell. The description “they will not be able to keep the Fire off their faces or backs” is graphic and all-encompassing. The face and back represent the front and rear; the punishment will surround them completely, and they will be powerless to ward it off even for a moment. The face, the most honored part of the body, will be exposed to the flames, and there will be no shield. The mention of “backs” suggests that the fire will pursue them from every direction, making escape impossible. The final clause, “nor will they be helped,” extinguishes any hope of rescue. No idol, no ancestor, no intercessor will come to their aid. The verse answers the sarcastic question “When will this threat come to pass?” by asserting that it will come at a time when all mockery will vanish and be replaced by overwhelming terror and helplessness. The lesson is that the delay of punishment should not be mistaken for its cancellation. The verse is a powerful appeal to the imagination, urging the listener to picture that scene now and take protective action through faith and righteousness, rather than waiting until the moment when protection is impossible. 
40 | In fact, the Hour will take them by surprise, leaving them stunned. So they will not be able to avert it, nor will it be delayed from them. | This verse reinforces the sudden and inescapable nature of the Hour. “In fact” corrects any assumption that the Day of Judgment will come with predictable warning signs that allow for last-minute repentance. “The Hour will take them by surprise, leaving them stunned.” The suddenness is a key feature of the end of the world. People will be going about their daily business, and suddenly the trumpet will be blown, and they will be struck with overwhelming shock, unable to think or act. The word “stunned” conveys a state of paralysis, a complete loss of composure. The verse then emphasizes two negations: “they will not be able to avert it, nor will it be delayed from them.” There is absolutely no escaping the Hour once its time has come. No amount of running, hiding, or calling on false gods will turn it away. It cannot be pushed back even for a moment, so that someone might quickly believe or make amends. This finality is crucial. The disbelievers’ demand for the threat to be hastened is shown to be foolish, because when it arrives, it will be so sudden and final that their past mockery will become an endless source of regret. The verse also serves as a comfort to the believers, who were weary of the taunts; it assures them that the justice they long for is certain and will come at its appointed time, unstoppable and undeniable. The overall effect of this passage is to shift the listener’s concern from the “when” to the “how,” urging immediate preparation for a day that gives no second chances.
41 | ˹Other˺ messengers had already been ridiculed before you ˹O Prophet˺, but those who mocked them were overtaken by what they used to ridicule. | Following the terrifying description of the sudden Hour, the surah now consoles the Prophet Muhammad by anchoring his experience in prophetic history. The opening phrase “Other messengers had already been ridiculed before you” immediately places his suffering in a long chain of noble predecessors. The mockery he faced from the Quraysh was not unique; it was the bitter cup that Noah, Hud, Moses, and Jesus all drank from. The verse offers profound psychological comfort. When a person is mocked for the truth, it is easy to feel isolated or to question one’s own message. But here Allah reminds the Prophet that ridicule is a badge of the truthful messenger, not a sign of failure. The second part delivers a decisive warning: “but those who mocked them were overtaken by what they used to ridicule.” The very punishment they laughed at and dismissed as impossible descended upon them and enveloped them. The word “overtaken” conveys a sense of being surrounded and seized, with no escape. The historical examples are plentiful. The people of Noah scoffed as he built the ark, only to be overtaken by the flood. The people of Lot mocked the warning of a rain of stones, which then rained down upon them. The surah has already recounted in verses 11–15 how the wrongdoers cry out in vain when the torment arrives. This verse links those past destructions directly to the Prophet’s situation, implying a clear threat to the Quraysh: if they persist in mockery, they will be overtaken by the very resurrection and punishment they mock. At the same time, it is a lifeline of hope for the believers, showing that the mockers do not have the final word; divine justice does. It teaches us today that when faith is belittled, we should remember the stories of the prophets, remain steadfast, and trust that the outcome is with Allah. 
42 | Ask ˹them, O Prophet,˺ “Who can defend you by day or by night against the Most Compassionate?” Still they turn away from the remembrance of their Lord. | The surah now directs the Prophet to confront the pagans with a deeply personal and unsettling question. The command “Ask them” is a rhetorical tool to force introspection. The question “Who can defend you by day or by night against the Most Compassionate?” shatters the illusion of security. The Quraysh felt safe in their wealth, their tribal alliances, and their idols. But the verse asks them to consider: if the Most Compassionate were to send a punishment, who could shield them during the day when they are active, or at night when they sleep? The mention of “day or night” covers all times, leaving no gap in vulnerability. The use of the name “The Most Compassionate” (Ar-Rahman) is particularly striking. It reminds them that the very God whose mercy is infinite is the One they should fear offending. His compassion does not mean that injustice and mockery go unpunished; rather, His mercy is a call to repent before His justice arrives. The concluding observation “Still they turn away from the remembrance of their Lord” exposes their tragic response. Faced with a question that should shake them to the core, they do not reflect; they simply turn aside. The “remembrance” refers to the Quran, which is the ultimate reminder, and also to the natural inclination to remember Allah. Their turning away is not due to a lack of intellectual understanding but a moral and spiritual aversion. This verse teaches that true security cannot be found in any worldly power; it is found only in the protection of Allah. It also serves as a warning: the mercy of Allah must not be taken for granted by persisting in sin, for no one can defend against His will. 
43 | Or do they have gods—other than Us—that can protect them? They cannot ˹even˺ protect themselves, nor will they be aided against Us. | Building on the previous question, this verse sharpens the challenge by targeting the idols directly. The interrogative “Or do they have gods—other than Us—that can protect them?” exposes the absurdity of their reliance on false deities. The word “gods” here refers to the idols and the angels they worshipped. The question is dripping with irony because the answer is obvious to any rational mind. The verse then demolishes any possible defense of these gods with two devastating statements. First, “They cannot even protect themselves.” The idols are carved from stone or wood; they cannot fend off a fly, let alone a catastrophe. If someone were to smash them, as Abraham would later do in this very surah, they could not defend themselves. The angels, though noble servants, are utterly under Allah’s command and cannot act independently. So, if these so-called gods cannot protect themselves, how can they protect anyone else? Second, “nor will they be aided against Us.” This means that no one will come to the rescue of these gods or their worshippers when Allah’s judgment descends. The false gods will not receive any reinforcement; they and their followers will be fuel for Hell, as later verses will explicitly state. The logic is airtight and forms a complete refutation of intercessory idol worship. The verse encourages believers to place their trust solely in the One who can protect, and to see the utter powerlessness of all that is worshipped besides Him. It is a call to break the psychological chains of fear and hope that bind people to false objects of worship, and to anchor the heart in the reality of Allah’s absolute power and independence. 
44 | In fact, We have allowed enjoyment for these ˹Meccans˺ and their forefathers for such a long time ˹that they took it for granted˺. Do they not see that We gradually reduce ˹their˺ land from its borders? Is it they who will then prevail? | The verse now explains a common cause of disbelief: prolonged ease. Allah states that He allowed these Meccans and their ancestors to enjoy a long life of relative prosperity and security. This long duration of enjoyment led them to take it for granted, mistakenly believing that it was a permanent entitlement or a sign of divine favor that meant they were safe from accountability. This is a universal human delusion; when punishment is delayed, people feel invincible, forgetting that respite is a form of test and a chance for repentance, not an acquittal. The rhetorical question “Do they not see that We gradually reduce their land from its borders?” has been interpreted in several ways by commentators. Many see it as a reference to the gradual territorial gains of the Muslims, which nibbled away at the pagan sphere of influence in Arabia. Each treaty, each migration, and each conversion shrank the political and cultural dominance of Quraysh. Others interpret it as a reference to the loss of their best scholars and leaders who died or were killed in battles, diminishing their strength. A broader meaning is that any seemingly powerful empire is subject to gradual erosion by Allah’s will, proving that power is borrowed, not owned. The final question “Is it they who will then prevail?” is a blunt challenge. The answer is an emphatic no. The verse teaches believers not to be deceived by the temporary flourishing of unbelief. It also encourages strategic patience: just as Allah uses gradual processes to bring down falsehood, the struggle for truth may take time, but the ultimate victory belongs to righteousness. 
45 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “I warn you only by revelation.” But the deaf cannot hear the call when they are warned! | The Prophet is instructed to clearly define the nature of his mission. “I warn you only by revelation” means that his authority and message come exclusively from what Allah has revealed to him, not from his own whims, guesses, or personal ambitions. The warning is not a human threat but a divine one. The word “only” emphasizes that he has no other source of knowledge about the unseen, no supernatural power of his own, and no interest in fabricating anything. This is a powerful declaration of integrity. The second part of the verse uses a poignant metaphor: “But the deaf cannot hear the call when they are warned.” The disbelievers are compared to the deaf. Their ears physically function, but their spiritual hearing is blocked. When someone calls out a warning to a physically deaf person, the caller is not at fault if the person does not respond. Likewise, the Prophet’s duty is to deliver the warning clearly; he is not responsible for their failure to internalize it. This metaphor also contains a subtle rebuke: their inability to hear is not a natural defect but a willful deafness caused by their heedlessness and arrogance. The verse thus comforts the Prophet, reminding him that the effectiveness of the warning is ultimately in Allah’s hands, and he should not blame himself for their rejection. It also serves as a timeless lesson for those who call others to truth: the message must be based firmly on revelation, and the results are not in the caller’s control. The sincerity of the caller and the clarity of the warning are what matter; the response rests with the hearts of the listeners, which only Allah can open. 
46 | If they were touched by even a breath of your Lord’s torment, they would certainly cry, “Woe to us! We have really been wrongdoers.” | This verse intensifies the warning by describing the overwhelming effect of even a minimal touch of divine punishment. The word translated as “breath” or “whiff” (nafha) conveys a tiny, slight contact, a mere fraction of the full torment. It is not the overwhelming flood or the final earthquake, but just a touch. Yet, if that were to reach them, their immediate reaction would be a loud and desperate cry of “Woe to us! We have really been wrongdoers.” The verse highlights three things. First, the terrifying power of Allah’s punishment, which is so immense that even its slightest sample is enough to shatter all arrogance and bring forth an instant confession. Second, it underscores the truth of what the prophets taught: deep down, the disbelievers know they are wrong, but they suppress that knowledge until a crisis forces it to the surface. Their cry is not a new discovery but an admission of something already known in their conscience. Third, the verse carries a profound tragedy: this confession, which would have saved them had it come earlier with sincere repentance, is now merely a statement of fact that brings no benefit. The verse links back to the destroyed nations described earlier, who cried the very same words as the punishment consumed them. The lesson is to not wait for a calamity to acknowledge the truth. Instead, one should listen to the warning delivered by the Prophet and turn to Allah now, while the door of repentance is open and the cry of “woe to us” can still be followed by genuine change. The verse is a mercy wrapped in a threat, urging immediate self-reflection. 
47 | We will set up the scales of justice on the Day of Judgment, so no soul will be wronged in the least. And ˹even˺ if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth. And sufficient are We as a ˹vigilant˺ Reckoner. | After the repeated warnings of torment, the surah now provides a majestic description of divine justice. The imagery of the scales is concrete and powerful, easily visualized by the listeners. “We will set up the scales of justice” indicates that the Day of Judgment is not a time of arbitrary decisions; it is governed by precise, balanced equity. The purpose is explicitly stated: “so no soul will be wronged in the least.” This is the bedrock of Islamic eschatology. Allah, the Absolutely Just, will ensure that every individual receives exactly what their deeds merit, without the slightest atom of injustice. The verse then declares the incomprehensible precision of this accounting: “And even if a deed is the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth.” The mustard seed, in Arabic usage, represents the smallest imaginable weight, something almost negligible. No good deed, no matter how tiny, will be forgotten or lost. No sin, no matter how hidden, will escape. Everything will be produced and weighed. This fills the believer with hope: every small act of kindness, every suppressed moment of patience, every silent prayer, is recorded and will be rewarded. It also fills the wrongdoer with dread: every secret mockery, every private scheme, every grain of arrogance is likewise on record. The final declaration “And sufficient are We as a vigilant Reckoner” closes the verse with divine self-sufficiency. Allah needs no assistants, no books, no technology to keep account; His own perfect knowledge suffices. The word “vigilant” (hasib) also implies a swift and thorough account. This verse is a central pillar of moral consciousness in Islam, anchoring everyday actions in the awareness of an ultimate, detailed, and absolutely fair reckoning. 
48 | Indeed, We granted Moses and Aaron the standard ˹to distinguish between right and wrong˺—a light and a reminder for the righteous, | After the description of the scales of justice, the surah connects the message of Prophet Muhammad to the earlier scriptures by mentioning Moses and Aaron. The word translated as “the standard” is “Furqan,” a powerful Quranic term that denotes a criterion or a discriminator between truth and falsehood. It was granted to Moses and Aaron, primarily referring to the Torah, which gave clear laws and moral guidance. The same word is also used for the Quran itself, creating a direct link between the revelations. The Torah is further described as “a light and a reminder.” The “light” illuminates the path through the darkness of ignorance, showing what is right and what is wrong, what leads to Allah’s pleasure and what leads to His wrath. The “reminder” (dhikr) serves to wake the soul from heedlessness and remind it of its covenant with its Creator. Crucially, this light and reminder are specifically for “the righteous,” those who are already inclined to piety and reverence. This does not mean that others are prohibited from benefiting; rather, it means that only the righteous will truly open their hearts, absorb the guidance, and be illuminated by it. The verse subtly prepares the way for the mention of the Quran in verse 50, showing that just as the earlier scriptures were divine gifts, the Quran is likewise a blessed reminder. The mention of Aaron alongside Moses highlights the mercy of Allah in providing support to His messengers, a comfort to Prophet Muhammad who often faced the burden alone, and a reminder of the importance of righteous companionship in the mission of calling to truth. 
49 | who are in awe of their Lord without seeing Him, and are fearful of the Hour. | This verse completes the description of the righteous mentioned in the previous verse. They are characterized by two profound inner qualities. First, they “are in awe of their Lord without seeing Him.” This is the essence of true faith. Their reverence and fear are not based on visible threats or the presence of a watchful human authority; they are rooted in an unseen reality. They live their lives with an acute awareness that Allah is watching them, even though His form is beyond human perception in this world. This “awe of the unseen” elevates their morality from mere social conformity to deep, principled integrity. They do the right thing not because people are looking, but because Allah is looking. This is the highest station of spiritual excellence, often described as Ihsan, that you worship Allah as if you see Him, and if you do not see Him, He sees you. Second, they “are fearful of the Hour.” This fear is not a paralyzing terror but a motivating consciousness that the Day of Judgment is real and approaching. It inspires them to prepare, to repent, and to keep their accounts clean. The combination of these two fears—fear of the unseen Lord and fear of the coming judgment—creates a complete spiritual framework. The verse thus describes the ideal recipients of the earlier scriptures and, by extension, of the Quran. It serves as a mirror for the believers reading or hearing it: do they possess this awe of Allah in private and this acute awareness of the Hour? It also implicitly rebukes the Meccan pagans who lacked both, demanding visible signs and scoffing at the resurrection. The lesson is that true guidance requires a heart that is receptive, humble, and constantly oriented toward the unseen reality. 
50 | And this ˹Quran˺ is a blessed reminder which We have revealed. Will you ˹pagans˺ then deny it? | The surah now transitions from the mention of the Torah back to the central theme: the Quran itself. It is described as “a blessed reminder.” The word “blessed” (mubarak) conveys abundant, continuous goodness. The Quran is full of blessings: it heals hearts, guides to what is most upright, intercedes for its reciters, and brings immeasurable reward. It is a source of goodness in this life and the next. The word “reminder” (dhikr) ties it directly to the description of the Torah as a reminder for the righteous. The Quran is the universal, final reminder for all humanity. It carries the same core message of pure monotheism, accountability, and righteousness, but in a perfected and preserved form. The phrase “which We have revealed” emphasizes its divine origin. It is not a human composition; it descended from the same source that granted the Furqan to Moses. The rhetorical question “Will you pagans then deny it?” is a direct challenge to the Quraysh. Having heard the description of its qualities, having acknowledged that previous scriptures were divine gifts, on what basis can they reject this one? The question contains a tone of astonishment and disapproval. It calls them to reflect on the irrationality of their denial. For all believers, this verse reinforces the immense value of the Quran. It is not just a book to be recited ritually; it is a blessed, transformative reminder that should be received with gratitude, pondered over, and allowed to change one’s life. To deny it or to neglect it is to turn away from a continuous stream of divine blessings. 
51 | And indeed, We had granted Abraham sound judgment early on, for We knew him well ˹to be worthy of it˺. | The surah now begins a lengthy account of Prophet Abraham, the father of monotheism and a direct ancestor of the Quraysh, whom they also revered. This strategic choice of narrative serves to argue against their idolatry using a figure they claimed to respect. The verse begins with “Indeed, We had granted Abraham sound judgment early on.” The term “sound judgment” translates “rushd,” which implies mature wisdom, right guidance, and the ability to distinguish truth from falsehood. The phrase “early on” indicates that this wisdom was given to him from a young age, before he received prophethood. Even as a youth, Abraham’s pure intellect, uncorrupted by the idolatrous environment, led him to recognize the falsehood of star-worship and idol-worship, as detailed in other surahs. The reason for this divine gift is stated: “for We knew him well to be worthy of it.” Allah’s knowledge encompasses the inner qualities of individuals. He knew Abraham’s pure heart, his sincerity, and his innate inclination to truth, and therefore granted him this special guidance. This teaches a profound lesson: divine favors are not arbitrary; they are given to those whose hearts are ready to receive them. It also highlights that Abraham’s subsequent greatness was built on this early foundation of sound judgment. The verse sets the stage for the iconic confrontation with his people, showing that his challenge to their gods was not a reckless youthful rebellion but the fruit of divinely granted wisdom. It reminds believers that seeking sound judgment and nurturing it in youth through reflection and sincerity is the path to receiving further guidance from Allah. 
52 | ˹Remember˺ when he questioned his father and his people, “What are these statues to which you are so devoted?” | The narrative moves to a specific, defining moment in Abraham’s life. The instruction to “remember” invites the Prophet and the believers to reflect on this event as a timeless model. Abraham’s question is directed first to his father, Azar, who was a maker and seller of idols, and then to his people. His approach is one of gentle inquiry rather than immediate condemnation. He asks, “What are these statues to which you are so devoted?” The word “statues” (tamathil) emphasizes their nature as mere carvings, inert objects shaped by human hands. The phrase “to which you are so devoted” highlights the irony: they are utterly devoted, spending time, money, and emotion on lifeless figures. The question is designed to provoke thought. It is not a request for information; it is a way of opening their minds to the absurdity of their actions. Abraham is using a Socratic method, asking a question that forces them to articulate what they are doing, hoping that in the articulation they might recognize its foolishness. This teaches an important principle of dawah: sometimes a thoughtful question is more effective than a direct attack. It also shows Abraham’s respect for his father, as he begins with him privately before addressing the wider community. The devotion of the people to these statues was so deep that they had emotional attachments to them; they would seek their blessings, cry before them, and defend them. Abraham’s simple question cuts through all that emotional complexity and goes straight to the core issue: what exactly are you worshipping, and why? 
53 | They replied, “We found our forefathers worshipping them.” | This is the quintessential response of blind traditionalism. Faced with a rational question about the nature of their worship, the people do not attempt to present any logical argument or evidence. They do not claim that the statues benefit or harm, or that they have any divine power. Instead, they appeal entirely to the precedent of their ancestors. “We found our forefathers worshipping them.” This answer reveals that the entire foundation of their religion is the imitation of previous generations. It is a confession of intellectual bankruptcy. The Quran records this response to highlight one of the greatest barriers to truth: the unquestioning following of cultural and familial traditions. People often resist the truth not because they have examined it and found it wanting, but because accepting it would mean breaking with the ways of their parents and ancestors. This argument from antiquity is a logical fallacy, but it holds immense emotional power. Abraham’s people felt a sense of identity and loyalty to their forefathers, and they considered any deviation from the ancestral path an act of betrayal. The verse exposes this mindset for what it is: an empty shell. The fact that something is old does not make it true; error can be as ancient as truth. The lesson for believers is that faith must be built on conviction and evidence, not on blind inheritance. Even as Muslims, we must ensure that our own practice of Islam is based on knowledge and understanding, not merely on what our families have always done, because on the Day of Judgment, the excuse “we found our forefathers” will not be accepted. 
54 | He responded, “Indeed, you and your forefathers have been clearly astray.” | Abraham’s response is bold and uncompromising. Having heard their admission that their worship is based solely on ancestral tradition, he delivers the unavoidable conclusion: “Indeed, you and your forefathers have been clearly astray.” The word “clearly” emphasizes that the error is not subtle or hidden; it is obvious to anyone who uses their reason. He does not sugarcoat the truth to spare their feelings. While his initial question was gentle, once the fault line of their belief is exposed, he speaks the truth plainly. This is a crucial balance in calling to Allah: begin with wisdom and kindness, but when the core falsehood is laid bare, do not hesitate to name it for what it is. Abraham’s statement includes both the present generation and their ancestors, making it clear that the mere longevity of a practice does not validate it. By mentioning the forefathers alongside them, he demonstrates that his critique is not a personal insult to his contemporaries alone; it is a verdict on an entire system of misguidance that spans generations. The statement is also an expression of courage. Abraham is alone, a young man facing his entire community and his own father, yet he does not flinch from the truth. He shows that loyalty to the truth must override loyalty to tribe and family when the two are in conflict. The verse models the prophetic trait of intellectual and moral clarity, teaching believers that while we must respect our parents and elders, we cannot follow them into error, and at times we must have the difficult conversation that their way, if contrary to divine guidance, is a path of clear loss. 
55 | They asked, “Have you come to us with the truth, or is this a joke?” | The response of the people to Abraham’s clear verdict is a mixture of incredulity and sarcasm. They cannot comprehend that this young man could be serious in challenging their entire religious heritage. Their question “Have you come to us with the truth, or is this a joke?” reflects their deep confusion. In their worldview, the ancestral religion is an unshakeable default. So, they wonder, is Abraham making a sincere claim to possess a higher truth, or is he just playing around and mocking them? The question reveals that they are still not engaging with the content of his argument; they are questioning his motives and his seriousness. The idea that their way of life could be “clearly astray” is so foreign to them that they can only interpret it as either a revolutionary prophetic claim or an immature jest. This reaction is a common defense mechanism. When faced with a truth that threatens one’s core identity, people often try to dismiss the messenger rather than consider the message. By asking if it is a joke, they are giving themselves a comfortable way out: if he is joking, they can ignore him without further thought. Abraham’s reply, which follows in the next verse, immediately eliminates the second option. The verse teaches that callers to the truth should expect such reactions. They will be accused of having hidden motives or of not being serious. The remedy is to respond with clarity and gravity, making it unmistakably clear that the message is divine truth, not a human jest. For the believers, it is a lesson to always present the truth with the solemnity it deserves, leaving no room for it to be mistaken for entertainment. 
56 | He replied, “In fact, your Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth, Who created them ˹both˺. And to that I bear witness.” | Abraham immediately sets the record straight and elevates the discussion from mere argumentation to a powerful declaration of faith. He begins with “In fact,” dismissing any notion that he is joking. He is completely serious. He then directs their attention away from the statues and toward the undeniable reality of the universe. “Your Lord is the Lord of the heavens and the earth.” Notice that he says “your Lord,” not “my Lord,” making it personal and immediate for them. He is not introducing a new, foreign deity; he is pointing to the Creator who already sustains them, the One they instinctively know is the true Lord, even if they have obscured that knowledge with idols. He describes this Lord as the One “Who created them both,” meaning the heavens and the earth. This is a simple, irrefutable argument. The creation of the massive, complex universe is the ultimate proof of the Creator’s existence and power. By focusing on creation, Abraham bypasses the petty disputes about idols and goes straight to the foundation. The idols cannot create anything; they are themselves created. So the only one worthy of worship is the Creator. Abraham concludes with the solemn testimony: “And to that I bear witness.” This is his personal commitment. He is not just an arguer; he is a witness to the truth. He stakes his own life on it. This declaration of witnessing is a model for every believer. When declaring the truth, one should not only present logical arguments but also bear witness with one’s whole being, embodying the truth one proclaims. The verse shows that the core of the prophetic call is to connect people to the Creator of the universe, moving from the signs in creation to the exclusive worship of the One who made them. 
57 | ˹Then he said to himself,˺ “By Allah! I will surely plot against your idols after you have turned your backs and gone away.” | This verse reveals Abraham’s plan, which he kept to himself. The phrase “he said to himself” indicates a private determination, a strategic resolution that he did not share publicly beforehand. The oath “By Allah!” demonstrates the solemnity and sincerity of his intention. He is not acting out of petty anger or destructive impulse; he is swearing by the very Lord he serves that he will take a bold action to expose the falsehood of the idols. The word “plot” (kayd) is a clever, planned scheme. Abraham’s plot is a counter-action to the spiritual plot of idolatry that has enslaved his people’s minds. He declares he will do this “after you have turned your backs and gone away.” This refers to a specific occasion, which scholars of tafsir explain was a festival day when the people, including his father, would go out of the town. They expected Abraham to accompany them, but he excused himself, perhaps feigning illness as another surah mentions, in order to be left alone with the idols. This verse highlights a crucial concept in Islamic theology: the use of measured, clever action in the service of truth. Abraham is not attacking people; he is going to smash lifeless idols to demonstrate a profound lesson. His plot is not deceit against individuals but a dramatic, visual argument against falsehood. It teaches that sometimes a powerful symbolic act is necessary to shake people out of their complacency. The oath “By Allah!” also shows that Abraham saw his plan as an act of worship, a service to his Lord, not a mere act of vandalism. This verse builds suspense in the narrative, setting the stage for the dramatic event and teaching believers that zealous defense of monotheism requires both wisdom and courage. 
58 | So he smashed them into pieces, except the biggest of them, so they might turn to it ˹for answers˺. | The action follows the resolve. Abraham smashed the idols into pieces. The word used conveys a thorough destruction, reducing them to rubble. This act was a direct, physical demonstration of the idols’ powerlessness. If these idols were gods, they should have defended themselves. Their silence and inability to prevent their own destruction is the loudest possible testimony to their falsehood. Crucially, Abraham did not destroy the biggest idol; he left it standing and even placed the axe or tool he used on its shoulder or around its neck, according to some reports. The reason is explicitly given: “so they might turn to it for answers.” Abraham’s strategy was brilliant. He knew his people would immediately suspect him, as he had openly criticized the idols. He wanted to force them to confront the logical conclusion themselves. By leaving the largest idol intact, he created a scenario where they would have to ask the big idol who did this. If the idol could not answer, they would be forced to admit the idols’ impotence, and that even the biggest one was powerless. This act reveals the pedagogical wisdom of Abraham. His goal was not just destruction but enlightenment. He wanted them to use their reason and to arrive at the truth through their own reflection. The lesson for all callers to truth is profound: sometimes, rather than just giving the answer, one should create a situation that compels people to think and question their own assumptions. The verse also demonstrates that taking a stand against falsehood may require bold, confrontational actions that challenge the symbols of that falsehood, always with the intention of guiding people back to truth. 
59 | They protested, “Who dared do this to our gods? It must be an evildoer!” | When the people returned from their festival and saw the shattered remains of their deities, their reaction was one of shock, fury, and indignation. Their protest “Who dared do this to our gods?” reveals their deep emotional attachment and their personification of the idols. They speak of the idols as if they were living beings who have been wronged. The phrase “our gods” shows that they perceived this not merely as property damage but as a direct assault on their religion and identity. Their immediate conclusion, “It must be an evildoer!” is tragically ironic. The real evildoers are those who worship helpless statues and attribute partners to the Creator of the heavens and the earth. But in their worldview, the one who exposes the truth is the evildoer. This complete inversion of morality is typical of a society that has lost its spiritual bearings. The verse captures the psychology of the mob. They do not pause to reflect on the fact that their gods did nothing to stop the destruction; they immediately look for a human culprit to punish. The idols’ inability to protect themselves should have been the wake-up call, but instead of questioning the gods, they question the community to find the saboteur. This teaches that when falsehood is deeply entrenched, the initial reaction to the truth is often anger and labeling the truth-teller as a troublemaker or evil. The lesson for believers is to expect such accusations and not be deterred by them. Abraham’s calm execution of his plan in the face of the inevitable uproar is a testament to his complete trust in the power of the truth and in Allah’s ultimate support. 
60 | Some said, “We heard a young man, called Abraham, speaking ˹ill˺ of them.” | Amidst the commotion, a few voices recall the critical words they had heard from Abraham. The phrase “a young man” is significant. It highlights Abraham’s youth, which would have been seen as a reason to dismiss him. In a society that revered elders and their traditions, a young man challenging the entire religious establishment was an audacious anomaly. The fact that he is identified as “called Abraham” shows that he was known among them, but known precisely as a critic of their gods. The phrase “speaking ill of them” refers to his verbal critiques and his questioning of their worship. They had dismissed his words then, perhaps considering them the naive ramblings of youth. But now the smashed idols have given those words a terrifying reality. The connection is instantly made: the one who spoke against the idols is the one who acted against them. The verse demonstrates that a person’s consistent stance and speech will eventually be remembered when events unfold. Abraham had not hidden his beliefs; he had openly declared the truth, and now his words serve as testimony against the idols. This moment also sets up the confrontation where Abraham is brought before the people, the very next episode. The lesson for believers is the importance of speaking the truth consistently, even if it is initially ridiculed or ignored. Words of truth plant seeds. When the time is right, those seeds bear witness and can lead to decisive moments of change. Abraham’s story thus far is a masterclass in blending verbal challenge with symbolic action, all under the guidance of profound wisdom granted by Allah.
61 | They demanded, “Bring him before the eyes of the people, so that they may witness ˹his trial˺.” | The leaders, furious and humiliated, decide to make a public spectacle of Abraham. Their demand, “Bring him before the eyes of the people, so that they may witness his trial,” shows their intention to turn his punishment into a deterrent and a reaffirmation of their authority. They wanted the entire community to see what happens to someone who dares to insult their gods. This was not to be a quiet execution; it was a staged public event. The phrase “before the eyes of the people” emphasizes the visual, collective nature of the trial. They understood the power of public witnessing in solidifying social norms and intimidating dissenters. By making everyone a witness, they also aimed to implicate the whole community in the condemnation of Abraham, ensuring that no one else would dare to follow his path. However, what they intended as a show of force to crush truth, Allah would soon transform into a show of power to vindicate truth. Abraham himself is brought forward, but the narrative does not depict him as afraid or humiliated. Instead, the stage is set for his calm, brilliant response. This demand also reveals the arrogance of a society that thinks it can judge a prophet of God. They treat Abraham as a criminal, but in reality, they are the ones standing on the wrong side of divine judgment. This verse teaches that the forces of falsehood often seek to silence the voice of truth through public intimidation. Yet, what they design as a platform for punishment often becomes, by divine will, a platform for the truth to shine and for the falsehood to be exposed in front of all eyes. The believer should not fear being brought “before the eyes of the people” for their faith, because Allah is the ultimate director of the scene. 
62 | They asked, “Was it you who did this to our gods, O Abraham?” | The official interrogation begins. The question is direct and accusatory: “Was it you who did this to our gods, O Abraham?” The phrasing “our gods” again demonstrates their personal and emotional investment. They address him by name, which, in the context of a trial, is a formal identification of the accused. There is a tone of disbelief that this lone young man could have been so audacious. The question is essentially a request for a confession, and they expect either a denial, which they would refute with witnesses, or a trembling admission, which would lead directly to sentencing. But Abraham is not a typical defendant; he is a prophet on a mission. This question sets up his famous, devastating reply. The moment is filled with dramatic tension. The shattered idols lie before them, the largest one still standing with the axe on its shoulder. The people are gathered, angry and expectant. Abraham stands before them, calm and composed. The verse captures a turning point: the battle has shifted from the private sphere of words to the public arena of trial. For believers, this is a reminder that moments of confrontation and questioning are opportunities to demonstrate the truth with wisdom. Abraham does not panic; he uses the question itself as a launchpad for a lesson. The leaders, by asking “was it you,” have inadvertently opened the door for Abraham to redirect the blame to the very object they worship, forcing them to face the logical inconsistency of their beliefs. 
63 | He replied ˹sarcastically˺, “No, this one—the biggest of them—did it! So ask them, if they can talk!” | Abraham’s reply is one of the most brilliant examples of rhetorical strategy in the Quran. He says, “No, this one—the biggest of them—did it!” This statement is clearly not a literal assertion intended to deceive; it is a satirical and argumentative device aimed at exposing the absurdity of idol worship. By attributing the act to the biggest idol, Abraham forces his audience to immediately recognize the impossibility. The largest idol is as lifeless and powerless as the smaller ones; the claim that it smashed the others and then stood there holding the tool is visibly ridiculous. The phrase “So ask them, if they can talk!” is the piercing conclusion. He invites them to interrogate the idols directly. Since the idols cannot speak, the very act of being asked to ask them highlights their utter impotence. This is not a lie but a rhetorical method known as “ta‘ridh” or indirect speech to establish a truth. Some reports indicate that Abraham’s intention was to make them admit that what cannot speak cannot be god. The response masterfully achieves his goal of forcing a moment of self-reflection. The use of sarcasm is a rare but powerful tool in the Quranic narrative, permitted in such a context to shatter a deeply entrenched falsehood. This teaches that while truthfulness is paramount, there are rhetorical techniques that can be used to expose falsehood, provided the intention is to guide people to the truth and not to cause harm. Abraham’s reply is not a nervous evasion but a pre-planned, divinely guided strike at the heart of polytheism. 
64 | So they came back to their senses, saying ˹to one another˺, “You yourselves are truly the wrongdoers!” | Astonishingly, Abraham’s arrow hits its mark. The verse says “they came back to their senses,” a profound phrase indicating that for a brief, lucid moment, their innate human reason broke through the clouds of tradition and stubbornness. They turned to one another and admitted, “You yourselves are truly the wrongdoers!” This is an incredible confession. They realized that by worshipping lifeless statues that cannot even defend themselves or speak, they were the ones committing a great injustice against their own intellects and against the Creator. The word “wrongdoers” is the exact word Abraham had used earlier when he said they were clearly astray. Now, their own tongues repeat the truth. This moment demonstrates the power of a single, well-placed argument that resonates with the natural disposition. It shows that even the most hardened idolaters have a core of fitrah (innate human nature) that recognizes truth when it is starkly presented. The admission was not coerced; it came from within. This fleeting moment of clarity is a testament to the effectiveness of Abraham’s method and the truth of monotheism. However, as the next verse shows, this awakening was tragically short-lived. The verse serves as a powerful reminder that acknowledging the truth intellectually is not enough if it is not followed by a firm, sustained change of heart and action. The pressure of social conformity and the fear of losing status often cause people to retreat from a truth they have just recognized. The believer must seize such moments of clarity, nurture them, and not allow the surrounding environment to pull them back into darkness. 
65 | Then they ˹quickly˺ regressed to their ˹original˺ mind-set, ˹arguing,˺ “You already know that those ˹idols˺ cannot talk.” | The moment of clarity evaporates as quickly as it came. The verse describes their regression with a word that suggests being turned upside down or thrown back into their previous state. They regressed to their original mindset of stubborn defense of their tradition. The argument they now use is utterly self-defeating and shows that they have no logical ground left. They say to Abraham, “You already know that those idols cannot talk.” This is a fascinating shift. In their moment of confusion, they appeal to the very fact that Abraham’s rhetorical trap was designed to highlight. By admitting the idols cannot talk, they are conceding Abraham’s fundamental point: that these statues are incapable of any action, let alone divine power. Yet, instead of concluding that they should not worship them, they use this admission to argue that Abraham’s question was unfair. It is a classic case of cognitive dissonance; they hold two contradictory beliefs—that the idols are gods, and that they cannot speak—and resolve the tension by dismissing the challenger rather than the false belief. Their statement “You already know” is almost an accusation of bad faith against Abraham, as if he were exploiting their gods’ known silence. This verse brilliantly exposes the irrationality of blind traditionalism. When evidence is presented, the response is not to reconsider the tradition but to attack the presenter for pointing out the obvious flaw. The lesson is profound: people can be so deeply attached to their inherited beliefs that even when the truth is undeniable, they find ways to circumvent it, preferring the comfort of the familiar over the challenge of the truth. 
66 | He rebuked ˹them˺, “Do you then worship—instead of Allah—what can neither benefit nor harm you in any way?” | Having forced them to admit the idols’ impotence, Abraham now drops all rhetorical subtlety and delivers a direct, passionate rebuke. He asks, “Do you then worship—instead of Allah—what can neither benefit nor harm you in any way?” This question goes to the very essence of what worship means. A god is supposed to be a source of benefit and protection, a being to whom one turns for help and from whom one fears harm. Yet, by their own admission, these idols cannot talk, let alone provide benefit or cause harm. Abraham is pointing out the complete irrationality of their actions: they have abandoned the worship of the Almighty Creator for objects that are absolutely neutral, offering no advantage or disadvantage. The phrasing “instead of Allah” underscores the gravity; it is not just that they worship these things, but that they do so in place of the true God. This is the heart of shirk. The verse strips away all the elaborate mythologies and rituals to reveal the stark emptiness of idol worship. It challenges every human being to examine what they place their hopes and fears in. Is it something that can truly bring benefit or ward off harm? Only Allah holds absolute power over benefit and harm; all other entities are themselves dependent. Abraham’s rebuke is not just for his people; it is a timeless call to pure monotheism. The lesson is to constantly evaluate one’s life: are we directing our ultimate hopes, fears, and devotion to things that cannot, in reality, benefit or harm us without Allah’s permission? 
67 | Shame on you and whatever you worship instead of Allah! Do you not have any sense?” | Abraham intensifies his rebuke with a declaration of disgust and a rhetorical question that appeals to the intellect. “Shame on you” translates an Arabic expression of strong disapproval, literally “Uff to you,” indicating that he is utterly done with their foolishness and their false gods. The phrase “and whatever you worship instead of Allah” extends the shame to the idols themselves, emphasizing their worthlessness. He is not merely criticizing the people; he is expressing contempt for the entire system of idolatry. The climax is the stinging question: “Do you not have any sense?” This is a call to the most basic human faculty of reason. Abraham is astounded that intelligent human beings, capable of speech, thought, and complex social organization, could stoop to worshipping carved stones. The question implies that idol worship is not just a sin but an insult to human intelligence. It appeals to the inherent dignity of the human mind, which is designed to recognize its Creator. This verse is a powerful testament to the Quranic emphasis on using reason in matters of faith. Belief is not blind; it is the fruit of sound intellect. Abraham embodies the prophetic tradition of awakening the mind as a path to the heart. For believers, this is a reminder that Islam elevates the intellect and demands that faith be built on understanding. A faith that cannot withstand rational scrutiny is not the faith of Abraham. The verse also teaches that when falsehood is stubborn and has exhausted all other arguments, it is sometimes necessary to express moral and intellectual outrage to shake the foundations of complacency. 
68 | They concluded, “Burn him up to avenge your gods, if you must act.” | Unable to defeat Abraham in argument, and now thoroughly humiliated and enraged, the people resort to brute force. Their logical defeat leads to a murderous verdict. They “concluded,” meaning after deliberation, they decided on the most severe punishment: to burn him alive. The phrase “to avenge your gods” reveals their true motive. This is not about justice or protecting society; it is about exacting revenge for the insult to their deities. The idols, which could not protect themselves from being smashed, now need human hands to avenge them. This irony is completely lost on the mob. The call “if you must act” is a rallying cry, urging the community to unite in this act of collective vengeance. It is a statement that combines religious zeal with mob psychology. The decision to use fire was particularly significant; fire was often associated with divine punishment and was used by some ancient cultures in sacrificial rituals. By burning Abraham, they perhaps hoped to offer him as a sacrifice to their gods, turning the punishment into a twisted act of worship. This verse illustrates the dangerous transition from ideological defeat to violence. When falsehood cannot defend itself with reason, it often tries to silence the truth with force. The entire episode reaches its peak of tension here. For the believers, the verse is a reminder that the struggle for truth is often met with violent opposition. Yet, the very next verse will show that when human power reaches its limit, divine intervention begins. The resolution comes not from Abraham’s own strength, but from the command of Allah. 
69 | We ordered, “O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham!” | This verse marks the dramatic divine intervention, a miracle that stands as one of the most famous events in Abraham’s life. After the people built a massive pyre and threw Abraham into it—according to some reports, using a catapult because the heat was so intense they could not approach—Allah issued His command. The command is direct and clear: “O fire! Be cool and safe for Abraham.” The fire, a creation of Allah, is ordered to lose its burning property and instead become a source of coolness and peace. The word “cool” indicates a complete reversal of its nature, and “safe” (salam) adds the dimension of peace and well-being. Some commentators note that if Allah had only said “be cool,” the extreme cold could have been harmful; thus He added “and safe” to perfect the miracle. Abraham emerged from the fire after several days, completely unharmed, much to the astonishment of his people. This event is a profound sign of Allah’s power over the laws of nature and His protection for His chosen servants. The fire, which the people thought would destroy the truth, became the very instrument of Abraham’s deliverance and a public sign of his prophethood. The lesson for believers is immense: when one stands for the truth, and the forces of falsehood seem overwhelming, Allah has the power to change the nature of things to protect His servant. The fire of tribulation can be transformed into a garden of peace by a single divine word. The Prophet Muhammad is comforted: just as Abraham was saved from the fire, Allah will protect and grant victory to His final messenger. 
70 | They had sought to harm him, but We made them the worst losers. | This verse provides the concise verdict on the entire incident. The people’s intention was explicitly malicious: they “had sought to harm him.” The Arabic word for “harm” here implies a carefully plotted scheme, reflecting their deliberate plan to annihilate Abraham and his message. But their plot, for all its ferocity, resulted in complete failure. “We made them the worst losers” is the divine judgment. They lost in every conceivable way. They lost their argument, they lost their public display of power, they lost their credibility as their gods were proven helpless again, and ultimately, they lost the spiritual battle. Abraham emerged alive and even more respected, while their rage and plot only served to highlight the truth of his message. This principle runs throughout the Quran: the plotting of the disbelievers is always encompassed by Allah’s plan, and in the end, their schemes only destroy themselves. The phrase “worst losers” indicates a comprehensive, humiliating defeat. This verse is a source of immense comfort and optimism for believers. No matter how powerful the opposition appears, no matter how meticulously they plan harm, the outcome is in Allah’s hands. If one is on the side of truth, the harm intended by others can become the very means of one’s elevation and the enemy’s downfall. The Prophet Muhammad is reminded that the Quraysh’s plots against him will similarly end in their own loss. The verse teaches a deep trust in Allah’s wisdom; sometimes He allows the wicked to go far in their plots, only to make their failure all the more spectacular and the lesson all the more enduring. 
71 | Then We delivered him, along with Lot, to the land We had showered with blessings for all people. | After the failed execution, Abraham’s position in his homeland became untenable, though he was victorious. The verse narrates the next stage: Allah delivered him. The “deliverance” here is both physical migration and spiritual salvation. Abraham did not leave alone; he took with him Lot, his nephew, who was also a believer and a prophet. They migrated to the land that Allah “had showered with blessings for all people.” The majority of commentators identify this blessed land as greater Syria, encompassing Palestine. The Quran describes it as blessed because of its fertility, its abundance of rivers and fruits, and most importantly, because it would become the homeland of many prophets and a center of divine revelation. The phrase “for all people” hints at the universal nature of the blessing, not confined to a particular tribe. Abraham’s migration was a pivotal moment in prophetic history. It was not a retreat but a strategic relocation to a land destined for a great spiritual mission. This verse parallels the Prophet Muhammad’s own hijra from Mecca to Medina. Just as Abraham left the land of his people when their rejection became entrenched, the Prophet would later leave Mecca for a land of blessing. This parallel was deeply comforting to the Prophet. It taught that migration for the sake of Allah is a prophetic legacy that leads to the expansion of the faith and the establishment of a righteous community. The verse also teaches that when a place becomes irredeemably hostile to faith, a believer should not hesitate to move to a place where they can worship freely and where the land itself seems to assist in spiritual growth. 
72 | And We blessed him with Isaac ˹as a son˺ and Jacob ˹as a grandson˺, as an additional favour—making all of them righteous. | The narrative shifts from the physical deliverance to the personal, familial blessings Allah granted Abraham. After the supreme sacrifice of leaving his homeland and the trials he endured, Allah rewarded him with a righteous progeny that would carry the torch of prophethood. Isaac is mentioned as a son given to him in his old age, a miraculous birth from his wife Sarah, who had been barren. This was a direct fulfillment of divine promise. Then, as an extension of that blessing, Jacob is mentioned as a grandson, the son of Isaac. The phrase “as an additional favour” indicates that Jacob was a gift beyond what was originally requested or expected; Abraham not only got to see a son but a grandson, and both were righteous and prophets. The verse specifies “making all of them righteous,” which includes Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. This highlights that the greatest blessing is not just having children, but having righteous children who follow the path of guidance. The Quraysh, who were descendants of Abraham through Ishmael, are implicitly reminded that the true legacy of Abraham is not mere lineage but righteousness and pure monotheism. The verse also touches on a common human longing: the desire for a pious and continuing legacy. Allah’s favor to Abraham shows that He rewards His faithful servants not only in the Hereafter but also in this world with gifts that bring peace and joy, such as a righteous family. It teaches believers to aspire not just for children but for children who will be imams of righteousness, carrying the faith forward after them. 
73 | We ˹also˺ made them leaders, guiding by Our command, and inspired them to do good deeds, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax. And they were devoted to Our worship. | This verse magnifies the status of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob by describing their spiritual leadership. Allah “made them leaders” (A’immah) guiding humanity by His command. The word “leader” here implies not just political or social authority but a role of spiritual guidance, pointing people to the truth. The phrase “guiding by Our command” means their guidance was not from their own whims but strictly according to divine instruction. The verse then details what they were inspired to do: “do good deeds, establish prayer, and pay alms-tax.” This tripartite description encompasses comprehensive righteousness. Good deeds represent all actions of virtue; establishing prayer is the pillar of the connection with Allah; and paying alms-tax is the pillar of social responsibility and compassion. Remarkably, the alms-tax (zakat) is mentioned, even though the specific institutionalized form would later be legislated in the Sharia of Muhammad. The essence of giving a portion of wealth for the sake of Allah has always been part of prophetic guidance. The final declaration, “And they were devoted to Our worship,” seals their character: their entire life was an act of worship, done with complete devotion and sincerity. This verse shows that the true legacy of Abraham is a model community built on leadership, divine guidance, prayer, charity, and devotion. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on being descendants of Abraham, are challenged to examine whether they actually follow his way. The believers are given a clear blueprint of the qualities that make a person a true leader in the sight of Allah. Leadership is not about power but about being a guide, and the source of that guidance must be revelation, manifested in a life of prayer, charity, and wholehearted worship. 
74 | And to Lot We gave wisdom and knowledge, and delivered him from the society engrossed in shameful practices. They were certainly an evil, rebellious people. | The surah continues the caravan of prophets by briefly mentioning Lot, who had accompanied Abraham and was then sent as a messenger to the people of Sodom. The verse states that Allah gave Lot “wisdom and knowledge.” The word for wisdom (hukm) can also mean sound judgment and the ability to adjudicate between people justly, while knowledge refers to prophetic understanding of divine law and truth. So Lot was fully equipped for his mission. The verse then says “and delivered him from the society engrossed in shameful practices.” The “society” is identified as a township that committed the abominable acts, primarily the homosexual acts that were unprecedented among humanity at that time. The word “engrossed” indicates that these practices were not rare individual sins but the dominant, characteristic behavior of the entire community, corrupting their society from top to bottom. The deliverance came through the divine command to leave the city at night before the punishment descended, as detailed in other surahs. The final sentence, “They were certainly an evil, rebellious people,” gives the reason for their destruction. Their evil was not just in the act itself but in their rebellion against all moral bounds and against the messenger sent to guide them. The verse is brief but powerful, serving as a warning about the collective consequences when a society normalizes and flaunts what is morally corrupt. Lot’s story is a sign of Allah’s justice and a reminder that the standard of right and wrong is not determined by human desire but by divine law. The mention of Lot immediately after Abraham also emphasizes the theme of prophetic brotherhood and the continuity of the message. 
75 | And We admitted him into Our mercy, ˹for˺ he was truly one of the righteous. | This short verse provides the beautiful conclusion to Lot’s earthly journey. After the destruction of his people, Lot and the believing members of his household were saved, and his ultimate reward was to be admitted into Allah’s mercy. This admittance into mercy is a profound phrase that indicates entrance into Paradise and the divine good pleasure. The mercy of Allah envelops all the hardships Lot endured: the daily grief of witnessing his people’s depravity, their mockery and threats, the failure of his own wife who looked back and was destroyed, and the loss of his homeland. All that sorrow is washed away and replaced by the boundless mercy of the Hereafter. The reason is stated: “for he was truly one of the righteous.” The emphasis on “truly” underscores that his righteousness was not superficial or temporary; it was his inherent quality, demonstrated through his unwavering patience and his commitment to calling his people to purity despite their aggression. The verse encapsulates the ultimate happy ending for a prophet. It is a pattern seen throughout the Quran: a messenger is rejected by his people, he endures with patience, and then he is saved and granted a high rank in the Hereafter. This pattern provides solace to the Prophet Muhammad, who is told implicitly that his own story, and that of his believing followers, will follow the same arc. The trials of this world are temporary, but the mercy into which the righteous enter is eternal. The verse is also a reminder that righteousness is the key that unlocks Allah’s mercy. 
76 | And ˹remember˺ when Noah had cried out to Us earlier, so We responded to him and delivered him and his family from the great distress. | The surah now moves to an earlier prophet, Noah, who is described as having “cried out to Us earlier.” The word “earlier” anchors the chronological sequence, as Noah predates Abraham and Lot. His cry was the supplication he made after centuries of patient, persistent calling to his people with almost no positive response, culminating in his plea, “My Lord, help me because they have denied me.” This cry was not one of impatience but a call for divine intervention after all human efforts had been exhausted. The verse states that Allah “responded to him,” demonstrating that the supplication of a prophet is never ignored, even if the answer is manifested after a long test of patience. The response involved delivering him and his family—the believers from his household who boarded the ark—from “the great distress.” This distress encompasses the flood itself, the panic, the drowning of the disbelievers, and the immense psychological burden of seeing a whole civilization destroyed. The deliverance was total and miraculous: the ark sailed over the water, preserving the remnant of faith for a new beginning. The verse ties back to the surah’s theme of Allah’s promise to save the messengers and the believers. Noah’s cry and its acceptance are a powerful precedent for the Prophet Muhammad, who is assured that his own prayers for his people and for relief are heard, and the answer, in its perfect timing, will come. The lesson for all believers is the power of sincere supplication; no matter how long the distress lasts, the cry to Allah is the lifeline that leads to deliverance. 
77 | And We made him prevail over those who had rejected Our signs. They were truly an evil people, so We drowned them all. | The response to Noah’s prayer is further detailed: Allah made him prevail. This prevailing was not a military victory in the conventional sense but a complete vindication through the utter destruction of the rejecters while Noah and his followers were saved. The phrase “over those who had rejected Our signs” identifies the enemy clearly; their crime was the rejection of the clear signs and proofs Noah brought from Allah. The statement “They were truly an evil people” provides the moral justification for their drowning. Their evil was so deeply entrenched and their rejection so absolute that they had become a hopeless case, a threat to the very possibility of faith on earth. The flood was not an arbitrary act of rage but a surgical removal of pervasive corruption. The verse emphasizes that “We drowned them all,” leaving no survivors among the disbelievers, not even Noah’s own son, who refused to board the ark. This totality demonstrates the finality of divine judgment when it arrives. The verse serves as both a comfort and a warning. It comforts the Prophet by showing that Noah too faced a people who rejected his signs, yet in the end, truth was made to prevail and falsehood was eradicated. It warns the Quraysh that if they continue to reject the signs brought by Muhammad, they risk a similar fate, whether in this world or the next. For believers, the lesson is that ultimate success is not measured by immediate worldly standards but by the final outcome under Allah’s decree, where the rejecters of truth can never truly prevail. 
78 | And ˹remember˺ when David and Solomon passed judgment regarding the crops ruined ˹at night˺ by someone’s sheep, and We were witness to their judgments. | The narrative shifts to another pair of prophets, father and son, highlighting the beauty of prophetic heritage and divine wisdom in judgment. The story alluded to is a famous incident. A flock of sheep belonging to one person entered the field of another person at night and ruined his crops by eating and trampling them. The case was brought before Prophet David for judgment. The verse says “David and Solomon passed judgment,” indicating that both father and son were involved. The phrase “We were witness to their judgments” assures that Allah was fully aware of their reasoning and that what transpired was part of a divine teaching. According to authentic narrations, David initially judged that the owner of the field should receive the sheep as compensation, since the value of the lost crops roughly equaled the value of the sheep. However, the young Solomon, who was present, offered a different judgment: the owner of the sheep should give his flock to the farmer to benefit from their wool and milk, while the farmer should work on the damaged land to restore it, and once the land was restored to its original state, the sheep would be returned to their owner. This solution avoided immediate transfer of capital and instead facilitated restoration of the damaged property while the owner of the sheep bore the cost of lost productivity. The verse sets up the next verse, which will clarify how Allah guided Solomon to an even more equitable settlement. The story demonstrates the dynamism of Islamic law within the prophetic tradition, the encouragement of thoughtful ijtihad, and the special blessing of divine wisdom that can be bestowed upon a young person who respects their father but also thinks deeply about justice. It is an example of how two prophets, both guided, can arrive at different rulings, and Allah can make one ruling more optimal, all while both are rewarded for their sincere efforts. 
79 | We guided ˹young˺ Solomon to a fairer settlement, and granted each of them wisdom and knowledge. We subjected the mountains as well as the birds to hymn ˹Our praises˺ along with David. It is We Who did ˹it all˺. | The verse clarifies that the superior judgment in the case was inspired by Allah to young Solomon, who was not yet a prophet at that time but was being trained and endowed with exceptional insight. The phrase “We guided young Solomon to a fairer settlement” acknowledges that both judgments were valid, but Solomon’s was the more optimal and complete solution, demonstrating the principle of seeking excellence in judgment. The verse then ensures that no one thinks David was deficient; it immediately adds “and granted each of them wisdom and knowledge.” Both were divinely blessed with prophethood, wisdom, and deep knowledge. This is a beautiful model of how a teacher (David) and a student (Solomon) can both be honored, and how a younger person’s insight can be a source of enrichment rather than competition. The verse then transitions to a special favor to David: the subjection of the mountains and the birds to hymn praises along with him. David had an incredibly beautiful voice, and when he recited the Psalms, the mountains would echo his glorification, and the birds would gather and join in a synchronized chorus of divine praise. This was a miracle that demonstrated how all of creation is in harmony with the worship of Allah, and that a pure servant can become a conductor of this cosmic symphony. The final phrase “It is We Who did it all” emphasizes that these extraordinary gifts are purely from Allah’s grace. This teaches believers to recognize their talents as divine gifts and to use them in worship, and to never allow feelings of jealousy when a younger or less experienced person offers a better solution, for it is Allah who guides whom He wills to understanding. 
80 | We taught him the art of making body armour to protect you in battle. Will you then be grateful? | The surah continues with another specific favor granted to David, one of a practical and worldly nature. Allah taught him the art of making body armor. Before this, armor was typically made of heavy, inflexible plates. David was given the knowledge to work with iron in a new way, crafting interlocking rings of chainmail that were flexible, lighter, and provided good protection in battle. This was a technological advancement for his time, directly inspired by Allah. The purpose is stated: “to protect you in battle.” The phrase “you” addresses the community and all who would benefit from this invention in subsequent generations. The gift was not just for David but was a mercy for all who fight in the path of Allah. The verse ends with the rhetorical question “Will you then be grateful?” This connects the blessing directly to the response demanded from humanity: gratitude. Gratitude is not just a feeling but a practical use of the blessing in accordance with its divine purpose, acknowledging its source, and not using it for oppression or vanity. For the Prophet Muhammad and his companions, who were soon to face battles, this verse was a reminder that even the technology of war is a blessing from Allah, and that seeking means of protection is not a contradiction to trusting Allah; indeed, David combined utmost trust in Allah with the skillful making of armor. The verse teaches the balance between spiritual reliance and practical effort. It also refutes the idea that religion is only about abstract worship; it encompasses the development of craftsmanship, industry, and defensive capability, all of which can be acts of worship if done with the right intention and followed by gratitude to the true Provider.
81 | And to Solomon We subjected the raging winds, blowing by his command to the land We had showered with blessings. It is We Who know everything. | After mentioning David, the surah highlights the extraordinary gifts bestowed upon his son Solomon. The winds were made subservient to him, described here as “raging winds” that nonetheless obeyed his command. The term “raging” conveys their powerful, swift nature, yet they were tamed by Allah for Solomon’s use. They blew “by his command to the land We had showered with blessings.” This blessed land is generally understood to be greater Syria and Palestine, the same land to which Abraham had migrated and which was filled with divine barakah. Solomon’s ability to direct the wind meant he could travel vast distances in an incredibly short time, facilitating his governance, trade, and communication across his expansive kingdom. The wind, a natural force of immense power, became a docile servant, demonstrating Allah’s absolute control over creation and the honor given to a grateful prophet. The verse concludes with “It is We Who know everything.” This reminds Solomon and all who hear the story that this miraculous power was not Solomon’s own but a gift from the All-Knowing, who perfectly understood how to grant him such a dominion and who also knows how the gift is being used. It serves as a subtle caution against pride and a reminder of accountability. For believers, this verse illustrates the principle of taskhir: the subjection of the natural world to humanity in general, and to righteous servants in particular. While we do not command the wind literally, we are entrusted with harnessing nature’s forces, and this trust must be exercised with the consciousness that the One who subjected them knows everything we do. The verse also evokes the transitory nature of worldly power; Solomon’s wind-borne journeys carried him across a blessed land, pointing to the ultimate journey toward the Hereafter, the land of eternal blessing. 
82 | And ˹We subjected˺ some jinn that dived for him, and performed other duties. It is We Who kept them in check. | In addition to the winds, Allah subjected a faction of the jinn to Solomon’s service. The verse specifies that they “dived for him,” retrieving pearls, corals, and other treasures from the depths of the sea. They also “performed other duties,” which included building lofty chambers, statues, and great basins as mentioned in Surah Saba. The jinn are beings created from smokeless fire, normally invisible to humans and possessing abilities beyond human capacity. Their subjection to a human prophet was a unique miracle granted to Solomon, emphasizing his unmatched dominion. This service was not oppressive or demonic; it was a disciplined labor under divine command. The verse emphasizes, “It is We Who kept them in check.” The jinn, being powerful and potentially rebellious, were only held in this service by Allah’s direct control. This prevented them from harming Solomon or escaping their duties. The statement underscores that no creature, no matter how powerful or cunning, operates outside Allah’s authority. For the Prophet Muhammad, who was offered a similar dominion over the jinn and the mountains but chose instead to remain a humble servant, this verse provided context. It showed that such miracles were given to previous prophets, but the final prophet’s greatest miracle is the Quran. The verse also serves as a warning against seeking help from jinn through forbidden means, a practice common among the Arabs. The jinn are not independent gods or helpers; they are creatures under Allah’s control, and any service they render is only by His permission. The righteous engage only with what Allah has made lawful. Solomon’s story thus demystifies the jinn and places them firmly within the framework of divine sovereignty. 
83 | And ˹remember˺ when Job cried out to his Lord, “I have been touched with adversity, and You are the Most Merciful of the merciful.” | The surah now turns to Prophet Job (Ayyub), whose story is one of the most profound examples of patience and trust in divine mercy. The command “remember” invites reflection on his ordeal. Job was a wealthy, healthy, and righteous man blessed with a large family. Then he was tested with the loss of his wealth, the death of his children, and a severe, prolonged illness that left him physically ravaged. His society shunned him, and only his faithful wife remained by his side. After years of suffering, he turned to Allah with this beautiful prayer. The prayer is remarkable for what it does not say. He does not demand a cure, does not complain about injustice, does not ask why he was singled out. He simply states his condition: “I have been touched with adversity.” The word “touched” minimizes the suffering in his own expression, showing profound restraint. Then he addresses Allah with the most exquisite courtesy: “You are the Most Merciful of the merciful.” By beginning with a description of Allah’s mercy rather than his own pain, he places his hope in the one attribute that can transform his situation. He does not even explicitly ask for healing; the mention of Allah’s mercy in the context of his suffering is itself the most eloquent supplication. This prayer teaches believers the etiquettes of dua: acknowledging one’s state without complaining, and magnifying Allah’s attributes with hope. It is a model for those enduring chronic illness, loss, or hardship. The focus is not on the severity of the test but on the boundlessness of the tester’s mercy, trusting that the Most Merciful will respond in the most merciful way. 
84 | So We answered his prayer and removed his adversity, and gave him back his family, twice as many, as a mercy from Us and a lesson for the ˹devoted˺ worshippers. | The divine response to Job’s prayer was immediate and generous. “We answered his prayer” affirms the direct connection between his humble, trust-filled supplication and the relief that followed. The phrase “removed his adversity” means his illness was completely cured. According to exegetical reports, Job was instructed to strike the ground with his foot, from which a cool spring gushed forth; he bathed and drank, and all his afflictions vanished. Then, “gave him back his family, twice as many.” This indicates that the children who had died were restored in the form of new children, and the number was doubled, a gesture of overflowing divine generosity. Some scholars suggest that the deceased children themselves were resurrected and additional children were granted. In either case, the blessing exceeded what was lost. The verse states the purpose of this restoration: “as a mercy from Us and a lesson for the devoted worshippers.” The mercy was for Job personally, a tangible embrace from the Most Merciful. The lesson extends to all devoted worshippers across time. Job’s story teaches that patience in the face of suffering is not in vain, that Allah’s mercy is always present even when veiled by pain, and that relief follows hardship with divine precision. The “devoted worshippers” are those who learn from Job that the essence of worship is not only to serve in times of ease but to maintain devotion in times of trial, knowing that the One they worship is the Healer and the Restorer. This story deeply comforted the Prophet Muhammad during the long years of Meccan persecution, reassuring him that the testing of a prophet is a prelude to immense divine mercy and victory. 
85 | And ˹remember˺ Ishmael, Enoch, and Ⱬul-Kifl. They were all steadfast. | The surah briefly mentions three more prophets, grouping them together under the quality of steadfastness. Ishmael (Isma‘il) was the elder son of Abraham, the forefather of the Arabs, and a prophet who settled in Mecca and helped build the Kaaba. His life was marked by absolute submission, most famously when he willingly lay down for sacrifice at Allah’s command. Enoch (Idris) is praised elsewhere in the Quran as a man of truth and a prophet who was raised to a lofty station. His patience and dedication to calling his people are legendary, and he is considered one of the earliest prophets after Adam. Dhul-Kifl, whose name means “the one of the fold” or “the one of the pledge,” is identified by many scholars as a righteous prophet who took a solemn pledge to judge his people with justice and to spend his nights in worship and his days in fasting, fulfilling his covenant perfectly. Others identify him with Ezekiel or a different righteous figure. The verse singles out their shared quality: “They were all steadfast.” Patience and perseverance are the backbone of prophethood. Each of these men faced daunting challenges, hostile audiences, or severe personal trials, yet they remained unwavering in their faith and mission. By mentioning them, the surah reinforces the universal theme that the path of truth requires sabr. The Prophet Muhammad is implicitly told that he is in the company of these giants of patience. For the believers, the verse is a reminder to honor all the prophets equally, to study their lives, and to extract the elixir of patience from their stories. Steadfastness is not passive endurance but active, consistent commitment to God regardless of circumstances. 
86 | We admitted them into Our mercy, for they were truly of the righteous. | The verse delivers the ultimate verdict on Ishmael, Enoch, and Dhul-Kifl. After their lives of steadfastness, the final destination was entrance into Allah’s mercy. This phrase “admitted them into Our mercy” is a beautiful metaphor for Paradise and the divine good pleasure. It echoes the same phrase used for Lot earlier in verse 75, creating a thread of continuity in the surah’s prophetic biographies. The mercy here is not a temporary relief but an eternal state of bliss and divine nearness. The reason for this admittance is given: “for they were truly of the righteous.” The word “truly” (inna) emphasizes the depth and authenticity of their righteousness. It was not a superficial label but a reality that permeated their entire being, from their beliefs to their actions, from their public lives to their private moments. Righteousness (salah) is the key that unlocks divine mercy. The verse teaches that prophethood and divine favor are not based on lineage or worldly status but on genuine righteousness. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on descent from Ishmael, are implicitly reminded that the honor of Ishmael is rooted in his righteousness and steadfastness, not just his bloodline, and they must emulate his faith to claim any true connection to him. For the believers, this verse provides a clear roadmap: strive for authentic righteousness in your character and deeds, and the outcome, by Allah’s mercy, will be admittance into an eternal abode of mercy. The sequence also reassures the Prophet that those who are steadfast in the face of rejection, as these prophets were, are never forgotten and their end is secure with Allah. 
87 | And ˹remember˺ when the Man of the Whale stormed off ˹from his city˺ in a rage, thinking We would not restrain him. Then in the ˹veils of˺ darkness he cried out, “There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except You. Glory be to You! I have certainly done wrong.” | The narrative now focuses on Jonah (Yunus), referred to as “the Man of the Whale” (Dhu’n-Nun). His story is a powerful lesson in prophetic fallibility, repentance, and divine mercy. Jonah was sent to the people of Nineveh, who rejected his call. Frustrated and angry, he “stormed off” from his city without waiting for Allah’s explicit command. This act was a lapse in prophetic patience, though not a major sin. He thought “We would not restrain him,” meaning he assumed that his departure would not result in immediate divine constriction or punishment for him. He underestimated the accountability that comes with the prophetic mission. Allah’s power, however, does restrict and test. He boarded a ship, was thrown overboard, and was swallowed by a massive whale. In the “veils of darkness” — the darkness of the whale’s belly, the deep sea, and the night — he cried out the famous supplication. This prayer is a masterpiece of monotheistic devotion. He begins with the negation of all false gods and the affirmation of Allah’s absolute oneness: “There is no god worthy of worship except You.” Then he glorifies Allah, declaring Him far above any injustice: “Glory be to You!” Only then does he confess his own fault: “I have certainly done wrong.” The order is significant: he places Allah’s transcendence and perfection first, then his own error. This supplication, known as the prayer of Dhun-Nun, is so powerful that the Prophet Muhammad said that no Muslim who supplicates with it for anything will have it except it is granted. The verse teaches that even a prophet can experience a moment of haste, but what distinguishes the righteous is their immediate return to Allah, acknowledging His glory and their own need, especially in the depths of darkness. 
88 | So We answered his prayer and rescued him from anguish. And so do We save the ˹true˺ believers. | The divine response was immediate. “We answered his prayer” is a testament to the power of sincere repentance and the glorification of Allah. No matter how dark the situation — literally in the belly of a whale at the bottom of the sea — the call of a sincere heart reaches Allah and is answered. “Rescued him from anguish” refers to both the physical deliverance from the whale and the spiritual anguish of his separation from his mission and his error. The whale ejected him onto a shore, where a gourd plant grew to protect and nourish him, and he was restored to health and returned to his people. The verse then expands this individual salvation into a universal principle: “And so do We save the true believers.” The deliverance of Jonah is a prototype of how Allah rescues the believers from their own mistakes, from the consequences of their sins, and from the oppressive situations they find themselves in. The phrase “true believers” indicates that this divine rescue is contingent upon genuine faith. The conditions are the same as those demonstrated by Jonah: sincere recognition of Allah’s oneness, glorification of Him, humble admission of one’s own wrongdoing, and turning to Him in supplication with a heart full of hope. This verse is a source of immense hope for every believer who has erred. It promises that no matter how far one has strayed or how deep the “darkness” is, the path back to light is open through the words of tawheed and istighfar. It also comforts those who are unjustly oppressed, assuring them that the same Lord who rescued Jonah from the whale’s belly can rescue them from the grip of the oppressor, as long as they turn to Him sincerely. 
89 | And ˹remember˺ when Zachariah cried out to his Lord, “My Lord! Do not leave me childless, though You are the Best of Successors.” | The surah now turns to Zachariah (Zakariyya), the righteous prophet from the lineage of Aaron and the guardian of Mary. Zachariah and his wife had reached extreme old age without having a child, a source of deep personal sorrow and a concern for the continuation of his prophetic mission. His cry to his Lord is introduced with the same “remember” that links all these prophetic stories, emphasizing their relevance to the Prophet Muhammad. Zachariah’s prayer is tender and respectful. He begins with “My Lord,” expressing intimate, personal devotion. His request, “Do not leave me childless,” reveals a profound human longing, not merely for an heir to his property but for a righteous successor who would carry on the legacy of prophethood and divine service in the temple. Yet, immediately after stating his need, he adds a statement of complete submission: “though You are the Best of Successors.” This phrase is deeply moving. Zachariah is saying that even if Allah were to leave him without a child, he acknowledges that Allah is the ultimate inheritor of everything; all things return to Him, and He needs no heir. His own personal desire is subordinated to his recognition of Allah’s perfection. This teaches the balance in supplication: one should present one’s needs with urgency and hope, but always with the underlying acknowledgment that Allah’s wisdom is supreme, and His decree is better than our plans. The verse highlights the etiquettes of dua in the lives of the prophets: a blend of frank expression of need, intimate address, and glorification of the Lord. For those who suffer from childlessness or any unfulfilled desire, this is a model of how to turn to Allah with hope while finding peace in His perfect dispossession. 
90 | So We answered his prayer, granted him John, and made his wife fertile. Indeed, they used to race in doing good, and call upon Us with hope and fear, totally humbling themselves before Us. | The divine response to Zachariah’s heartfelt prayer was swift and miraculous. “We answered his prayer” indicates that his cry was heard and accepted. The answer took the form of granting him a son, John (Yahya), a name chosen by Allah Himself, as mentioned in another surah. The miracle involved not only Zachariah’s old age but also the healing of his wife’s barrenness, as the verse says “and made his wife fertile.” This double miracle — curing both male and female infertility — signified the absolute power of Allah over what humans deem biologically impossible. The verse then shifts from the miracle itself to the spiritual character of Zachariah, his wife, and John. “Indeed, they used to race in doing good,” meaning they were not passive recipients of divine favor; they actively competed with one another in righteous deeds, seizing every opportunity for charity, prayer, and service. “And call upon Us with hope and fear” describes their state of worship: a perfect balance between hoping for Allah’s mercy and fearing His punishment, never becoming complacent or despairing. “Totally humbling themselves before Us” refers to khushu‘, a profound state of inner and outer humility, devotion, and stillness before Allah. This description is a window into the household that produced John, the forerunner of Jesus. It reveals that divine miracles are granted in an atmosphere of sincere, balanced worship. The lesson for believers is that while we may ask for specific worldly blessings, the greatest goal is to cultivate a home environment characterized by racing in good deeds, balanced hope and fear, and deep humility before the Creator. When a household is built on these qualities, it becomes a fitting receptacle for divine grace. 
91 | And ˹remember˺ the one who guarded her chastity, so We breathed into her through Our angel, ˹Gabriel,˺ making her and her son a sign for all peoples. | The surah now mentions the Virgin Mary and the miraculous birth of Jesus. The phrase “the one who guarded her chastity” is a beautiful, honoring description of Mary (Maryam). Her guarding of chastity was not limited to physical virginity; it encompassed complete moral purity, modesty, and devotion. She is the highest example of feminine piety in the Quran. The verse says, “so We breathed into her through Our angel, Gabriel.” This refers to the immaculate conception, where the angel Gabriel appeared to Mary in the form of a man and blew into her garment or into her, by which Allah’s creative command “Be” took effect, and she conceived Jesus without a human father. This breath was not a paternal contribution; it was the means by which the divine miracle was initiated, a direct act of creation. The result was “making her and her son a sign for all peoples.” Mary, a woman who conceived without a man, and Jesus, a prophet who spoke in the cradle and performed miracles, together constitute a sign that testifies to Allah’s power over nature and His ability to create as He wills. The sign is “for all peoples,” transcending the Children of Israel, indicating the universal significance of this miracle. This verse is a powerful refutation of both the pagan Arab denial of miraculous birth and the later Christian deification of Jesus. It affirms the miracle while keeping Jesus firmly within the category of a created being and a prophet, born of a chaste servant. The Prophet Muhammad is reminded of this profound sign, reinforcing that his own miraculous Quran is part of a long chain of divine signs meant for all humanity. 
92 | ˹O prophets!˺ Indeed, this religion of yours is ˹only˺ one, and I am your Lord, so worship Me ˹alone˺. | After the panoramic survey of so many prophets, the surah now addresses them collectively, and through them, all of humanity. The address “O prophets” (or “O messengers,” according to variant readings) is remarkable. It implies that in the divine communication, all prophets are addressed as a single community with a unified mission. The core message is stated: “Indeed, this religion of yours is only one.” This is the essence of the entire chapter and indeed the entire Quran. From Noah to Abraham, to Moses, David, Solomon, Job, Jonah, Zachariah, Jesus, and finally Muhammad, the religion is fundamentally the same. It is the religion of Islam in its broadest sense: complete submission to the one true God. The differences in legal codes, rituals, and languages are superficial branches; the trunk is pure monotheism and moral guidance. Then comes the direct divine command: “and I am your Lord, so worship Me alone.” The logical link is clear: since there is only one religion, there is only one Lord, and therefore worship must be directed exclusively to Him. This verse is a powerful declaration against all forms of religious division and polytheism. For the Prophet Muhammad, it confirmed that he was not bringing a new, separate religion but was restoring and completing the same religion preached by all his brothers. For the Quraysh, it was a direct challenge: if they honored Abraham, they must follow his monotheistic religion. For the believers, this verse fosters a profound sense of solidarity with all genuine followers of the prophets throughout history. The unity of religion is a source of strength, and the exclusive worship of Allah is the foundation of human dignity and freedom from servitude to created things. 
93 | Yet the people have divided it into sects. But to Us they will all return. | Despite the clear divine mandate of religious unity, the verse laments the historical reality: “Yet the people have divided it into sects.” The “people” refers to the followers of the prophets, the communities that came after them. After the passing of their messenger and the passage of time, they fragmented their one religion into numerous sects, each claiming exclusivity and often accusing others of misguidance. This division is a human innovation, a deviation from the pristine unity commanded by Allah. The causes of division are many: jealousy, love of power, blind following, misinterpretation of scripture, and the influence of cultural prejudices. The Quran and the Prophet Muhammad frequently warned against this sectarianism, urging Muslims to hold fast to the rope of Allah and not be divided. The verse then closes with a sobering reminder: “But to Us they will all return.” This return to Allah is for judgment. Every sect, every group, every individual who divided the religion will stand before the One Lord and be questioned. The truth will be made clear, and those who divided for worldly or egotistical reasons will face the consequences of their actions. This statement is both a comfort and a warning. It comforts the true believers who uphold the one religion and are grieved by the divisions, assuring them that ultimate justice rests with Allah. It warns those who cause and perpetuate divisions without sincere scholarly basis, reminding them that they will answer to the One who commanded unity. The verse calls for a conscious effort to minimize division by returning to the original sources, the Quran and the authentic Sunnah, and by maintaining a spirit of brotherhood despite legitimate differences of scholarly interpretation. 
94 | So whoever does good and is a believer will never be denied ˹the reward for˺ their striving, for We are recording it all. | After the warning about sectarian division, the verse opens a door of hope and clarifies the standard of acceptance with Allah. The standard is twofold: belief and good deeds. “Whoever does good” refers to righteous actions, whether prayer, charity, justice, or kindness. But action alone is not sufficient; the person must also be “a believer.” Faith is the soul of the deed; without faith, good deeds are like a body without life. This combination of iman and amal salih is the consistent Quranic condition for salvation. The verse promises that such a person “will never be denied the reward for their striving.” The word “never” is emphatic, a divine guarantee. No effort, however small, will be lost or unappreciated. This is a direct rebuttal to any notion that salvation is restricted to a particular tribe, sect, or label. The field is open: whoever meets the universal criteria of sincere faith and righteous action, regardless of their historical or social context, will find their reward secure with Allah. The last phrase, “for We are recording it all,” reinforces the guarantee with the certainty of divine record-keeping. Every prayer, every act of charity, every moment of patience, every struggle against temptation is meticulously inscribed by the angels who never miss a thing. This awareness is intended to motivate the believer to fill their record with good, especially in the face of those who divide the religion. Instead of engaging in sectarian disputes, the verse directs the believer’s energy toward personal faith and righteous action, leaving the judgment to the All-Knowing Recorder. It fosters a hopeful, action-oriented spirituality grounded in the absolute justice and mercy of Allah. 
95 | It is impossible for a society which We have destroyed to ever rise again, | This verse transitions to a discussion of the end times and the finality of divine punishment for those destroyed nations. The statement “It is impossible for a society which We have destroyed to ever rise again” refers to the people whom Allah annihilated due to their disbelief and iniquity — the people of Noah, ‘Ad, Thamud, Lot, and others. Their destruction was total, and they will not return to this world for a second chance at life. This refutes the pagan Arab notion that after death, there is nothing, or that souls might reincarnate or return. It also emphasizes the finality of Allah’s judgment in this world for those who have reached a point of no return. The apparent contradiction with the concept of resurrection is resolved by noting that “rise again” here means returning to the worldly life, not the Hereafter. They will indeed be resurrected on the Day of Judgment for accounting, but they will not come back to the earthly existence to make amends. The verse serves as a stern warning to the Quraysh, telling them that the destroyed nations they pass by in their travels are gone forever, and if they persist, they too could face a destruction from which there is no return to worldly power. It also carries a deep philosophical point about the precious, irreplaceable nature of the current life as the only arena for earning one’s eternal fate. There is no second chance after death or after collective destruction. This urgency is meant to shake the heedless from their complacency, as mentioned in the opening verses. The verse forms a crucial link to the next verses, which describe the release of Gog and Magog as a sign of the approaching Hour and the finality of the disbelievers’ doom. 
96 | until ˹after˺ Gog and Magog have broken loose ˹from the barrier˺, swarming down from every hill, | The surah introduces the apocalyptic figure of Gog and Magog (Ya’juj and Ma’juj). Their story is mentioned in detail in Surah Al-Kahf, where a righteous king, Dhul-Qarnayn, built a massive iron barrier to contain their destructive raids. The verse here says that the destroyed nations will not rise again “until Gog and Magog have broken loose from the barrier.” The word “until” connects the previous verse to the events of the end times. The release of Gog and Magog is one of the major signs of the approaching Hour. They will break through the barrier that held them for centuries and will “swarm down from every hill.” This description paints a terrifying picture of an overwhelming, unstoppable horde pouring forth from all directions, spreading chaos and corruption across the earth. They are a vast number of people known for their ferocity and destruction. Their release signals the near end of the world as we know it. The verse serves a dual purpose. First, it is a proof of the Quran’s knowledge of the unseen, giving a specific future event that will occur. Second, it is a warning to all humanity that the current order of the world is not permanent; a time will come when divine restraint will be lifted, and immense tribulations will sweep the earth. This ties directly to the heedlessness mentioned in the first verses; people are turning away, but the forces that will overwhelm them are as real and certain as the dawn. The verse teaches believers to be prepared for the great trials of the end times by strengthening their faith and holding fast to righteousness before the great upheavals make such preparation impossible. 
97 | ushering in the True Promise. Then—behold!—the disbelievers will stare ˹in horror, crying,˺ “Oh, woe to us! We have truly been heedless of this. In fact, we have been wrongdoers.” | The release of Gog and Magog directly precedes the fulfillment of the True Promise, which is the Day of Judgment. They are the vanguard of the final upheaval. When the Hour is established, the scene shifts dramatically to the disbelievers. The word “behold!” (fa-idha) indicates a sudden, shocking change of reality. Their state is one of staring in horror, eyes wide with terror, fixed on the unimaginable scene before them. Their cry echoes the cry of the destroyed nations in earlier verses: “Oh, woe to us! We have truly been heedless of this. In fact, we have been wrongdoers.” This is the ultimate confession, but it is far too late. The phrase “heedless of this” directly recalls the opening verse of the surah, “yet they are heedlessly turning away.” The circle is complete. The very heedlessness they indulged in during their worldly life becomes the source of their eternal regret. They admit to two things: their heedlessness of the warning and their status as wrongdoers. The admission is clear and unambiguous, stripping away all the excuses, mockery, and arguments they used to dismiss the messengers. The repetition of “woe to us” throughout the surah (verses 14, 46, and now 97) creates a haunting refrain, a warning that persistently calls out to the listener not to become the one who utters these words when it is too late. The verse is a masterful piece of narrative symmetry, bringing the themes of warning, heedlessness, and belated confession to a climax, urging immediate repentance before the day when the cry will bring no relief. 
98 | Certainly you ˹disbelievers˺ and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell. You are ˹all˺ bound to enter it. | The surah now delivers a crushing verdict upon the disbelievers and their false gods. The address is direct: “Certainly you disbelievers and whatever you worship instead of Allah will be the fuel of Hell.” The word “fuel” (hasab) originally means the kindling or firewood thrown into a furnace to keep it burning. Both the worshippers and the worshipped — the idols of stone or wood, and even the false deities among the jinn or humans who accepted worship — will be cast into the Fire as its fuel. This is the ultimate humiliation for the idols: they, who were set up as divine, are reduced to mere firewood. The phrase “You are all bound to enter it” leaves no room for escape. The polytheists believed that their idols might intercede for them or that they themselves were too honored to be punished. This verse extinguishes that hope completely. The verse echoes the scene of Abraham smashing the idols; just as the idols could not defend themselves then, they cannot defend themselves or their worshippers from Hell. This is a powerful psychological attack on the foundation of shirk. It teaches the absolute principle of Tawheed: anything worshipped besides Allah is not only powerless to help but will itself become a part of the punishment. The verse is harsh but just. It aims to save its hearers by presenting the horrific end with stark clarity. For the believer, it reinforces the duty to call people away from all forms of false worship, knowing the catastrophic eternal consequences. The imagery of “fuel” also reminds us that the Fire of Hell is a real, consuming entity, and those who enter it will be consumed in a cycle of perpetual punishment, as the next verses will describe. 
99 | Had those idols been ˹true˺ gods, they would not have entered it. And they will be there forever. | The logic of monotheism is presented with devastating simplicity. “Had those idols been true gods, they would not have entered it.” A true god would be self-sufficient, powerful, and honored. The fact that these idols will be cast into the Fire alongside their worshippers is the ultimate proof that they are not divine. If they possessed any real power or deserved any worship, they would have been able to save themselves from such an end. Their entry into Hell is the definitive empirical refutation of their divinity. This addresses the pagans’ last shred of hope that their gods might somehow be real and able to rescue them. On the Day of Judgment, the falsehood will be exposed not through argument but through observable reality. The verse then states, “And they will be there forever.” This eternity applies to the idols, their worshippers, and the false deities. For human and jinn worshippers, the punishment for shirk and disbelief, if they die without repentance, is eternal abode in Hell, as established in the Quran. The mention of the idols’ eternal presence there emphasizes their permanence as symbols of the eternal nature of the punishment; they are not burned up and annihilated but remain as part of the torment. Some scholars clarify that lifeless idols will be given a form of life so that they can feel the pain and so that their worshippers can see their humiliation. The verse is a final, crushing blow to any remaining pagan sentiment among the Quraysh and a clear call to the absolute worship of the one God who is far above entering any fire, the Ever-Living, the Self-Sustaining. 
100 | In it they will groan, and will not be able to hear. | The surah closes the description of the people of Hell with a vivid sensory image. “In it they will groan” conveys the continuous, involuntary sound of extreme agony and distress. The word translated as “groan” (zafir) refers to a deep, heavy exhale, the kind emitted from the depths of a tormented chest. This is not a calm existence; it is an unceasing state of suffering where relief is not even a distant hope. The second phrase, “and will not be able to hear,” has layers of meaning. It may mean that their own groaning and the roaring of the Fire are so loud that they cannot hear anything else, including any possible call of mercy or reprieve. It may also mean that they are deaf to anything that could bring them comfort; their auditory senses are filled entirely with the sounds of punishment. Some commentators add that they will not hear anything from the outside world, completely cut off from any source of joy or communication. The combination of continuous groaning and inability to hear paints a picture of absolute isolation within torment. There is no companionship in misery, only a cacophony of shared suffering that drowns out everything else. This is the final destination of the heedlessness and mockery described at the beginning of the surah. The contrast with the people of Paradise, described in the very next verse, is stark: the people of Paradise will not even hear the faintest sound of Hell, and they will have whatever their souls desire. The surah thus masterfully uses vivid, sensory contrasts to motivate the listener to seek one destination and flee from the other. The verse is a sobering conclusion to the passage of warning, driving home the urgency of the opening call.
101 | Surely those for whom We have destined the finest reward will be kept far away from Hell, | After the terrifying description of the groaning inhabitants of the Fire, the surah immediately turns to the contrasting fate of the righteous. The verse opens with a statement of absolute assurance: “Surely those for whom We have destined the finest reward.” The word “destined” translates “sabaqat” which conveys a preceding decree from Allah, a preordained good that has already been written for them in the Preserved Tablet. This destiny is based on Allah’s eternal knowledge of their sincere faith and righteous deeds. The “finest reward” (al-husna) is understood by all commentators to be Paradise and the beatific vision of Allah’s countenance, the ultimate good that eclipses all other blessings. The verse then declares that these people “will be kept far away from Hell.” The phrase indicates a complete spatial and experiential separation. They will not merely be at a distance; they will be actively kept away, protected by divine grace. The concept of being “far away” is both physical and psychological. Their hearts will be entirely free from the fear and horror of the Fire. This mirrors the Quranic promise that the righteous will not hear the faintest sound of it, as the next verse elaborates. The verse provides a profound sense of security for the believer. The destiny of the finest reward is not an arbitrary selection; it is earned through a life of faith, and it culminates in a salvation that removes every trace of anxiety. For the Prophet’s companions who endured relentless persecution in Mecca, this verse was a balm, assuring them that their present suffering was a prelude to an eternal position of safety and honor. It also implicitly calls the heedless to reflect: while they mock, there are those for whom the finest reward has already been decreed, and they will be shielded from the catastrophe they ridicule. 
102 | not even hearing the slightest hissing from it. And they will delight forever in what their souls desire. | The separation of the righteous from Hell is described with extraordinary sensory precision. “Not even hearing the slightest hissing from it” means that the sense of hearing, which is often the first to perceive danger, will receive absolutely no signal of the Fire’s presence. The word “hissing” translates “hasis,” which refers to a low, faint sound, like the sizzle of a flame or the whisper of movement. The people of Paradise will not be subjected to even this minimal auditory reminder of the torment. This complete sensory isolation indicates a perfect peace of mind; their environment is so thoroughly shielded that the very existence of Hell, though real, will not intrude upon their consciousness in any distressing way. The verse then reveals the positive side of their state: “And they will delight forever in what their souls desire.” This is the ultimate fulfillment. Paradise is not merely a place of generic pleasures; it is a realm where the deepest, most personal and purified desires of the soul are continuously satisfied. Whatever a righteous soul yearns for—be it reunion with loved ones, knowledge, beauty, tranquility, or the vision of the Lord—will be granted. The word “forever” (khalidun) seals the deal with permanence, eliminating the fear of loss that mars even the greatest worldly pleasures. The verse draws a stark contrast with the people of Hell, who groan in agony and cannot hear. The believers, in contrast, enjoy a silence broken only by peace and the fulfillment of every heartfelt longing. This teaching has always inspired Muslims to orient their desires toward what is permissible and noble, knowing that purified desires will be eternally fulfilled, while base desires, if not repented from, lead to a place where not even the faintest sound of relief is heard. 
103 | The Supreme Horror ˹of that Day˺ will not disturb them, and the angels will greet them, ˹saying,˺ “This is your Day, which you have been promised.” | The surah now mentions a specific event of the Day of Judgment: the “Supreme Horror” (al-faza‘ al-akbar). This refers to the overwhelming terror that will seize all creation when the Trumpet is blown, the heavens are split, and the universe collapses. It is a panic so immense that every nursing mother will forget her child and every pregnant female will drop her burden, as described in other surahs. Yet, the verse promises that this supreme terror “will not disturb them.” The righteous will be in a state of such tranquility that the cosmic upheaval around them will not cause them fear or grief. Their hearts are secure in the knowledge of their Lord’s pleasure. This is a special divine gift: immunity from the greatest psychological shockwave that will ever occur. Instead of terror, they are met with a completely different experience: “the angels will greet them.” The angels, descending from every gate, will approach them with peace and salutations, conveying congratulations and welcome. The greeting “This is your Day, which you have been promised” transforms the Day of Judgment from a court of dread into a day of celebration for them. The promise they heard from their prophets in the world—that a day of eternal reward awaits the believers—is now visibly, tangibly realized. The verse thus provides a beautiful frame: the very same Day that is the source of ultimate horror for the disbelievers is a day of joyous reunion and honor for the believers. This reinforces the Quran’s message that the objective reality of the Hereafter is filtered through the state of one’s own soul. For the believers, the instruction is to live in such a way that the thought of that Day brings not dread but anticipation, by believing in the promise and preparing for it with righteous deeds. 
104 | On that Day We will roll up the heavens like a scroll of writings. Just as We produced the first creation, ˹so˺ shall We reproduce it. That is a promise binding on Us. We truly uphold ˹Our promises˺! | This verse describes one of the most dramatic physical transformations of the universe on the Day of Judgment. “We will roll up the heavens like a scroll of writings” is a powerful visual simile. The vast, seemingly infinite expanse of the sky, with its stars and galaxies, will be folded up as effortlessly as a scribe rolls up a finished parchment. The mention of a “scroll of writings” (sijill) evokes the image of a document that has served its purpose and is now closed and put away. The heavens, which were spread out as a sign and a canopy, will be deconstructed with ease by their Creator. This demonstrates that the physical world is not eternal; it is a contingent creation that will be terminated when its term ends. The verse then draws a logical parallel to refute the disbelievers’ skepticism about resurrection: “Just as We produced the first creation, so shall We reproduce it.” The initial act of creating everything from nothing is, by logical necessity, a greater or at least equally great act of power compared to recreating it. Therefore, the One who did the first can certainly do the second. This argument, repeated throughout the Quran, appeals to human reason. The verse concludes with emphatic assurance: “That is a promise binding on Us. We truly uphold Our promises!” Allah binds Himself by His own word, not because of any external compulsion, but as an expression of His absolute truthfulness and mercy. The phrase “binding on Us” conveys an unshakeable certainty. The believers are called to internalize this promise so deeply that no doubt can touch their hearts. The verse also connects beautifully to the earlier mention of the books and records; just as the scrolls of deeds will be laid open, the scroll of the universe will be rolled up, and a new creation will unfold. 
105 | Surely, following the ˹heavenly˺ Record, We decreed in the Scriptures: “My righteous servants shall inherit the land.” | This verse reveals a profound divine decree that was inscribed in the scriptures given to earlier prophets. The phrase “following the heavenly Record” refers to the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawh al-Mahfuz), the original divine register from which all sacred scriptures descend. From that heavenly source, Allah decreed and wrote down in the revealed Scriptures—such as the Torah, the Psalms, and the Quran itself—the promise: “My righteous servants shall inherit the land.” This exact phrase is found in the Psalms (Zabur) of David, specifically Psalm 37:29: “The righteous shall inherit the land and dwell upon it forever.” The Quran confirms and universalizes this promise. The “land” here can be understood in two complementary ways. First, it refers to the earth in this world; the righteous, though often persecuted, will ultimately be granted authority and succession, as the Muslim community later experienced. Second, and more profoundly, it refers to the “land” of Paradise, the eternal inheritance of the believers. In the Hadith, the Prophet explained that the “land” is the land of Paradise. This interpretation aligns with the immediate context of the Hereafter. The verse thus bridges the two worlds: the righteous will inherit both the moral and physical successorship on earth and the everlasting gardens of the Hereafter. For the persecuted believers in Mecca, this was a powerful promise. They were a small, landless, and oppressed group, yet the ancient scriptures had prophesied their ultimate inheritance. This verse elevates the discourse, showing that the Quran is in harmony with the earlier revelations, fulfilling their promises. It underscores that righteousness, not wealth or power, is the true key to inheritance, a principle that stands in stark contrast to the values of the Quraysh. 
106 | Surely this ˹Quran˺ is sufficient ˹as a reminder˺ for those devoted to worship. | After the grand cosmic promises, the verse returns to the immediate value of the Quran itself. It declares, “Surely this Quran is sufficient as a reminder.” The word “sufficient” (balagh) also carries the meaning of a clear proclamation, a message that reaches its intended audience completely. The Quran, in its entirety, contains all the reminders, warnings, laws, stories, and arguments that a human being needs to achieve guidance and salvation. It does not require supplementation by any other source of ultimate truth. The phrase “for those devoted to worship” defines the receptive audience. The Quran’s sufficiency is actualized specifically for people who are already oriented toward worshipping Allah, who are engaged in devotion and are seeking the truth with sincerity. For such hearts, the Quran is a complete and self-sufficient guide. It answers their deepest questions, heals their spiritual ailments, and directs them to the straight path. This verse is a testament to the comprehensiveness of the Quran. It also subtly addresses the pagans’ demand for different kinds of signs; the answer is that the Book itself is the ultimate, sufficient sign. The Prophet is thus reminded that he does not need to worry about those who turn away; the reminder is fully present and effective for its intended recipients. For the believers, this verse instills a deep reliance on the Quran as the primary source of guidance. It encourages a lifelong engagement with its verses, understanding that every era, every problem, and every spiritual need can find its answer therein. The condition is to be among the “devoted to worship,” those who approach the Book not as a literary curiosity but as a life-giving message from their Lord, read with humility and a yearning for guidance. 
107 | We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ only as a mercy for the whole world. | This is one of the most beautiful and comprehensive descriptions of the Prophet Muhammad’s mission. The verse addresses him directly with a statement of profound compassion. “We have sent you only as a mercy” uses the particle “illā” which indicates exclusivity. His primary, overarching quality and purpose is mercy. Everything about his mission—his teachings, his example, his intercession, the legislation he brought—is a manifestation of divine mercy. The phrase “for the whole world” (lil-‘ālamīn) expands this mercy to encompass all of creation: humans, jinn, animals, the environment, and even the angels. His mercy is not confined to the believers alone; even the disbelievers benefit from the mercy of the Prophet’s presence. Because of him, the immediate and total destruction that wiped out previous nations was withheld from his rejecters. His mercy extended to captives, enemies, children, and the natural world. A famous hadith illustrates that when the Prophet was asked to curse the polytheists, he replied, “I was not sent as a curser, but I was sent as a mercy.” Even in the conquest of Mecca, those who had persecuted him for years were granted amnesty. His mercy is also seen in the balanced and compassionate law he brought, which provides ease, forgiveness, and gradual reform. The verse refutes any notion that the Prophet was sent as a harsh warner only. The warning is itself a mercy, alerting people to danger. The believers are thus called to embody this mercy in their own behavior, to be merciful to all of Allah’s creation, following the example of the one sent as a mercy to the worlds. This verse provides the ultimate context for all the warnings in the surah: even the descriptions of Hell are a merciful call to avoid it. 
108 | Say, “What has been revealed to me is this: ‘Your God is only One God.’ Will you then submit?” | The Prophet is instructed to boil down his entire message to its absolute core and present it with crystal clarity. “Say” is a command to openly declare the essence of revelation. “What has been revealed to me is this” narrows the focus, preparing the listener for the fundamental truth that underlies everything else. That truth is the purest form of monotheism: “Your God is only One God.” The word “only” (innamā) confines divinity exclusively to Allah. There is no partner, no offspring, no associate. This is the same message brought by every prophet, the foundation of all faith. The verse then follows this declaration with the logical and emotional conclusion: “Will you then submit?” The Arabic word used is “tuslimūn,” which directly links to Islam—submission to the one God. The question is an invitation, a challenge, and a plea all at once. It appeals to the innate human disposition, which recognizes the truth of monotheism when it is presented without distortion. After all the lengthy arguments, the stories of the prophets, the descriptions of the universe, and the warnings of the Day of Judgment, the matter is brought to a single point: one God, so will you submit? This simplicity is the hallmark of the Islamic message. The verse teaches that the invitation to Islam should be direct and focused on Tawheed. It also implies that those who persist in polytheism after this clear declaration are not just mistaken but are consciously refusing to submit to a truth they can understand. For the believers, it is a reminder to keep their own submission pure, constantly renewing their commitment to the One God, and to use this clear, unambiguous language when calling others to the faith. 
109 | If they turn away, then say, “I have warned you all equally. I do not know if what you are threatened with is near or far. | The surah now addresses the scenario of rejection. After the clear call to submit to the One God, some will still turn away. The Prophet is instructed on what to say. First, he affirms that the warning has been delivered fairly and openly: “I have warned you all equally.” The word “equally” (ʿalā sawā’) indicates that the message has been given to everyone without discrimination, without hidden knowledge for a select few, and with complete transparency. No one can claim they were not informed or that the warning was unclear. The second part of the verse is a profound statement of prophetic humility and the limits of human knowledge: “I do not know if what you are threatened with is near or far.” The Prophet is commanded to explicitly disclaim any knowledge of the timing of the punishment. The pagans often demanded to know when the threatened torment would occur, seeking to disprove him if it was delayed. Here, the Prophet is told to openly acknowledge that the knowledge of the unseen, specifically the timing of the Hour and the punishment, belongs exclusively to Allah. This admission is a powerful refutation of any claim to divinity and a clear boundary between the Creator and the servant. It also adds urgency to the warning: since no one knows if the punishment is near, it is foolish to delay repentance. The verse teaches believers to be content with the limits of their knowledge, to deliver the message without distortion, and to leave the timing of outcomes to Allah. The fact that the punishment might be far is not a comfort; it is a test, as the next verses will explain. 
110 | Allah surely knows what you say openly and whatever you hide. | This verse reinforces the previous point by emphasizing the comprehensive knowledge of Allah, who knows both the public and the private. The pagans’ open mockery, their public challenges, and their verbal attacks are all known to Allah. But more importantly, their hidden whispers, their secret conspiracies, and the inner doubts and arrogance they conceal in their hearts are equally known. Nothing escapes His knowledge. This is a direct response to the secret conversations mentioned earlier in the surah (verse 3), where the evildoers whispered among themselves. It reminds them that while the Prophet may not know the hidden timing of the punishment, and may not be privy to their secret talks by his own power, his Lord knows everything. Their hidden schemes are laid bare before the divine gaze, and they will be held accountable for not only their open words but also their concealed intentions. The verse serves both as a comfort to the Prophet and a warning to the disbelievers. The Prophet is comforted because he does not need to uncover their secrets; Allah, who hears all, will deal with them justly. The disbelievers are warned that their internal state is fully exposed, so pretending to be open-minded while hiding deep-seated rejection is futile. For believers, this verse cultivates a profound sense of muraqabah, the awareness that Allah knows every thought that crosses the mind, every feeling in the heart. It encourages sincerity (ikhlas) and a deep cleansing of the inner self, knowing that one’s hidden state is as transparent to Allah as one’s outward speech. The verse seamlessly links to the next, which addresses the possible reasons for the delay of punishment. 
111 | I do not know if this ˹delay˺ is possibly a test for you and an enjoyment for a while.” | The Prophet is instructed to continue his declaration of limited knowledge, this time speculating on the possible wisdom behind the delay of punishment. He says, “I do not know if this delay is possibly a test for you.” The “delay” refers to the fact that the promised punishment has not yet arrived. Rather than being a sign of the message’s falsehood, as the pagans assumed, the delay could be a profound test from Allah. The test is to see how they will use the extra time granted to them: will they persist in disbelief and mockery, deepening their guilt, or will they reflect and repent? The delay, therefore, is an extension of the trial of life. The second possibility mentioned is “and an enjoyment for a while.” The delay also serves as a period of temporary enjoyment, allowing them to take pleasure in their worldly lives. This enjoyment is not a reward but a respite, a provision that will eventually run out, making the sudden arrival of the punishment all the more devastating. The phrase “for a while” underscores the temporary nature of this worldly life, no matter how long the delay lasts. This verse teaches an essential theological principle: the delay of punishment is not evidence of divine indifference or approval, nor is it a weakness of the messenger. It is a part of the divine plan, a period of either testing or temporary provision. The Prophet Muhammad’s open admission that he does not know the precise reason for the delay in a specific case is a model of intellectual humility. It demonstrates that even a prophet does not claim to know the details of Allah’s decree except what has been revealed to him. This closes the door on any who might later claim a leader has full knowledge of the unseen. The believers are taught to always see time, whether of ease or hardship, as a test and an opportunity that will soon end. 
112 | ˹In the end,˺ the Prophet said, “My Lord! Judge ˹between us˺ in truth. And our Lord is the Most Compassionate, Whose help is sought against what you claim.” | The surah concludes with a powerful supplication that the Prophet is instructed to make, and which serves as the final word on the entire dispute. The phrase “In the end, the Prophet said” signals the closing argument, the ultimate appeal to the highest court. The prayer is “My Lord! Judge between us in truth.” This is a request for a decisive, truthful judgment from Allah that will settle the conflict between the believers and the disbelievers once and for all. It is not a prayer for personal revenge but for the establishment of truth and justice. The word “in truth” (bil-ḥaqq) is pivotal; the Prophet seeks a verdict based on absolute reality, not on whims or appearances. The supplication acknowledges that ultimate judgment belongs to Allah alone. Then the prayer concludes with a beautiful invocation: “And our Lord is the Most Compassionate, Whose help is sought against what you claim.” The Prophet turns to the attribute of supreme mercy, even as he seeks judgment. This indicates that even in the face of false accusations and mockery, the heart of the believer rests in the boundless compassion of Allah. The phrase “against what you claim” refers to all the false allegations made by the pagans: that the Quran is poetry or sorcery, that Allah has partners, that there is no resurrection. The Prophet seeks help from the Most Compassionate against the harm and falsehood of these claims. The surah thus ends with a perfect balance: a call for truthful judgment and a refuge in divine compassion. It is a model for the believers in every age when faced with falsehood and slander: turn to Allah, ask for a judgment that will vindicate the truth, and seek shelter in His mercy, leaving the outcome to Him. The surah, which began with the heedlessness of people as their judgment draws near, ends with the Prophet entrusting the entire matter to the Judge, the Most Compassionate.
