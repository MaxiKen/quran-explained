1 | Alif-Lãm-Mĩm-Ra. These are the verses of the Book. What has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ from your Lord is the truth, but most people do not believe. | The surah opens with the separate letters Alif, Lām, Mīm, and Rā’. These disconnected letters appear at the beginning of a number of Quranic chapters, and while their precise meaning is known fully only to Allah, they serve to capture attention and to underscore the miraculous nature of the Book that follows. They are drawn from the very alphabet of the Arabs, yet no one can produce anything comparable to the revelation that comes after them. This serves as an immediate challenge to those who doubt. The phrase “These are the verses of the Book” identifies the revelations that follow as the clear signs of the Quran, a scripture full of guidance and wisdom. It is not an ordinary composition but the speech of the Almighty. Then the address turns directly to the Prophet, peace be upon him, assuring him that what has been sent down to him from his Lord is the truth. The source is emphasized: it is “from your Lord,” the One who creates, sustains, and commands. Therefore the message carries absolute certainty, untouched by falsehood. Yet despite this divine guarantee, the verse ends with a painful reality: “but most people do not believe.” This is not a statement about all people, but about the predominant human response. Many are held back by arrogance, heedlessness, or blind adherence to tradition from accepting what their very hearts recognize as true. The disjunction between the certainty of revelation and the widespread rejection of it becomes one of the central themes of the surah. The opening thereby prepares the listener for a series of arguments, signs, and parables that will address the root causes of disbelief, calling human beings to reflect on the world around them and on the message itself, so that they might move from doubt to conviction.
2 | It is Allah Who has raised the heavens without pillars—as you can see— then established Himself on the Throne. He has subjected the sun and the moon, each orbiting for an appointed term. He conducts the whole affair. He makes the signs clear so that you may be certain of the meeting with your Lord. | This verse directs our gaze upward to the overwhelming evidence of Allah’s power and wisdom. It declares that it is Allah Who raised the heavens high above the earth, and He did so without pillars that are visible to the eye. The words “as you can see” have been understood in two complementary ways. Some explain that there truly are no pillars at all, meaning the sky is held aloft purely by the command and might of the Creator without any physical support. Others note that the phrase means “as you can see, there are no visible pillars,” implying that there may be supports beyond human perception, but the point is that the heavens stand by Allah’s power, not by anything we can observe. After mentioning the raising of the heavens, the verse tells us that He then established Himself on the Throne in a manner that befits His majesty, a station of supreme authority over creation. He has also subjected the sun and the moon, making them run their courses with perfect consistency, each moving for an appointed term until the Day of Judgement. He conducts the entire affair of the universe: every decree, every event, every movement is under His management. All of these cosmic signs are made clear so that human beings may reach certainty about the meeting with their Lord. The orderly display of the heavens and the relentless precision of the celestial bodies are not random phenomena; they are deliberate signs pointing to the reality of resurrection and accountability. When a person reflects that the One who set these vast systems in motion with such effortless control is fully capable of bringing the dead back to life, the heart begins to accept what the prophets have taught about the Hereafter.
3 | And He is the One Who spread out the earth and placed firm mountains and rivers upon it, and created fruits of every kind in pairs. He covers the day with night. Surely in this are signs for those who reflect. | Turning from the sky to the earth, the verse continues the argument from creation. It is Allah Who spread out the earth, making it habitable and expansive so that living creatures could dwell upon it. Had it been left rugged and entirely impassable, life would not flourish. But by His mercy, He stretched it and prepared it as a resting place. Within that broad expanse, He placed firm mountains that stabilize the earth’s crust, preventing it from shaking violently with its inhabitants. This anchoring function of mountains is a mercy that we often take for granted. Alongside the mountains, He made rivers flow, carving pathways of fresh water that sustain life across vast regions. The water cycle and river systems are among the most tangible signs of a Provider who cares for His creation. Then the verse mentions that He created fruits of every kind in pairs. The word “pairs” here points to the male and female elements required for reproduction, but it also speaks of the duality and variety that characterize the plant world—sweet and sour, different colours, shapes, and flavours all emerging from the same soil. After this, the verse highlights the alternation of day and night: He covers the day with night, bringing rest after activity, darkness after light. All of these phenomena contain clear signs, but the verse specifies that they benefit only “those who reflect.” Reflection is not a casual glance; it is the deep, sustained thought that moves from the created thing to the Creator, recognizing His wisdom, power, and deliberate design. Such reflection naturally leads the heart toward faith and gratitude.
4 | And on the earth there are ˹different˺ neighbouring tracts, gardens of grapevines, ˹various˺ crops, palm trees—some stemming from the same root, others standing alone. They are all irrigated with the same water, yet We make some taste better than others. Surely in this are signs for those who understand. | The Quran now draws our attention to an even closer and more intimate sign: the variety on the earth itself. Within one piece of land, you find neighbouring tracts that are side by side yet completely different in their soil composition, fertility, and the types of plants they produce. One piece of ground may be rich and productive, while the plot right next to it is barren and dry, despite receiving the same rain and the same sunlight. The verse mentions gardens of grapevines, various crops, and palm trees. Some of these palm trees stem from a single root system and share a common trunk base, while others stand alone as separate trees. All of them are irrigated with the same water, yet the fruits they bear differ remarkably in taste, colour, aroma, and texture. One water source produces a grape that is intensely sweet and another that is tart; a date that is honey-like and another that is firm and mildly sweet. This diversity within unity is a profound sign. It demonstrates that the outcome does not depend merely on material inputs, because the water is one and the earth is adjacent. The decisive factor is the will and creative command of Allah, who gives each thing its distinct character and quality. This is why the verse concludes that these are signs “for those who understand.” Understanding here is more than sensory perception; it is the intellectual and spiritual insight that perceives the hand of the Maker in the variety and harmony of the natural world, and that draws from it the lesson that the One who can distinguish between plants with the same water can certainly distinguish between the obedient and the rebellious, and will bring them to separate ends.
5 | ˹Now,˺ if anything should amaze you ˹O Prophet˺, then it is their question: “When we are reduced to dust, will we really be raised as a new creation?” It is they who have disbelieved in their Lord. It is they who will have shackles around their necks. And it is they who will be the residents of the Fire. They will be there forever. | After the powerful display of signs in the natural world, the surah addresses the Prophet directly about a truly astonishing attitude. The opening clause is emphatic: if anything should cause amazement, it is not the signs of creation themselves but the question posed by the disbelievers. They ask, with mocking skepticism, whether when they have become dust and their bodies have completely decayed, they will really be brought back as a new creation. This question reveals a failure to grasp the very point of the earlier verses, for the One who brought them into existence from nothing the first time and who upholds the vast heavens and the intricate earth is certainly able to bring them back again. The verse does not leave their state ambiguous; it delivers three decisive statements about them. First, it is they who have disbelieved in their Lord. Their question is not an innocent inquiry but a symptom of a deeper rejection of His power and their accountability. Second, it is they who will have shackles around their necks. This is a vivid description of the punishment that awaits them, with chains binding them in humiliation, a fitting recompense for their refusal to be bound by the truth. Third, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire, and they will remain there forever. The triple repetition of “it is they” hammers home the certainty and the justice of this outcome, connecting their present disbelief to their future state. The verse serves as both a warning and a reassurance: a warning to those who mock the resurrection and a reassurance to the Prophet that the disbelief he encounters is truly irrational given the overwhelming evidence.
6 | They ask you ˹O Prophet˺ to hasten the torment rather than grace, though there have ˹already˺ been ˹many˺ torments before them. Surely your Lord is full of forgiveness for people, despite their wrongdoing, and your Lord is truly severe in punishment. | The previous verse exposed the disbelief that lies behind the mockery of resurrection. Here we see another dimension of that mockery. The disbelievers do not merely doubt the hereafter; they recklessly challenge it by asking the Prophet to bring on the punishment immediately. Instead of seeking the mercy and grace that Allah offers, they demand the torment be hastened, as if daring Allah to act. This is an attitude of insolence born of the illusion that the delay in punishment is a sign of weakness or that the threat is empty. The verse responds by reminding them that there have already been many exemplary torments before them. The destroyed nations of the past—the people of Noah, Hūd, Sālih, and others—are evidence that divine punishment is real and can descend when least expected. Yet even in the face of such provocation, the verse emphasizes two balancing attributes of the Lord. First, He is full of forgiveness for people despite their wrongdoing. His mercy is vast and His readiness to forgive is not cancelled by human sin, so long as the person turns back to Him. Second, He is truly severe in punishment. This severity is not arbitrary; it is the just response to persistent rebellion and mockery of His signs. The combination of these two attributes gives the full picture: no one should mistake Allah’s patience for powerlessness, and no one should despair of His mercy after having wronged themselves. The verse is both a warning to the mockers and an invitation to them to change their stance before the door of forgiveness closes and the torment they so foolishly demand actually arrives.
7 | The disbelievers say, “If only a sign could be sent down to him from his Lord.” You ˹O Prophet˺ are only a warner. And every people had a guide. | Even though the previous verses have laid out the cosmic and earthly signs in abundance, the disbelievers persist in their demands. They ask why no specific miraculous sign is sent down to the Prophet from his Lord, much as earlier nations asked their prophets for visible miracles tailored to their desires. Often this demand is not a genuine search for truth but a tactic of evasion, a way of setting conditions that they imagine will justify their continued disbelief. The verse instructs the Prophet about the nature of his mission: he is only a warner. His role is not to produce miracles on demand or to compel people to believe; it is to convey the message clearly and to warn of the consequences of turning away. The signs that matter most are already present in the revelation and in the creation around them. Then the verse makes a profound statement: “And every people had a guide.” This means that Allah, in His justice, never left any community without someone to guide them to the truth. The guide may be a prophet, a messenger, or the legacy of their teachings. In the context of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, he is that guide for his people, and his message is the final, universal guidance. The response, therefore, turns the demand for a sign back upon itself: the ultimate sign is the guide and the guidance itself. The presence of a warner and a clear scripture is the most valuable sign any people can receive, and those who reject it will have no excuse when they stand before their Lord.
8 | Allah knows what every female bears and what increases and decreases in the wombs. And with Him everything is determined with precision. | After clarifying the role of the Prophet, the surah moves to the perfect knowledge of Allah, which stands in stark contrast to human ignorance and the petty demands of the disbelievers. Allah knows what every female bears. This encompasses every pregnancy across all species, at every moment. He knows the exact moment of conception, the gender of the child, the number of offspring in the womb, and every detail of its development. Even before a mother knows she is pregnant, Allah’s knowledge has already encompassed the life forming within her. The verse continues: He also knows what increases and what decreases in the wombs. This refers to the entire process of gestation: the growth of the foetus, the periods of development, whether the pregnancy goes to full term or ends early, and every cellular change that occurs within the protective darkness of the womb. No human knowledge, no matter how advanced, can ever fully comprehend or monitor these processes with the exhaustive, simultaneous, and effortless knowledge that belongs to the Creator. Then the verse makes a crucial connection: “And with Him everything is determined with precision.” The intricate knowledge of the womb is not passive observation; it is tied to a precise measure and decree. Every stage, every characteristic, the full span of life, provision, and deeds—all of it is determined with exactness by the One whose knowledge covers all things. This serves as both a comfort to the believer and a warning to the denier. Nothing is random; all is under the meticulous control of the All-Knowing, so the reckoning and resurrection are entirely within His plan.
9 | ˹He is the˺ Knower of the seen and the unseen—the All-Great, Most Exalted. | Building upon the mention of Allah’s precise knowledge of what is hidden in the wombs, this verse expands the description of His knowledge to encompass everything. He is the Knower of the seen and the unseen. The “seen” refers to the entire observable world, what human senses can perceive, and what is apparent to creatures. The “unseen” includes all that is hidden from creation: the secrets of hearts, the events of the future, the realities of the heavens and the unseen realms, the world of the angels, and the exact moment of the Last Hour. No barrier divides the seen and the unseen for Allah; to Him, all is equally present and fully known. Then two majestic names are given: the All-Great and the Most Exalted. His greatness indicates the perfection of His attributes and the immensity of His being, far beyond anything that human minds can comprehend or language can capture. The name “Most Exalted” emphasizes His transcendence above all deficiency, similarity to creation, and the limits that constrain everything else. Together these names paint a picture of a Lord whose knowledge is absolute and whose greatness is unmatched, directly countering the false assumptions of those who worship idols that have no knowledge, no power, and no greatness. The believer who absorbs these two verses about Allah’s knowledge will find their heart filled with awe and also a deep sense of accountability. They know that nothing they hide can escape His sight and that the One before whom they stand is unimaginably great, so their worship and their conduct must reflect that realization.
10 | It is the same ˹to Him˺ whether any of you speaks secretly or openly, whether one hides in the darkness of night or goes about in broad daylight. | The all-encompassing knowledge of Allah is now made intensely personal. To Him, it is exactly the same whether a person speaks in secret or aloud, and whether someone conceals themselves in the darkness of night or walks openly in the light of day. Human beings rely on cover: we think that whispered words, closed doors, and the cover of darkness can hide our actions and intentions from others. But this verse demolishes that illusion. The one who speaks secretly, believing that his words are hidden, is as exposed before Allah as the one who shouts from the rooftops. Similarly, the one who moves under the veil of night is just as visible to Allah as the one who strides out under the noonday sun. The point is not merely that Allah sees and hears, but that the categories of “hidden” and “apparent” do not exist for Him. This has profound implications for a person’s spiritual state. It means that sincerity cannot be faked, because Allah knows the hidden intentions behind every public act. It means that secret sins are no safer than open sins, because the One who matters most witnesses them fully. It also means that private acts of devotion and secret charity are fully recorded and rewarded, because the Lord who sees in the dark sees them with the same clarity as what is done before crowds. This verse, therefore, calls the believer to cultivate a constant awareness that their entire life is lived in the open before Allah. It is both a deterrent from wrongdoing and a tremendous encouragement to do good when no one else is watching.
11 | For each one there are successive angels before and behind, protecting them by Allah’s command. Indeed, Allah would never change a people’s state ˹of favour˺ until they change their own state ˹of faith˺. And if it is Allah’s Will to torment a people, it can never be averted, nor can they find a protector other than Him. | From Allah’s perfect knowledge, the surah moves to His protection and His laws governing human societies. Every individual has a succession of angels appointed to them, angels who come in shifts, positioned before and behind. They guard and protect the person by Allah’s command, watching over them throughout the day and night, recording deeds and shielding them from many unseen harms until the decreed moment of death arrives. This assurance of angelic presence reminds the believer that they are never alone, and it also reinforces the reality that nothing escapes divine notice. Then the verse articulates a universal principle of tremendous importance. Allah would never change the condition of a people, meaning He would never strip away the blessings, security, honour, and well-being they enjoy, until they change what is within themselves. The change that triggers the loss of divine favour is primarily a change in faith, righteousness, and gratitude. When a people replace obedience with rebellion, justice with oppression, and gratitude with ingratitude, they initiate the process of their own decline. This principle is a warning and a call to self-reflection at both the individual and the communal level. However, the verse does not end there. It balances this with a reminder of divine sovereignty: if Allah wills to inflict harm on a people as a just punishment, nothing can repel it, and they will find no protector besides Him. Human effort cannot reverse a divine decree when the appointed time of recompense arrives. The verse thus combines hope, responsibility, and reverent fear in a single, tightly woven statement.
12 | He is the One Who shows you lightning, inspiring ˹you with˺ hope and fear, and produces heavy clouds. | After the foundational truths about knowledge and divine decree, the surah returns to physical signs that people witness directly. Allah is the One who shows humanity the lightning. The flash of lightning across the sky is a powerful and dramatic occurrence, and the verse draws attention to the psychological effect it produces: it inspires both hope and fear. On one hand, lightning often heralds the coming of rain, which revives the earth, fills the wells, and brings sustenance. The farmer, the traveller, and all living beings look to the sky with hope when they see lightning, expecting the mercy of life-giving water. On the other hand, lightning carries an inherent terror. Its sudden, brilliant flash can be blinding, and thunderbolts can strike and destroy. This dual response of hope and fear perfectly mirrors the believer’s attitude toward Allah. The believer lives between hope in His mercy and fear of His punishment, between love and awe. The lightning, then, becomes a natural parable for the human relationship with the divine. The verse also mentions that Allah produces the heavy clouds, laden with water. The word “heavy” captures the weight of the clouds that are driven by the winds, carrying tons of water suspended in the sky by a command that defies gravity. All of this points to a power that can bring life from a dead land, and by clear extension, can bring life to the dead on the Day of Resurrection. The thunder and lightning, which give this surah its name, are not random meteorological events but deliberate signs carrying a spiritual message for those who are receptive.
13 | The thunder glorifies His praises, as do the angels in awe of Him. He sends thunderbolts, striking with them whoever He wills. Yet they dispute about Allah. And He is tremendous in might. | The surah now reveals a deeper layer of meaning behind the thunder. The thunder is not merely a sound; it glorifies Allah with His praise. According to a narration from Ibn ‘Abbās and others, thunder is an angel tasked with driving the clouds, and the sound we hear is the voice of that angel glorifying the Lord. Another interpretation holds that the sound itself is a form of tasbīh, a declaration of Allah’s transcendence that all creation utters, even if we do not understand its language. Alongside the thunder, the angels themselves glorify Allah in awe of Him. Their glorification is not mechanical but filled with profound reverence and fear of His majesty. This image elevates the storm from a mere natural phenomenon to a symphony of worship that engulfs the heavens. Yet in the midst of this universal adoration, Allah sends thunderbolts, striking with them whomever He wills. These bolts are instruments of destruction for those who have earned His wrath, a demonstration that the same power that sustains can also annihilate. The verse then expresses a note of astonishment: despite all these signs, the disbelievers still dispute about Allah. They argue about His oneness, His power, and His right to be worshipped alone, while the very thunder proclaims His glory and the angels tremble before Him. The verse concludes with the affirmation that He is tremendous in might, a might that is irresistible in both its creative and its destructive aspects. This should cause every human being to reflect on their own stance before such a Lord.
14 | Calling upon Him ˹alone˺ is the truth. But those ˹idols˺ the pagans invoke besides Him ˹can˺ never respond to them in any way. ˹It is˺ just like someone who stretches out their hands to water, ˹asking it˺ to reach their mouths, but it can never do so. The calls of the disbelievers are only in vain. | The profound display of Allah’s power in the storm leads to a clear conclusion about worship. Calling upon Him alone is the truth, meaning that sincere supplication and devotion are exclusively His right, and He alone is capable of answering. Any prayer directed to other than Him is fundamentally false. The verse then exposes the utter helplessness of the idols that the pagans invoke besides Allah. These idols, whether they are statues, celestial bodies, or imagined beings, cannot respond in any way whatsoever. They do not hear, they possess no power, and they cannot bring benefit or ward off harm even for themselves. To illustrate this futility, the Quran presents a remarkable parable. The situation of the idolater is like a person who stretches out his two hands toward water, calling out for the water to reach his mouth. The water is an inanimate object; it cannot hear the call, it cannot move by itself, and it will never rise up to meet the man’s lips no matter how desperately he begs. Similarly, the idols are utterly unresponsive, and the one who prays to them is reaching out toward something that can never deliver. The verse seals the image with the statement that the calls of the disbelievers are only in vain, or literally, in error and misguidance. The word “only” emphasizes that there is no benefit, no partial answer, and no hope in such supplication. The lesson is as practical as it is theological: every human being must direct their deepest hopes, fears, and requests to the One who actually hears and has the power to respond.
15 | To Allah ˹alone˺ bow down ˹in submission˺ all those in the heavens and the earth—willingly or unwillingly—as do their shadows, morning and evening. | While the previous verse established that calling upon Allah alone is the truth, this verse expands the picture to a cosmic scale. To Allah alone bow down in submission all those who are in the heavens and the earth. This prostration is not limited to voluntary acts of worship. The verse makes a profound distinction: they bow down willingly or unwillingly. The believers, the angels, and those who recognize the truth submit willingly out of love, awe, and understanding. The disbelievers and the rebellious, on the other hand, submit unwillingly in the sense that they are subject to the universal laws that Allah has decreed. They cannot escape birth, death, the pull of gravity, the functioning of their own bodies, or the decrees that govern their lives. In their very physical existence they are prostrating to His command even while their hearts and tongues refuse. The verse then adds a beautiful and often overlooked detail: as do their shadows, morning and evening. The shadow of every object and every living being stretches out and contracts, moves and stills, in perfect compliance with the laws of light and the motion of the earth. Shadows cannot rebel; they shift from right to left, lengthen and shorten, solely by Allah’s order. The mention of morning and evening ties this universal submission to the daily rhythm of life. Every sunrise and every sunset witnesses the bowing of shadows, a silent, continuous testimony that all of creation is inherently oriented toward its Creator. The verse calls humans to align their voluntary worship with the involuntary worship of their own shadows.
16 | Ask ˹them, O Prophet˺, “Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth?” Say, “Allah!” Ask ˹them˺, “Why ˹then˺ have you taken besides Him lords who cannot even benefit or protect themselves?” Say, “Can the blind and the sighted be equal? Or can darkness and light be equal?” Or have they associated with Allah partners who ˹supposedly˺ produced a creation like His, leaving them confused between the two creations? Say, “Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Supreme.” | The surah now presents a powerful rational argument in the form of a dialogue. The Prophet is instructed to ask the idolaters a direct question: Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth? They could not answer with the names of their idols because deep down they acknowledged that Allah was the Creator and Sustainer of the universe. So the Prophet is told to answer on their behalf: Say, “Allah!” Having established this admission, the next question exposes the contradiction: Why then have they taken besides Him lords that cannot even benefit or protect their own selves? If the idols cannot manage their own affairs, how can they possibly manage the affairs of their worshippers? The logical flaw is laid bare. The verse then uses two powerful analogies. The blind and the sighted are not equal, and darkness and light are not equal. One who worships an idol is like a blind person stumbling in darkness, while the believer walks with sight in the light. The two states cannot be equated. The argument then shifts to another angle: have these false partners ever created anything like Allah’s creation, causing confusion between the two? The obvious answer is no. They have created nothing, so why are they worshipped alongside the true Creator? The verse concludes with a definitive declaration: Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Supreme. His oneness is unmatched, and His power over all creation is absolute. This verse trains the mind to think clearly, to reject contradiction, and to see that pure monotheism is the only intellectually and spiritually coherent position.
17 | He sends down rain from the sky, causing the valleys to flow, each according to its capacity. The currents then carry along rising foam, similar to the slag produced from metal that people melt in the fire for ornaments or tools. This is how Allah compares truth to falsehood. The ˹worthless˺ residue is then cast away, but what benefits people remains on the earth. This is how Allah sets forth parables. | This verse is one of the Quran’s most vivid parables, drawing from everyday phenomena to explain the nature of truth and falsehood. Allah sends down rain from the sky, and the water rushes into the valleys. Each valley flows according to its capacity: a wide, deep channel carries a great volume of water, while a narrow one carries less. The flowing water picks up foam and scum that rise to the surface and float along. This foam is temporary and insubstantial, soon to disappear. The verse then draws a parallel to the process of smelting metal. When people heat ore in the fire to make ornaments or tools, a similar slag or dross rises to the top, an impurity that is skimmed off and discarded. The pure metal remains and is what actually benefits people. Allah explicitly states that this is how He compares truth to falsehood. The rain and the pure metal represent the truth: it is substantial, lasting, and beneficial. The foam and the slag represent falsehood: it appears on the surface, sometimes swelling impressively, but it is ultimately worthless and is quickly swept away or discarded. When truth and falsehood clash, falsehood may froth and bubble and momentarily catch the eye, but it has no solid foundation. In the end, what benefits people remains on the earth—truth, justice, and righteousness survive, while falsehood vanishes. This parable comforts believers that the apparent strength of disbelief is merely foam on the surface of a mighty torrent of truth, and it assures them that persistence in the truth, even when it seems small, will yield lasting fruit.
18 | Those who respond to ˹the call of˺ their Lord will have the finest reward. As for those who do not respond to Him, even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over, they would certainly offer it to ransom themselves. They will face strict judgment, and Hell will be their home. What an evil place to rest! | The parable of truth and falsehood leads naturally to the consequences of accepting or rejecting the truth. The verse first describes the fate of those who respond to the call of their Lord. This response is not merely a verbal affirmation but a comprehensive obedience that follows from recognizing the truth depicted in the previous parable. For them is the finest reward, al-husnā, which encompasses all that is good and beautiful in the Hereafter, especially the vision and pleasure of Allah. Then the verse turns to the other side. Those who do not respond to Him, who turn away from the call of truth and follow the foam of falsehood, will face a terrifying reality. Even if they owned everything in the world twice over—all its treasures, wealth, and luxuries doubled—they would certainly offer it all as a ransom to save themselves from the punishment. This image is repeated throughout the Quran to stress that no amount of worldly wealth can buy escape from divine justice. The verse intensifies the warning: they will face a strict judgment, one in which every deed is examined with precision and no injustice is done. Their final abode will be Hell, and the verse ends with a chilling exclamation: “What an evil place to rest!” The word “rest” carries bitter irony, for Hell is a place of eternal torment without any true rest or peace. The verse thus completes the picture that the parable began: just as the foam is cast away, so the rejecters of truth will be cast into a punishment from which there is no ransom and no escape.
19 | Can the one who knows that your Lord’s revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ is the truth be like the one who is blind? None will be mindful ˹of this˺ except people of reason. | This verse sharpens the contrast between those who respond to the truth and those who do not by using the powerful metaphor of sight and blindness. It asks a rhetorical question: can the person who knows with certainty that what has been revealed to the Prophet from his Lord is the truth be compared to someone who is blind? The knowledge here is not mere information but a deep conviction that illuminates the heart and guides behaviour. The one who possesses this knowledge sees reality as it truly is: they recognize their Creator, understand the purpose of life, and walk a straight path. The blind one, by contrast, is spiritually blind. Despite having functioning eyes, they cannot perceive the light of revelation. They stumble through life, mistaking falsehood for truth and the transient for the eternal. The two are utterly unequal, just as a seeing person and a blind person cannot be equated in their ability to navigate the physical world. The verse then makes a crucial observation: none will be truly mindful of this difference except people of reason, ulū al-albāb. These are people who possess a core of sound intellect, unclouded by desires and arrogance. They use their reason to reflect on the signs and to connect the evidence of creation with the message of revelation. The verse is both a compliment to those who believe and an implicit call to those who hesitate. It urges every person to examine whether they are living with true sight or with a blindness that will lead them to the abyss described in the preceding verses. The following verses will now describe the qualities of these people of reason in beautiful detail.
20 | ˹They are˺ those who honour Allah’s covenant, never breaking the pledge; | Having mentioned the people of reason who take heed, the surah begins to describe them so that no one is left merely with an abstract concept. The first quality of these ulū al-albāb is that they honour Allah’s covenant and they never break the pledge. The covenant of Allah mentioned here encompasses the primordial covenant taken from the souls of all human beings when they testified that Allah is their Lord, as well as the covenant established through the sending of prophets and scriptures, which requires belief, obedience, and the worship of Allah alone without partners. To honour the covenant means to remain faithful to that original testimony and to the demands of the revealed guidance. The verse emphasizes the totality of their loyalty: they never break the pledge. The word “never” indicates a consistent pattern of life, not a sporadic or selective faithfulness. They do not break their promise to Allah when tested, nor do they abandon their religious duties when circumstances become difficult. This quality is foundational because all other virtues flow from a heart that is true to its covenant with its Creator. In a world where human agreements are often broken and commitments are treated lightly, these believers stand out as people whose word, once given to Allah and to others, is their bond. The mention of the covenant also reminds us that the relationship between Allah and the believer is not a vague sentiment but a defined, solemn pledge with responsibilities and, by Allah’s grace, a magnificent reward. The description of these people of reason will continue in the verses that follow, painting a complete portrait of the ideal believer.
21 | and those who maintain whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, stand in awe of their Lord, and fear strict judgment. | The description of the people of reason continues from the previous verse, where they were characterised as those who honour Allah’s covenant without breaking it. Now we learn that they maintain whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained. This refers first and foremost to the ties of kinship, the bonds of blood and family that Allah has commanded to be upheld. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that severing these ties leads to grave consequences, and he promised that whoever wishes to have their provision expanded and their life blessed should keep good relations with their relatives. But the ties go beyond family: they include all relationships of faith, community, and justice that Allah has commanded to be nurtured. The believers do not treat these bonds casually; they actively guard and strengthen them. Alongside this outward action, they possess an inward state: they stand in awe of their Lord. The Arabic word used here, khashyah, is a profound fear rooted in knowledge of Allah’s majesty and one’s own shortcomings. It is not a terror that makes one despair, but a reverential awe that keeps the heart humble and the limbs obedient. Furthermore, they fear a strict judgment, an evil reckoning. Even though they are righteous, they do not feel secure from the scrutiny of the Day of Resurrection. They live with a balanced consciousness, hoping in Allah’s mercy yet trembling at the thought of answering for their deeds. This internal fear fuels their external obedience and ensures that their maintenance of ties is not merely social formality but an act of sincere devotion. Thus the verse ties together social responsibility with deep spiritual awareness, showing that true piety integrates inner devotion with outward conduct, all flowing from a heart that never feels safe from accountability.
22 | And ˹they are˺ those who endure patiently, seeking their Lord’s pleasure, establish prayer, donate from what We have provided for them—secretly and openly—and respond to evil with good. It is they who will have the ultimate abode: | The portrait of the people of reason grows richer with each quality mentioned. Here they are described as those who endure patiently, and their patience is not the grudging tolerance of one who has no choice; it is a patience that actively seeks the face of their Lord and His pleasure. This transforms every trial, every act of self-restraint, and every perseverance in obedience into a form of worship laden with hope. Then the verse mentions the central pillar of their daily lives: they establish the prayer. The prayer is not merely performed occasionally but established with consistency, mindfulness, and in its proper manner, becoming the fixed point around which the rest of life revolves. Alongside prayer, they donate from what Allah has provided for them, spending their wealth in charity both secretly and openly. The mention of both modes indicates a wisdom that knows when discretion is better to protect sincerity and when public giving can encourage others. Then comes a quality that tests the limits of human nature: they respond to evil with good. When they are wronged, their response is not vengeance but forgiveness, kindness, and even active goodness toward the one who harmed them. This is one of the highest stations of character, and it requires immense spiritual strength. The Prophet exemplified this when he forgave the people of Ta’if who had pelted him with stones, praying for their guidance rather than their destruction. For all of these, the verse declares a magnificent promise: it is they who will have the ultimate abode. The word used, ‘uqbā ad-dār, signals the final, permanent home of peace and honour in the Hereafter, a reward that exceeds all imagination.
23 | the Gardens of Eternity, which they will enter along with the righteous among their parents, spouses, and descendants. And the angels will enter upon them from every gate, ˹saying,˺ | The ultimate abode is now named and described. It is the Gardens of Eternity, a place of perpetual residence where life does not end and joy does not fade. The believer will not enter this paradise alone. The verse contains a deeply comforting promise: they will be joined by the righteous among their parents, their spouses, and their descendants. This shows that faith and good deeds create bonds that transcend this world, reuniting families in a perfect state where no grief or separation can touch them. A person who strove for righteousness while yearning for their loved ones to be guided will see their hope fulfilled in the most beautiful way. This promise was profoundly moving to the companions, for it meant that the separation caused by death was temporary and that the family unit, purified of all worldly strains, would be restored in eternal bliss. Moreover, the angels will enter upon them from every gate. The image is one of honour and ceaseless welcome. The angels do not merely pass by; they come to the believers as honoured guests would be received, appearing from every entrance of their lofty dwellings. Their entry signals safety, divine acceptance, and the beginning of a dialogue of peace. The atmosphere is one of sublime serenity where the saved believer is surrounded by family, embraced by angelic beings, and immersed in the continuous manifestation of Allah’s mercy. This verse gives the believer a vivid picture to hold onto during the trials of this world, transforming the abstract hope of paradise into a warm, personal, and relational reality.
24 | “Peace be upon you for your perseverance. How excellent is the ultimate abode!” | The angels’ greeting is recorded here, and every word of it carries meaning. “Peace be upon you” is the salutation of salam, a word that denotes safety, security, and complete well-being. When it comes from the angels on behalf of the Most Merciful, it is the final assurance that all danger, all fear, and all grief have passed forever. The reason given for this peace is “for your perseverance.” This tells us that the patient endurance the believers displayed in the world—the sabr that the earlier verse mentioned—is the direct cause of this honour. Every difficulty they bore, every temptation they resisted, every act of obedience they persisted in, and every wrong they forgave has been noted and now repaid with eternal peace. The angels then exclaim, “How excellent is the ultimate abode!” This is not a question but a declaration of admiration. They marvel at the splendour Allah has prepared for the believers. The exclamation also serves to heighten the joy of the inhabitants, confirming through the testimony of the angels themselves that this reward is truly magnificent. The verse, therefore, links the struggle of this life with the rest of the next in a single, seamless chain: perseverance now leads to peace then. For a believer facing hardship, this verse is a balm. It assures them that their pain is not unnoticed, their patience is not wasted, and their final home will be one about which even the angels, who have witnessed the grandeur of the heavens, will exclaim in wonder. This is the tremendous outcome of being among the people of reason.
25 | And those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed, break whatever ˹ties˺ Allah has ordered to be maintained, and spread corruption in the land—it is they who will be condemned and will have the worst abode. | After the luminous description of the people of reason and their reward, the surah now presents the contrast. The balance of the Quran always places light against darkness so that the path is unmistakably clear. Those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed are the direct opposite of the ones who honoured the covenant. They made a pledge of faith and obedience, either through their innate nature or through their verbal acceptance of the message, yet they broke it deliberately. This breach is not a moment of weakness but a settled pattern of treachery. They also break whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained, severing the bonds of kinship and community that the righteous work so hard to preserve. Their hearts are hard, and their relationships are marked by division, selfishness, and disregard for divine command. Moreover, they spread corruption in the land. This corruption takes many forms: it includes moral decay, injustice, violence, and leading others away from the path of Allah. Their very presence is a source of harm rather than benefit. The result for such people is stark and twofold. First, they will be condemned; the curse and alienation from Allah’s mercy fall upon them. Second, they will have the worst abode. Just as the righteous received the best home, these will receive the most terrible dwelling, which is the Fire of Hell. The symmetry is deliberate: faithful maintenance of covenants and ties leads to the finest reward; violation and corruption lead to the worst punishment. The verse is a sobering warning that human choices in this life shape the eternal dwelling in the next with absolute justice.
26 | Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills. And the disbelievers become prideful of ˹the pleasures of˺ this worldly life. But the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment. | The surah now turns to one of the greatest tests that lead people to violate their covenant: wealth and worldly provision. Allah gives abundant sustenance to some and limited provision to others. This distribution is not arbitrary but based on His perfect wisdom, testing some with wealth to see if they are grateful and others with poverty to see if they are patient. The problem arises when the disbelievers become prideful and exult in the pleasures of this worldly life, thinking that their wealth is a sign of divine favour or their own power, and failing to see it as a temporary test. Their hearts become attached to what is fleeting, and they mock those who have been given less. The verse then delivers the divine assessment that recalibrates all values: the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment. The Arabic term used, matā‘, describes a small provision taken on a journey, something quickly consumed and left behind. No matter how vast one’s worldly riches, they are insignificant when measured against the eternal duration of the afterlife. If the entire world were a single meal, the Hereafter would be an endless feast; the comparison is not even close. This perspective liberates the believer from envy and despair. The wealthy believer sees their wealth as a trust to be spent for the Hereafter, and the poor believer understands that their true wealth is yet to come. The verse strips the glamour from worldly life and invites every soul to invest in what lasts.
27 | The disbelievers say, “If only a sign could be sent down to him from his Lord.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Indeed, Allah leaves to stray whoever He wills, and guides to Himself whoever turns to Him— | The demand for a tangible miracle surfaces again, echoing verse 7. The disbelievers persist in their request for a special sign, believing that such a spectacle would compel them to believe. They fail to see that the entire Quran is an enduring intellectual and spiritual sign, and the universe around them is full of wonders. Allah instructs the Prophet to answer with a profound theological truth that addresses the root of guidance and misguidance. He is to say that Allah leaves to stray whoever He wills, and He guides to Himself whoever turns to Him. This statement upholds divine sovereignty while simultaneously affirming human responsibility. The one who is left astray is the one who has chosen to turn away, who has no sincere will to find the truth, and Allah, knowing this state, seals that person in their chosen misguidance. As for the one who “turns to Him,” who repents, seeks truth earnestly, and directs their heart toward Allah, Allah guides that person to Himself. The word “turns” translates anāba, which implies a continuous returning, a reorientation of the whole being. The answer thus reframes the issue: the problem is not a lack of signs but a lack of the sincere turning that opens the heart to the signs already given. The miracle of guidance is not in mountains moving but in a heart that turns and is met by divine grace. This verse protects the believer from the false idea that guidance is arbitrary, while also preventing arrogance, because guidance remains ultimately in Allah’s hands and is given to those who humbly seek it.
28 | those who believe and whose hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort. | The description of those who are guided continues. They are the ones who believe, and their belief is not a cold, intellectual assent but a living faith that has settled deep into their hearts. The consequence of this faith is that their hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. The remembrance, dhikr, encompasses the recitation of the Quran, the pronouncement of His names and praises, prayer, and the conscious awareness of His presence throughout the day. When the heart is agitated by fears, anxieties, grief, or the turbulence of life, it is the remembrance of Allah that brings it back to a state of stillness and peace. This is not merely a psychological suggestion but a spiritual law: the heart is created to know and love its Creator, and when it turns to what it was made for, it finds its missing piece. The verse then emphasizes this truth with an independent, emphatic statement: Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort. The Arabic construction conveys absolute certainty. No heart, no matter how troubled, is beyond the reach of this tranquillity if it sincerely engages in dhikr. The Prophet, peace be upon him, exemplified this when he would tell Bilal to call the prayer so that they could find rest in it. He lived in constant remembrance, and his heart was the most tranquil of all, even amidst the greatest trials. This verse offers a direct remedy to modern anxiety and spiritual emptiness. When people chase comfort in entertainment, substances, or material gain, they find only temporary distraction. The lasting comfort, the true sakīnah, descends only through the remembrance of the One who holds all hearts in His hand.
29 | Those who believe and do good, for them will be bliss and an honourable destination. | This short, powerful verse serves as the summary and the promise for the guided ones described in the previous two verses. Those who combine sincere belief with righteous deeds—the two inseparable pillars of salvation—are given a two-part reward. The first part is tūbā, translated as bliss. This Arabic word carries connotations of goodness, joy, and a blessed state that is both comprehensive and enduring. Some scholars have interpreted it as a tree in Paradise, or the specific delight and congratulations the believers receive. Whatever its exact nature, it represents the pinnacle of happiness and divine approval. The second part is an honourable destination, a beautiful place of return. This destination is Paradise itself, but the phrasing emphasizes not just the place but the honour with which they are received there. They do not creep into forgiveness; they are welcomed with dignity, their faces bright, their records clean, and their status elevated. The linking of belief and good deeds throughout these verses reinforces that faith that does not translate into action is incomplete, and actions without faith lack foundation. The beauty of this verse lies in its simplicity. It offers a compact, memorable formula for the ultimate success. Every believer can hold onto it: if I truly believe and strive to do good, then tūbā and a beautiful return await me. The promise is immediate enough to provide hope in this life and grand enough to sustain the soul through any hardship. It is a verse that the companions would have cherished, and it serves as a constant reminder that our small, sincere efforts are seen, valued, and will be rewarded beyond measure.
30 | And so We have sent you ˹O Prophet˺ to a community, like ˹We did with˺ earlier communities, so that you may recite to them what We have revealed to you. Yet they deny the Most Compassionate. Say, “He is my Lord! There is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Him. In Him I put my trust, and to Him I turn ˹in repentance˺.” | This verse turns directly to the Prophet, peace be upon him, to clarify his mission and the painful response he receives. Allah tells him that he has been sent to a community in the same pattern as earlier communities received their messengers. There is a long chain of prophethood, and Muhammad is its final link, sent with the same essential call to a people who need guidance. His task is to recite to them what has been revealed to him, so the Quran is the centerpiece of his mission. The response, however, is staggering: they deny the Most Compassionate. The disbelievers of Makkah were known to reject even the name “Ar-Rahmān,” the Most Compassionate, as they did not want to acknowledge this attribute of Allah. They preferred their tribal idols. Their denial of the Most Compassionate was a denial of the very source of mercy and guidance. The Prophet is then instructed to declare his own firm stand: “He is my Lord! There is no god worthy of worship except Him.” He is to affirm pure monotheism regardless of their rejection. Then he adds, “In Him I put my trust, and to Him I turn in repentance.” This is a profound model for every caller to truth. When people reject the message, the response is not frustration or compromise but a renewed personal attachment to Allah through trust and repentance. Tawakkul, or trust, means handing over all affairs to Him, and inābah, or turning in repentance, means constantly returning to Him with devotion, seeking His help, and purifying one’s own state. The verse teaches that the rejection of others should drive the believer closer to Allah, not further into despair. The Prophet’s mission is secure because his trust is in the Lord of the universe.
31 | If there were a recitation that could cause mountains to move, or the earth to split, or the dead to speak, ˹it would have been this Quran˺. But all matters are by Allah’s Will. Have the believers not yet realized that had Allah willed, He could have guided all of humanity? And disasters will continue to afflict the disbelievers or strike close to their homes for their misdeeds, until Allah’s promise comes to pass. Surely Allah never fails in His promise. | The disbelievers’ demand for spectacular signs is met with this powerful statement. The Quran itself possesses such immense spiritual power that, were any recitation capable of causing mountains to move, splitting the earth, or making the dead speak, it would be this very Quran. However, the manifestation of such cataclysmic signs is not the purpose of the revelation; its primary purpose is to guide hearts. The mountains will indeed be moved on the Day of Judgement, but here, the verse emphasizes that the transformative power of the Quran is moral and spiritual, not a tool for human spectacle. Then the verse delivers a humbling question: have the believers not yet realized that if Allah had willed, He could have guided all of humanity? This comforted the early Muslims who wondered why so many refused to believe. Forced universal guidance is not the divine plan. Instead, humans are given free will, and the truth is made clear so that belief is a meaningful choice. The verse then shifts to a warning about the immediate consequences of disbelief: disasters will continue to afflict the disbelievers or strike close to their homes as a recompense for their deeds. This refers to the ongoing trials, defeats, and calamities that serve as reminders and punishments in this life, all leading up to the ultimate promise of the Hereafter. The verse concludes by affirming that Allah never fails in His promise. The delay is not a cancellation; every threat and every promise will come to pass at its appointed time. The believer is thus taught patience, while the disbeliever is warned that the apparent respite is a test, not an escape.
32 | Other messengers had already been ridiculed before you, but I delayed the disbelievers ˹for a while˺ then seized them. And how ˹horrible˺ was My punishment! | To comfort the Prophet, peace be upon him, in the face of the mockery mentioned throughout the surah, this verse reveals that the pattern of ridicule is not new. Other messengers before him were similarly mocked by their people. The path of prophethood is paved with rejection, sarcasm, and hostility. But Allah did not leave those mockers unpunished indefinitely. He delayed them for a while, granting them time to repent, to reflect, and to exhaust their excuses. This respite is a manifestation of divine patience and mercy, but it is also a test. When they persisted in their arrogance, the seizure came. The Arabic word “akhadhtuhum,” translated as “seized them,” conveys a sudden, firm, and inescapable grip. The punishment descended, and the mockery ceased. The verse then poses an exclamatory question: “And how horrible was My punishment!” This is not a question seeking information but a rhetorical emphasis on the severity of what befell those nations. It serves as a warning to the mockers among the Quraysh that the same divine law applies to them. The delay they enjoy is not evidence of safety but an opportunity. For the Prophet and the believers, this verse provides immense solace. It reminds them that they are walking a well-trodden path of honour, that Allah’s justice is certain, and that the laughter of the disbelievers will turn to anguish. The believer is not to be shaken by mockery but to recall the fate of the ancient peoples and trust that the Lord who avenged His earlier messengers will uphold His final messenger and his followers.
33 | Is ˹there any equal to˺ the Vigilant One Who knows what every soul commits? Yet the pagans have associated others with Allah ˹in worship˺. Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Name them! Or do you ˹mean to˺ inform Him of something He does not know on the earth? Or are these ˹gods˺ just empty words?” In fact, the disbelievers’ falsehood has been made so appealing to them that they have been turned away from the Path. And whoever Allah leaves to stray will be left with no guide. | The argument returns to the core of monotheism by challenging the very notion of associating partners with Allah. The verse begins with a rhetorical question: Is there any equal to the Vigilant One Who knows what every soul commits? The implied answer is a resounding no. Allah is al-Qayyūm, the Self-Subsisting Sustainer, who watches over every soul and records every deed. No idol, no false god, possesses even a fraction of this knowledge or vigilance. Yet despite this clear truth, the pagans have set up partners with Allah. The Prophet is instructed to demand: “Name them!” Let them identify these gods, describe their attributes, their powers, their lineage. They cannot, because they are empty figments of imagination. The next question is razor-sharp: “Or do you mean to inform Him of something He does not know on the earth?” This is a devastating irony. Are they claiming to know of partners that Allah Himself is unaware of? It exposes the absurdity of their position. Then the third option: “Or are these gods just empty words?” They are names without reality, sounds with no substance, a hollow claim with no truth behind it. The verse then explains the psychological state that makes such irrationality possible: the disbelievers’ falsehood has been made so appealing to them that they have been turned away from the Path. Their desires and the delusions of Satan have beautified their shirk to the point where they cannot see its emptiness. The verse ends with a sobering truth: whoever Allah leaves to stray will have no guide. When a person persistently chooses misguidance, Allah seals that choice, and no one can bring them back. This underscores the urgency of turning to the truth while the door is open.
34 | For them is punishment in this worldly life, but the punishment of the Hereafter is truly far worse. And none can shield them from Allah. | After detailing the misguidance of the idolaters, this verse pronounces their sentence. It states that for them there is a punishment in this worldly life. This refers to the various calamities, defeats, humiliations, and afflictions that befall the stubborn disbelievers as a consequence of their rebellion. For the Quraysh, this included the devastating defeat at Badr and other trials. However, the punishment of this world, no matter how severe, is only a foretaste. The verse then declares that the punishment of the Hereafter is truly far worse. The Arabic phrasing emphasizes the magnitude of the difference. Hellfire is incomparably more intense, more lasting, and more comprehensive in its suffering than any worldly pain. The two punishments are not in the same category. This should shake any rational person into reconsidering their path, for enduring a temporary difficulty or giving up a sinful pleasure is trivial compared to eternal torment. The verse concludes with the chilling statement: “And none can shield them from Allah.” No protector, no intercessor that they falsely imagined would save them, no wealth, no social status, and no power will be able to defend them when the divine punishment arrives. The absolute helplessness of the disbelievers on that Day is a recurring Quranic theme, and it strips away all the false confidence they had in this life. The believer reads this and feels both a healthy fear that drives them to obedience and a deep gratitude that Allah has saved them from such a fate by guiding them to faith. It is a verse that cuts through complacency.
35 | The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that under it rivers flow; eternal is its fruit as well as its shade. That is the ˹ultimate˺ outcome for the righteous. But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire! | The surah now provides a summary description of Paradise that balances the warning of the previous verse. The Paradise that is promised to the righteous, those who embody the qualities of taqwa, is a place of abundance and permanence. Its most famous feature is that rivers flow beneath its gardens and dwellings. Water in the arid landscape of Arabia was the symbol of life, luxury, and refreshment, and the idea of eternal, flowing rivers captures the heart’s deepest longing for peace and provision. The verse then highlights two specific elements: its fruit is eternal, and its shade is likewise everlasting. In this world, fruits are seasonal, they spoil, and they eventually cease. Shade shifts with the sun, and trees lose their leaves. In Paradise, there is no autumn, no decay, and no deprivation. The blessings are constant and unchanging, yet always enjoyable. This is the ultimate outcome for the righteous, the final destination that their faith and deeds have earned them by Allah’s mercy. The verse then presents the stark contrast in a single, terse sentence: “But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire!” The abruptness of this statement after the flowing description of Paradise creates a powerful emotional jolt. It is a pattern used throughout the Quran: Paradise is painted in alluring detail, and then the alternative is named with terrifying brevity, as if to say that the mere mention of the Fire should be enough to make any soul flee from it. The verse leaves the listener with a clear choice and an urgent incentive to be among the righteous.
36 | ˹The believers among˺ those who were given the Scripture rejoice at what has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺, while some ˹disbelieving˺ factions deny a portion of it. Say, “I have only been commanded to worship Allah, associating none with Him ˹in worship˺. To Him I invite ˹all˺, and to Him is my return.” | The surah now addresses the reception of the Quran among the People of the Book. Among those who were given the Scripture in previous generations, there are believers who truly recognize the truth. When the Quran was revealed, they rejoiced because they found in it the confirmation of what was in their own scriptures and the culmination of the prophetic promise. This includes individuals like ‘Abdullāh ibn Salām, a Jewish scholar who embraced Islam, and the Christian delegation that recognized the truth when they heard the Quran. Their joy is a sign for the skeptics. However, the verse also notes that some factions among them deny a portion of the revelation. They accept parts that align with their desires and reject others, especially those verses that clearly affirm the prophethood of Muhammad and the purity of monotheism. The Prophet is then instructed to declare his simple, unwavering mission: “I have only been commanded to worship Allah, associating none with Him. To Him I invite, and to Him is my return.” This declaration leaves no room for compromise or hidden motives. His message is purely the worship of the One God, and his ultimate accountability is to Allah alone. The verse teaches that the truth of the Quran is attested to by the sincere among previous religious communities, and it calls the Prophet to stand firm on this clear foundation, unaffected by the selective acceptance or rejection of any group. The final words, “to Him is my return,” reflect the eschatological perspective that governs all his actions.
37 | And so We have revealed it as an authority in Arabic. And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or shield you from Allah. | This verse addresses the nature of the Quran itself and the seriousness of adhering to it. Allah has revealed the Quran as an authority, a decisive criterion that separates truth from falsehood, and He has revealed it in clear Arabic so that its first audience can understand it directly and grasp its profound meanings. The Arabic language was chosen as the vehicle for the final revelation, not out of ethnic preference, but so that the message could penetrate hearts without linguistic barriers for its initial recipients, and so that its eloquence could stand as a lasting miracle. However, with this great gift comes a strict warning. The Prophet is told that if he were to follow the desires of the disbelievers after the knowledge that has come to him—knowledge of pure monotheism, of the divine law, and of the truth—then he would find no protector or shield against Allah. While the address is to the Prophet, the warning extends to the entire community. No one, no matter how elevated their status, is exempt from the consequences of compromising the truth. The verse reinforces the absolute integrity of the revelation. It cannot be bent to please human whims. The Arabic Quran is an immovable standard. This also serves as a source of strength for the believers: they possess a revelation whose authority is not derived from human approval but from divine origin. They are not to dilute it to gain acceptance. The warning is severe precisely because the trust is immense, and it teaches that true security lies not in aligning with the powerful or the majority, but in steadfastly holding onto the revealed knowledge, no matter the pressure.
38 | We have certainly sent messengers before you ˹O Prophet˺ and blessed them with wives and offspring. It was not for any messenger to bring a sign without Allah’s permission. Every destined matter has a ˹set˺ time. | This verse serves multiple purposes. First, it consoles the Prophet by reminding him that the messengers before him were also fully human. Allah blessed them with wives and children. They lived normal human lives, married, had families, and faced the same human needs and relationships. This counters any argument that a prophet should be an angel or a supernatural being untouched by ordinary life. The Prophet’s humanity, including his marriage and offspring, is consistent with the pattern of all previous messengers. Second, the verse repeats a key principle: no messenger had the authority to bring a sign independently. Signs come only by Allah’s permission. This addresses the repeated demand of the disbelievers for miracles, telling them that the Prophet does not control the extraordinary; his task is to convey the message, and Allah sends signs according to His wisdom. Third, the verse grounds everything in divine decree: “Every destined matter has a set time.” The word “ajal” refers to an appointed term. Every event, every manifestation of punishment or victory, the rise and fall of nations, and the lifespan of every individual and every scripture’s dominance, all run according to a timeline established by Allah. The believers are being taught patience; the delay in seeing the full triumph of truth is not neglect but precise timing. For the disbelievers, it is a warning that their respite is measured, and when their term arrives, they cannot delay it by a single moment. The verse connects the life of the Prophet to the broad fabric of prophetic history and divine timing, giving his mission a cosmic context.
39 | Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills, and with Him is the Master Record. | This verse expands on the idea of divine decree mentioned in the previous verse. Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills. This refers to His absolute authority over the decrees, laws, and events that unfold. He can abrogate previous rulings, change circumstances, erase a punishment that was decreed due to a person’s repentance, or confirm a blessing that was promised. The scholars have understood this verse in multiple valid ways. Some relate it to the abrogation of certain laws within the scriptures; Allah may replace one commandment with another according to His wisdom. Others view it in the context of the Preserved Tablet, where the angels record the decrees. There are matters that are conditional, such as a person’s lifespan being extended by maintaining family ties or a provision being increased by charity. Allah eliminates or confirms these conditional matters based on human actions, while the ultimate, unchangeable decree resides with Him in the Master Record, the Umm al-Kitāb. This concept should not be misunderstood as Allah changing His mind, which is impossible for the All-Knowing. Rather, it means that within His eternal knowledge, certain outcomes are contingent upon actions, and He brings those to pass in real time. The verse inspires both hope and fear. A sinner should never despair, because sincere repentance may eliminate a decreed punishment. A righteous person should remain humble and not feel secure, understanding that ultimate outcomes are written with Allah. The verse deepens the believer’s trust in divine wisdom while emphasizing that the final, unalterable source of all decrees is the Master Record with Allah, above all change and beyond full human comprehension.
40 | Whether We show you ˹O Prophet˺ some of what We threaten them with, or cause you to die ˹before that˺, your duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. Judgment is for Us. | This verse provides profound relief and clarity regarding the Prophet’s responsibility. The threats of punishment that have been repeatedly mentioned in the surah may or may not be witnessed by the Prophet during his lifetime. The verse presents two possibilities: either Allah will show him some of the punishment He has promised the disbelievers during his life—as indeed happened with the defeat of the Quraysh at Badr and other victories—or Allah will cause the Prophet to die before that punishment fully materializes. In either case, the Prophet’s duty does not change. His task is solely the delivery of the message, the clear and complete conveyance of the revelation. He is not responsible for the results, nor is he required to witness the final judgment. The word “balāgh” denotes a communication that reaches its target, and the Prophet fulfilled this to perfection. The verse then declares, “Judgment is for Us.” The accountability of people, the punishment of the rejecters, and the final reckoning belong entirely to Allah. This absolves the Prophet of the psychological burden of having to see every denier punished in his lifetime. It also teaches the believers that their duty mirrors this: they must convey the truth with wisdom and kindness, but they cannot force outcomes. The ultimate judgment of hearts and the execution of justice are reserved for the Lord of the worlds. This verse, therefore, is a foundation for a balanced understanding of activism in faith: do your part with excellence, then leave the rest to Allah with full trust. It frees the soul from despair over delayed justice and from the temptation to take matters into its own hands.
41 | Do they not see that We gradually reduce their land from its borders? Allah decides—none can reverse His decision. And He is swift in reckoning. | After assuring the Prophet that his duty is only to deliver the message and that judgment belongs to Allah, the surah now directs a sharp question to the disbelievers. “Do they not see that We gradually reduce their land from its borders?” This question is a call to observation and reflection. The phrase “gradually reduce their land” has been interpreted by many companions and early scholars, including Ibn ‘Abbās, as referring to the progressive military and political losses suffered by the disbelievers. In the immediate context, it pointed to the gradual expansion of the Muslim community and the shrinking of the pagan strongholds around Makkah. Territory after territory, tribe after tribe, came under the authority of Islam, and the power base of the Quraysh was being steadily eroded. This was not a sudden collapse but a slow, unmistakable process that they could witness if they only paid attention. Others have broadened the meaning to include the general principle that when a people persist in wrongdoing, Allah diminishes their dominion, their influence, and their resources step by step, often in ways they do not immediately perceive, until their strength is gone. The verse then declares a fundamental truth: “Allah decides—none can reverse His decision.” Once His decree of decline or punishment is set in motion, no power on earth can repel it or overturn it. All the alliances, wealth, and strategies of the disbelievers cannot undo what Allah has willed. Finally, the verse concludes with the warning that He is swift in reckoning. His accounting is not slow in the sense of being delayed beyond its appointed time; when it comes, it is executed swiftly and decisively. The disbelievers should not mistake the gradual nature of the process for weakness or inaction, for the final settlement will come suddenly. This verse thus serves as both a reassurance to the believers that the tide is turning and a final caution to those who still oppose the truth to read the signs of the times before it is too late.
42 | Those ˹disbelievers˺ before them ˹secretly˺ planned, but Allah has the ultimate plan. He knows what every soul commits. And the disbelievers will soon know who will have the ultimate outcome. | Building on the warning of the previous verse, this verse places the plotting of the Quraysh into the larger context of history. Those who came before them also secretly planned and schemed against their prophets and their messages. They plotted in the darkness, thinking their conspiracies were hidden and their strategies were foolproof. But the verse delivers the crushing counter-statement: “but Allah has the ultimate plan.” The Arabic word “makr” used for planning often carries a connotation of secret, cunning plotting. When attributed to the disbelievers, it is a negative scheming. When attributed to Allah, it denotes His perfect, hidden counter-plan that nullifies their schemes and brings about justice in a way they never anticipated. Allah’s plan does not need to be devised in reaction; it is the eternal, all-encompassing decree that already surrounds and overrides every human plot. The disbelievers might think they are acting independently, but in reality, their every move is known and is woven into a larger tapestry of divine justice that leads to their own undoing. The verse then reinforces the completeness of divine knowledge: “He knows what every soul commits.” Every secret meeting, every whispered conspiracy, every hidden intention is fully open before Him. Nothing escapes His record, and nothing is omitted from the reckoning. The verse ends with a powerful and ominous promise: “And the disbelievers will soon know who will have the ultimate outcome.” The Arabic phrase “‘uqbā ad-dār,” the ultimate outcome or the final home, points directly to the Hereafter. The disbelievers assume that worldly victory is the measure of truth, but they will soon discover, at the moment of death and on the Day of Judgment, who truly secures the best final destination. For the believers, this verse is an immense comfort. They may be plotted against, but they are under the umbrella of the ultimate Plan, and their outcome is secure. For the plotters, it is a terrifying thought that their most hidden schemes are not only known but are being used against them.
43 | The disbelievers say, “You ˹Muḥammad˺ are no messenger.” Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Allah is sufficient as a Witness between me and you, as is whoever has knowledge of the Scripture.” | The surah reaches its final verse by confronting the ultimate denial head-on. The disbelievers of Makkah explicitly declare to the Prophet that he is not a messenger. They have seen his character, heard the Quran, and witnessed the signs, yet they make this categorical rejection. This statement is the culmination of all the arguments and demands that came before. The Prophet is instructed not to engage in a futile argument or to become distressed, but to respond with dignified reliance on the highest authority. He is to say, “Allah is sufficient as a Witness between me and you.” The testimony of Allah is enough. Allah Himself bears witness to the truth of the Prophet’s mission through the miraculous nature of the Quran, the clear signs, and the undeniable transformation that the message brings. If the disbelievers reject him despite Allah’s witness, then the matter is settled; their denial does not alter reality. The Prophet’s conscience is clear because the Creator of the universe is his witness. Then the verse adds a second witness: “as is whoever has knowledge of the Scripture.” This refers to those among the People of the Book who recognized the truth of the Prophet’s message because they found his description in the Torah and the Gospel. Their knowledge bore witness, and their acceptance was a testimony against the pagan Arabs. It may also include any person of sincere knowledge who studies the Quran and acknowledges its divine origin. By invoking these two witnesses—the divine and the learned among previous scripture—the verse establishes an unshakable foundation for the truth. The surah that began with the separate letters and the declaration that the Quran is the truth now ends with the affirmation that Allah’s witness and the testimony of knowledgeable believers are sufficient. The Prophet is vindicated, and the deniers are left with the weight of their own rejection. This verse teaches believers to anchor their confidence in divine validation rather than in human acceptance, and to trust that the truth will always have witnesses among those endowed with knowledge and sincerity.
