1 | By the fig and the olive ˹of Jerusalem˺, | The surah opens with an oath of deep symbolic and geographical significance: “By the fig and the olive of Jerusalem.” The fig and the olive are not merely fruits; they are ancient, blessed trees that have sustained human life for millennia. The fig is a sweet, seed-filled fruit that spoils quickly, while the olive yields a long-lasting, pure oil that provides light, nourishment, and healing. Together they represent the richness of divine provision. But the oath reaches far beyond botany. The translation explicitly adds “of Jerusalem,” because the land where these trees famously flourish—the hills of Palestine, around Jerusalem and Damascus—is the theatre of many prophetic missions. It is the land of David, Solomon, Zachariah, John, and Jesus, peace be upon them all. By swearing by the fig and the olive of that sacred region, Allah draws the Quraysh’s attention to the chain of prophets who came before Muhammad, and to the holy sites they knew from their trade journeys. The oath declares that the message now being revealed in Mecca is a continuation and culmination of the truths preached in those ancient, blessed lands. The fig and olive are also symbols of human nature: the fig can represent the fragility and sweetness of life, while the olive’s enduring oil represents the lasting light of guidance. Some scholars link the fig to the mount where Noah’s ark rested, and the olive to the mount of Olives associated with Jesus, building a bridge of sanctity across prophetic history. The oath thus places the listener in a vast landscape of revelation, preparing the heart to accept what follows as an extension of the same divine wisdom that made those lands holy. The Meccans, who respected the sanctity of Jerusalem even as they opposed the Prophet, are silently reminded that the God of those lands is the God now speaking to them through Muhammad. This opening oath, therefore, is not a random mention of pleasant fruits but a carefully chosen signpost that directs the mind to the unity of prophecy and the seriousness of the message about to be delivered.
2 | and Mount Sinai, | The second oath shifts to “and Mount Sinai.” The Arabic term Tūr Sīnīn refers to the mountain where Allah spoke directly to Moses, giving him the Torah and the Ten Commandments. This mountain is one of the most significant geographical locations in the history of revelation. It was there that the earth trembled, the bush blazed without burning, and a human being received the direct speech of the Almighty. By swearing by Mount Sinai, the surah invokes the entire legacy of Mosaic law and the covenant with the Children of Israel. The Quraysh were not ignorant of this story; Jewish tribes lived in their midst, and the tales of Moses and Pharaoh were widely known in Arabia. The oath by Sinai asserts that the same God who manifested His glory to Moses is now revealing the Qur’an. It establishes a prophetic lineage that invalidates the Quraysh’s claim that a human messenger was an impossibility or an innovation. The mountain also symbolizes the awesome weight of divine responsibility. When the Torah was revealed, the mountain itself was levelled by the impact of divine manifestation, teaching that revelation is not a light matter but a tremendous trust. The Quraysh who rejected the Prophet were, in essence, rejecting a call that had its precedent in the most awe-inspiring theophany of history. Furthermore, Sinai represents the giving of a detailed law, a “best form” of ethical and ritual guidance for a community. This connects to the surah’s later declaration about human creation in the best form and the final judgment. The oath by Sinai thus reinforces the continuity of guidance, the authority of prophetic experience, and the certainty that the Creator who once spoke on a mountain will one day speak as the Most Just of judges. The listener is led from the fertile lands of fig and olive to the harsh, majestic heights of the desert mountain, tracing the geography of faith itself.
3 | and this secure city ˹of Mecca˺! | The third and climactic oath is “and this secure city of Mecca!” The use of “this” makes the oath immediate and personal. The listeners are standing in that very city. Mecca is described as “secure” because within its sacred precincts, bloodshed, hunting, and violence are forbidden. It is a sanctuary established by Abraham, the father of monotheism, who prayed that this valley be made a place of safety and provision. By swearing by Mecca, Allah honors it above all other lands in this sequence of oaths. Yet, as in Surah Al-Balad, the honor is also an indictment. The Quraysh were the custodians of this secure city, but they had filled it with idols and made it unsafe for the Prophet and his followers. The oath reminds them that the city’s sanctity is not their own achievement; it is a gift from the Lord of the Kaaba, and He can withdraw His protection if they persist in their injustice. The pairing of Mecca with the fig and olive lands and Mount Sinai completes a trinity of sacred geography representing three stages of Abrahamic revelation: the earlier prophets in Jerusalem, Moses at Sinai, and now Muhammad in Mecca. This shows that the Prophet’s mission is not a break with the past but its fulfillment. Mecca is the birthplace of Ishmael, the site of the first house built for the worship of the one God. The oath affirms that the Prophet’s claim is rooted in this ancient, secure sanctuary, and that the ultimate judgment about human worth will proceed from the Lord of this city. The “security” of Mecca is also a metaphor for the security of faith; those who enter the fold of true belief find an inner peace that mirrors the outer sanctity of the Haram. The oath thus concludes with a powerful blend of honor, warning, and hope, setting the stage for the universal pronouncement that follows.
4 | Indeed, We created humans in the best form. | After the threefold oath, the surah declares its central truth: “Indeed, We created humans in the best form.” The word “taqwīm” means a perfect proportioning, an upright and balanced shaping. The human being is created physically erect, with a posture that distinguishes them from animals that crawl or stoop. Their hands are instruments of skill, their eyes are capable of discerning beauty and danger, their tongue and lips can articulate the most intricate thoughts, and their brain is a universe of cognition unmatched in the known world. But “the best form” goes beyond anatomy. It encompasses the human spiritual and moral constitution. Allah created Adam with His own hands, breathed into him of His spirit, and taught him the names of all things. The human soul is endowed with an innate disposition toward recognizing its Creator, a moral compass inspired with the knowledge of right and wrong. This noble form is a gift that elevates humanity above the angels in potential, though it also carries the burden of free will and accountability. The verse stands as a ringing affirmation of human dignity, a direct rebuttal of any worldview that sees humans as mere evolved animals or accidental specks in a meaningless universe. The Quraysh, who boasted of their physical prowess and noble lineage, are reminded that their true honor lies not in tribal pride but in the divine mould in which they were cast—and that this mould comes with a purpose. The statement also serves as the foundation for what follows: because the human was given such an exalted form, the fall from it is catastrophic, and the rise through faith is glorious. The Prophet himself, when reciting this verse in prostration, would glorify Allah as the Best of Creators, recognizing the intimate link between the Creator’s perfection and the creature’s form. This verse calls every human to reflect on their own body and soul, to see the signature of the Divine Artist, and to ask: what have I done with this magnificent gift?
5 | But We will reduce them to the lowest of the low ˹in Hell˺, | The surah immediately introduces the tragic alternative: “But We will reduce them to the lowest of the low in Hell.” The Arabic “asfala sāfilīn” paints a picture of the bottommost pit, the most abased condition imaginable. After reaching the height of the best form, the human being can plunge to a depth lower than that of any beast. This reduction is a divine recompense, not a random degeneration. While some commentators also explain this as the physical weakness of extreme old age—when the erect body bends, the sharp mind dulls, and the once-strong form returns to helplessness—the majority tafsir, as the translation indicates, points to the Hereafter. The “lowest of the low” is the state of the disbelievers in Hell, where they are humiliated, tormented, and stripped of all dignity. The worldly old age affects both believers and disbelievers, but the following exception of the reward specifically separates the outcomes in the next life, making Hell the primary referent. The verse is a stark warning: the human who was created upright and noble, if they deny their Lord and corrupt their soul, will be turned upside down in the Fire. The very faculties that were gifts—the eyes that did not see the truth, the tongue that denied the Judgment, the heart that refused to love its Maker—will become instruments of punishment. The reduction is not an arbitrary divine cruelty but the natural consequence of misusing the best form. When a soul chooses filth over purity, arrogance over submission, it deforms itself from within, and the outer punishment merely manifests that inner ugliness. This verse shatters any illusion of automatic salvation based on mere human identity. The best form is a starting point, not a guarantee. The Quraysh who considered themselves the noblest of people are warned that their denial will plummet them to the vilest station. For the believer, this verse inspires a healthy fear that drives one to protect the gift of faith and to seek refuge from the lowest depths.
6 | except those who believe and do good—they will have a never-ending reward. | A ray of light breaks through the warning: “except those who believe and do good—they will have a never-ending reward.” The word “except” is a divine rescue clause. The descent into the lowest of the low is not an inescapable fate; a specific group is exempted. That group is defined by two inseparable qualities: genuine belief and righteous action. Belief in Allah, His messengers, His books, and the Last Day is the root, and good deeds—prayer, charity, honesty, kindness, and all forms of obedience—are the fruit. Together, they preserve the human being in the “best form” spiritually, and elevate them even higher. Instead of being reduced in Hell, these individuals are granted a reward that is “never-ending,” not cut off, not tainted with reproach, not followed by any decline. This reward is the opposite of the “lowest of the low”; it is the highest of the high, the eternal bliss of Paradise, culminating in the vision of Allah and His good pleasure. The verse clarifies that the previous warning about reduction does not mean that every human will suffer that fate; it means that this is the destination of those who turn away from faith, while the believers are kept safe and rewarded. It also answers the question about old age: the physical decline of the righteous in this world is a temporary, superficial “low” that is utterly eclipsed by the endless reward awaiting them. The Meccan believers, many of whom were poor and despised, are told that their present lowliness in the eyes of society is an illusion; their real position is one of sublime elevation in the divine scale. The “never-ending reward” is a promise that transforms suffering into patience and worldly loss into eternal gain. The verse thus completes the stark binary of the surah: all of humanity is either falling into the pit through denial or rising into endless reward through faith and good works. There is no middle ground.
7 | Now, what makes you deny the ˹final˺ Judgment? | After laying out the creation in the best form and the two contrasting destinies, the surah turns to confront the human being directly: “Now, what makes you deny the final Judgment?” The Arabic addresses the denier with a sharp, personal question: “fa-mā yukadhdhibuka baʿdu bid-dīn?” It is as if Allah says: after all this evidence, after the oaths by the sacred lands, after witnessing your own creation in the finest proportion, after hearing the clear logic of reward and punishment, what is it that still causes you to call the Day of Recompense a lie? The question is rhetorical, leaving the denier speechless. There is no rational answer; the denial is rooted in pride, in the desire to chase desires without restraint, and in the fear of accountability. The “dīn” here means the religion of truth, the system of judgment, the day when deeds are weighed. The denial of this Judgment is the root of all moral corruption, because if there is no final court, then right and wrong become mere human conventions, and the powerful can oppress without fear. The verse forces every listener to examine their own heart. Even in a secular age, the question resonates: what makes you deny that your choices have ultimate consequences? Is it because you have never seen a resurrection? But you have never seen your own soul, yet you know it exists. The verse is both a warning and an invitation to intellectual honesty. It connects back to the earlier declaration of the “best form”: the same God who shaped the human from a drop of fluid and gave them a balanced body and a reasoning mind is certainly able to bring them back to life for judgment. To deny this is to insult the very intelligence and form one has been given. The surah thus strips away all excuses and leaves the human soul face to face with the undeniable logic of the Creator’s justice.
8 | Is Allah not the most just of all judges? | The surah closes with a majestic, unanswerable question: “Is Allah not the most just of all judges?” The Arabic superlative “aḥkam al-ḥākimīn” means the wisest, the most decisive, the most just of all who ever judge or rule. The question expects the immediate and total affirmation of the heart: “Yes, indeed, He is.” This closing verse is the seal on the entire argument. If Allah is the Most Just of judges, then there must be a Judgment. A perfectly just judge does not leave the scales unbalanced forever. The oppressor who escaped earthly justice and the martyr who received no earthly reward will both stand before the One whose wisdom and justice are absolute. To deny the Judgment is, in effect, to deny that Allah is the Most Just, for it implies that He created humans in the best form, gave them moral law, and then abandoned them without reckoning. That would be a contradiction unworthy of the Creator. The verse also carries an implicit warning to the Quraysh, who prided themselves as judges and arbiters in their tribal society. Their judgments were often biased, favoring the strong over the weak. Allah tells them that their petty courts are nothing before His tribunal. For the oppressed believers, this verse is a deep well of comfort: the Judge who will preside over their case is not corrupt, not forgetful, not weak. He sees every tear and hears every sigh. The final word “judges” (ḥākimīn) resonates with the name of the surah and the oaths that opened it, tying the whole composition into a flawless circle. The soul is left in a state of awe, forced to admit the truth of the Judgment and the absolute justice of the One who will preside. And with that admission, the surah ends, having moved from the gardens of Jerusalem and the heights of Sinai to the sanctuary of Mecca and, ultimately, to the throne of the Most Just.
