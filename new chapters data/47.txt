1 | Those who disbelieve and hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, He will render their deeds void. | The opening verse lays down a foundational principle of divine justice, drawing a stark contrast between two groups that the rest of the surah will examine. When the verse speaks of "those who disbelieve and hinder others from the Way of Allah," it specifically targets a category of people whose offence is twofold. Disbelief alone is a grave matter, but combining it with active obstruction of others multiplies the severity. The historical context immediately points to the Quraysh leaders in Mecca who not only rejected the Prophet’s message themselves but also used their wealth, influence, and persecution to prevent others from embracing Islam. They would physically block access to the Prophet, torture converts, and spread slander so that the call to truth could not reach hearts. The phrase "hinder from the Way of Allah" encompasses every form of obstruction: mockery, economic pressure, propaganda, and outright violence. The consequence is devastating: "He will render their deeds void." Even the good deeds they might have performed, such as feeding the poor, maintaining kinship ties, or hosting pilgrims, will be nullified and carry no weight on the Day of Judgment. This is because deeds are only accepted when rooted in faith, and when a person not only rejects faith but also works to extinguish the light of guidance, whatever worldly good they did loses its value entirely. The imagery is that of a building erected on a crumbling foundation; it collapses into worthlessness. A well-known hadith recorded by Aisha, may Allah be pleased with her, reports that she once asked the Prophet about the pre-Islamic good deeds of Abdullah ibn Jud‘an, who was known for generosity, and the Prophet replied that they would not benefit him because he never said, “My Lord, forgive my sins on the Day of Judgment.” This illustrates the principle that without true faith, deeds are like scattered dust. The verse also serves as a warning that those who actively mislead others and block their path to Allah will face a special kind of loss, seeing their life’s efforts evaporate. For the believers, this is both a comfort and a call to reflect: it assures them that the apparent success and charity of disbelieving oppressors will not save them, and it reminds them to protect their own deeds through sincerity and to never become obstacles to others seeking the truth.
2 | As for those who believe, do good, and have faith in what has been revealed to Muḥammad—which is the truth from their Lord—He will absolve them of their sins and improve their condition. | Immediately after the grim warning for the disbelievers, the surah turns to the beautiful promise for the believers, establishing the complete opposite outcome. The verse carefully outlines three interrelated qualities. It begins with "those who believe," referring to the inner conviction and acceptance of the heart. Then it adds "do good," meaning they translate that faith into righteous actions, fulfilling obligations and treating creation with kindness. The third quality is highly significant in the context of this particular surah: "and have faith in what has been revealed to Muḥammad." This clause emphasizes that true belief requires accepting the specific revelation sent to the final Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him. The Quraysh and many others acknowledged Allah but refused to accept the Quran and the prophethood of Muhammad, and thus their supposed faith remained incomplete and invalid. By specifically mentioning the revelation to Muhammad, the verse cuts through any attempt to claim faith while rejecting the final message. It then reinforces this point by describing the revelation as "the truth from their Lord," leaving no room for doubt or selective acceptance. The two-part reward is beautifully suited to human needs. "He will absolve them of their sins" means that Allah will cover, forgive, and erase the wrongs they committed in the past. The Arabic expression carries the sense of wiping something away so completely that no trace remains, a deeply reassuring thought for anyone burdened by regret. The second part, "and improve their condition," goes beyond forgiveness. It means that Allah will set right their affairs, mend what is broken in their lives, remove their anxieties, guide them to wholesome relationships, and grant them inner peace. The early believers in Mecca experienced this transformation tangibly: people who were once feuding tribes became loving brothers, and individuals lost in vice became paragons of virtue. The verse assures that when a person sincerely turns to Allah with faith and good deeds, acknowledging the truth of the final revelation, the outcome is not merely punishment averted but a comprehensive betterment of one’s entire state in this world and the next.
3 | This is because the disbelievers follow falsehood, while the believers follow the truth from their Lord. This is how Allah shows people their true state ˹of faith˺. | Having described the two fates, the surah now explains the underlying reason for the divergence. "This is because" signals that the voiding of deeds and the forgiveness and improvement are not arbitrary decisions but have a clear cause rooted in the very orientation of each group’s life. "The disbelievers follow falsehood" means that their entire worldview, their worship, their ethics, and their goals are built upon something unreal and ultimately vanishing. Falsehood here includes idol worship, the denial of resurrection, arrogance, following ancestral customs without evidence, and any system that puts the creation above the Creator. Because their foundation is false, whatever they construct upon it inevitably collapses. On the other hand, "the believers follow the truth from their Lord," meaning they cling to the divine revelation, the reality of monotheism, the certainty of accountability, and the moral guidance that aligns with their innate nature. The truth is not something they invented; it comes "from their Lord," and that divine origin gives it its power, stability, and ability to transform lives. The concluding part of the verse is profound: "This is how Allah shows people their true state." The Arabic word for "their true state" can also mean their examples, their conditions, or the descriptions that distinguish them. By presenting these vivid contrasts, Allah makes it clear to everyone where they stand. The parables and descriptions in the Quran act as a mirror in which a person can see the nature of his own soul. If someone is honest with himself, he can recognize whether he is walking on the path of truth or slipping into the pursuit of falsehood. This self-awareness is a gift; without these clear revelations, people might remain deluded, thinking their good deeds will save them despite a corrupted foundation. The verse also teaches an essential lesson about the nature of action: the value of a deed is determined by its source and its goal. A person who follows truth is connected to the eternal, and so his deeds acquire permanence and worth, while the one who follows falsehood is chasing shadows.
4 | So when you meet the disbelievers ˹in battle˺, strike ˹their˺ necks until you have thoroughly subdued them, then bind them firmly. Later ˹free them either as˺ an act of grace or by ransom until the war comes to an end. So will it be. Had Allah willed, He ˹Himself˺ could have inflicted punishment on them. But He does ˹this only to˺ test some of you by means of others. And those who are martyred in the cause of Allah, He will never render their deeds void. | This verse contains detailed rules of engagement in war, and it must be understood within its original battlefield context and the broader principles of Islamic law. The instruction "when you meet the disbelievers in battle" specifies that the command applies to an active armed conflict, not to everyday life or to non-combatants. The phrase "strike their necks" is an idiomatic expression in Arabic meaning to strike decisively and disable the enemy’s fighting capacity, similar to saying “strike at the head.” It does not mandate beheading in every case; rather, it signifies intense combat until the adversary's power is broken. The goal is clearly stated: "until you have thoroughly subdued them," meaning you have gained the upper hand, the immediate threat has been neutralized, and the enemy force is no longer capable of waging war against you. At that point, the command shifts to the treatment of captives: "then bind them firmly." Prisoners of war are to be secured to prevent them from returning to the fight or escaping. The capture itself is not the end; immediately, a humane choice is offered: "Later free them either as an act of grace or by ransom." The captives may be released purely out of kindness, without demanding anything in return, which was a revolutionary concept, or they may be exchanged for Muslim prisoners or ransomed. The phrase "until the war comes to an end" indicates that these rulings are temporary measures during hostilities, not a permanent state of enslavement. The verse then provides a profound spiritual perspective on warfare: "Had Allah willed, He Himself could have inflicted punishment on them." Allah does not need human armies to destroy the disbelievers; He could command an angel, a natural disaster, or a sudden plague. The reason He legislates fighting is "to test some of you by means of others." The battlefield becomes a trial for believers, testing their courage, obedience, selflessness, and trust in Allah, while the disbelievers are tested through whether they persist in aggression or reconsider. The verse closes by honouring those who give their lives: "And those who are martyred in the cause of Allah, He will never render their deeds void." Even in discussing war, the Quran redirects attention to the eternal reward, ensuring that the fighter’s intention is purified and that the believer understands that death in this path is not loss but the culmination of a life’s striving that will be fully and permanently preserved.
5 | He will guide them ˹to their reward˺, improve their condition, | After mentioning the martyr’s preserved deeds, the surah now describes the specific blessings that await them, and by extension all sincere believers, in the Hereafter and the transitional state. "He will guide them to their reward" means that Allah will lead them to the bliss of Paradise with ease and certainty. The journey through the stages after death can be daunting, but the martyrs and the righteous will be guided every step: in the grave, during the resurrection, across the bridge, and finally to their eternal abode. This guidance is not merely showing the way but actively escorting them, ensuring that no confusion, fear, or deviation can touch them. The expression evokes the image of a caravan leader who knows the path intimately and brings the travellers safely to their destination. The phrase "improve their condition" complements the previous verse’s promise for the living believers and applies it to the state of the martyrs after death. In the grave, their resting place will be made spacious and illuminated; their souls will be in the company of green birds perching in the trees of Paradise, as narrated in an authentic hadith from Sahih Muslim. Their condition, which might have appeared bleak from a worldly perspective when they were killed, will be utterly transformed into a state of joy, honour, and contentment. Even any worldly shortcomings they may have had will be set right, and their families left behind will be cared for. This verse connects back to verse two’s promise that Allah "improves their condition," linking the fate of the believers generally with the special destiny of the martyrs. The continuity underscores that the same Lord who mends the affairs of the faithful during life continues that care after death, and the ultimate improvement is the transition from the pain and limitation of this world to the boundless perfection of the next. For the living who hear this verse, it serves as a powerful reassurance that those who sacrifice their lives for truth have not lost anything but have gained a perpetual guidance and an ever-improving state of being.
6 | and admit them into Paradise, having made it known to them. | This verse concludes the description of the martyr’s reward by focusing on the moment of entry into the eternal garden and the special familiarity that accompanies it. "And admit them into Paradise" uses a verb that implies being caused to enter, with a sense of honour and generous reception. The martyrs will not merely be allowed into Paradise; they will be ushered in with welcome and ceremony, their sacrifices recognized and their status celebrated. The angels will greet them from every gate, and the delights of the garden will be opened to them without delay. What makes this admission unique is the clause "having made it known to them." Scholars have offered two beautiful interpretations, both of which are true and can apply simultaneously. The first is that Allah, in His infinite grace, will have already made Paradise known to the believers through the descriptions in the Quran and the teachings of the Prophet, so that when they enter, they will recognize what was promised and their faith will be visually confirmed. Their immediate reaction will be, “This is exactly what our Lord told us,” and that recognition will deepen their joy. The second, more personal interpretation is that each believer, particularly the martyr, is shown his specific dwelling in Paradise at the moment of death, so that by the time he formally enters, he knows his home, his gardens, his companions, and everything feels intimately familiar rather than strange. A hadith found in Sahih al-Bukhari relates that the soul of the believer is shown its place in Paradise morning and evening after death, creating an intense longing and familiarity. This prior knowledge removes any disorientation, making the transition into the eternal life smooth and comforting. The verse thus assures that the martyr, who left his worldly home behind, is not entering an unknown realm but rather a homecoming to a place prepared and announced to him long before. This adds a layer of divine tenderness to the idea of reward, showing that Allah’s care extends to the psychological and emotional comfort of His servants, not just to their physical enjoyment.
7 | O believers! If you stand up for Allah, He will help you and make your steps firm. | After the detailed discussion on warfare and martyrdom, the surah addresses the believers with a call to action and a majestic promise. "O believers!" is a direct and affectionate summons that reminds them of their identity and the responsibilities tied to it. The command "if you stand up for Allah" uses a verb that conveys the sense of rising, supporting, and taking a firm stance. Standing up for Allah means to champion His cause, to uphold His religion in one’s life and community, to defend the truth against falsehood, and to dedicate one’s efforts sincerely for His sake. It includes both internal standing, by purifying one’s soul and remaining steadfast in obedience, and external standing, by spreading the message, promoting justice, and, when necessary, defending the community against aggression. The condition "if" is important because it makes the subsequent divine help conditional upon the believers’ own initiative and sincerity. Allah’s help is not automatic for those who claim faith while sitting idly; it flows to those who rise and exert themselves. The promised outcome has two components. "He will help you" is a comprehensive guarantee of victory, support, and divine intervention in ways that may be visible or invisible. The help of Allah can manifest as strength in battle, wisdom in decision-making, influence over hearts, or even a change in circumstances that removes obstacles. The second component, "and make your steps firm," is equally vital. It means that Allah will grant them steadfastness, keep them from wavering in moments of fear, and prevent them from retreating or losing resolve. During the lifetime of the Prophet, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, this promise materialized repeatedly: at Badr, the small and poorly equipped Muslim army stood firmly and achieved a decisive victory; at Hunayn, after an initial shock, Allah restored their firmness. In the life of a believer today, standing up for Allah could mean opposing a societal wrong, maintaining religious practice under pressure, or striving to provide for one’s family through lawful means while resisting corruption, and the same promise of help and firmness remains valid.
8 | As for the disbelievers, may they be doomed and may He render their deeds void. | The surah now turns its attention back to the disbelievers, presenting a sharp contrast to the hopeful promise given to the believers in the previous verse. The Arabic phrase that is translated as "may they be doomed" carries the weight of a curse and a declaration of inevitable ruin. It is not merely a wish but a statement of fact about their fate, pronounced by the Lord of all worlds. The original word suggests being cast down, perishing, and facing complete loss and misery. This doom is not arbitrary; it is the direct consequence of their rejection of truth after it has been made clear, and their active efforts to obstruct the path of Allah, as outlined at the start of the surah. The second part of the verse, "and may He render their deeds void," returns to the theme that opened this chapter, reinforcing the message that the apparent good works of those who disbelieve will count for nothing on the Day of Judgment. When Allah renders deeds void, they become like scattered dust, offering no protection, no intercession, and no reward. The repetition of this concept throughout the surah underscores its importance in the consciousness of the believers, who might be tempted to envy the worldly success and visible charity of the disbelievers. The verse serves to reassure the faithful that no matter how impressive the disbelievers’ achievements appear, they carry no weight in the scale of ultimate justice. It also functions as a warning to those believers who might be tempted to compromise or slacken, reminding them that without faith and sincerity, even their own deeds could be at risk. The immediate historical reference was to the powerful leaders of Quraysh whose wealth and influence were about to be shattered, but the principle extends to every age. The verse creates an emotional pivot from the encouragement of standing up for Allah to the sobering reality of what awaits those who stand against Him, thereby strengthening the believer’s resolve to be among those helped, not those doomed.
9 | That is because they detest what Allah has revealed, so He has rendered their deeds void. | This verse provides the explicit reason behind the doom and the nullification of deeds mentioned just before, digging into the inner psychological and spiritual sickness of the disbelievers. "That is because they detest what Allah has revealed" pinpoints the root cause not as ignorance or lack of access to the truth, but as a deep-seated aversion and hatred. The word used for "detest" conveys not just disagreement but an emotional repulsion and active dislike. The object of their detestation is "what Allah has revealed," which includes the Quran, the prophetic message, the laws, and the ethical system of Islam. The Quraysh leaders, for instance, understood the beauty and power of the Quran; they secretly listened to it at night, but they publicly hated it because it challenged their authority, their idols, their tribal pride, and their unchecked desires. Their hatred was therefore not intellectual but rooted in arrogance and vested interests. The consequence flows logically: "so He has rendered their deeds void." When a person reaches a state where he loathes the very guidance sent by his Creator, he has severed the connection that could give his actions meaning. Allah’s rendering their deeds void is both a punishment and a natural outcome of their own choice. Their deeds were directed toward worldly goals and driven by ego, and because they detested the one standard that could have given those deeds eternal value, the deeds collapse into nothingness at death. The verse implicitly warns believers to examine their own hearts. A person may outwardly perform acts of worship, but if deep inside there is resentment toward some aspect of the divine law, or a feeling that Islam’s teachings are burdensome, then that person must urgently correct their attitude. The story of the Prophet’s companion who said, “The Prophet has commanded us with something that I feel in my heart is too heavy,” and was rebuked, illustrates the danger. Love for what Allah has revealed is the lifeblood of faith, and detesting it, even in part, is a symptom of a disease that can lead to the ruin of all one’s good works.
10 | Have they not travelled throughout the land to see what was the end of those before them? Allah annihilated them, and a similar fate awaits the disbelievers. | The surah now appeals to reason and historical observation, inviting the disbelievers to learn from the visible ruins and the well-known accounts of past civilizations. The rhetorical question "Have they not travelled throughout the land" assumes that travel and observation are accessible. The Arabs of the Prophet’s time routinely journeyed for trade, passing by the remnants of ‘Ad, Thamud, and the people of Lot. These were not mythical tales but physical sites: carved-out dwellings in mountains, massive stone structures, and abandoned wells that travellers could see with their own eyes. The question stirs the conscience and challenges the arrogance that makes a people think they are exempt from the laws of history. The phrase "to see what was the end of those before them" is not about mere tourism; it is about reflecting on the pattern of how nations that rejected their prophets and indulged in oppression were brought to ruin. Then the verse states the historical fact bluntly: "Allah annihilated them." The destruction was total, leaving no room for recovery or return. The power, wealth, and numbers of those ancient peoples could not save them when divine punishment came. The verse then turns the lens onto the current disbelievers: "and a similar fate awaits the disbelievers." This is both a prophecy and a principle. It prophesied the downfall of the Quraysh pagans who were opposing the Prophet, a prophecy fulfilled within a few years at Badr and then the conquest of Mecca. More broadly, it establishes the sunnah, or consistent way of Allah, that societies built on the rejection of divine guidance, oppression, and moral corruption eventually crumble, and the disbelievers in any era who follow that same pattern will meet a similar end. For the believers, this verse serves as a historical anchor for their faith, reminding them that their struggle is not new and that the forces of falsehood, no matter how powerful they seem, are ultimately fragile and doomed. It also encourages travel and reflection as a means of spiritual growth, an invitation that remains open for anyone who walks through archaeological sites or reads history with a heart willing to learn.
11 | This is because Allah is the Patron of the believers while the disbelievers have no patron. | The verse succinctly summarises the deeper reason behind the historical destruction of disbelieving nations and the ultimate success of the believers. The word translated as "Patron" is Mawla in Arabic, a term rich with layers of meaning. It encompasses protector, guardian, ally, supporter, and master. To say that "Allah is the Patron of the believers" means that He is their guardian in every sense: He defends them against their enemies, guides them through confusion, provides for them from sources they could not imagine, and ultimately will deliver them safely to Paradise. This patronage is not a distant, abstract relationship; it is active, ongoing, and deeply personal. The believers, by virtue of their faith and their standing up for Allah as mentioned earlier, have aligned themselves with the ultimate source of power in the universe, and therefore no enemy can truly prevail against them in the grand scheme of things. Even when they suffer temporary setbacks, their Patron ensures that those setbacks are transformed into expiation or elevation in rank. In stark contrast, "the disbelievers have no patron." They may have worldly allies, riches, and armies, but none of these can function as a true Mawla that provides, protects, and saves from eternal punishment. Their idols, their ancestors, their wealth, and their status all desert them at the moment of death, and in the grave and the Hereafter they stand utterly alone, exposed to the consequences of their rejection. This verse also connects back to the earlier command about fighting, explaining that the ultimate reason for the believers’ eventual victory is not their own strength but the patronage of Allah. The believers can therefore rely not on their numbers or equipment but on this divine guardianship, while the disbelievers, no matter how strong, are orphaned in the spiritual sense, devoid of any real support. This knowledge should inspire in the believer a combination of profound humility and unshakable confidence, humility because the patronage is entirely from Allah’s grace, and confidence because that patronage is unfailing.
12 | Surely Allah will admit those who believe and do good into Gardens under which rivers flow. As for the disbelievers, they enjoy themselves and feed like cattle. But the Fire will be their home. | This verse brings the discussion back to the final destination, painting a vivid contrast between the two groups in terms of both their worldly behaviour and their eternal outcome. The promise for the believers begins with "Surely," which in the Arabic original is a particle of emphasis, confirming that this is an unquestionable certainty. "Those who believe and do good" combines the two wings of faith and righteous action once more, showing that Paradise is not earned by mere verbal claim but by a combination of inner conviction and outward practice. The reward is described as "Gardens under which rivers flow," an image that would be especially powerful for the desert-dwelling Arabs, who knew the value of shade, water, and lush greenery. The flowing rivers signify continuous, effortless abundance, a sharp contrast to the harsh, barren environment they endured in Arabia. Then the verse turns to the disbelievers with a devastatingly accurate description of their temporal existence: "they enjoy themselves and feed like cattle." The phrase captures a life entirely consumed by the pursuit of sensory pleasures, without any higher purpose, reflection, or preparation for an eternal life. Cattle eat, drink, and reproduce, living moment to moment, completely oblivious to any meaning beyond instinct. The disbeliever who denies resurrection, indulges his desires, and never pauses to think about his Creator or his ultimate end, is reducing himself to the level of an animal, despite his human intelligence. The tragedy is that this enjoyment is brief and deceptive. The verse concludes bluntly: "But the Fire will be their home." The word for "home" implies a final, permanent dwelling, and using it for Hell underscores the horrific irony: the comfortable homes they built on earth will be replaced by a blazing abode from which there is no departure. The verse acts as a powerful corrective to the human tendency to envy the disbeliever’s apparent ease; their eating and enjoying is not a sign of favour but a brief respite before an inevitable and terrible reckoning.
13 | ˹Imagine, O Prophet,˺ how many societies We destroyed that were far superior in might than your society—which drove you out—and there was none to help them! | This verse directly addresses the Prophet, peace be upon him, to comfort him and to warn his opponents. The rhetorical opening "Imagine, O Prophet, how many societies We destroyed" draws his attention and that of every listener to the undeniable pattern of history, but the way the verse is worded also contains a personal, empathetic dimension. The phrase "far superior in might than your society" points to past nations such as ‘Ad, Thamud, and the people of Pharaoh, whose physical strength, architectural achievements, and military power far exceeded anything the Meccans could boast. Despite this superiority, when they crossed the limits and rejected their messengers, Allah’s punishment came, and no force could repel it. Then the verse adds a poignant detail: "which drove you out." This reference to the Prophet’s expulsion from Mecca is significant. The Quraysh had forced the Messenger of Allah to leave his beloved hometown, a painful event that still stung the hearts of the early Muslims. By mentioning this injury, Allah shows that He is fully aware of the injustice and suffering inflicted upon His Prophet and his followers. Yet the verse immediately puts this trial into perspective: the society that expelled him, though arrogant in its moment, is no match for the divine might that effortlessly annihilated far stronger civilizations. The final clause "and there was none to help them" drives the point home. The mighty nations of the past had no protector against Allah, and similarly, the Meccans will find no helper when their appointed time comes. This verse thus serves a dual function: it consoles the Prophet by assuring him that his exilers are not beyond the reach of divine justice, and it warns the disbelievers that their current immunity is only a delay, not an exemption. The believers can also draw comfort from this, understanding that their present weakness does not indicate divine displeasure, and that the apparent strength of the oppressors is an illusion that will shatter.
14 | Can those ˹believers˺ who stand on clear proof from their Lord be like those whose evil deeds are made appealing to them and ˹only˺ follow their desires? | The surah now poses a rhetorical question that demands the obvious answer: no, they cannot possibly be equal. This verse distinguishes between two mindsets and ways of life, aiming to solidify the believer’s sense of identity and the vast difference in value between the guided and the misguided. The description of the believers is profound: they "stand on clear proof from their Lord." The word "clear proof," bayyinah, indicates revelation that is self-evidently true, a light that leaves no room for genuine doubt. This clear proof includes the Quran itself, the miracles, the sound arguments, and the innate human disposition that recognizes monotheism and morality. To stand on this proof means to base one’s entire life, decisions, and worldview upon a solid foundation of certainty. It is an image of stability, light, and direction. The other group is described in a completely opposite manner: their "evil deeds are made appealing to them." Through self-deception, satanic influence, and gradual desensitization, their sins begin to look attractive and justified. A liar sees himself as clever, a tyrant sees himself as maintaining order, and a hedonist sees his indulgence as living fully. This distortion of perception is a punishment in itself, because it locks the door to repentance. The second characteristic of this group is that they "only follow their desires." Their ultimate guide is not revelation, reason, or conscience, but the whims and impulses of their lower self. A person enslaved to his desires is like a boat without an anchor, tossed in every direction and eventually crashing. The rhetorical question forces the listener to reflect on which group he belongs to. The contrast also serves to remind believers that they should never envy the disbelievers’ lifestyle or imagine that both paths simply lead to different but equally valid outcomes. One leads to truth and eternal salvation, the other leads to delusion and destruction. In a contemporary context, this verse powerfully challenges the modern notion that all beliefs and moral systems are equally valid, asserting that there is an objective truth from the Lord, and standing upon it is fundamentally different from being adrift in subjective desires.
15 | The description of the Paradise promised to the righteous is that in it are rivers of fresh water, rivers of milk that never changes in taste, rivers of wine delicious to drink, and rivers of pure honey. There they will ˹also˺ have all kinds of fruit, and forgiveness from their Lord. ˹Can they be˺ like those who will stay in the Fire forever, left to drink boiling water that will tear apart their insides? | Having established the fundamental difference between the two groups, the surah now elaborates on their respective eternal abodes in a way that appeals directly to human senses and imagination. The description of Paradise begins with four rivers, each carrying a different delightful drink. The first is "rivers of fresh water," water that is perfectly pure, free from stagnation or contamination, a supreme luxury in the arid climate of Arabia. The second is "rivers of milk that never changes in taste," meaning milk that will never spoil, sour, or lose its creamy freshness, always ready to be enjoyed without the need for preservation. The third is "rivers of wine delicious to drink," a wine that produces only exquisite pleasure and no intoxication, headache, or loss of reason, a heavenly counterpart to the harmful wine of this world that the believers abstained from by obedience. The fourth is "rivers of pure honey," filtered and unblemished by impurities, representing the ultimate natural sweetness. These rivers are not mere metaphorical images; they are real, physical enjoyments in Paradise, yet infinitely superior to anything earthly. The verse then adds, "There they will also have all kinds of fruit," indicating boundless variety beyond the drinks. Then it elevates the description to a spiritual peak: "and forgiveness from their Lord." This is the greatest gift, for even in Paradise, the realization that one’s sins are completely pardoned and that Allah is pleased is the deepest source of joy. The verse then turns to the terrifying fate of the disbelievers with a concise but graphic image: they "will stay in the Fire forever, left to drink boiling water that will tear apart their insides." The boiling water, hamim, is not refreshing but a scalding punishment that, when brought near, causes the skin to recoil, and when drunk, shreds the internal organs. The contrast could not be more extreme: cooling, nourishing, delightful rivers versus an agonizing, destructive drink. The final rhetorical question hammers the point home, compelling each listener to choose which group they want to be among.
16 | There are some of them who listen to you ˹O Prophet˺, but when they depart from you, they say ˹mockingly˺ to those ˹believers˺ gifted with knowledge, “What did he just say?” These are the ones whose hearts Allah has sealed and who ˹only˺ follow their desires. | The surah shifts from describing Paradise and Hell to exposing the inner state and behaviour of a specific group present in the Prophet’s community: the hypocrites or those weak in faith who would attend the gatherings of knowledge but derive no benefit. "There are some of them who listen to you" indicates physical presence; they sat in the mosque, heard the words of the Quran and the Prophet’s teachings, but their listening was superficial and devoid of the sincere intention to understand and obey. The proof of their insincerity is what happens immediately afterward: "when they depart from you, they say mockingly to those believers gifted with knowledge, 'What did he just say?'" This question was not a genuine request for clarification. It was a sarcastic remark meant to belittle the Prophet’s speech and signal that they considered it incomprehensible or irrelevant. The fact that they directed this mockery to "those believers gifted with knowledge" shows their tactic; they picked out the learned companions, those who understood the religion deeply, and tried to create a wedge or plant doubt, as if to say, “You are supposed to be knowledgeable, so can you make any sense of this nonsense?” Allah then passes judgment on them: "These are the ones whose hearts Allah has sealed." The sealing of the heart is a divine consequence of their persistent mockery, arrogance, and refusal to approach the truth with humility. When a heart is sealed, no genuine faith can enter, and the person becomes spiritually numb. The second characteristic is "and who only follow their desires." Their decisions and reactions are not driven by a search for truth but by what pleases their ego and aligns with their comfort. This verse serves as a timeless warning against the attitude of listening to religious teachings with a critical, dismissive mindset, and it highlights the high rank of those who are gifted with true knowledge, as they are the ones who can differentiate between sincere inquiry and mockery.
17 | As for those who are ˹rightly˺ guided, He increases them in guidance and blesses them with righteousness. | In stark contrast to the hypocrites whose hearts are sealed, this verse describes the beautiful reciprocal relationship between the believer and his Lord. It begins with "those who are rightly guided," referring to those who have already accepted faith, responded to the call, and begun the journey of obedience. Their initial guidance was itself a gift from Allah, but the verse promises something more. "He increases them in guidance" means that guidance is not a static state; it deepens, expands, and becomes more luminous over time. The more a person acts on the knowledge he has, the more Allah opens doors to new understanding, deeper insight, and greater certitude. Every sincere prayer, every act of charity, every moment of patience adds a layer of light to the heart, enabling it to see truths it could not perceive before. This principle is echoed in the verse, "And those who strive in Our way, We will surely guide them to Our paths," confirming that guidance is earned through active seeking and application. The second part, "and blesses them with righteousness," is translated from an Arabic phrase that can also mean "He gives them their piety" or "He grants them the quality of God-consciousness." This indicates that taqwa, the all-important virtue of being mindful of Allah, avoiding sin, and acting with reverent care, is itself a divine gift. While a person must make the effort, the actual attainment of a steady, consistent state of righteousness requires Allah’s enabling grace. The verse thus instils in the believer both hope and humility: hope that his present level of faith can always grow, and humility that even his piety is not purely his own accomplishment but something his Lord has blessed him with. For the early Muslims listening to this, the contrast with the previous verse was immediate and personal; they were being told that unlike the mockers who stagnate and regress, their own path is one of continuous spiritual ascent, a momentum that Allah Himself nurtures and sustains.
18 | Are they only waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise? Yet ˹some of˺ its signs have already come. Once it actually befalls them, will it not be too late to be mindful? | The surah now turns to address the disbelievers and the mockers with a warning about the suddenness of the final reckoning. The question "Are they only waiting for the Hour to take them by surprise?" implies that their current attitude of denial, mockery, and procrastination is a gamble that they cannot afford. The Hour, meaning the Day of Resurrection, will come with such abruptness that even those who theoretically believed in it will be stunned, let alone those who dismissed it. The verse then delivers a crucial piece of information: "Yet some of its signs have already come." These signs serve as alarm bells, early warnings that the event is inevitable and perhaps not far. According to many scholars, the Prophet himself, may Allah’s peace and blessings be upon him, was one of the greatest signs of the Hour, as his coming signaled the end of prophecy and the beginning of the last era of human history. Other signs include the splitting of the moon, which the Quraysh witnessed and dismissed as magic, and the widespread prevalence of certain moral and social changes that the Prophet foretold. By stating that these signs have already appeared, the verse undermines any argument that the Day of Judgment is a remote, abstract concept. The final clause, "Once it actually befalls them, will it not be too late to be mindful?" is a piercing rhetorical question. The Arabic word for "be mindful" comes from the same root as dhikr, remembrance and reflection. When the Hour arrives, the veil of heedlessness will be ripped away violently, and every person will suddenly see the truth with absolute clarity. However, that moment of clarity will not constitute faith that benefits, because faith is only acceptable when it is based on belief in the unseen, before the reality is unveiled. The question is designed to jolt the listener out of complacency. There is a window of opportunity now, in this life, to reflect, repent, and align one’s life with the truth, but once that window closes with the trumpet blast, no amount of belated mindfulness will change the outcome.
19 | So, know ˹well, O Prophet,˺ that there is no god ˹worthy of worship˺ except Allah. And seek forgiveness for your shortcomings and for ˹the sins of˺ the believing men and women. For Allah ˹fully˺ knows your movements and places of rest ˹O people˺. | This verse addresses the Prophet directly, though its lessons extend to the entire community. "So, know well, O Prophet, that there is no god worthy of worship except Allah" is a command to possess firm, unwavering, and deeply internalized knowledge of the core truth of tawhid. The Prophet, of course, already knew this with absolute certainty, but the command serves to highlight the supreme importance of this knowledge and to instruct the believers through him. It is a reminder that even the highest station of prophethood is grounded in the constant renewal and affirmation of the oneness of Allah. The next instruction carries a deeply humbling lesson: "And seek forgiveness for your shortcomings." The Arabic word dhanb, translated as shortcomings, does not imply major sins but rather the small lapses, the instances where even a prophet might not have chosen the absolute best course among permissible options, or the natural human limitations in giving the fullest possible worship due to Allah. By commanding the Prophet to seek forgiveness, the verse teaches all believers that no one is self-sufficient or beyond the need for istighfar, and that seeking forgiveness should be a continuous practice, not an occasional one. The command then expands to include others: "and for the sins of the believing men and women." This establishes the beautiful principle of intercessory supplication and solidarity within the Muslim community. The Prophet is instructed to pray for the entire ummah, and by extension, every believer is encouraged to pray for fellow believers. The verse concludes with a statement of divine knowledge that is both comforting and sobering: "For Allah fully knows your movements and places of rest." Your movements cover all your outward activities during the day, your business, your travels, your struggles, and your interactions. Your places of rest refer to your private moments, your homes, your beds, and the secrets you harbor when no one else is watching. Nothing escapes Allah’s knowledge, and that comprehensive awareness means that His forgiveness and care are dispensed with perfect wisdom. Knowing that Allah observes every movement and every rest motivates the believer to fill both public and private life with goodness.
20 | And the believers say, “If only a sûrah was revealed ˹allowing self-defence˺!” Yet when a precise sûrah is revealed, in which fighting is ˹explicitly˺ mentioned, you see those with sickness in their hearts staring at you like someone in the throes of death. It would have been better for them | The surah now reveals an episode from the early Medinan period that exposes the true character of the hypocrites. Before the command to fight was revealed, some sincere but zealous believers, and perhaps also those who were overly confident, would express their eagerness: “If only a surah was revealed allowing self-defence!” They longed for permission to confront their oppressors and end the humiliation they had suffered. However, when the actual command came, the situation sorted the sincere from the insincere. "Yet when a precise surah is revealed, in which fighting is explicitly mentioned," the matter became serious and binding, not just a theoretical wish. The surah is described as "precise," meaning its rulings are clear, decisive, and leave no room for avoidance. At that moment, a striking transformation becomes visible in a certain group: "you see those with sickness in their hearts staring at you like someone in the throes of death." The phrase "sickness in their hearts" refers to hypocrisy, doubt, and weak faith. Their reaction to the command to fight is not courageous acceptance or even understandable human fear, but a look of sheer terror, as if they are already witnessing their own death. The comparison to "someone in the throes of death" captures the glassy-eyed, pale, and helpless stare of a person overwhelmed by fear. This visual description was something the believers could recognize in the faces of the hypocrites among them. The verse then breaks off with the phrase "It would have been better for them," which creates a sense of suspense. The completion comes in the next verse, but even in this suspense, the meaning is clear: it would have been better for them to have matched their earlier words with sincere obedience and brave acceptance of the divine decree, rather than revealing their inner sickness through such a display of cowardice. The verse teaches that a person’s claims of eagerness for religious duty are tested when that duty actually becomes obligatory, and it warns against being like those who love the idea of sacrifice in theory but crumble when faced with its reality.
21 | to obey and speak rightly. Then when fighting was ordained, it surely would have been better for them if they were true to Allah. | This verse continues directly from the suspended phrase at the end of verse twenty, “It would have been better for them,” and now explains exactly what would have been better. The hypocrites are first told that it would have been better for them “to obey and speak rightly.” Obedience here refers to wholehearted compliance with the divine command, especially the command to fight when it was revealed, but encompassing all obligations. Speaking rightly means uttering words of sincere acceptance, affirmation, and readiness, rather than the terrified silence or the sarcastic whispers they resorted to. The combination of inward obedience and outward upright speech would have aligned them with the believers and would have preserved their honour. The verse then takes this reasoning a step further by looking at the exact moment of obligation: “Then when fighting was ordained, it surely would have been better for them if they were true to Allah.” The Arabic expression for “true to Allah” carries the sense of being sincere, loyal, and truthful in one’s relationship with the Creator. The logic is powerful. Even if someone initially felt hesitation or fear, the act of rising to the occasion and proving truthful to their claim of faith at the critical moment would have been a source of immense good for them. They would have gained the reward of the strugglers, the purification of their souls through sacrifice, and the honour of standing with the Prophet in the most difficult of times. By failing at that test, they revealed that their earlier eager words were hollow. The verse teaches a profound principle of spiritual growth: the value of a person’s faith is proven not in moments of ease and theoretical discussion, but at the juncture where obedience clashes with personal fear, desire, or worldly interest. The one who is true to Allah at that point will find that the very act of stepping forward brings blessings, strength, and divine approval that far outweigh any temporary difficulty avoided by holding back. In a broader sense, it calls every believer to examine the moments when a religious obligation feels heavy; that is precisely the moment when being true to Allah can transform the situation from a burden into a means of elevation.
22 | Now if you ˹hypocrites˺ turn away, perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ˹ties of˺ kinship! | Having exposed the hypocrisy of those who shrank from the command to fight, the verse now addresses them directly with a warning about the natural consequences of their attitude. “Now if you turn away” refers to turning away from obedience, from the revealed command, and from the sincerity that was demanded. The Arabic verb implies a deliberate turning of one’s back, a rejection and abandonment of the path. The response to this hypothetical but highly probable turning away is severe: “perhaps you would then spread corruption throughout the land and sever your ties of kinship.” The word “perhaps” does not indicate doubt from Allah’s perspective; rather, it is a rhetorical device that presents the outcome as a strong and likely possibility, and it also carries a nuance of challenge. The two evils mentioned are not random. Spreading corruption, fasad, encompasses all forms of wrongdoing that disrupt peace, justice, and moral order: violence, theft, oppression, and sowing discord among people. The mention of severing ties of kinship is especially poignant for the Arab society of that time, which took immense pride in family bonds. The Quraysh and the Arab tribes considered the maintenance of kinship ties as one of the highest virtues, even in their pre-Islamic state. Thus, to warn them that their turning away from Islam could lead them to sever those very ties they boasted of protecting was a shocking wake-up call. Historically, the hypocrites in Medina did indeed engage in activities that spread corruption, such as building a rival mosque to split the community and conspiring with external enemies during the Battle of the Trench. The severing of kinship ties manifested in how they abandoned familial loyalty to the believers and allied with hostile tribes. The deeper lesson is that when a person turns away from divine guidance, the moral restraints that even culture and tradition provide begin to erode. Without the anchor of revelation, even the natural good impulses can be corrupted, and a person may fall into the very evils he once condemned. The verse thus argues that obedience to Allah is the true preserver of all social goods, including family ties, while hypocrisy and rejection of faith ultimately unravel the fabric of society.
23 | These are the ones who Allah has condemned, deafening them and blinding their eyes. | The surah now delivers the decisive verdict upon those characterized by hypocrisy and the potential for corruption and severing of family ties described in the previous verse. The opening phrase “These are the ones” points a direct finger at that specific group, removing any ambiguity. “Who Allah has condemned” uses a word, la‘anah, that means being expelled from divine mercy, cursed, and distanced from any access to goodness. Condemnation by Allah is the ultimate ruin, because it means that the door to forgiveness and spiritual light has been shut by their own persistent choices. The verse then details two specific forms of this condemnation using sensory language: “deafening them and blinding their eyes.” The deafening refers to a spiritual deafness. They had ears that functioned physically, and they heard the words of the Quran and the Prophet’s teachings, but their hearing did not lead to understanding or acceptance. The faculty of beneficial hearing was disabled as a punishment, so that the truth entered their ears as mere sound without penetrating the heart. The blinding of their eyes similarly refers to their spiritual sight. They could see the signs of Allah in creation, they witnessed the transformation of the believers, they observed the truthfulness of the Prophet, but their insight was veiled. The use of “eyes” specifically emphasizes that the means of clear perception were rendered useless. This description of being deaf and blind is a recurring Quranic motif for those who reject faith. It does not imply that Allah unfairly disabled them; rather, it is the natural consequence of their own repeated turning away. When a person persistently ignores guidance, mocks it, and closes himself off from it, Allah seals that state upon him, and the deprivation becomes fixed. The verse serves as a stark warning to all believers to keep their hearts receptive. It also reassures the faithful that the inability of some to see the truth, no matter how clearly it is presented, is not a failure of the message but a tragic outcome of a condemned spiritual condition. For the early Muslims, it helped them understand why the hypocrites could sit in the Prophet’s company and yet remain utterly unchanged.
24 | Do they not then reflect on the Quran? Or are there locks upon their hearts? | This verse issues a penetrating challenge that cuts to the very root of the problem of hypocrisy and disbelief. The rhetorical question “Do they not then reflect on the Quran?” invites the listener to consider that the Quran itself is the remedy and the criterion. The Arabic word for reflect, tadabbur, means more than a cursory glance; it implies a deep, careful, repeated contemplation that seeks to understand the meaning, the implications, and the application of the verses. The Quran is accessible, its recitation was heard daily, its verses were discussed, and yet these individuals failed to benefit. The question implies that if they had genuinely engaged in tadabbur, their doubts would have dissolved, their hypocrisy would have been exposed to themselves, and the call to sacrifice would have become bearable and even desirable. The second part of the verse, “Or are there locks upon their hearts?” suggests the only alternative explanation for their failure to reflect. A lock upon the heart is a powerful metaphor. A heart that is locked cannot receive anything; whatever is outside, even if it is the most precious light, remains inaccessible. The lock is not placed arbitrarily; it is forged by sins, arrogance, love of the world, and persistent turning away. When the heart is locked, the person hears the verses but the meanings do not settle. The verse thus diagnoses the issue as not a lack of intellectual capacity or insufficient evidence, but a spiritual blockage. This verse had a profound effect on many early Muslims. It is reported that when some companions heard it, they wept and increased their personal engagement with the Quran, fearing that their hearts might become locked. It also challenges modern readers, who may read or listen to the Quran frequently, to ask themselves whether they are truly reflecting or merely passing the words through their ears and minds without letting them penetrate the heart. The verse connects to the preceding verse’s theme of deafness and blindness, concluding that the root of all these sensory blockages is a locked heart, and the key to unlocking it is sincere, humble reflection on the book of Allah.
25 | Indeed, those who relapse ˹into disbelief˺ after ˹true˺ guidance has become clear to them, ˹it is˺ Satan ˹that˺ has tempted them, luring them with false hopes. | The surah now turns to address a particular group of people in Medina who had outwardly accepted Islam but then turned back to disbelief and conspiracy against the faith. The verse begins with “Indeed,” emphasizing the certainty and seriousness of what follows. “Those who relapse into disbelief after true guidance has become clear to them” describes a catastrophic spiritual fall. This is not about someone who never knew the truth; it is about those who recognized the truth, understood its clarity, perhaps even practiced it for a time, but then deliberately abandoned it and returned to disbelief. Historically, this refers to certain individuals among the People of the Book and the hypocrites who initially acknowledged the prophethood of Muhammad, peace be upon him, but when their worldly interests or pride were threatened, they retracted and began working against Islam. The verse then explains the mechanism behind their relapse: “it is Satan that has tempted them, luring them with false hopes.” Satan’s method, described by the verb sawwala, involves beautifying, adorning, and making attractive that which is ugly and false. He whispers that their betrayal is a clever strategy, that their worldly gains are worth the temporary compromise, that they can always repent later, or that the believers are deluded and will eventually fail. The phrase “luring them with false hopes” refers specifically to the extended, empty promises Satan makes. The Arabic word amani includes the concept of long, baseless wishes and fantasies. Satan makes a person imagine a long life with ample time to correct their course, a comfortable existence free from the demands of faith, and a peaceful reconciliation between their disbelief and their worldly success. The verse thus provides both a warning and an insight into spiritual psychology. It warns those who might be wavering that the path of relapse is a trap carefully laid by an enemy who knows their weaknesses. It also explains that the initial clarity of guidance does not permanently immunize a person; constant vigilance and sincere supplication for steadfastness are needed. The mention of Satan’s role does not absolve the individual of responsibility; it exposes the external tempter so that the believer can recognize and resist him.
26 | That is because they said ˹privately˺ to those who ˹also˺ detest what Allah has revealed, “We will obey you in some matters.” But Allah ˹fully˺ knows what they are hiding. | This verse continues the explanation of the hypocrites’ relapse by revealing a specific act of betrayal that occurred behind closed doors. “That is because” signals that what follows is the direct cause of their condemnation and the Satanic seduction described earlier. The verse uncovers a secret conversation: “they said privately to those who also detest what Allah has revealed, ‘We will obey you in some matters.’” The setting is one of conspiracy. These relapsers were not openly confrontational with the believers; instead, they sought out the avowed enemies of Islam, be they the pagan Quraysh or the Jewish tribes who harboured hatred for the revealed message, and made a clandestine pact. The phrase “those who also detest what Allah has revealed” groups them with their interlocutors, showing that their inner state matched that of the open enemies, despite their outward Muslim identity. The promise “We will obey you in some matters” is deliberately vague, allowing them to offer collaboration in plots against the Muslim community while maintaining plausible deniability. It reveals the duplicitous nature of hypocrisy: a willingness to serve opposing causes, taking from each side what suits one’s interests, with no genuine loyalty to any principle. The verse then counters this secret scheming with a powerful declaration: “But Allah fully knows what they are hiding.” No matter how carefully they whisper, how deep their conspiracy is buried, or how skillfully they present a different face in public, the knowledge of Allah encompasses it all. He hears the whispered words, sees the hidden intentions, and is aware of the full scope of their plotting. This statement serves as a comfort to the Prophet and the believers, assuring them that they need not uncover every conspiracy themselves; divine justice is not dependent on human intelligence gathering. It also serves as a terrifying warning to the hypocrites, reminding them that their secrecy is an illusion before the All-Knowing. In a broader lesson, the verse calls every believer to examine his or her own private speech and hidden alliances. Are there areas of life where one pledges obedience to values or groups that detest what Allah has revealed, while outwardly maintaining an Islamic identity? The verse demands absolute sincerity and warns against a divided heart.
27 | Then how ˹horrible˺ will it be when the angels take their souls, beating their faces and backs! | After exposing the secret conspiracies of the hypocrites, the surah now draws their attention away from their hidden meetings and toward the inescapable moment that every human being must face. The verse opens with the exclamation “Then how horrible will it be,” a rhetorical device that evokes dread and forces the listener to imagine a scene of utter terror. The focus is the moment of death, specifically when “the angels take their souls.” For the disbelieving and hypocritical sinners, the angel of death and his assistants do not come with gentle, reassuring words as they do for the believers. Instead, they come as agents of punishment. The description that they will be “beating their faces and backs” is graphic and shocking. The face, being the most honoured part of the body and the seat of identity and dignity, is struck in a manner that brings extreme humiliation. The back is also struck, indicating that the punishment surrounds them from all sides, front and rear, and that there is no escape. According to some exegetes, the beating occurs because the disbelievers try to flee from the angels or turn their faces away in refusal, and the angels strike their faces to drive them forward and their backs to push them from behind. This terrifying extraction of the soul is just the beginning of the afterlife punishment. The verse serves a critical psychological and pedagogical function. The hypocrites of Medina were deeply invested in their worldly safety, their social standing, and the pleasures of their secret dealings. This verse shatters that sense of security by reminding them that the moment of death will strip away all their carefully constructed facades, and no amount of worldly scheming can prevent what is coming. For the believers, it is a verse that stirs the heart to always prepare for that ultimate transition, and to ensure that one’s soul is in a state that will receive the angels with a face of welcome rather than of terror. It also reinforces the principle that hidden crimes, even those concealed from the entire community, will be met with a visible, physical, and painfully real punishment when the divine hour arrives.
28 | This is because they follow whatever displeases Allah and hate whatever pleases Him, so He has rendered their deeds void. | This verse provides the explicit rationale for the horrific punishment described in the previous verse, summarizing the entire life orientation of the hypocrites and apostates in one concise statement. “This is because” answers the unspoken question of why they deserve such a dreadful end. The first part of the answer is that “they follow whatever displeases Allah.” Their guiding principle in life was not truth, morality, or divine command, but the pursuit of whatever gratified their egos, their desires, and their worldly ambitions, regardless of whether it incurred divine wrath. Following what displeases Allah includes engaging in secret conspiracies, mocking the believers, obstructing the path of Islam, and aligning with the enemies of the faith. The second, complementary aspect is that they “hate whatever pleases Him.” They did not merely neglect obedience; they developed an active aversion to the things that bring Allah’s pleasure, such as sincere faith, struggle in His way, charitable spending, humility before the truth, and loyalty to the Prophet and the community of believers. This hatred could manifest as resentment toward religious obligations, annoyance at the Quran’s moral constraints, or disdain for the simple and devoted believers. The combination of these two traits—actively pursuing what angers Allah and detesting what pleases Him—defines a state of enmity against the divine will itself. The result, repeated throughout this surah, is then stated: “so He has rendered their deeds void.” Even the prayers they may have performed outwardly, the charity they may have given for show, and the kinship ties they maintained for social pride are stripped of any eternal value. The deeds are left like a mirage, appearing as something but utterly hollow in the scales of the Hereafter. This verse forces a believer to examine his or her own internal compass. It is not enough to avoid major sins; one must also cultivate a love for what pleases Allah and a distaste for what displeases Him, so that the heart and limbs are aligned with the direction that gives deeds their lasting weight.
29 | Or do those with sickness in their hearts think that Allah will not ˹be able to˺ expose their malice? | The surah now returns to the theme of the hypocrites’ hidden corruption, directly challenging their assumption that they can remain undetected. The opening “Or” connects this verse to the preceding discussion, as if to say: Do they imagine that their secret plotting and their ability to hide their true feelings will continue indefinitely? The phrase “those with sickness in their hearts” is the Quranic diagnosis of hypocrisy. The sickness here includes doubt, envy, hatred of the truth, and a diseased attachment to worldly power. The question “think that Allah will not expose their malice?” uses the word malice, or adghan in Arabic, which refers to deep-seated rancour, spite, and hidden hatred. The hypocrites harboured intense animosity toward the Prophet and the believers, but they carefully covered it with a mask of courtesy and false piety. They thought that as long as they managed their public relations skilfully, their secret would remain safe. This verse challenges that delusion by invoking Allah’s power and will to expose them. The exposure could happen in various ways: through a slip of the tongue, through a situation that forces their true colours to appear, through a revelation that identifies their traits, or ultimately on the Day of Judgment when all secrets are laid bare. Historically, some hypocrites were indeed exposed during the lifetime of the Prophet. Abdullah ibn Ubayy, the head of the hypocrites, had his true nature made manifest through several incidents, and the Prophet, informed by revelation, even refused to lead his funeral prayer. The verse teaches that hidden evil cannot be hidden from God, and that He has the power to unveil it whenever He wills, for the protection of the community and as a lesson. For the individual believer, it prompts a serious introspection about any malice one might be nurturing in the heart, hidden even from close friends and family. The awareness that Allah can expose what is within should motivate a person to cleanse the heart of rancour, envy, and hatred, replacing them with sincerity and goodwill, so that if any exposure occurs, it is an exposure of good rather than of malice.
30 | Had We willed, We could have truly shown them to you ˹O Prophet˺, and you would have certainly recognized them by their appearance. But you will surely recognize them by their tone of speech. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows your doings ˹O people˺. | This verse directly addresses the Prophet, comforting him and educating him about the nature of the hypocrites in his midst. “Had We willed, We could have truly shown them to you, and you would have certainly recognized them by their appearance.” This indicates that Allah possesses the power to make the hypocrites physically identifiable, perhaps by a mark on their faces or a distinct sign that would leave no room for doubt. The fact that He did not do so is part of the divine wisdom; the test of this world requires that people’s inner states be veiled so that they may exercise their free will and that the community may learn to deal with matters of the heart with careful judgment rather than relying on supernatural labels. However, the Prophet is not left without a means of discernment. The verse continues, “But you will surely recognize them by their tone of speech.” The Arabic word lahn refers to the style, tone, inflection, and subtle mannerisms in a person’s speech that reveal their underlying intentions and attitudes. The hypocrites might say the right words, but their tone carries a hint of mockery, resentment, or insincerity that a perceptive listener can detect. The Prophet, with his divinely sharpened insight, was particularly skilled at this. For example, it is reported that he could detect the hypocrisy of certain individuals simply from the way they spoke about the believers or about a revealed command. The verse then concludes with a broader declaration: “And Allah fully knows your doings, O people.” This extends the scope beyond the Prophet’s recognition to remind everyone, both believer and hypocrite, that their deeds are fully known to Allah. Nothing is hidden. The practical lesson for the community is that while we do not claim to know people’s hearts, we are encouraged to be perceptive, to listen carefully to the discourse of those around us, and to gauge sincerity from consistent behaviour and speech. It also teaches believers to watch their own tone of speech when discussing matters of faith, ensuring that it reflects genuine love and respect rather than a hidden complaint or doubt.
31 | We will certainly test you ˹believers˺ until We prove those of you who ˹truly˺ struggle ˹in Allah’s cause˺ and remain steadfast, and reveal how you conduct yourselves. | After the exposure of the hypocrites, the surah turns back to the sincere believers with a declaration that explains the divine purpose behind the trials they were facing, including the command to fight and the presence of hypocrites among them. The verse opens with “We will certainly test you,” a statement of absolute certainty that establishes testing as a universal law for all who claim faith. The test is not a sign of abandonment but a necessary process. The purpose is explicitly stated: “until We prove those of you who truly struggle in Allah’s cause and remain steadfast.” The Arabic word for struggle, mujahidin, includes both the act of physical fighting when required and the broader spiritual struggle against one’s own desires and against the forces of falsehood. To be proven means that the reality of one’s faith becomes manifest in action, distinguished from a mere claim. The second quality, “and remain steadfast,” refers to patience, perseverance, and constancy in the face of hardship. Struggle might be a burst of effort, but steadfastness is the quality that sustains that effort over time, through pain, loss, and fear. The test thus sifts the community, separating the genuine from the counterfeit. The verse adds, “and reveal how you conduct yourselves,” indicating that the test also exposes the true nature of people’s characters. News of one’s good or bad conduct spreads, and reputations are formed based on what trials bring out of a person. The early Muslims endured severe tests: the migration, the battles of Badr, Uhud, and the Trench, the internal sabotage of the hypocrites, and the constant threat of poverty and loss. Each of these trials was a manifestation of this verse, providing opportunities for individuals to rise in ranks through sincere struggle and patient endurance. For the believer today, this verse provides a framework for understanding life’s difficulties. Financial hardship, health crises, temptations, and social pressures are all tests that prove the truthfulness of one’s claim to faith. Instead of asking “Why me?”, the believer learns to ask “How can I prove my sincerity through this?” The verse transforms the perception of trials from meaningless suffering into a purposeful examination conducted by a merciful Lord who rewards every effort.
32 | Indeed, those who disbelieve, hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and defy the Messenger after ˹true˺ guidance has become clear to them; they will not harm Allah in the least, but He will render their deeds void. | This verse serves as a powerful refrain, revisiting the themes of the surah’s opening but with added intensity. It lists three grave offences: disbelief, actively hindering others from the path of Allah, and defying the Messenger after the truth has become unmistakably clear. The sequence is cumulative. Disbelief is the root, hindering others is the branch that multiplies evil, and defying the Messenger is the personal, rebellious act that compounds the crime. The phrase “after true guidance has become clear to them” is critical; it removes the excuse of ignorance. The Quraysh leaders, for example, knew the Prophet’s truthfulness intimately. They called him Al-Amin, the Trustworthy, for decades before his prophethood, and they recognized the Quran’s inimitability. Their defiance was not out of confusion but out of arrogant resistance. Similarly, the Jewish tribes in Medina recognized the Prophet’s description in their scriptures but rejected him out of tribal pride and worldly interest. The verse then makes a profound theological statement: “they will not harm Allah in the least.” Human rebellion, no matter how widespread or powerful, does not diminish the majesty, sovereignty, or perfection of the Creator. Allah is entirely self-sufficient. The harm of their actions rebounds only upon themselves, corrupting their own souls and societies. The consequence is therefore directed at them: “but He will render their deeds void.” This echoes the very first verse of the surah, creating a structural ring that emphasizes the central message. Even the most impressive worldly achievements of such people, the charities, the cultural advancements, the legacy they sought to build, will be stripped of all reward on the Day of Judgment because the foundation of faith was absent and the active enmity toward the truth corrupted everything. For the believer, this verse offers consolation when witnessing the apparent success of those who oppose the faith. Their power is an illusion; they cannot harm Allah, and their ultimate loss is guaranteed, so one should not envy their position or despair at their temporary dominance.
33 | O believers! Obey Allah and obey the Messenger, and do not let your deeds be in vain. | This brief but immensely weighty verse is a direct call to the community, summarizing the path to safety and the preservation of good works. The address “O believers!” reminds them of the bond of faith that ties them together and obligates them to heed what follows. The command is twofold: “Obey Allah and obey the Messenger.” Obedience to Allah is fulfilled by following the Quran, the divine commands, and the principles of faith. The repetition of the verb “obey” before “the Messenger” emphasizes that obedience to the Prophet is a distinct and necessary requirement, not merely a subset of obedience to Allah. The Prophet is the one who explains, demonstrates, and embodies the divine message, and so his authentic teachings and example must be followed. This dual obedience is the comprehensive formula for a life that pleases God. The verse then issues a warning that directly ties to the fate of the hypocrites and disbelievers repeatedly described in this surah: “and do not let your deeds be in vain.” The Arabic word for “in vain,” ibtal, means to nullify, cancel, or render worthless. After hearing so much about the deeds of the disbelievers being rendered void, the believers are instructed to actively guard against the same fate. Deeds can become vain through various means: by following obedience with disobedience, by hypocrisy, by showing off, by apostasy, by harbouring resentment against the commands of the religion, or by a lack of sincerity in the heart. The fear instilled by this warning was deeply felt by the companions, who were constantly vigilant about their intentions and the consistency of their actions. This verse serves as a regular check for every believer. After performing a good deed, one should not become complacent but should reflect on whether any subsequent behaviour might nullify its reward. It ties the entire practical discourse of the surah together: all the commands regarding fighting, steadfastness, and spending are wrapped in this call to sincere, unwavering obedience that ensures the fruits of one’s efforts are preserved for the Hereafter.
34 | Surely those who disbelieve, hinder ˹others˺ from the Way of Allah, and then die as disbelievers; Allah will never forgive them. | The surah now closes the door on a specific false hope that some may have entertained: the idea that a person who spent their life opposing the truth could somehow find forgiveness at the end without genuine repentance. The verse begins with “Surely,” a particle of strong emphasis. It again lists the same three compounding crimes: disbelief, hindering others from the path of Allah, and the crucial additional condition, “and then die as disbelievers.” The emphasis on the state at death is central to the verse’s message. The verse does not address those who committed these sins but then repented sincerely before death. The Quran is replete with verses that open the door of repentance for all sins, and many of the fiercest enemies of Islam in Mecca, like Abu Sufyan and Ikrimah ibn Abi Jahl, eventually embraced the faith and were forgiven. This verse instead describes those who persist in their disbelief and obstruction until the very end, dying in that state without ever turning back to God. For such people, the verdict is absolute and terrifying: “Allah will never forgive them.” The negation of forgiveness is final because the opportunity to believe and repent has expired. Death closes the account, and whatever comes after is based entirely on what preceded it. The verse serves a dual function. For the disbelievers, it is a final, urgent warning to not delay repentance, because the continued pattern of hindering truth and clinging to disbelief can harden the heart, making death in that state a real and catastrophic possibility. For the believers, it solidifies their understanding of divine justice and prevents them from lapsing into a sentimental universalism that imagines forgiveness for those who actively fight against God until their last breath without any change. It also provides a stark contrast to the promises of forgiveness for the believers, urging them to value the gift of faith and to strive to maintain it until death, so that they may be among those whose sins are absolved rather than those for whom forgiveness is eternally barred.
35 | So do not falter or cry for peace, for you will have the upper hand and Allah is with you. And He will never let your deeds go to waste. | In this verse, the surah directly addresses the believing fighters and the community at a moment when weakness and the desire for an easy resolution might have crept into their hearts. The command “So do not falter” derives from a root that means to be weak, to lose heart, or to show feebleness in the face of the enemy. It is a call to maintain courage, energy, and resolve. The second prohibition, “or cry for peace,” addresses the temptation to sue for a humiliating truce or to abandon the struggle prematurely out of fear or war-weariness. In the context of the early Medinan battles, the Muslims faced tremendous pressure. The enemy was numerically superior, better equipped, and motivated by deep hatred. The natural human response was to feel intimidated and to wish for the fighting to end at any cost. This verse prohibits that specific response when it stems from weakness rather than from a position of strength and wisdom. The reasons for this prohibition are then given in the form of three powerful reassurances. First, “for you will have the upper hand.” This is a divine promise of eventual victory and dominance for the believers. It is not conditional on their numbers or material power but on the truth they carry and the help of Allah. Second, “and Allah is with you.” This is the ultimate guarantee. The presence of Allah with the believers, in the sense of His support, protection, and guidance, ensures that they cannot be truly defeated in the final analysis. Third, “And He will never let your deeds go to waste.” The struggles, sacrifices, hardships, and even the moments of fear endured for His cause are all being preserved and will be rewarded in full. None of the pain or effort is lost. This verse does not command reckless aggression or forbid negotiated peace under all circumstances. It specifically forbids faltering and suing for peace from a position of cowardice and despair. When the Muslims later grew strong, the Prophet did agree to the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah, but that was from a position of strategic wisdom, not fear. The lesson for the believer in any struggle, whether against external enemies or internal desires, is to reject despondency and to trust that Allah’s help will come if one remains steadfast, and that no sincere effort in His path is ever wasted.
36 | This worldly life is no more than play and amusement. But if you are faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, He will grant you your ˹full˺ reward, and will not ask you ˹to donate all˺ your wealth. | The surah now anchors the entire preceding discussion of struggle, spending, and sacrifice in a correct understanding of the true value of this world. The opening statement, “This worldly life is no more than play and amusement,” is a recurring Quranic theme that does not deny the reality of worldly pleasures but categorizes them in relation to the Hereafter. Play and amusement are activities that are temporary, distracting, and ultimately insubstantial. They can engage a person for a while but produce nothing of lasting value. The entire dunya, with its wealth, rivalries, adornments, and comforts, is like a game that children play and then abandon when it is time to go home. This perspective is essential for a believer who is asked to sacrifice wealth and life; only when one sees the world as transient can one give it up freely. The verse then provides the alternative focus: “But if you are faithful and mindful of Allah.” Faithfulness refers to maintaining belief and fulfilling its obligations, while mindfulness, or taqwa, means living with constant consciousness of Allah, avoiding what He forbids, and acting with reverent care. Those who combine these two qualities are given two glad tidings. First, “He will grant you your full reward.” The reward is not diminished or delayed; it will be given in its entirety in the Hereafter, compensating many times over for whatever was sacrificed. Second, “and will not ask you to donate all your wealth.” This is a specific mercy that addresses a possible concern of the believers. Some might have worried that the call to spend in the path of Allah meant giving up everything they owned, leaving themselves destitute. The verse clarifies that while some earlier nations may have been given such rigorous obligations, this ummah is not required to donate all its wealth. Islam only obligates the zakat, a small percentage, and encourages voluntary charity according to one’s ability. The door of goodness is open without imposing an unbearable burden. This verse beautifully balances the call to detachment from the world with a practical, merciful alleviation of fear, encouraging generosity without demanding impoverishment.
37 | If He were to do so and pressure you, you would withhold and He would bring out your resentment. | This verse continues the theme of divine mercy in legislation by explaining the wisdom behind not obligating the believers to donate all their wealth. It speaks directly about human nature with profound insight. “If He were to do so and pressure you,” refers to the hypothetical scenario in which Allah had demanded the entirety of their possessions. The verb “pressure” conveys the sense of urgency and heavy insistence that would accompany such a demand. The verse then predicts the human response with absolute certainty: “you would withhold.” Despite any initial enthusiasm or claims of devotion, the average human heart would eventually cling to its wealth, finding the demand too harsh and too threatening to its worldly security. This is not a condemnation but a recognition of the psychological reality that Allah Himself has created. People naturally love wealth and find it extremely difficult to part with it entirely. The second part of the prediction is even more revealing: “and He would bring out your resentment.” The Arabic word adghan, translated as resentment, is the same deep-seated rancour and hidden grudge mentioned earlier in the surah. If an overly strict obligation were imposed, the believers, even if they outwardly complied, would secretly harbour feelings of bitterness, frustration, and resentment against the religion. This inner resentment would be spiritually destructive, poisoning their relationship with Allah and with the community. By not imposing such a heavy obligation, Allah protects the believers from falling into this spiritual disease. He knows that obedience must be sustainable, and that the goal is not to extract maximum wealth but to purify hearts and build a community of willing, joyful givers. This verse demonstrates the divine attribute of gentleness and the practical wisdom of Islamic law. Obligations are calibrated to what humans can bear, and the door to voluntary supererogatory giving remains open for those with stronger faith and more abundant resources. The early believers would have heard this and felt immense gratitude, recognizing that their Lord was not seeking to burden them but to guide them toward a path that is both spiritually elevating and realistically achievable.
38 | Here you are, being invited to donate ˹a little˺ in the cause of Allah. Still some of you withhold. And whoever does so, it is only to their own loss. For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need ˹of Him˺. If you ˹still˺ turn away, He will replace you with another people. And they will not be like you. | The final verse of the surah brings the discussion of spending and sacrifice to a sharp, poignant conclusion by directly confronting the reality within the community. The opening phrase, “Here you are, being invited to donate a little in the cause of Allah,” emphasizes the present, ongoing nature of the call. The required donation is described as “a little,” highlighting the contrast with the hypothetical demand for all wealth mentioned in the previous verses. Islam only asks for the obligatory zakat and encourages whatever additional charity one can afford, a truly minimal portion compared to the total blessings one receives. Yet, despite the ease of this invitation, the verse laments, “Still some of you withhold.” This exposes the stinginess present in certain hearts, even among those who profess faith. The verse then delivers a decisive blow to the logic of withholding: “And whoever does so, it is only to their own loss.” The miser does not harm Allah, the community, or the cause in any ultimate sense by withholding. The only person harmed is the miser himself, who loses out on the reward, the purification of the soul, and the divine pleasure that comes from generous giving. This is reinforced by the majestic declaration, “For Allah is the Self-Sufficient, whereas you stand in need of Him.” Allah, Al-Ghani, has no need for any of creation’s wealth or charity. He is absolutely rich and independent. In contrast, every human being is in a state of total poverty before Him, utterly dependent on His provision, mercy, and guidance at every instant. The idea that one is doing Allah a favour by donating is thus completely inverted; charity is a favor Allah bestows upon the giver, an opportunity to benefit oneself. The surah then ends with a powerful warning that also contains a note of honour for those who respond: “If you still turn away, He will replace you with another people. And they will not be like you.” This final sentence summarizes the principle that the mission of Islam is not dependent on any particular group. If the Arabs of that time, or the current community, fail in their duty, Allah can and will raise a new people who will love Him, serve Him, and give generously. The description “they will not be like you” is both a threat to the stingy and a promise of a better, more devoted community. The surah ends on this stirring note, leaving the listener with a profound sense of responsibility and a fear of being replaced by those who are more sincere and more willing to sacrifice.
