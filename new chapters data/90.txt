1 | I do swear by this city ˹of Mecca˺— | The surah opens with a solemn oath that arrests attention immediately: "I do swear by this city." When Allah swears by something, He draws our focus to its immense significance. The phrase "this city" refers unmistakably to Mecca, the sacred sanctuary where the Kaaba stands. That city is the birthplace of the Prophet, the location of the first house of worship built for humanity, and a place Allah designated as a haven of safety. Yet the oath comes with a piercing irony. At the time this surah was revealed in Mecca, the very people who claimed to be the custodians of the sacred city had filled it with idolatry, oppression, and the persecution of the Messenger who called them to the pure faith of Abraham. The oath is not merely a geographical reference; it is a moral indictment. By swearing "by this city," Allah reminds the Quraysh that they are standing on holy ground and their deeds are witnessed by the Lord of that city. The word "I do swear" underscores the certainty of the message that follows. In the Qur’an, divine oaths often introduce truths that people tend to neglect or dispute. Here, the oath sets the stage for a profound revelation about human nature and accountability. The listener is forced to think: what is so special about this city that the Almighty swears by it? Mecca is the valley where Abraham left Hagar and Ishmael, where the well of Zamzam gushed forth, and where the Prophet would eventually return victorious, purifying it from idols. By swearing by Mecca, Allah simultaneously honours the city and rebukes those who violate its sanctity. The oath also intimately links the city to the Prophet’s own experience, which the next verse immediately brings into focus, showing that the honour of the place had been trampled by the abuse heaped upon Allah’s beloved. Thus the opening verse is both a declaration of love for a blessed place and a warning that sacred locations do not benefit people unless they honour the faith they are called to.
2 | even though you ˹O Prophet˺ are subject to abuse in this city— | The verse continues the solemn oath with a statement that heightens its gravity: "even though you O Prophet are subject to abuse in this city." The original wording carries the sense that the Prophet is made lawful to be attacked, that his sanctity has been violated precisely where it should have been most protected. The parenthesis "O Prophet" in the translation makes the addressee clear: it is the Messenger himself being addressed. This direct address comforts the Prophet while at the same time exposing the criminality of his persecutors. In Mecca, the Quraysh called him a liar, a sorcerer, and a madman. They threw filth upon him, they strangled him while he prayed, they placed thorns in his path, and they imposed a merciless boycott on his family and followers. All this happened inside the sacred precincts where bloodshed and oppression were supposed to be forbidden. The verse therefore expresses a painful paradox: the city that Allah swore by is the very city where His Messenger is made to suffer unjustly. By swearing by Mecca "even though you are subject to abuse" there, Allah testifies that the Prophet’s suffering is not hidden from Him, that the sacredness of the city is being desecrated, and that a day of reckoning will surely come. The phrase acts as a divine witness statement. It also reassures the Prophet that his position is honoured in the heavens despite the humiliation he endures on earth. Moreover, it prepares the listener for the theme of struggle that follows. The Prophet is living the reality of human hardship in the same city that the disbelievers think gives them status. Their abuse of him is itself proof of their corruption and their false claim to be the rightful inheritors of Abraham’s legacy. For believers today, this verse offers immense consolation. When a person of faith feels isolated, mocked, or mistreated—even in places that are supposed to be safe or sacred—they can remember that the Prophet experienced the same, and that Allah was fully aware of every insult and injury. The abuse in the city did not mean Allah had abandoned him; it meant the city’s people had abandoned their own honour.
3 | and by every parent and ˹their˺ child! | The oath now expands from the blessed city to the fundamental human bond: "and by every parent and their child!" This sweeping expression encompasses every father and mother and every son and daughter across time. Some scholars explain that it refers specifically to Adam and all his offspring, making the oath cover the entirety of the human family. Others say it refers to Abraham and Ishmael, the father and son who built the Kaaba and prayed for this very city, thus linking the oath back to Mecca’s origins. Yet the more general understanding embraces all parents and children, and that gives the verse a universal dimension. The parent-child relationship is at the core of human existence. Through it, life continues, values are transmitted, and societies are built. A parent sacrifices, nurtures, and hopes. A child represents both vulnerability and future. By swearing by this bond, Allah draws our attention to the moral and emotional structure He has placed in our nature. The disbelievers to whom the surah is addressed understood the sacredness of lineage; they prided themselves on ancestry. But their arrogance had corrupted even this natural tie, as they often neglected orphans and severed kinship obligations when pride or commerce demanded it. The oath by parent and child therefore serves as a subtle rebuke: honour the bond that Allah has made a sign of His creative wisdom. The pairing with the previous oath by the city also suggests that just as the holy city should have been a place of peace, so the family should be a haven of mercy. The Prophet himself was an orphan; his own parent-child experience was marked by loss, yet he became a mercy to children and an exhorter to parent-kindness. This verse prepares the ground for what follows, because the struggle of human life described next begins from birth itself—the painful labour of the mother, the helplessness of the child, and the ongoing duty of care. Thus the oath speaks to the deepest threads of human connection and responsibility.
4 | Indeed, We have created humankind in ˹constant˺ struggle. | With this verse the surah moves from oaths to the core statement about the human condition: "Indeed, We have created humankind in constant struggle." The word translated as "constant struggle" carries the rich meaning of toil, hardship, distress, and continual exertion. Human life, from the moment of conception to the grave, is filled with effort and difficulty. The child struggles to be born, the mother endures the pangs of labour, the infant struggles to learn to crawl and walk, the student wrestles with learning, the adult labours to earn a livelihood and raise a family, and every soul faces illness, loss, and eventually death. Even the wealthiest and most powerful cannot escape this reality; their struggles may be of different kinds—boredom, anxiety, betrayal, the fear of losing what they have hoarded. This verse establishes that struggle is not a divine punishment falling only on the sinful; it is woven into the very fabric of human existence. The statement serves as a direct response to the arrogant attitude the next verses will condemn. There were wealthy disbelievers in Mecca who imagined that their riches proved they were favoured and beyond accountability. They mistook material ease for being exempt from life’s inherent struggle. But Allah declares that every human being, without exception, has been created into a condition of hardship. The Prophets lived this most acutely. The Prophet Muhammad, despite being the most beloved of creation, faced orphanhood, poverty, persecution, the loss of children, and relentless opposition. His struggle was the greatest, yet it was not a sign of being forsaken; it was the path to the highest station. Understanding this verse transforms a believer’s outlook on hardship. Instead of complaining that life is hard, the believer recognises that difficulty is the normal design, and what matters is the direction of one’s striving. Does one struggle for truth, justice, and goodness, or does one exhaust oneself in arrogance and the accumulation of fleeting wealth? The phrase "constant struggle" is a key that unlocks the rest of the surah, for immediately after this, the two ways of dealing with struggle are unveiled.
5 | Do they think that no one has power over them, | The surah now turns to confront a specific type of arrogance born from forgetfulness of the human condition just described: "Do they think that no one has power over them?" The question is rhetorical, filled with warning. The wealthy disbelievers of Mecca, intoxicated by their affluence and status, behaved as though they were completely autonomous, as if no power could ever subdue them or call them to account. They were secure in their fortresses, their trade caravans journeyed safely, and their idols stood in the Kaaba. They looked at the poor and the oppressed believers with contempt, confident that might meant right. This verse shatters that illusion by posing a simple question that forces them to think: is there truly no one with power over them? The implied answer is that Allah, the Creator who gave them life and can take it away, who can strike them with calamity, disease, or death, certainly has absolute power over them. On a deeper level, the verse challenges the belief that they can escape resurrection and judgment. They acted as though their wealth purchased immunity from consequences, both in this life and in the next. The phrasing "no one has power over them" also hints at the arrogance of thinking they can never be overcome by an enemy, a famine, or a reversal of fortune. But history had shown many mighty civilizations crumble. The question is meant to unsettle the conscience. It prepares for the boast that follows in the next verse, because the claim that one has "wasted enormous wealth" is rooted precisely in this delusion of being untouchable. For the contemporary reader, the verse remains sharp: how many people today live as if they will never face a day of reckoning, as if their money, connections, or technology have placed them beyond the reach of any higher authority? The verse calls us to pause, look at the night sky, and remember the One whose power governs the universe and whose grasp will close around every arrogant soul when death arrives and the scales are set.
6 | boasting, “I have wasted enormous wealth!”? | The verse zooms in on the arrogance just questioned by quoting the boast itself: "boasting, 'I have wasted enormous wealth!'?" According to several early commentators, this refers to a specific incident. A wealthy Quraysh chief—said by some to be al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah or al-As ibn Wa’il—would loudly proclaim that he had spent and exhausted huge sums of money in enmity to Muhammad and the Qur’an, believing this expenditure proved his power and the falsehood of the message. He bragged about draining his wealth to oppose the truth, as if to say: "Look how much I have sacrificed to combat this religion; if it were true, would I waste such riches?" The word "wasted" implies spending not for good but for destruction and hostility. In his delusion, he considered this a badge of honour. The verse exposes the utter perversion of values: the very wealth that could have been a means of earning Paradise through charity, freeing slaves, and feeding the hungry was instead squandered on blocking the path to guidance. The boast is presented by Allah with a question mark that drips with condemnation—"boasting, 'I have wasted enormous wealth!'?" It is as if Allah says: is this really something to be proud of? The man thought he was demonstrating strength, but in reality he was demonstrating the depth of his own loss. He had toiled and struggled to amass wealth, only to throw it into a fire of enmity that would benefit no one, least of all himself on the Day of Judgment. The verse also highlights a psychological trap: the need to publicly proclaim one's spending to gain status and intimidate others. The disbeliever wanted his rivals and followers to know how much he had sacrificed, hoping to rally support and demoralize the believers. But Allah reduces his supposed epic sacrifice to a foolish boast. For believers, this verse serves as a powerful mirror. Wealth spent in showing off, in harming others, or in opposing truth is not a sign of success but of being utterly lost, regardless of the numbers.
7 | Do they think that no one sees them? | The surah continues to dismantle the arrogant mindset by adding a second piercing question: "Do they think that no one sees them?" The boastful spender imagined that his wealth and public proclamations were witnessed only by his admirers, that his secret scheming and his motivations were hidden from any real accountability. He may have thought that because no human authority could challenge him, he was operating in complete secrecy as far as ultimate consequences were concerned. The verse demolishes this assumption. It reminds him—and every person who acts as if they are unobserved—that there is an all-seeing Witness whose gaze never sleeps. Allah sees not only the outward spending and the boastful words, but the inner arrogance, the hatred of the truth, and the intention to extinguish the light of revelation. The Arabic phrasing is simple yet devastating: does he reckon that no one sees him? The disbeliever would have to answer, if he were honest, that of course there is a Creator who sees. But his actions betrayed a functional atheism; he lived as though the unseen world were a myth. This verse connects directly to the earlier statement that humankind is created in constant struggle, for one of the greatest struggles is to live with the constant awareness that every deed is being recorded. The verse also sets the stage for the mention of the two eyes and the tongue and lips in the following passage. The very organs that enable the disbeliever to boast and to see the Prophet as an object of ridicule are gifts from the One who observes how they are used. Nothing escapes Him—not the flicker of a glance, not the whisper of a plot. For the believer, this verse cultivates the quality of ihsan, which the Prophet described as "to worship Allah as if you see Him, and if you cannot see Him, know that He sees you." The man who boasted of his wasted wealth forgot that his boast was being recorded against him, and that the wealth he thought he had destroyed would one day testify about its misuse.
8 | Have We not given them two eyes, | After the rhetorical questions about power and observation, the surah shifts to a series of reminders about Allah’s generous gifts, beginning with: "Have We not given them two eyes?" The question draws the listener to reflect on a blessing that is so familiar it is often taken for granted. The gift of two eyes, working in coordination to provide depth perception and a world of colour and light, is an intricate miracle. Through the eyes, a person reads the Qur’an, sees the signs of creation spread across the heavens and the earth, recognises the faces of loved ones, and avoids dangers on the path. The disbeliever to whom this is addressed used his eyes to look with contempt upon the believers, to gaze at his own wealth with pride, and to search for ways to harm the Prophet. The question "Have We not given them" implies that these eyes are a trust. They have been granted by the same Creator who will ask how they were employed. The pairing of two eyes rather than one is itself a profound favour; if one is injured, the other remains. Their placement in the front of the head allows forward movement and study. The verse naturally flows into the next, which mentions the tongue and lips, because together they enable the disbeliever to articulate his boast and witness the world around him. By reminding him of his eyes, Allah is saying: I gave you the instruments of perception; did you use them to perceive the truth or to blind yourself to it? The verse also prepares the way for the mention of the two paths, because with eyesight a person is able to discern road signs and choose a direction. Similarly, the spiritual insight that should accompany physical sight enables a person to distinguish between the way of right and the way of wrong. Thus the eyes are not merely biological marvels; they are symbols of moral responsibility and the capacity to seek guidance.
9 | a tongue, and two lips; | The list of blessings continues: "a tongue, and two lips." These two are mentioned together because they work in perfect partnership to produce speech. The tongue, a muscular organ capable of an astonishing range of movements, shapes sounds that are then modulated and articulated by the lips. Without lips, speech would be slurred and indistinct; without the tongue, the most expressive thoughts would remain trapped inside. By specifically pointing to the combination of the tongue and two lips, Allah draws attention to the very faculties the boasting disbeliever used to utter his arrogant declaration, "I have wasted enormous wealth!" The same tongue and lips could have proclaimed the testimony of faith, spoken words of kindness, or recited the verses of the Qur’an. Instead, they were employed in pride and enmity. This verse contains a powerful lesson about the gift of communication. Human beings are uniquely equipped among earthly creatures with the ability to articulate complex ideas, to teach, to comfort, and to warn. The tongue can be a source of immense good or catastrophic evil. The Prophet taught that most people are thrown into the Fire on their faces because of the harvest of their tongues, meaning that reckless speech often leads to destruction. Here, the disbeliever’s boast is a perfect example of the tongue’s misuse. The mention of the lips also evokes the idea that speech can be controlled and restrained; the lips act as a gate that can be closed. The believer uses that gate to hold back falsehood, gossip, and arrogance. This verse, together with the previous one about the eyes, builds an overwhelming case against the disbeliever: Allah provided the hardware for seeing and speaking, and then, as the next verse states, He even provided the guidance to know what to see and what to say. No one can claim they lacked the tools to find and follow the truth.
10 | and shown them the two ways ˹of right and wrong˺? | The rhetorical sequence culminates with the most decisive gift: "and shown them the two ways of right and wrong?" Allah has not only given physical faculties like eyesight, a tongue, and lips; He has also provided moral and spiritual guidance, making the distinction between good and evil clear. The expression "the two ways" refers to the paths of virtue and vice, belief and disbelief, Paradise and Hell. Some early commentators linked the phrase to the two paths that appear in the geography of a mountain pass, but the dominant interpretation is the ethical and eschatological dual path. The addition in the translation "of right and wrong" captures the essence. This showing of the two ways has been done through multiple channels: the innate natural disposition that inclines towards recognising the Creator, the sending of prophets with scriptures, and the countless signs within oneself and in the universe. So the disbeliever cannot argue that he was left without direction or that he did not know. He was shown the steep ascent of goodness as well as the slippery descent of evil. He chose to ignore the guidance and instead boasted about squandering wealth in enmity. The question mark at the end of the verse brings the argument to a powerful climax. After recalling the blessings of eyes, tongue, and lips, Allah essentially asks: with all these gifts and with the two ways plainly displayed, what excuse do you have for your arrogance? The verse serves as a bridge to the following section, which explains the challenging path of goodness. Having established that every human is created in struggle and shown two ways, the surah now invites the listener to examine which path they have taken. The verse also reminds the believer that guidance is a trust; knowing the difference between right and wrong increases accountability, and the truly wise person uses their God-given faculties and knowledge to climb the steep path rather than sliding down the easy slope of arrogance.
11 | If only they had attempted the challenging path ˹of goodness instead˺! | After exposing the arrogance of those who waste wealth and deny accountability, the tone shifts from condemnation to a poignant expression of regret and invitation: "If only they had attempted the challenging path of goodness instead!" The phrase "challenging path" translates a word that literally means a steep, difficult mountain road. In the landscape of Arabia, ascending a steep pass requires effort, sweat, endurance, and determination. The metaphor is instantly vivid to the original listeners. Instead of spending their wealth in showing off and fighting the truth, they should have directed their struggle towards this uphill climb of righteousness. The expression "if only they had attempted" conveys a sense of a missed opportunity, a door that was open but left ignored. It is a lament, yet also an implicit call to the listener: it is not too late to turn to that path. The addition of "instead" in the translation emphasizes the contrast between the two ways just mentioned. The disbeliever chose the broad, easy-looking road of arrogance, while the steep path of goodness lay before him unrecognized. The verse makes clear that the path to salvation is not one of comfort and indulgence; it is inherently challenging. Climbing it means struggling against one’s ego, sacrificing wealth and desires, and persisting in good works even when they are difficult. This directly ties back to the earlier declaration that humankind is created in constant struggle. The difference is whether that inevitable struggle is channeled into destructive boasting or into a purposeful ascent towards Allah. The verse thus redefines success: it is not about how much wealth one can waste to gain worldly reputation, but about whether one is willing to take on the arduous but honourable climb that leads to divine pleasure. By using the rhetorical "if only," the verse gently stirs the heart to wish for that better choice, setting the stage for the detailed explanation of what exactly that challenging path entails in the next verses.
12 | And what will make you realize what ˹attempting˺ the challenging path is? | The surah now employs a powerful Qur’anic device to magnify the importance of what is about to be described: "And what will make you realize what attempting the challenging path is?" The question is not asked because the answer is unknown, but to create a sense of awe and anticipation. The phrasing "what will make you realize" suggests that this reality is so immense that ordinary human imagination cannot fully grasp it without divine clarification. The listener’s attention is gripped; the mind is pulled away from the mundane boasting about wasted wealth and directed towards something of far greater weight. The challenging path is not merely a figure of speech—it is a concrete set of actions and qualities that define the life of a true believer. By asking the question and then immediately answering it in the subsequent verses, Allah teaches that the steep path cannot be adequately defined by human speculation. Only the revelation can draw back the curtain and show what true, uphill goodness looks like. The question also serves as a subtle rebuke to the arrogant disbeliever who thought that his wasteful spending was a monumental achievement. Allah is saying: you have no idea what real difficulty and real accomplishment are. The challenging path involves freeing a slave, feeding the hungry, and embodying faith and mutual encouragement. These are the deeds that actually matter. The question creates a dramatic pause, as if the speaker pauses to make sure every heart is attentive before unveiling the description. For the believer reciting this surah, the question invites a moment of self-examination: "Have I truly understood the steep path? Have I been climbing it, or have I settled for something far easier and far less meaningful?" The verse, though brief, functions as a hinge, turning the surah from the depiction of the boastful disbeliever to the portrait of the righteous soul. Its role is to ensure that the following verses are received with the seriousness they deserve.
13 | It is to free a slave, | Now the surah begins to detail the components of the challenging path, and the first concrete action mentioned is: "It is to free a slave." In the society of seventh-century Arabia, slavery was an entrenched institution. Freeing a slave—loosening a person from bondage—was therefore not a simple, inexpensive gesture. It required a substantial outlay of wealth and, often, a willingness to go against social norms that treated certain human beings as property. By listing this act as the first explanation of the steep path, the Qur’an makes a profound statement about human dignity and the use of wealth. The very wealth that the arrogant disbeliever boasted of wasting could have been directed to this noble purpose. Instead of spending fortunes to oppose the truth, he could have purchased the freedom of men and women, thereby uplifting lives and earning eternal reward. The phrase "to free a slave" captures the essence of the uphill struggle: it is difficult, it costs something dearly, and it brings genuine liberation to others. The act is not merely a financial transaction; it is a blow against oppression and a declaration that every human being has a right to freedom under Allah. The Prophet Muhammad and his companions exemplified this command in a transformative way. Abu Bakr, for instance, spent a great portion of his wealth freeing slaves who had embraced Islam and were being tortured, such as Bilal. The early Muslim community understood that the steep path meant actively dismantling injustice with one’s resources. The verse also has a broader spiritual application. Freeing a slave can be understood as liberating someone from any form of bondage—debt, ignorance, or harmful habits—or even striving to free one’s own soul from the slavery of desires and sin. Nevertheless, the primary, outward meaning stands as a powerful social ethic. This opening of the list of righteous deeds immediately challenges any believer who reads it: what am I doing with my wealth and my influence to free people from the chains they suffer, and am I willing to take on the difficulty that such a path requires?
14 | or to give food in times of famine | The steep path continues to unfold with another act of compassion that directly counters the disbeliever’s behaviour: "or to give food in times of famine." The translation captures the specific and heightened difficulty implied in the original text, which refers to a day of severe hunger, a time when food itself is scarce and precious. To give food in such a time is vastly more meritorious than giving out of surplus. It demands sacrifice, generosity, and a trust in Allah that transcends the instinct of self-preservation. The one who embarks on the challenging path does not wait until his own storehouses are overflowing; he stretches out his hand even when his own stomach might feel the pinch of scarcity. The verse directly mirrors the earlier complaint of the disbeliever who boasted about "wasting enormous wealth." Allah shows what a truly worthwhile "waste" of wealth looks like: not throwing it into the fire of enmity, but placing it into the hands of the hungry, even when doing so is difficult. The famine mentioned may also allude to the actual famines that struck Mecca from time to time, times when the poor suffered terribly while the rich hoarded or spent on trivial pursuits. By singling out this deed, the surah teaches that faith cannot be separated from feeding the hungry. The Prophet would later say that the best of you are those who feed others, and he described a person who lets their neighbour go hungry while they themselves are full as lacking complete faith. This component of the steep path also highlights the social dimension of Islam. It is not a spirituality of isolation; it is a struggle to build a community where the most basic needs of every human being are met, even in the hardest of times. The believer is called to see famine not merely as a natural disaster but as a test that reveals whether the heart is generous or miserly, whether the struggle of life pushes one towards selfishness or towards the mercy that climbs the steep ascent.
15 | to an orphaned relative | The surah now specifies the recipient of the food mentioned in the previous verse: "to an orphaned relative." This specification adds layers of meaning and virtue. An orphan already carries the pain of having lost a father, the primary provider and protector. When that orphan is also a relative, there is a double claim upon the giver: the claim of general human compassion and the claim of family ties. In pre-Islamic Meccan society, orphans—especially female orphans—were often treated callously, their inheritance seized and their rights trampled. Even among relatives, the weak could be neglected while the strong devoured everything. The Qur’an consistently urges the care of orphans and the maintenance of kinship bonds, but here the two are combined to show that the steep path reaches its most excellent expression when family obligations are honoured despite hardship. Feeding an orphaned relative in times of famine means that you are not only sharing your scarce food but also fulfilling a familial duty that others might conveniently forget. It is easy to give charity to strangers for public recognition; it is far more challenging, and more purifying, to quietly look after a nephew, a cousin, or a distant relative who has no father and no one else to turn to. The verse also subtly recalls the Prophet’s own experience. He was himself an orphan, cared for first by his grandfather and then by his uncle. Allah’s special care for orphans is reflected throughout the revelation. By making the orphaned relative a central figure on the challenging path, the surah teaches that measuring the health of a community is not done by looking at its wealthy elite, but by seeing how its most vulnerable members are treated, especially by their own kin. The steep ascent demands that we overcome the natural human tendency to prioritise our immediate nuclear family in a narrow sense and extend our provision to the orphaned blood relative who might otherwise fall through the cracks of both family and society.
16 | or to a poor person in distress, | Another category of recipient is introduced, further broadening the circle of compassion on the steep path: "or to a poor person in distress." The original expression conjures a vivid image of a destitute person pressed into the dust, someone so crushed by poverty that they have no home, no bedding, and no protection from the elements. They cling to the ground in their misery. The word "distress" captures extreme deprivation, a state of being utterly broken and without any visible means of support. To give food in times of famine to such a person is to reach out to the very bottom of the social ladder. This person may not be a relative, and there may be no blood tie to urge one's generosity. The deed is therefore done purely for the sake of Allah, with no expectation of repayment or social advantage. The disbeliever who boasted of wasting enormous wealth would never stoop to feed a dust-covered beggar; his idea of spending was tied to power, display, and enmity. The steep path, by contrast, leads the believer to the dusty, forgotten corners of society. This verse, together with the previous ones about freeing a slave and feeding the orphaned relative, constructs a comprehensive portrait of righteousness. It involves liberating the oppressed, caring for vulnerable family members, and extending mercy to the utterly destitute stranger. All of these acts demand the sacrifice of wealth, time, and social pride. The sequence also demonstrates that faith is not a private sentiment; it is a force that rearranges economic priorities and human relationships. For the modern reader, "the poor person in distress" may be the refugee in a camp, the homeless person on the street, or the family devastated by war and famine. The steep path has not changed; it still runs through the places where human suffering is most raw and where aid is most costly and most needed. The believer who reads this is compelled to ask where such poor people are in their own world and whether they are truly climbing that difficult ascent.
17 | and—above all—to be one of those who have faith and urge each other to perseverance and urge each other to compassion. | After listing the outward deeds of liberation and feeding, the surah crowns the challenging path with its essential soul: "and—above all—to be one of those who have faith and urge each other to perseverance and urge each other to compassion." The phrase "above all" in the translation indicates that these inner and communal qualities are what give the previous actions their value and eternal weight. Freeing a slave and feeding the hungry, if done without faith, can be mere acts of human decency, but they do not translate into salvation. It is faith in Allah, His messengers, and the Last Day that transforms them into acts of worship that ascend the steep path. Yet faith is not isolated individualism. The believer must belong to the community of those who "urge each other to perseverance." Perseverance—the ability to remain steadfast in obedience, to endure hardship without complaint, and to resist sin—is the fuel that keeps the climber on the steep ascent. Believers do not sustain this alone; they form circles where they exhort one another, remind each other that the struggle is worth it, and strengthen each other’s resolve. Alongside perseverance is the mutual urging to compassion, or mercy. The Arabic term used encompasses tenderness, kindness, and empathy. The steep path is not a harsh, grim climb; it is softened by the mercy that believers show one another and the creation at large. They remind each other to forgive, to be gentle, and to let the heart remain soft. This combination of perseverance and compassion prevents the community from falling into either a weak sentimentality that cannot endure or a harsh rigidity that lacks love. The verse connects profoundly with Surah Al-Asr, where humanity is described as being in loss except those who believe, do righteous deeds, and mutually enjoin truth and patience. Here, truth and patience are echoed with faith, perseverance, and compassion. The steep ascent is therefore a communal journey, not a solitary sprint, and it is marked by words of encouragement exchanged among believers who help each other keep climbing.
18 | These are the people of the right. | The surah now delivers a concise and majestic verdict on those who climb the steep path: "These are the people of the right." The phrase "people of the right" is one of the Qur’an’s great eschatological categories. On the Day of Judgment, humanity will be divided into three groups: the foremost near ones, the people of the right, and the people of the left. The people of the right are those who receive their record of deeds in their right hand, a sign of acceptance, honour, and an easy reckoning. Their destination is Paradise, and their state is one of joy and contentment. By identifying those who attempt the steep path—those who combine faith, righteous deeds, and mutual encouragement—as the people of the right, this verse bridges the ethics of this surah with the vivid scenes of the Hereafter found in other parts of the Qur’an. The phrase "these are" carries an emphatic certainty; there is no ambiguity about their status. They are not merely hopeful or aspiring; Allah Himself classifies them among the saved. The designation also answers the earlier question about the two ways shown to humankind. Having been shown the paths of right and wrong, those who choose the steep path of right become the companions of the right hand. The verse thus provides immense hope and reassurance to believers who, in a world of arrogant boasters, may feel small and insignificant. Their names might never appear in the halls of worldly power, and their acts of feeding a poor orphan might be unknown to anyone except the One who sees, but in the divine register they are stamped as the people of the right. The verse also serves as a beautiful conclusion to the previous portrait: freeing slaves and feeding the hungry are not random acts of kindness; they are the signature characteristics of a group destined for eternal bliss. The next verse will immediately present the sharp, tragic contrast of those who took the other path.
19 | As for those who deny Our signs, they are the people of the left. | The surah now pivots to the opposite group with a stark and sobering statement: "As for those who deny Our signs, they are the people of the left." The opening words "as for those who deny Our signs" refer directly back to the arrogant boaster and all who share his attitude. Their defining trait is not merely a lack of charitable deeds; it is a deliberate rejection of Allah’s signs—the verses of the Qur’an, the evidence in creation, and the message brought by the Prophet. Denial of the signs is the root that produces the fruit of wasted wealth, neglect of the orphan, and hardness of heart. Because they denied the signs, they did not attempt the steep path, and their destination is summed up in the chilling phrase "the people of the left." In the Qur’anic imagery, the people of the left are those who will receive their record in their left hand or behind their backs. They will face a severe reckoning and will be driven towards the Fire. The left side connotes disgrace, misfortune, and separation from Allah’s mercy. The verse leaves no room for a neutral middle ground. Just as the eyes and the tongue were given and the two ways were shown, every soul ends up either on the right or on the left based on their response to the signs. The contrast with the previous verse is deliberately sharp: the same path that some climb to become people of the right, others ignore or mock, becoming people of the left. This binary outcome underscores the gravity of human choices. The wealthy Quraysh who thought their spending gave them status will discover that their worldly left-handed dealings have led them to an eternal left-hand position. The verse acts as a powerful warning, but also as a call to repent before the books are closed. It reiterates that the division is not based on wealth, lineage, or outward power, but on faith and the deeds that spring from it. The final verse will now describe the horrific fate that awaits the people of the left, sealing the surah’s message of accountability.
20 | The Fire will be sealed over them. | The surah closes its description of the people of the left with a brief but terrifying image: "The Fire will be sealed over them." The word translated as "sealed over" conveys the idea of an enclosing vault or a locked door that is shut tight with no possibility of escape. The fire is not merely a blaze in one direction; it surrounds its inhabitants completely, and it is firmly sealed so that neither relief nor exit can ever be found. This is the fitting end for those who boasted that no one had power over them and that no one saw them. They rejected the signs, wasted their wealth in arrogance, and refused to attempt the steep path. Now they face a prison from which there is no key. The verse draws together the themes of the entire surah. The eyes that were given to see the truth but were used to look with scorn are now sealed in darkness. The tongue that boasted "I have wasted enormous wealth" now finds no words to argue or plead. The two paths that were shown have narrowed into a single, inescapable reality. The imagery of sealing also implies finality. There is no second chance, no appeal process, no reduction in sentence. The arrogance that once strutted through the streets of Mecca is reduced to a voiceless confinement inside the fire. For the believer, this verse inspires a profound sense of urgency and gratitude. It shows what we have been saved from through faith and the deeds of the steep path. It also stirs a deep compassion for those still walking the path of denial, because the verse is not merely a description of a distant future but a warning meant to wake up the living. The seal of the fire is presented so that hearts might break their own seals of heedlessness before it is too late. As the batch of verses ends here, the surah’s first twenty verses leave us with a vivid portrait of the two ways and their destinations, urging every soul to look honestly at the path they are climbing today.
