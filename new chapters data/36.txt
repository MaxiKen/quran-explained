1 | Yâ-Sĩn. | The surah opens with these two Arabic letters, Yâ’ and Sĩn, which belong to the category of the disjointed letters that appear at the beginning of a number of Quranic chapters. Their precise meaning is known fully only to Allah, yet scholars have drawn several important lessons from them. One widespread understanding is that these letters serve as a dramatic wake-up call, grabbing the attention of the Arab listeners who were masters of the language. When they heard these individual letters recited, they would fall silent, and then the powerful message would follow. The letters are composed of the very alphabet they used daily, yet they could not produce anything like the Quran. This immediately challenges anyone who doubts the divine origin of the Book: here are the letters you know, so if you claim the Quran is human speech, then bring something like it. This interpretation makes the disjointed letters a miraculous challenge. Some early commentators also noted that “Yâ-Sĩn” is one of the names of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, meaning “O human being” or “O master,” as a term of endearment. Others related it to the phrase “Yâ insân” in certain dialects. However, the majority of reliable scholars view it primarily as part of the Quran’s miraculous structure, a sign that this revelation cannot be matched. The Meccan context of the surah is vital here; the Quraysh were denying the prophethood and the Quran. Starting with mysterious letters followed by an oath on the wise Quran immediately undermines their claim that the Prophet was a poet or a madman. It forces the listener to reflect: how can an unlettered man bring forth a book of such depth that it stuns the eloquent Arabs, and it is made of the very letters they use? Thus, the opening is not merely a set of symbols; it is a profound statement about the source and authority of what follows.
2 | By the Quran, rich in wisdom! | Allah takes an oath by the Quran, describing it as “rich in wisdom,” which translates the Arabic word Ḥakīm. An oath in the Quran is a means of drawing solemn attention to the thing sworn by, as well as to the statement that comes after it. The Quran is called Ḥakīm for several complementary reasons. It means that the Quran is full of profound wisdom, containing guidance that directs human beings to what is right in belief, conduct, and society. It also means that the Quran is firmly constructed and free from any flaw, contradiction, or inconsistency—it judges between truth and falsehood with perfect precision. Moreover, Ḥakīm can imply that the Quran is the source of wise judgment, teaching its followers how to distinguish right from wrong and how to live in a way that leads to success in this life and the Hereafter. By swearing by the wise Quran, Allah prepares the listener to accept the following declaration about the Prophet’s messengership. The oath also functions as a response to the Meccan disbelievers who dismissed the Quran as poetry, magic, or ancient fables. Swearing by something in this manner was a recognised Arabic rhetorical device that conveyed the speaker’s absolute seriousness and confidence in the truth of what was being said. The choice of the Quran as the object of the oath highlights its supreme status. It is not an ordinary book; it is the speech of the All-Wise, and its wisdom is evident to those who approach it with an open heart. This verse, therefore, establishes the Quran as the ultimate criterion and alerts the listener that what follows is grounded in unshakeable truth.
3 | You ˹O Prophet˺ are truly one of the messengers | Here the object of the oath in the previous verse is revealed. The solemn statement is a direct address to the Prophet, peace and blessings be upon him, affirming with the utmost emphasis that he is indeed one of the messengers sent by Allah. The use of “truly” captures the Arabic particle inna, which conveys certainty and removes any doubt. This verse came in response to the polytheists of Makkah who denied his prophethood, calling him a poet, a soothsayer, or a man possessed. By linking this affirmation to the oath on the wise Quran, Allah is declaring that the Quran itself is the decisive proof of his messengership. The content, the wisdom, and the miraculous nature of the Quran all testify that the one who brought it cannot be an impostor. Furthermore, the phrase “one of the messengers” reminds both the Prophet and his audience that he is not a solitary figure making an unprecedented claim; he stands within a long line of chosen men who received revelation and were sent to guide their peoples. This connection to the chain of prophethood was important because the Arabs had some historical awareness of earlier prophets, and it countered the accusation that Muhammad, peace be upon him, had invented something new. The verse also provides comfort and reassurance to the Prophet himself, who faced relentless rejection. Allah himself testifies that he is truly a messenger, which is the greatest honour and the strongest source of strength in the face of hostility.
4 | upon the Straight Path. | This phrase continues the description of the Prophet’s status. He is not only a messenger, but he is firmly established “upon the Straight Path” (ʿalā ṣirāṭin mustaqīm). This path is the clear, direct way of monotheism, justice, and righteousness that leads to Allah’s pleasure and eternal salvation. The wording indicates that the Prophet himself is guided and upright in his own person, in his conduct, and in the message he delivers. This refutes any claim that he was misguided or teaching error. The Straight Path is the same path that all the prophets called to: the worship of Allah alone without partners, the belief in the unseen, and the observance of moral and spiritual duties. It is the path mentioned in Sūrah al-Fātiḥah, for which every believer prays many times a day. By describing the Prophet as being upon this path, Allah is telling the disbelievers that rejecting him is tantamount to rejecting the path of guidance itself. For the believers, it is a reminder to follow the Prophet’s example because he is walking on the most certain and correct way. The phrase also connects to the nature of the Quran revealed to him, which is a book of guidance that maps out that very same Straight Path for all who wish to follow it. Therefore, the messenger, the message, and the way of life are all perfectly aligned in truth.
5 | ˹This is˺ a revelation from the Almighty, Most Merciful, | This verse explains the source of the message the Prophet brings. It is a revelation (tanzīl) sent down from the Almighty (al-ʿAzīz), the Most Merciful (ar-Raḥīm). The word “revelation” implies that the Quran is not the Prophet’s own words or ideas; it is divinely sent down in stages, originating from the highest authority. The pairing of the two divine names, the Almighty and the Most Merciful, carries deep significance. “The Almighty” stresses Allah’s irresistible power, honour, and supremacy. This assures the Prophet that the One who sent the revelation is fully able to protect him, defeat his enemies, and establish the truth. “The Most Merciful” highlights that this revelation is primarily an act of immense mercy. The Quran was not sent to destroy people arbitrarily but to guide them out of darkness into light, to warn them of what harms them, and to give them glad tidings of forgiveness and reward. The Meccan polytheists acknowledged Allah as the Creator but associated partners with Him, and some of them even claimed it was unfitting for Allah to send a human messenger. By affirming that the revelation is from the Almighty, Most Merciful, the verse overturns their false assumptions: the Almighty does whatever He wills, and His mercy is precisely why He sends guidance. This combination of might and mercy gives the believer both awe and hope. Thus, rejecting this revelation is not merely refusing a man’s advice; it is turning away from the powerful and merciful Lord.
6 | so that you may warn a people whose forefathers were not warned, and so are heedless. | The purpose of the revelation is now stated: the Prophet is sent to warn the Arabs. The description “a people whose forefathers were not warned” refers to the long interval (fatrah) between the time of Prophet Jesus, peace be upon him, and the advent of Prophet Muhammad. For centuries, the Arabs had not received a messenger with a fresh scripture. They had lost the pure teachings of Ishmael and Abraham and had fallen into idolatry, superstition, and tribal vendettas. Their immediate forefathers lived and died without a warner, so over generations, they became heedless (ghāfilūn) of the truth about God, the resurrection, and accountability. This historical context shows that the sending of the Prophet was an extraordinary act of mercy after a prolonged period of spiritual neglect. The verse also contains an important logical point: being heedless due to lack of warning does not mean they are innocent if they then reject the clear warning when it arrives. Their heedlessness was the very condition that necessitated a warner. Now that the warner has come with clear proofs, they have no excuse. This also answers those who might ask why Allah would punish a people without sending a messenger; the messenger has now been sent, and his warning is clear. The verse thus establishes the Prophet’s mission as a revival of the Abrahamic faith and a final call before accountability.
7 | The decree ˹of torment˺ has already been justified against most of them, for they will never believe. | This verse delivers a severe verdict concerning the majority of the Meccan disbelievers. The “decree” refers to the word of punishment that has become binding upon them. This is because Allah, with His perfect knowledge, knows that they will persist in their disbelief and arrogance, refusing to believe even after the clear warning and signs. The phrase “has already been justified” translates the Arabic ḥaqqa, meaning it has been established as true and necessary. This does not imply that they were forced to disbelieve; rather, it is a statement of divine foreknowledge and the consequence of their own freely chosen path. When a person continuously rejects the truth with full awareness and deliberate stubbornness, a point is reached where the heart becomes sealed and the decree of punishment becomes inevitable. However, the verse says “against most of them,” not all. This indicates that a minority among them would eventually accept faith, and indeed many of the early Muslims were from the Quraysh. This nuance is important because it shows that the door to guidance remains open for those whose hearts are not hardened. The verse serves as a sobering warning to the listener: do not be among those upon whom the decree of torment is justified. It also comforts the Prophet, explaining that the stubborn rejection of many is not due to a deficiency in his message, but due to a decree that follows their own arrogance.
8 | ˹It is as if˺ We have put shackles around their necks up to their chins, so their heads are forced up, | This verse presents a powerful metaphor for the spiritual state of the disbelievers. They are likened to people with iron shackles encasing their necks, extending from the chin upwards so that their heads are forcibly raised and they cannot lower them. The image is one of complete restriction, humiliation, and an inability to see what is directly in front of them. In a spiritual sense, the shackles represent the chains of their own stubbornness, pride, and sins that have taken hold of their hearts. Because their heads are forced up, they cannot bow in humility to Allah, nor can they look down to reflect upon the signs on the earth. They walk with arrogant upturned faces, blind to the truth that lies at their feet. Some commentators also connect this imagery to the Hereafter, where the disbelievers will be literally shackled, but here the phrasing “it is as if” indicates a parable depicting their current psychological and spiritual imprisonment. Their refusal to submit has ironically enslaved them, making it impossible for them to humble themselves. This metaphor is not about physical coercion but about the natural consequence of persistent rejection; when a person keeps rejecting faith, Allah places a barrier in their perception, making guidance inaccessible. It is both a warning and an explanation for why they do not respond to the Prophet’s call.
9 | and have placed a barrier before them and a barrier behind them and covered them ˹all˺ up, so they fail to see ˹the truth˺. | The imagery continues, describing how these disbelievers are completely surrounded and blocked from perceiving the truth. A barrier (saddan) stands in front of them, and a barrier behind them, and they are also covered from above. This triple obstruction means that no matter which direction they turn, they cannot reach or see the light of guidance. The barrier in front prevents them from moving forward toward faith; the barrier behind prevents them from returning to the truth they have abandoned; and the covering over them prevents heavenly light from reaching their hearts. The result is that “they fail to see,” not because their eyes are physically blind, but because their inner sight has been rendered useless. This is a divinely imposed consequence of their own persistent choice to reject the signs. Allah does not wrong them; they wrong themselves, and then as a just recompense, they are left to wander in confusion. The metaphor also extends to the Day of Judgment, where the disbelievers will find barriers between them and Paradise, but in this world the barriers are spiritual. The lesson for the believer is to fear the state of the heart that leads to such blindness, and to constantly ask Allah for light and guidance. It also clarifies to the Prophet why his sincere efforts do not seem to penetrate their hearts: they have been sealed off by their own doing. This is not a reason to give up on calling people, for one does not know whom Allah will guide, but it explains the reality of those who have reached a point of no return.
10 | It is the same whether you warn them or not—they will never believe. | After depicting their sealed state, Allah plainly tells the Prophet that for these hardened individuals, his warning makes no difference. Their disbelief is so entrenched that no amount of preaching, argument, or miracle will bring them to faith, because the decree has been issued based on Allah’s knowledge that they will never choose belief. This statement must be understood in context: it applies to those specific obstinate leaders among the Quraysh like Abū Jahl, not to every single Meccan, for we know many later accepted Islam. The verse serves multiple purposes. It consoles the Prophet, freeing him from excessive grief over their rejection, because the outcome is not due to any shortcoming on his part. It is also a warning that there is a limit to the heart’s capacity to accept guidance; if a person continually pushes away the truth, a time comes when the heart is irreversibly sealed. The verse does not encourage abandoning the duty of warning—the command to warn is still in place—but it provides a realistic perspective. A messenger’s task is to deliver the message with clarity, not to force acceptance. This truth is echoed elsewhere in the Quran, such as “You cannot guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills.” Thus, the verse balances the Prophet’s deep concern for his people with the reality that guidance is ultimately in Allah’s hands.
11 | You can only warn those who follow the Reminder and are in awe of the Most Compassionate without seeing Him. So give them good news of forgiveness and an honourable reward. | In contrast to the previous verses, this verse identifies the audience who will truly benefit from the warning. The “Reminder” is the Quran itself, and those who “follow” it are the ones who not only hear it but also act upon its teachings. Their key quality is that they stand in awe of the Most Compassionate (ar-Raḥmān) “without seeing Him,” meaning they have a profound inner consciousness of Allah’s presence, majesty, and mercy, even though He is hidden from their physical sight. This is the essence of true faith: a heartfelt fear and reverence of God that is not dependent on visible phenomena but is rooted in an awareness of the unseen. Because they possess this reverent fear, their hearts are receptive to warning and eager to obey. The command then shifts to positive encouragement: “So give them good news of forgiveness and an honourable reward.” The Prophet is not only a warner but also a bearer of glad tidings. Forgiveness (maghfirah) means that their sins will be completely erased, and the generous reward (ajrun karīm) refers to the delights of Paradise that are honourable and eternal. This verse beautifully balances warning with hope, and it teaches that the true followers are those who combine study of the Quran with a living consciousness of their Merciful Lord. It also clarifies that effective preaching depends on the spiritual receptivity of the listener, not merely on the eloquence of the preacher.
12 | It is certainly We Who resurrect the dead, and write what they send forth and what they leave behind. Everything is listed by Us in a perfect Record. | This verse makes a definitive statement about resurrection and divine record-keeping, which were central points of contention for the Meccans. “It is certainly We Who resurrect the dead” uses the emphatic “certainly” (innā) to counter their denial of life after death. By presenting resurrection as an act of Allah, the verse links it to His power and will. Then it moves to the recording of deeds: “what they send forth” refers to the good and bad actions that people perform during their lives and send ahead to the Hereafter. “What they leave behind” includes the lasting effects of their deeds, such as beneficial knowledge they taught, charitable endowments they established, or even the evil consequences of their misdeeds that continue after their death. Everything is meticulously “listed” in a “perfect Record,” which is the Preserved Tablet (al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ), a comprehensive register that contains the decree of all things. This recording is not limited to major events; every thought, word, and action is accounted for. The mention of this record serves as a powerful motivation for mindfulness. If a person truly believes that every deed is being written and will be presented, they will be more careful. It also reassures the believers that no good deed is lost and no injustice will go unpunished. The connection to the surrounding verses is clear: after telling the Prophet who will benefit from his warning, Allah reminds him that the resurrection and judgment are certain and that all human actions are preserved, reinforcing the urgency of his message.
13 | Give them an example ˹O Prophet˺ of the residents of a town, when the messengers came to them. | Now the surah moves to a historical parable designed to mirror the situation in Makkah. The Prophet is commanded to set forth an example (mathalan) for his people. This is the story of a town—widely understood by commentators to be Antioch, in present-day Turkey—to which Allah sent messengers. The story is not told in exact historical names and dates, because its purpose is not a history lesson but a timeless illustration of the pattern of prophetic rejection and divine punishment. The fact that the Quraysh are asked to consider this example is meant to jolt them into reflection: their own treatment of the Prophet is exactly like the behavior of those who were destroyed. The sequence of the story will show the sending of messengers, the people’s rejection, the threat of violence, the support of a lone believer from the outskirts, and the final sudden destruction. Every element of the parable resonates with the Prophet’s experience in Makkah. This method of teaching through a narrative example is highly effective because it engages the listener’s imagination and makes the warning less abstract. It also provides a prophetic pattern of patience and trust in Allah’s plan.
14 | We sent them two messengers, but they rejected both. So We reinforced ˹the two˺ with a third, and they declared, “We have indeed been sent to you ˹as messengers˺.” | Allah initially sent two messengers to the people of the town. The townspeople rejected them outright, refusing to believe in their message. Rather than destroying them immediately, Allah showed mercy by strengthening the two with a third messenger, increasing the evidence and the force of the call. This sequence demonstrates a divine principle: Allah gives people every opportunity to accept the truth before punishment descends. The sending of a third messenger after the rejection of the first two is a sign of immense patience and an escalation of the argument against them. The three messengers then gave a unified, unambiguous declaration: “We have indeed been sent to you.” The words “have indeed been sent” (innā ilaykum mursalūn) are emphatic, asserting the certainty of their divine mission. The identity of these messengers is not definitively stated in the Quran or in an authentic hadith, though some narrations from the People of the Book mention that they were disciples of Jesus sent to Antioch. However, what matters is the lesson: when people persistently reject repeated calls to the truth, they are heading toward a fate like that of the people of this town. The Meccans were now hearing the same message from Prophet Muhammad, after previous prophets had come to humanity, making their rejection all the more blameworthy.
15 | The people replied, “You are only humans like us, and the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything. You are simply lying!” | This verse captures the classic arguments of disbelievers against God’s messengers throughout history. First, they rejected the messengers on the grounds that they were ordinary human beings who ate, drank, and walked in the markets. They believed that if God were to send a messenger, it should be an angel. This is the same objection the Quraysh raised: “Why has an angel not been sent down to him?” Second, they made a sweeping theological claim: “the Most Compassionate has not revealed anything.” They denied the very concept of divine revelation, effectively closing the door to any prophetic claim. Their use of the name “the Most Compassionate” is ironic; they acknowledge God’s mercy with their tongues but deny that His mercy entails sending guidance to humanity. Third, they accused the messengers of deliberate falsehood: “You are simply lying.” This triple rejection—attacking the messenger’s human nature, denying revelation itself, and labeling the messengers liars—reflects a deep-seated arrogance and a refusal to be accountable to a human like themselves. The Quran highlights this mindset to expose its irrationality: why would God leave humanity without guidance, and if He sent angels, how would humans interact with them? The human nature of messengers is in fact a mercy, as they serve as relatable role models.
16 | The messengers responded, “Our Lord knows that we have truly been sent to you.” | Faced with accusations of lying, the messengers did not resort to angry retorts or personal attacks. Instead, they calmly referred the matter to Allah, saying, “Our Lord knows.” This is the response of true prophets and sincere callers to God: when their truthfulness is questioned, they appeal to the divine knowledge as the ultimate witness. They are not merely claiming to be messengers; they are certain that Allah knows their mission, and this certainty is their strength. The statement implies that even if the people do not believe them, their Lord’s knowledge is sufficient for them, and He will ultimately vindicate the truth. This teaches a profound lesson in dealing with rejection: one’s concern should not be to convince every stubborn opponent but to maintain sincerity before God, who knows the reality of one’s intentions and mission. The phrasing “truly been sent” (la-ilaykum lamursalūn) is again emphatic, underscoring their unshakeable confidence. This verse also shows the moral high ground of the messengers; they do not descend into the language of abuse but leave judgment to the All-Knowing. For the Prophet Muhammad and his followers, this is a model of patient reliance on Allah when accused of falsehood.
17 | And our duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly. | Here the messengers define the exact scope of their responsibility. Their task is not to compel belief, to punish the rejecters, or to force the truth into people’s hearts. Their sole obligation is to convey the message (al-balāgh) in a clear manner (al-mubīn). The phrase “clearly” means that they must present the revelation without distortion, ambiguity, or concealment, using plain language and sound arguments, so that no one can claim they did not understand. This principle is central to Islamic theology: the job of prophets, and by extension all who call to Islam, is to deliver the message faithfully. Guidance and acceptance are entirely in Allah’s hands. This teaching frees the caller from the psychological burden of feeling responsible for the outcome. It also serves as a final witness against those who reject; once the message is delivered clearly, they have no excuse before God. The messengers’ statement also implicitly warns the people that persistence in disbelief after such a clear delivery will bring about severe consequences, but those consequences are from Allah, not from the messengers. The Meccan pagans needed to understand that Muhammad’s sole duty was communication, and their continued rejection did not harm him but spelled their own doom.
18 | The people replied, “We definitely see you as a bad omen for us. If you do not desist, we will certainly stone you ˹to death˺ and you will be touched with a painful punishment from us.” | The townspeople’s response escalated from verbal denial to superstitious accusation and physical threats. They claimed that the messengers were a bad omen (taṭayyur), believing that their presence had brought misfortune, division, or calamity. This was a common pagan superstition: if something disliked happened after a person’s arrival, that person was considered an ill omen. The Quraysh said similar things to Prophet Muhammad, blaming him for their hardships. The messengers’ call to abandon idols and reform society threatened the status quo, and the pagans interpreted the resulting social tension as bad luck rather than as a natural consequence of their own wrongdoing. Then they delivered a brutal ultimatum: stop preaching or be stoned to death, followed by a “painful punishment”—possibly torture or humiliating treatment before death. This reflects the typical pattern of tyrannical societies; when they cannot answer the message with reason, they resort to violence and intimidation. The verse highlights the severity of the persecution faced by the messengers, a situation the Prophet Muhammad and his early followers knew well. The threat of stoning and painful punishment reveals the depth of their hatred for the truth, and it sets the stage for the sudden entrance of a courageous believer who would speak up in defense of the messengers.
19 | The messengers said, “Your bad omen lies within yourselves. Are you saying this because you are reminded ˹of the truth˺? In fact, you are a transgressing people.” | The messengers refute the superstition of bad omens with a profound spiritual truth. They declare that any perceived ill fortune is not caused by their presence but “lies within yourselves.” That is, the real source of misfortune is the people’s own disbelief, sins, and moral corruption. Their attachment to idolatry and their rejection of God’s guidance invite divine displeasure and calamity. The messengers then pointedly ask: is your charge of bad omen simply because we reminded you of the truth? This rhetorical question exposes the irrationality of their stance. The reminder (dhikr) is in fact a great good, a lifeline sent by the Merciful to save them, yet they treat it as a curse. Finally, the messengers label them a “transgressing people” (qawmun musrifūn), meaning they have crossed all boundaries in their arrogance, superstition, and hostility. This response teaches that those who call to Allah should not be intimidated by false accusations of bringing bad luck; rather, they should redirect people’s attention to the real causes of their problems—their own transgression and distance from God. The messengers’ words are firm yet truthful, and they serve as a final warning before the narrative’s dramatic turn.
20 | Then from the farthest end of the city a man came, rushing. He advised, “O my people! Follow the messengers.” | Just when the situation seemed hopeless and the messengers faced death threats, a lone supporter emerged. A man came from the farthest part (aqṣā al-madīnah) of the town, which suggests he was from the outskirts, perhaps a poor or ordinary person, not from the elite. He came “rushing” (yasʿā), meaning he hurried with urgency and concern, having heard that the messengers were being threatened. His haste shows his courage and the depth of his faith; he did not wait to see what others would do but immediately ran to speak the truth. His address, “O my people! Follow the messengers,” is simple, direct, and full of sincere care. He does not claim authority or status; he appeals as a fellow citizen who desires their well-being. Commentators identify this man as Ḥabīb an-Najjār, a carpenter who had believed in the message. His intervention is a timeless example of an individual standing up for truth against an entire society, willing to risk his life. The mention that he came from the farthest end of the city symbolizes that faith sometimes springs from the margins, from those whom society overlooks, not from the powerful elite. His example serves to encourage every believer who feels alone in their environment to speak out and call their people to what is right, for one sincere voice can leave an enduring legacy.
21 | Follow those who ask no reward of you, and are ˹rightly˺ guided. | The believing man now gives his people a clear and practical instruction. He tells them to follow the messengers he is defending, and he highlights two compelling qualities that should make them attractive to any reasonable person. The first quality is that these messengers “ask no reward of you.” They do not seek any financial payment, material gain, or social status in return for their preaching. Their call is entirely free of worldly self-interest. This is a powerful proof of their sincerity, because a person who invents a religion or a political movement typically wants followers for power, wealth, or influence. The messengers, however, had nothing to gain from the people; they endured rejection and threats while asking for nothing. This same quality was evident in the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, who never accepted payment for his message and lived a life of simplicity and sacrifice. The second quality is that they are “rightly guided” (muhtadūn). This means they are not lost or wandering in error; they walk upon the Straight Path that leads to Allah and His pleasure. They are not merely good-intentioned people but divinely guided prophets whose own lives reflect the truth they preach. By linking sincerity with divine guidance, the believing man provides a complete argument: those who ask for nothing and are manifestly guided by God deserve to be followed. For the Quraysh hearing this parable, the lesson was unmistakable. Muhammad, peace be upon him, was known to them as al-Amīn, the trustworthy, and he never asked for a wage. His life, character, and message all testified that he was upon guidance. So the verse invites everyone to evaluate the callers to faith by these two standards: do they seek our wealth, and are they themselves following the truth? If the answer is no to the first and yes to the second, then the rational response is to follow them. The believer’s counsel here is as urgent as ever, for humanity continues to be surrounded by leaders and ideologies that demand sacrifice from followers while offering no divine guidance.
22 | And why should I not worship the One Who has originated me, and to Whom you will be returned. | After calling his people to follow the messengers, the believing man now explains his own personal conviction in the form of a logical question. “Why should I not worship the One Who has originated me?” The verb “originated” (faṭaranī) refers to the act of bringing something into existence from nothing, shaping it with purpose and precision. He is pointing to the very fact of his own creation as the ultimate reason for worship. Every human being knows they did not create themselves; their existence is owed to a Creator. Reason dictates that the one who gives life and form deserves gratitude, obedience, and devotion. To worship anything else would be an injustice to the Creator. He then immediately connects the beginning of life to its end: “and to Whom you will be returned.” This clause brings in the reality of the Hereafter, which was the central doctrine the messengers were calling to. If people will be returned to their Lord for judgment, then worshipping Him is not only a matter of gratitude but also an urgent necessity. The shift from “me” to “you” is subtle but powerful. In the first part he speaks of his own origin, and in the second he reminds his people that they too will return to God. He is telling them that his decision to worship the Creator is the only sensible path, and that their own eventual return should make them reconsider their rejection. This verse is a concise summary of pure monotheism rooted in creation and accountability. It echoes the Quranic argument that those who reflect on the heavens, the earth, and their own souls will inevitably recognize the Creator’s right to be worshipped. For the contemporary reader, this verse offers a refreshingly direct way to think about faith: since we did not bring ourselves into being, and since we will one day face our Maker, why would we refuse to worship Him alone?
23 | How could I take besides Him other gods whose intercession would not be of any benefit to me, nor could they save me if the Most Compassionate intended to harm me? | The believing man continues his logical exposition by rejecting idolatry in the strongest terms. He asks rhetorically, “How could I take besides Him other gods?” The question implies that such an action would be utterly irrational and self-destructive. He then outlines two fatal flaws in the idolatrous belief system. First, the so-called gods “whose intercession would not be of any benefit to me” are completely powerless. The Meccans and many polytheists throughout history believed that their idols could intercede with the supreme God and bring them closer to Him. The Quran repeatedly refutes this, asserting that intercession belongs entirely to Allah, and no one can intercede except with His permission and for those with whom He is pleased. An idol that is deaf, dumb, and lifeless cannot help anyone. Second, those false gods “nor could they save me if the Most Compassionate intended to harm me” are utterly incapable of providing protection or rescue. If Allah wills to bring a calamity, illness, or punishment, no idol, saint, or angel can avert it. Only the Almighty Himself can remove harm. The use of the divine name “the Most Compassionate” (ar-Raḥmān) here is striking. It reminds the listener that the true God is not a distant, wrathful being but the source of all mercy. Turning away from the Merciful Lord to lifeless objects is both folly and ingratitude. The believer’s argument demolishes the entire structure of polytheism by exposing its practical uselessness in the moments that matter most: when one needs intercession and when one needs rescue. His words teach that pure monotheism is not merely a theological preference but the only worldview that makes sense in the face of human vulnerability and need.
24 | Indeed, I would then be clearly astray. | This short verse is the logical conclusion to the previous one. The believing man states that if he were to take other gods besides Allah, he would be “clearly astray” (ḍalālin mubīn). The word “astray” here means far removed from the truth, lost in error with no excuse. “Clearly” indicates that such misguidance would be manifest and undeniable to any sound mind. He is not saying that he might be slightly mistaken; he is affirming that idolatry, after having known the truth, would place him in an obvious and profound error. This is a declaration of unwavering certainty. The believing man has examined the evidence of creation, the sincerity of the messengers, the powerlessness of the false gods, and the certainty of return to the Lord, and he has reached a firm conviction. He can see the Straight Path, and he knows that deviating from it would be a catastrophic loss. In a society that normalizes idolatry, such a confession requires immense courage. He is essentially telling his people that their way of life is clearly misguided, while he is upon obvious truth. This verse teaches the importance of drawing a clear line between truth and falsehood. Faith should not be a matter of vague feelings but of clear-sighted certainty that distinguishes right from wrong without ambiguity. The believer’s courage is rooted in this clarity; when one knows that a certain course of action leads to manifest ruin, one avoids it at all costs. For those who hear the Quran’s call, the verse poses an honest question: after all the proofs have been laid out, would not choosing a path other than God’s guidance be a clear, self-destructive straying?
25 | I do believe in your Lord, so listen to me. | With all his reasoning laid out, the believing man now makes an open and bold declaration of faith. He says, “I do believe in your Lord,” directly addressing his people. Notice that he does not say “my Lord” in a way that separates himself from them, but “your Lord,” which carries a profound rhetorical purpose. By saying “your Lord,” he reminds them that the God he believes in is not a foreign deity or an unknown being; He is their own Lord as well. The Creator who originated them, sustains them, and to whom they will return is the very God they should be worshipping. He is inviting them to recognize the Lord who already owns them, rather than asking them to accept something alien. Then he adds, “so listen to me.” This is not an arrogant command but a heartfelt plea. He knows his life is in danger, and he has rushed to speak before it is too late. He wants them to pay attention, not to his personal status, but to the truth he is conveying. He is willing to be heard, even if it costs him his life. This verse demonstrates the highest form of sincere concern for others. The believing man does not keep his faith private; he risks everything to share it, hoping to save his people from destruction. The sequence of his speech, ending with this public testimony, is a masterclass in prophetic-style preaching: he first removed obstacles by pointing to the messengers’ sincerity, then reasoned with the intellect using creation and accountability, then demolished false beliefs, and finally called them to listen to the truth. His example remains a timeless inspiration for believers who live among those who reject faith, showing that love for one’s people means calling them to their true Lord, even in the face of hostility.
26 | ˹But they killed him, then˺ he was told ˹by the angels˺, “Enter Paradise!” He said, “If only my people knew | The narrative takes a sudden and tragic turn, but immediately transforms into a scene of triumph. The people, enraged by his bold testimony and his defense of the messengers, martyred him. The Quran does not detail the manner of his killing, because the focus is not on the brutality of the disbelievers but on the honour Allah bestowed upon him instantly after his death. The phrase “he was told” refers to the angels conveying Allah’s greeting as his soul departs: “Enter Paradise!” There is no waiting, no intermediate state of punishment or delay; the gates of eternal bliss are opened for him at once. This is the immense reward for those who give their lives in the path of truth. Yet even in that moment of supreme personal joy, his first thought is not about his own comfort but about his people who killed him. He says, “If only my people knew.” His words are not a curse or a cry for revenge; they express a wish that his people could see what he is witnessing. He longs for them to know the forgiveness and honour that await those who believe, so that they too might be saved. This selfless compassion reflects the highest character of a true believer. Even death at the hands of his own people does not extinguish his love for them. The verse is also a powerful response to anyone who thinks that dying for faith is a loss; in reality, it is an immediate gateway to unimaginable reward. The believer’s wish highlights the profound gulf between the perception of the disbelievers and the unseen reality of the Hereafter. For the Prophet Muhammad and his followers who faced persecution in Makkah, this story offered deep consolation: the martyr is not defeated but instantly honoured, and his concern for his people remains alive in the presence of his Lord.
27 | of how my Lord has forgiven me, and made me one of the honourable.” | The believing man completes his wish by specifying what he longs for his people to know. He wants them to see “how my Lord has forgiven me.” This forgiveness means that all his previous sins, if any, have been completely erased by Allah’s mercy, and he now stands pure and unburdened. The emphasis on “my Lord” is deeply personal; he is boasting, not in himself, but in the generosity of his Master. He also wishes they could see that Allah has “made me one of the honourable” (al-mukramīn). This honour is the highest form of divine hospitality, which includes a lofty station in Paradise, respectful treatment from the angels, and the pleasure of Allah. The contrast is staggering: his people dishonoured him, tortured him, and killed him, yet Allah has granted him true and eternal honour. This demonstrates that human disrespect and persecution mean nothing when compared to the honour that comes from God. The verse also reveals the psychology of the believer who faces death for his faith. He does not feel that he is losing anything; he feels that he is gaining everything. His concern is entirely directed toward his people who remain behind, still living in the deception of this world. This teaches that those who are certain of the Hereafter do not cling to this life, nor do they harbor bitterness against their oppressors, because they know the ultimate justice of Allah. The believing man’s statement is preserved in the Quran for all time, so in a sense his wish was granted: countless people across centuries have come to know of his forgiveness and honour, and his story has guided many to faith. His death was not the end; it was the beginning of an enduring legacy.
28 | We did not send any soldiers from the heavens against his people after his death, nor did We need to. | After the martyrdom of the believing man, Allah describes how He dealt with the people of the town. The verse states that no heavenly army, no angels in ranks descending from the sky, was sent to destroy them. This statement is significant because it demolishes the arrogant assumption that the punishment of God requires a dramatic, overwhelming military force. The Quraysh often challenged the Prophet to bring down the punishment, imagining it would involve visible armies of angels or stones from the sky. Allah declares that He had no need to mobilize such forces. The phrase “nor did We need to” underscores the absolute ease with which divine punishment can be executed. Allah’s power is not dependent on means; He does not require soldiers, weapons, or preparation. The townspeople who seemed powerful and secure were completely vulnerable. This verse also contains a subtle element of mercy and justice. By not sending an army of angels, Allah shows that the punishment is not a retaliation on a personal scale; it is a cosmic decree that needs no theatrics. It is swift and final, leaving no room for negotiation or escape. For the believers who were oppressed, the verse reassures them that their Lord does not need human help or angelic legions to establish justice; He can avenge His righteous servants in a single instant. This is a profound source of comfort for anyone suffering injustice, teaching that patience is not weakness but trust in the One whose power is absolute and whose timing is perfect.
29 | All it took was one ˹mighty˺ blast, and they were extinguished at once. | Allah now reveals the exact nature of the punishment that wiped out the town. It was merely “one mighty blast” (ṣayḥatan wāḥidah). The word ṣayḥah refers to a loud, terrifying cry or shout, often associated with the angel’s trumpet or a divine command that shatters life. This single blast was enough to render them “extinguished at once” (khāmidūn). The Arabic verb khamada refers to a fire being completely put out, leaving only cold ashes. The imagery is vivid: the people who were burning with arrogance, activity, and plots against the messengers were suddenly reduced to silence and stillness, like a fire that has been extinguished. There were no survivors to mourn, no resistance, no lingering struggle. The blast came, and everything was over. This sudden destruction teaches that divine punishment does not follow human schedules or warnings that can be negotiated. When it comes, it is swift and final. It also highlights the fragility of human power. The town that dared to threaten and kill the messengers could not withstand a single divine command. The verse is a direct warning to the Makkans who mocked the Prophet and demanded the punishment. Allah is telling them that if He wills, their destruction is equally easy. Yet, in His mercy, He delayed it to give them a chance to repent. The story of this town serves as a mirror, showing the Quraysh their own potential fate if they persist in their arrogance. For all readers, the verse is a reminder not to be deceived by the apparent security of a life lived in defiance of God; the end can come at any moment, and no one can resist it.
30 | Oh pity, such beings! No messenger ever came to them without being mocked. | After recounting the destruction, Allah expresses a lament over the condition of such servants. The exclamation “Oh pity” translates “yā ḥasratan,” a word that conveys deep regret and sorrow. This is not the regret of a human who has lost something, but a divine expression intended to emphasize the tragedy of human stubbornness. The phrase “such beings” refers to those who consistently reject God’s messengers. Their tragedy is that “no messenger ever came to them without being mocked.” This is a pattern that spans human history. From Noah to Moses to Jesus to Muhammad, peace be upon them all, every prophet faced mockery from his people. The mockery is not a reflection on the messengers but on the mockers, whose hearts are so blinded by arrogance and love of worldly life that they cannot take the truth seriously. They ridicule what they do not understand, and in doing so, they seal their own doom. The verse is a profound consolation for the Prophet Muhammad, who was called a poet, a madman, and a sorcerer. It tells him that he is walking a path well-trodden by the noblest of human beings, and that the mockery he faces is a sign of his truthfulness, not an indication of failure. At the same time, the verse is a stark warning to those who mock: they are placing themselves in the category of those over whom divine pity is expressed, a pity that will turn into severe punishment if they do not change. The lesson for all generations is to take the call of faith seriously and never treat messengers of truth as objects of ridicule, for in doing so, one mocks the One who sent them.
31 | Have the deniers not considered how many peoples We destroyed before them who never came back to life again? | Now the surah turns to directly address the Meccan deniers, drawing their attention to history. The question “Have the deniers not considered” challenges them to use their reason and observation. The “peoples We destroyed before them” refers to the powerful nations of the past, such as the people of Noah, ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the Pharaoh’s host. These were civilizations far stronger and more established than the Quraysh. “Who never came back to life again” in this context primarily means that they did not return to their worldly life and power. Their cities lie in ruins, their wealth has vanished, and no one from among them has ever come back to rebuild their dominion. This is a clear sign that worldly might does not guarantee permanence. The verse implicitly refutes the materialist mindset that sees only the present world. It asks the Quraysh to travel and look at the ruins they pass on their trade routes, to see the fate of those who denied the truth. However, the phrase also carries a deeper significance concerning the denial of resurrection. The Meccans used to say, “When we are dead and become dust, will we really be raised again?” Here, the verse acknowledges that indeed these destroyed nations have not come back to this life, but that is because a greater return is yet to happen—the return to Allah on the Day of Judgment. The next verse immediately clarifies this. The historical reflection is meant to shake them out of their heedlessness and prepare their hearts for the truth.
32 | Yet they will all be brought before Us. | This short verse completes the thought of the previous one and strikes at the heart of the disbelievers’ error. The destroyed nations did not return to the life of this world, but “they will all be brought before Us.” The word “all” is comprehensive, including not only the past generations but also the Quraysh and all humanity until the end of time. The phrase “brought before Us” means gathered for the ultimate judgment, standing before their Lord with no escape. This verse corrects a common misconception: that death is the final end. The disbelievers assumed that since the destroyed nations had vanished without returning, there was no afterlife. Allah responds that their failure to return to this world is not because they have been annihilated into nothingness; they are in the intermediate realm of the grave (barzakh), and they will certainly be resurrected and presented for accountability. For the Quraysh, this was a thunderous warning. They felt secure in their trade, their social status, and their idols. They mocked the idea of resurrection. This verse tells them that the very nations they considered gone forever are in fact awaiting the same gathering that they themselves will face. The verse connects the historical signs in the earth with the reality of the Hereafter, reinforcing the central theme of the surah. It also provides a motivation for believers: the apparent triumph of disbelievers is temporary; they will all stand before the Just Judge, and on that day the truth will be made plain. Thus, the reflections on history are not meant for mere storytelling but to awaken a living consciousness of the ultimate return.
33 | There is a sign for them in the dead earth: We give it life, producing grain from it for them to eat. | With this verse, the surah transitions into a powerful series of signs in the natural world that prove Allah’s power to resurrect. The “sign” (āyah) is in the “dead earth”—barren, lifeless soil that seems utterly incapable of producing anything. The Meccans saw this with their own eyes in the desert landscape. Then Allah says, “We give it life,” referring to the transformative miracle of rain that causes seeds to sprout and vegetation to burst forth. This is a direct parallel to resurrection: just as the earth is dead and then brought to life, the human body that has decayed into dust can be brought back to life by the same divine power. The specific mention of “producing grain from it for them to eat” shifts the focus from abstract miracle to personal benefit. The grain that sustains their daily life—the bread they eat—comes from this divinely orchestrated revival of the earth. The sign is not distant or theoretical; it is right before them at every meal. If they can accept that Allah revives the dead earth every season, why do they find it strange that He can revive the dead on the Day of Judgment? The verse subtly shames the disbelievers for enjoying the fruits of this annual resurrection while denying the greater resurrection. It also cultivates gratitude in the believer, who is reminded that every morsel of food is a sign of God’s mercy and power. This method of reasoning from the observable to the unseen is a hallmark of the Quran, appealing to both the intellect and the heart.
34 | And We have placed in it gardens of palm trees and grapevines, and caused springs to gush forth in it, | The description of the earth’s revival continues with more specific blessings. After mentioning grain as a staple, Allah draws attention to “gardens of palm trees and grapevines.” These represent high-quality, delightful provisions. Dates and grapes were the most cherished fruits in the Arabian environment, providing not only nutrition but also pleasure and economic benefit. The mention of “springs to gush forth” highlights the source of this life: fresh water that Allah causes to burst from the ground. Without water, the earth remains dead, no matter how rich the soil. The gushing springs are signs of a deliberate, caring design. The water flows to nourish the trees, and the trees in turn provide fruit for human beings. This is not a random natural process but a carefully arranged cycle of mercy. The verse invites reflection on the fact that human beings do not control the rain, the springs, or the growth of these gardens; Allah alone manages them. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on their agricultural oases like Ta’if with its vineyards, knew full well that their efforts alone could not produce a single grape if the water and the growth were withheld. Thus, the sign is twofold: it demonstrates the power to give life, and it demonstrates the mercy that provides for human needs and delights. This combination of power and mercy is central to the Quranic argument for tawḥīd and resurrection. The natural world becomes a book of signs for those who look with the eyes of faith.
35 | so that they may eat from its fruit, which they had no hand in making. Will they not then give thanks? | This verse states the purpose of the gardens and the fruits: they are there so that people may eat. The phrase “which they had no hand in making” (mā ʿamilathu aydīhim) is particularly striking. Human beings plant seeds, tend the soil, and irrigate the land, but they do not create the fruit. The actual life within the seed, the transformation of soil and water into a sweet date or a juicy grape, is entirely beyond human ability. Human labour is only a secondary cause; the primary cause is the creative act of Allah. This humbles human pride and reminds people that their food is ultimately a gift, not a purely human product. Then comes the penetrating question: “Will they not then give thanks?” Gratitude (shukr) is the proper response to recognizing that all blessings come from God. Lack of gratitude is a sign of arrogance and a root cause of disbelief. The disbelievers enjoy Allah’s provisions yet refuse to worship Him alone, which is the height of ingratitude. The verse therefore links the physical act of eating to the spiritual duty of thankfulness. True thankfulness is not just saying “alhamdulillah” with the tongue; it is to use the energy gained from food in obedience to the Provider, and to direct worship only to Him. The sign of the earth and its fruits becomes a moral test. Will the human being merely consume like an animal, without reflection, or will he recognize the Giver and submit to Him? For the believer, this verse transforms the everyday act of eating into an act of worship and a reminder of the ultimate resurrection, when they hope to eat from the fruits of Paradise.
36 | Glory be to the One Who created all ˹things in˺ pairs —˹be it˺ what the earth produces, their genders, or what they do not know! | After detailing the earth’s produce, the surah lifts the gaze to a broader principle of creation: everything is created in pairs. This verse begins with “Glory be,” declaring Allah’s absolute perfection and transcendence above any deficiency or partner. The fact that He created all things in pairs is a profound sign of His wisdom and oneness. The verse lists three categories. First, “what the earth produces” refers to the plant kingdom, where male and female elements exist even in vegetation, enabling reproduction and the production of fruit. Second, “their genders” refers to human beings and animals, where the pairing of male and female is immediately obvious. This pairing is not only for reproduction but also for companionship, love, and the completion of life’s functions. Third, “what they do not know” extends the principle to realms of creation that were beyond the knowledge of the seventh-century Arabs, and indeed some of which are still being discovered. Modern science has revealed pairing at the atomic level, with positive and negative charges, and the pairing of matter with antimatter. The verse is not a scientific textbook, but it hints at a universal law of complementarity that points to a single, wise Creator. The pairing of things refutes the polytheistic idea of a multiplicity of independent gods, because a perfectly complementary system requires a single will to design and harmonise it. It also demonstrates the Creator’s power to bring life out of combinations, which supports the possibility of resurrection. The call to glorify Allah here inspires awe; the more one learns about the paired nature of the universe, the more one recognizes the majesty of its Maker.
37 | There is also a sign for them in the night: We strip from it daylight, then —behold!—they are in darkness. | The surah now moves from the sign in the earth to the sign in the heavens, beginning with the night. The verse describes a daily miracle that people often take for granted. “We strip from it daylight” uses a vivid metaphor: daylight is like a covering that is gradually peeled away, leaving the darkness of the night. The verb “strip” (naslakhu) implies a slow, inevitable, and silent removal. Before one realizes it, the bright day has disappeared, and darkness has enveloped the world. “Then —behold!—they are in darkness” creates a sense of sudden awareness. The transition from day to night is so smooth that people go about their business until suddenly they find themselves surrounded by darkness. This is a sign of Allah’s absolute control over time and light. No human power can hold back the night or extend the day by a single moment. The regularity of this alternation is a mercy, providing a cycle of activity and rest. The night, with its darkness, forces human beings to pause and recognize their vulnerability. It is also a reminder of the darkness of the grave and the darkness that would cover the soul without the light of revelation. For the Meccans who denied the resurrection, the daily death of the day and its rebirth the next morning was a clear sign that the One who can strip away the light and bring it back can also take away life and restore it. The verse invites contemplation of the ordinary in order to perceive the extraordinary power behind it.
38 | The sun travels for its fixed term. That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing. | From the general phenomenon of night and day, the surah focuses specifically on the sun. The statement “The sun travels” (ash-shamsu tajrī) indicates that the sun is in motion, not stationary as some ancient civilizations believed. It moves in a precise, calculated course. “For its fixed term” (li-mustaqarrin lahā) has been interpreted in two primary ways. One is that the sun runs toward an appointed end point, a final resting place where its motion will cease at the end of the world. The other is that it travels a fixed, predetermined orbit or course for a specified period of time, as part of its daily and annual cycles. Both interpretations emphasize that the sun’s movement is not random or eternal; it is subject to the decree of its Creator. The verse then adds, “That is the design of the Almighty, All-Knowing.” The sun’s precise orbit, which provides exactly the right amount of heat and light to sustain life on earth, is the work of the Almighty (al-ʿAzīz), whose power is irresistible and who set the sun on its path with authority. It is also the design of the All-Knowing (al-ʿAlīm), whose knowledge encompasses every detail of the cosmic system, ensuring that the sun’s motion serves a greater purpose and does not deviate. The verse refutes sun-worshippers who divinized the sun; it declares that the sun is a created entity, obeying the command of its Lord. For the deniers of resurrection, the sun’s obedience to its fixed term is a sign that the universe is governed by a command, and that same command can just as easily bring about the resurrection.
39 | As for the moon, We have ordained ˹precise˺ phases for it, until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk. | After mentioning the sun, the surah turns to the moon, describing its divinely appointed phases. “We have ordained precise phases for it” (qaddarnāhu manāzil) refers to the lunar mansions or stations that the moon passes through night after night, causing its visible shape to change from a thin crescent to a full moon and back again. These phases are perfectly measured, allowing people to calculate time, months, and seasons. The lunar calendar, which governs Islamic worship including Ramadan and Hajj, is a direct result of this precise ordainment. The description “until it ends up like an old, curved palm stalk” (al-ʿurjūn al-qadīm) is a magnificent metaphor drawn from the Arab environment. After a date cluster has been harvested, the remaining stalk dries, becomes yellow, curves, and withers. The waning crescent moon in its final nights resembles that old, bent palm stalk in shape, colour, and frailty. This image conveys the cycle of the moon’s life: it is born as a crescent, grows to fullness, then withers and fades away, only to be renewed again. This cycle of renewal is another sign of resurrection. If the moon can reappear after it has withered to a thin curve, then the human being can also be brought back to life after death. The precise phases are a manifestation of Allah’s perfect wisdom; they are not random but ordained for human benefit. The verse encourages the believer to see the moon not just as a celestial object but as a reminder of the Creator’s meticulous planning and the certainty of the appointed term for all things.
40 | It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon, nor does the night outrun the day. Each is travelling in an orbit of their own. | This verse beautifully concludes the description of the celestial bodies by highlighting the perfect order and absence of conflict in the universe. “It is not for the sun to catch up with the moon” means that the sun cannot overtake the moon to eliminate the night or to encroach upon the moon’s distinct dominion. Their functions are separate and complementary. The sun, with its blazing light, rules the day, while the moon, with its gentle reflected light, governs the night and the calendar. “Nor does the night outrun the day” means that the night cannot prematurely overtake the day or extend beyond its appointed period. The alternation of night and day is so precise that neither transgresses its boundary. The final statement, “Each is travelling in an orbit of their own” (kullun fī falakin yasbaḥūn), is a profound declaration of cosmic order. The word “falak” refers to a rounded course or orbit, and “yasbaḥūn” means they are swimming or gliding smoothly. The sun, the moon, the night, and the day are all moving in paths ordained by Allah, without collision, without deviation, and without the need for human intervention. This cosmic harmony is a sign that the universe has a single, All-Powerful Controller. If there were multiple gods, there would be chaos and competition. The orderliness thus testifies to the oneness of Allah. It also shows that just as Allah has set precise laws for the celestial bodies, He has set a moral law for humanity and a fixed term for every soul, after which will come the judgment. The believer who contemplates the sky can feel at peace, knowing that the same God who keeps the stars in their orbits holds the affairs of their life in perfect wisdom.
41 | Another sign for them is that We carried their ancestors ˹with Noah˺ in the fully loaded Ark, | After presenting the celestial signs, the surah moves to a historical sign on water—a sign of mercy and deliverance intertwined with warning. “Another sign for them” links directly to the sequence of signs in the earth, the night, the sun, and the moon. Now the Quraysh are reminded of the story of Noah, which they knew as part of their ancestral tradition but had ceased to reflect upon. The verse says, “We carried their ancestors,” referring to those who were saved in the Ark with Noah. This is a profound point: the Quraysh themselves are descendants of the few believers who boarded the Ark, because all humanity after the flood descends from Noah and those with him. They owe their very existence to that merciful carriage. The Ark was “fully loaded” (al-fulk al-mashḥūn), meaning it was packed with the believers and a pair of every animal species, carrying the seeds of a new world. The event is presented not merely as a story but as a personal sign for the Meccans. Their own lineage traces back to a moment of divine rescue from a catastrophic flood that drowned the deniers. This should stir gratitude, awe, and fear. If Allah saved their forefathers by carrying them on water, He can just as easily carry them on their sea journeys, and He can also drown them if they persist in denial. The Ark, a seemingly fragile wooden vessel, withstood the raging flood by Allah’s command. It stands as an enduring symbol that safety lies not in material strength but in following God’s messenger. For the Prophet Muhammad, the parallel was clear: his followers, though small and weak in Makkah, were like the passengers on the Ark, destined for salvation, while the disbelieving majority faced destruction if they did not board the ship of faith.
42 | and created for them similar things to ride in. | The sign is not limited to the historical Ark; Allah reminds the Quraysh that He has created for them “similar things to ride in.” This refers to the ships, boats, and vessels that humans build and use to traverse the seas. The Quraysh were a trading people who undertook two major caravan journeys mentioned in Sūrah Quraysh, but they also knew maritime travel and the ships that docked at the ports of the Red Sea. The verse teaches that human ingenuity in shipbuilding is itself a divine gift. The wood, the metals, the knowledge of engineering, and the buoyancy of water are all created by Allah. When a person boards a ship, they are placing their trust in a vessel that floats by the laws Allah has ordained. The phrase “similar things” extends beyond watercraft to include all modes of transportation—camels, horses, and later cars and planes—anything that carries people and their goods. All of them are signs of Allah’s provision and teaching. This connects to the earlier theme of gratitude: people use these conveyances daily without reflecting on the One who made them possible. The verse also contains a subtle warning: just as Allah provided ships, He can also take away their utility in an instant. The ships that sail smoothly can become fragile shells in a storm. The sign, therefore, reminds the traveler that every journey is by Allah’s leave, and that the same Lord who gave the means of transport can also decree its end. It encourages the believer to recite the travel supplication and to journey with consciousness of their utter dependence on the Sustainer.
43 | If We willed, We could drown them: then no one would respond to their cries, nor would they be rescued— | This verse pulls back the curtain on human vulnerability. After speaking of the ships that people enjoy, Allah reveals how easily He could transform that blessing into destruction. “If We willed, We could drown them” is a direct statement of absolute power. No ship is unsinkable by its own nature; the sea that carries it can also swallow it by a single divine command. The verse then paints a terrifying picture of helplessness. “Then no one would respond to their cries” (ṣarīkh) describes a drowning person screaming for help. In that moment, all human strength, technology, and emergency systems become useless. There would be no coast guard, no rescue team, no power on earth that could save them if Allah did not will it. “Nor would they be rescued” (lā yunqadhūn) means they would not be saved or delivered from their plight. This image is meant to shake the heedless heart. The disbelievers who feel secure in their worldly means are reminded that their security is an illusion. The same sea that floats their trading vessels could be the grave of all their hopes. The verse is not meant to cause despair but to redirect reliance from the creation to the Creator. When one truly internalizes that no one can rescue them from a divinely willed calamity, the only logical response is to turn to the One who controls all things and to seek His protection before it is needed. The verse also carries echoes of the flood of Noah, where cries for help were unavailing after the decree was issued. It connects the sign of the Ark to the present and the future, weaving a coherent warning.
44 | except by mercy from Us, allowing them enjoyment for a ˹little˺ while. | The verse immediately provides the exception, the source of all safety. People are not drowned “except by mercy from Us.” The word “mercy” (raḥmah) encompasses Allah’s compassion, protection, and forbearance. Every safe voyage, every ship that reaches its harbor, does so purely because of Allah’s mercy, not because of human skill or favorable winds alone. The second part of the verse, “allowing them enjoyment for a little while” (matāʿan ilā ḥīn), places this mercy in the context of the temporary worldly life. The safe passage, the successful trade, the return to family—all of this is a short-term enjoyment. The word “little” (ḥīn) can mean a specified term, which is the lifespan of a person or the duration of this world. The enjoyment is a test and an opportunity to be grateful. When people survive a storm or complete a journey safely, they are given more time. The question is how they will use that time. Will they use the saved moments to turn to Allah in gratitude and obedience, or will they remain heedless and return to their old ways? The Quraysh were granted safety in their caravans and respect among the tribes precisely so that they might worship the Lord of the Kaʿbah, yet they filled His house with idols. This verse reminds them that the postponement of punishment is a mercy, but it will not last forever. The little while is running out. The believer, therefore, sees every day of safety as a gift to increase in good deeds. The verse ties the maritime sign directly to the urgency of repentance: the Ark of salvation through faith is still open, but the time of enjoyment granted is limited.
45 | ˹Still they turn away˺ when it is said to them, “Beware of what is ahead of you ˹in the Hereafter˺ and what is behind you ˹of destroyed nations˺ so you may be shown mercy.” | The surah now shifts to describe the disbelievers’ reaction to clear advice. The phrase “Still they turn away” is implied and captures their habitual rejection. When they are told to “beware of what is ahead of you and what is behind you,” they ignore the warning. “What is ahead of you” refers to the Hereafter—the approaching Day of Judgment, the Reckoning, Paradise, and Hell. “What is behind you” refers to the historical punishments that befell previous nations like ʿĀd, Thamūd, and the people of Noah. These two sources of warning—the past and the future—combine to give a complete picture: look at what happened to those who denied the truth before you, and look at what awaits you if you follow their path. The purpose of this warning is stated at the end: “so you may be shown mercy.” The warnings are not sent to satisfy divine anger but to guide people to the mercy of Allah. The very act of warning is a form of mercy. Yet the disbelievers turn away. Their turning away is not due to a lack of clarity; the warning is clear and comprehensive. It is due to arrogance, love of worldly life, and the false sense of security that the previous verses dismantled. This verse teaches the proper response to admonition: the believer should not turn away from reminders about the Hereafter and the lessons of history but should receive them with an open heart, hoping for Allah’s mercy. For the Prophet, the verse explains the frustrating reality that even clear, merciful warnings are often met with rejection.
46 | Whenever a sign comes to them from their Lord, they turn away from it. | This verse generalizes the disbelievers’ attitude. The word “sign” (āyah) in this context includes the verses of the Quran, the natural signs in the horizon and within themselves, and the miracles that they witnessed. The phrase “from their Lord” underscores the source of the signs; they are not from a human being but from their Creator, Sustainer, and ultimate Judge. This makes their turning away all the more severe. The action “they turn away” (aʿraḍū) implies a deliberate, conscious rejection, not a passive neglect. They physically and mentally turn aside, refusing to engage with the truth, mocking it, or busying themselves with other things. The use of “whenever” (wa mā taʾtīhim) indicates that this is a persistent pattern. It is not a one-time refusal but a habitual response. Every new sign becomes another occasion for rejection. This is the tragic state of a heart that has become sealed due to continuous sin and arrogance. The verse also highlights a profound irony: the signs are given to guide them, but their rejection of the signs becomes the cause of their further misguidance and the justification for their punishment. This pattern is a warning for all believers to honour the signs of Allah when they come, whether it is a verse recited, a sermon heard, or a reflection upon creation. One should immediately turn towards the sign with acceptance and reflection, not away from it with indifference. The verse also served to comfort the Prophet; the rejection he faced was not personal but a symptom of a deeper spiritual sickness that had afflicted many before them.
47 | And when it is said to them, “Donate from what Allah has provided for you,” the disbelievers say to the believers, “Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed if He wanted to? You are clearly astray!” | This verse presents a concrete example of the disbelievers’ arrogance and twisted logic. They are invited to spend in charity from the wealth that Allah has provided them. The command to donate is a test of gratitude and compassion. However, the disbelievers respond with a sarcastic argument that sounds logical on the surface but is deeply flawed. They say, “Why should we feed those whom Allah could have fed if He wanted to?” This is an attempt to use divine decree as an excuse for miserliness and neglect. They argue that if Allah had willed, He could have made the poor rich, so the fact that they are poor must be what Allah wants, and they should not interfere. This argument is a gross misuse of the concept of predestination. Allah’s will includes the creation of rich and poor as a test: the rich are tested with their wealth to see if they will give, and the poor are tested with their poverty to see if they will be patient. The disbelievers twist this test into a justification for oppression. They then add, “You are clearly astray,” accusing the believers of being misguided for advocating charity. This is a complete inversion of morality: generosity is called misguidance, and selfishness is presented as wisdom. The verse exposes the mindset that leads to economic injustice and the dehumanization of the poor. It also shows that mocking the commands of Allah only compounds one’s guilt. For believers, it is a reminder to never use theological arguments to escape moral obligations, and to expect that doing good may invite ridicule from those who love this world.
48 | And they ask ˹the believers˺, “When will this threat come to pass, if what you say is true?” | The disbelievers shift from mocking charity to mocking the promise of the Hereafter. Their question is not a sincere inquiry but a challenge dripping with disbelief and sarcasm. “When will this threat come to pass?” refers to the Day of Judgment, the resurrection, and the punishment they were warned about. By demanding a specific date, they think they are exposing the believers as frauds. If the believers cannot name the hour, the disbelievers assume the whole thing is a myth. This is a classic tactic of those who reject the unseen: they demand empirical proof on their own terms. The question also reveals their underlying assumption that because the punishment has not yet arrived, it never will. They fail to understand that the delay is a mercy and a test, not a sign of falsehood. The phrase “if what you say is true” is a conditional challenge meant to belittle the believers. The verse captures the psychological state of the Meccan elite: they were so entrenched in their worldly power and so accustomed to immediate results that they could not fathom a delayed but certain reckoning. For believers, this verse is a reminder not to be disheartened by such mockery. The timing of the Hour is known only to Allah, and its suddenness is part of its terror and wisdom. The answer to their question is about to be given in the next verses, not with a date, but with a description of the suddenness that will leave them no time to prepare or escape.
49 | They must be awaiting a single Blast, which will seize them while they are ˹entrenched˺ in ˹worldly˺ disputes. | The answer to their mockery is a chilling warning. They are not awaiting a date they can mark on a calendar; they are awaiting “a single Blast” (ṣayḥatan wāḥidah). This is the first blow of the Trumpet, a terrifying cosmic shout that will bring the world to an end. It echoes the “one blast” that destroyed the town earlier in the surah, connecting the present warning to the past parable. The phrase “while they are entrenched in worldly disputes” paints a vivid picture of the moment it strikes. They will be going about their daily lives, arguing over business deals, debating politics, haggling in the marketplace, or quarrelling with their families, completely oblivious to what is about to happen. The word “entrenched” (yakhiṣṣimūn) implies intense engagement in conflict and worldly affairs. There will be no special preamble, no warning signal that gives them time to repent. The Blast will seize them in the midst of their heedlessness. This is a direct response to their question: they want to know when the threat will come, but when it comes, they will have no chance to ask. The verse is a powerful reminder to the living that worldly disputes, which seem so important, will be rendered utterly meaningless in an instant. The believer should not be among those who are caught up in such disputes at the expense of preparing for the Hereafter. It also assures the Prophet that the judgment is in Allah’s hands and will come at a time the mockers least expect.
50 | Then they will not be able to make a ˹last˺ will, nor can they return to their own people. | The suddenness of the Blast is further emphasized. After it strikes, they will not have the opportunity to make a last will or to give final instructions about their wealth and dependents. This detail is particularly sharp because the Arabs took great pride in leaving wills and being remembered through their inheritance arrangements. The Blast will cut off all such worldly concerns in an instant. Moreover, “nor can they return to their own people” means they will not be able to go back to their families or homes. This could refer to the fact that they are instantly killed and thus cannot return from wherever the Blast struck them, or that after the Blast, the world as they know it ends, and the phase of this life is permanently closed. There will be no second chance, no reprieve to make amends or to say goodbye. This verse reinforces the finality of the Blast. The opportunity to do good, to repent, to correct one’s affairs, ends with death. The Islamic teaching that one should have a will ready and that one should settle debts and disputes promptly is rooted in this awareness. Life is uncertain, and the end can come at any moment. The verse also serves as a warning to those who delay their repentance, thinking they have plenty of time. When the collective Blast comes, it is too late for everyone. The scene transitions now from the end of the world to the resurrection.
51 | The Trumpet will be blown ˹a second time˺, then—behold!— they will rush from the graves to their Lord. | After the first Blast which brings death to all, the second blowing of the Trumpet will occur. This is the blast of resurrection. The suddenness is captured by “then—behold!” (fa-idhā hum), a dramatic Quranic expression that depicts the scene as instantaneous. The dead will rise from their graves and “rush” (yansilūn) toward their Lord. The word “rush” implies speed, urgency, and an irresistible force drawing them to the place of gathering. They will not have time to hesitate or hide. Some will emerge in a state of confusion, others in fear, all moving toward the plain of assembly. The phrase “to their Lord” indicates the ultimate destination. They are not rising to resume their earthly lives but to stand before their Creator for judgment. This verse directly answers the earlier mockery about the bones being revived. The image is clear: the graves will split open, and humanity will stream forth like locusts, as described in other surahs. For the Meccans, who buried their dead in the desert sands and saw no possibility of return, this was an extraordinary claim. But the One who created them the first time can certainly recreate them. The verse turns a theoretical belief into a vivid, almost cinematic reality, urging the listener to live as if they can already see that day. The rushing to the Lord is a moment of utter accountability, and the believer hopes to be among those who rush with anticipation, not with terror.
52 | They will cry, “Woe to us! Who has raised us up from our place of rest? This must be what the Most Compassionate warned us of; the messengers told the truth!” | The resurrected disbelievers express their shock and sudden recognition. Their first cry is one of distress: “Woe to us!” (yā waylanā), a profound expression of regret and grief. They then ask, in their disorientation, “Who has raised us up from our place of rest?” The “place of rest” (marqad) refers to their graves, which they had assumed would be their final, eternal resting place. They had believed that death was the end, a long sleep from which there would be no awakening. Now that they are awake, they are stunned. The question is not asked for information but out of bewildered horror. Then immediately, without waiting for an answer, their own conscience or the angels supply the answer: “This must be what the Most Compassionate warned us of; the messengers told the truth!” The use of “the Most Compassionate” (ar-Raḥmān) is poignant. They now recall the very attribute of Allah that they were invited to trust. They had mocked the warnings of a merciful Lord, and now they see that those warnings were utterly true. The final admission, “the messengers told the truth,” is the bitter confession of those who have lost everything. In the world, they called the messengers liars; now, with the punishment before their eyes, they must acknowledge the truth. But this declaration of faith at that moment is of no benefit to them, because faith after seeing the punishment is not accepted. The verse stirs a deep fear of being among those who recognize the truth only when it is too late.
53 | It will only take one Blast, then at once they will all be brought before Us. | This verse reiterates the simplicity and suddenness of the resurrection event. The entire cosmic process of gathering humanity from the first to the last is accomplished with “one Blast” (ṣayḥatan wāḥidah). There is no lengthy procedure, no complicated machinery of resurrection; a single divine command uttered by the angel Isrāfīl upon the Trumpet is sufficient. The phrase “then at once” (fa-idhā hum) again creates the effect of a scene change that happens in the blink of an eye. “They will all be brought before Us” emphasizes the totality and the destination. No one, whether believer or disbeliever, high or low, will be left behind. They will be “brought before Us” meaning presented before Allah for the reckoning. This is a scene of complete exposure, where all of humanity stands in the court of the King of all kings. The repetition of the Blast imagery throughout this passage serves to dismantle the disbelievers’ argument that resurrection would require a great, observable, and perhaps impossible effort. Allah is declaring that it is astonishingly easy for Him. A single command, “Be,” and it is. This verse also ties back to the earlier destruction of the town with one blast. The same effortless power that destroys can also recreate. For the believer, the simplicity of the resurrection magnifies Allah’s greatness and serves as a constant reminder that the Day is not a distant possibility but an imminent reality that can arrive at any moment.
54 | On that Day no soul will be wronged in the least, nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do. | This verse establishes the absolute justice of the Day of Judgment. The first principle is that “no soul will be wronged in the least” (lā tuẓlamu nafsun shayʾan). This is a comprehensive guarantee against any injustice. No one will receive a punishment greater than they deserve, no sin will be invented for them, and no good deed will be diminished. This is a direct response to the fear that God might act arbitrarily. Allah, being the Most Just, will not wrong anyone by an atom’s weight. The second principle is that “nor will you be rewarded except for what you used to do.” This is the principle of recompense based on deeds. Paradise is not earned by mere claim or lineage; it is granted through divine mercy, but entry and the ranks within it correspond to the deeds performed with sincere faith. This verse eliminates two common misconceptions. The first is that a merciful God will simply forgive everyone regardless of their actions, making moral striving pointless. The second is that the judgment might be arbitrary or biased. Instead, the Quran presents a perfectly just system where every individual’s own actions—recorded in the perfect Record mentioned in verse 12—determine their outcome. This motivates the believer to fill their scale with good deeds and to avoid any transgression. It also warns the oppressor that every act of injustice will be accounted for, and the oppressed will receive their rights. The justice of that Day is a source of profound peace for those who were wronged in this world without redress.
55 | Indeed, on that Day the residents of Paradise will be busy enjoying themselves. | The surah now contrasts the fate of the disbelievers with the state of the people of Paradise. The word “Indeed” (inna) gives emphasis and certainty. The residents of Paradise are described as being “busy enjoying themselves” (fī shughulin fākihūn). The word “busy” might seem strange, as we associate busyness with work and stress, but here it refers to being delightfully occupied with pleasures that never grow tiresome. They are so engrossed in the joys of Paradise—its foods, drinks, companions, sights, and conversations—that they are completely free from any thought of the worldly suffering or of the fate of the condemned. Their busyness is not a chore; it is pure, rapturous enjoyment. Some commentators note that this implies they are too occupied with bliss to even notice the punishment of the disbelievers, which is a mercy for them, as seeing the suffering of others might cause them grief. They are in a state of perfect, all-encompassing happiness. This verse answers the deep human longing for a life free from anxiety and boredom. In this world, even moments of pleasure are tinged with the awareness that they will end. In Paradise, the enjoyment is continuous and ever-renewed. The verse invites the listener to desire that station and to work for it. It also implicitly contrasts with the believers in this world who were “busy” with worship and good deeds; their worldly busyness has now been transformed into eternal delight.
56 | They and their spouses will be in ˹cool˺ shade, reclining on ˹canopied˺ couches. | The description of the residents of Paradise becomes more detailed. “They and their spouses” (azwāj) refers to their wives or companions in Paradise. This promise includes the reunion of righteous spouses who both enter Paradise, as well as the spouses specially created for the believers in Paradise. The concept of azwāj indicates companionship, love, and the fulfilment of human relational needs in a pure and elevated form. The setting is described as “in cool shade” (ẓilāl). The shade in Paradise is not a dim shadow but a perfectly comfortable, protective covering that shields them from any excessive heat or glare, while allowing delightful light. For the Arabs, who lived under a scorching sun, the image of eternal, pleasant shade was particularly evocative of luxury. They will be “reclining on canopied couches” (ʿalā al-arāʾiki muttakiʾūn). The word arāʾik refers to raised, curtained couches or thrones, indicating honour, comfort, and royal treatment. Reclining is a posture of relaxation and security, as opposed to the anxious standing and rushing of the Day of Judgment. The canopies suggest privacy and elegance. This picture of marital harmony, physical ease, and luxurious surroundings shows that Paradise is not merely a spiritual state but a tangible, physical reality that satisfies the senses in the most beautiful way. It also gives believers a standard to look forward to, encouraging them to maintain their faith and chastity in this world for the sake of that eternal companionship.
57 | There they will have fruits and whatever they desire. | The provision of Paradise is highlighted. Fruits are specifically mentioned, echoing the earlier earthly sign of dates and grapes. The fruits of Paradise are similar in name to those of the world, but their taste, quality, and abundance are unimaginably superior. More importantly, they will have “whatever they desire” (mā yaddaʿūn). This opens the door to the fulfilment of every lawful longing and wish. Whatever the soul craves for pleasure, beauty, or delight, it will be granted. This is the ultimate freedom from lack and frustration. In this world, human desires are often unfulfilled or, if fulfilled, they bring with them consequences and dissatisfaction. In Paradise, desire and fulfilment are perfectly aligned, and there is no harm in anything they wish for. This verse also contains a deep spiritual meaning: the highest desire of the believers is to see Allah and to be close to Him, and that too will be granted. The phrase “whatever they desire” thus encompasses both the sensory delights and the spiritual ecstasy of divine proximity. The verse is a powerful motivator. It tells the believer that no sacrifice made for Allah in this life is in vain, because in the Hereafter, the doors of pure and complete satisfaction will be opened wide. The contrast with the disbelievers, who will have nothing they desire and will be in total deprivation, is stark and deliberate.
58 | And “Peace!” will be ˹their˺ greeting from the Merciful Lord. | This verse crowns the description of Paradise with its most sublime blessing. The greeting “Peace” (salām) is not merely a word spoken by the inhabitants to one another but a direct salutation from the Merciful Lord Himself. This is a mark of unparalleled honour and intimacy. Allah, the Lord of all worlds, addresses the believers with a word that conveys safety, serenity, and complete well-being. It means that they are now permanently safe from any harm, punishment, sorrow, or divine displeasure. The greeting is from “the Merciful Lord” (ar-Raḥmān), emphasizing that this peace flows directly from His attribute of mercy. The very Lord whom they worshipped and trusted in the unseen, the Lord they called upon in their distress, now welcomes them with this blessed word. Some scholars note that when the believers hear this greeting from Allah, they will experience a joy that surpasses all other pleasures of Paradise. It signifies the fulfillment of the verse “They will have whatever they wish for, and We have more” (50:35). The greeting of Salām also confirms that the relationship between the Lord and His servants has reached a state of complete mutual pleasure. There is no anger, no reproach, only peace. This verse gives the believer in this world a taste of that peace through the remembrance of Allah, knowing that the ultimate Salām awaits them. It also serves as a final, sharp contrast to the state of the disbelievers, who will now be addressed in a very different manner.
59 | ˹Then the disbelievers will be told,˺ “Step away ˹from the believers˺ this Day, O wicked ones! | The scene shifts dramatically. While the believers are enjoying peace and bliss, the disbelievers are given a harsh command. The phrase “Step away” (imtāzū) means to separate, to be distinguished, or to stand apart. On the Day of Judgment, the disbelievers will be sorted from the believers and ordered to distance themselves. This separation is a humiliation and a confirmation of their status as outcasts from Allah’s mercy. The address “O wicked ones” (ayyuhā al-mujrimūn) labels them by their identity: they are criminals. Their crime is not merely a minor offense; it is the major crime of disbelief, associating partners with Allah, rejecting His messengers, and leading a life of transgression. This command to separate is a public display of their disgrace. In the world, they mingled with the believers, perhaps even mocking them, but on this Day, the believers are honoured and they are shunned. The verse also indicates that there is no place for the wicked near the righteous. The communities that were once mixed are now divided for eternity. This separation begins at the moment of judgment and continues into their respective final abodes. The command is a powerful warning that association with believers in this life is a blessing that will be withdrawn from those who reject faith. It also comforts the believers who were ridiculed and marginalized; they will see their mockers publicly shamed and separated from them, while they are received with honour. The verse initiates the direct speech that will be addressed to the disbelievers in the following verses.
60 | Did I not command you, O Children of Adam, not to follow Satan, for he is truly your sworn enemy, | After the command to separate, Allah directly addresses the disbelieving humans as “O Children of Adam.” This address ties the warning back to the very beginning of creation, to the original enmity between Adam, peace be upon him, and Satan. Allah reminds them of a command that was embedded in the human conscience and conveyed through all the prophets: “Did I not command you not to follow Satan?” This question is a form of rebuke that carries the weight of undeniable truth. It reminds them that they were not left without guidance; the command was clear. The description of Satan as “your sworn enemy” (ʿaduwwun mubīn) is crucial. An enemy who is open and clear in his hostility should be fought against, not befriended or obeyed. Yet humanity repeatedly fell for Satan’s deceptions, treating him as a hidden advisor when he is in fact the most obvious enemy. This verse explains the root cause of the disbelievers’ ruin: they followed Satan’s whispers and footsteps instead of the divine command. The question format leaves them speechless; they have no excuse. They cannot say they were not warned, because the warning was given through the primordial covenant and through the succession of messengers. For the living, this verse is a stark reminder to examine one’s own life: are we, knowingly or unknowingly, following the path of an enemy who desires our eternal destruction? The only safe course is to adhere strictly to Allah’s command and to recognize Satan’s enmity in every temptation.
61 | but to worship Me ˹alone˺? This is the Straight Path. | This verse continues the divine rebuke on the Day of Judgment, completing the command given to the Children of Adam that began in the previous verse. Allah reminds them that the negative command—not to follow Satan—had a corresponding positive command: “but to worship Me alone.” The word “but” sets up a clear contrast. On one side is the path of Satan, which leads to destruction; on the other side is the worship of the One true God, which is the very purpose of creation. The exclusive worship of Allah is the heart of the message brought by every prophet. This command was not an arbitrary rule but a natural consequence of Allah being the Creator, Sustainer, and Lord of all. To worship Him alone means to direct all acts of devotion—prayer, supplication, sacrifice, hope, and fear—solely to Him, without any partner. The verse then declares, “This is the Straight Path.” The Straight Path is the religion of truth that leads directly to Allah’s pleasure and Paradise. It is the path described in Sūrah al-Fātiḥah, which the believer asks to be guided to multiple times a day. By defining the worship of Allah alone as the Straight Path, the verse explicitly states that any deviation into idolatry, whether in the form of worshiping idols, saints, angels, or one’s own desires, is a crooked path that leads to Hell. The use of the word “Straight” implies it is clear, uncomplicated, and direct. There are no hidden turns or confusing intersections for the one who sincerely seeks God. The rebuke on that Day will make the disbelievers realize that they abandoned a clear and simple truth for a complex web of falsehoods. For the living, this verse is a powerful call to constantly evaluate whether one’s life is truly aligned with pure monotheism and the Straight Path, or whether subtle forms of shirk have crept into one’s heart and actions.
62 | Yet he already misled great multitudes of you. Did you not have any sense? | The address to the disbelievers continues, now focusing on the reality of Satan’s success in leading humanity astray. “Yet he already misled great multitudes of you” is a statement of painful fact. The word “multitudes” (jibillan kathīran) indicates vast numbers of people, generation after generation, who fell into Satan’s trap. This includes the disbelievers being addressed, their ancestors, and many nations before them. The phrasing implies that they were part of a great crowd of the misled, which should have been a warning in itself. Following the crowd is not a sign of truth when the crowd is on the wrong path. Then comes the piercing question: “Did you not have any sense?” (afalam takūnū taʿqilūn). This rhetorical question appeals to the intellect that Allah bestowed upon human beings. Reason is a gift meant to distinguish truth from falsehood, right from wrong. Allah is asking: did you not use your minds to see through Satan’s deceptions? Did you not consider the evidence of creation, the message of the prophets, and the terrible end of those who followed misguidance? The question exposes the utter failure of the disbelievers to employ their God-given faculties. They had intellects but allowed desires, traditions, and blind following to override reason. This verse teaches that faith is not a leap into irrationality but a step of profound intelligence. True wisdom lies in recognizing one’s Creator and obeying Him, while disbelief is a form of stupidity that squanders the highest human faculty. The question also carries a deep sense of regret; on the Day of Judgment, the disbelievers will wish they had used their minds, but that realization will come too late. The verse encourages the living to reflect, to use their reason to assess the world, the self, and the revelation, and to conclude that worshiping Allah alone is the only sensible path.
63 | This is the Hell you were warned of. | With stern finality, the disbelievers are now presented with the reality of their punishment. The demonstrative “This” (hādhihi) makes the threat tangible and immediate. On the Day of Judgment, Hell will be brought near, and they will see it with their own eyes. The veil of the unseen is lifted, and what was once a distant warning becomes a terrifying presence. The phrase “you were warned of” (kuntum tūʿadūn) emphasizes that this is not a surprise. The messengers came with clear warnings, describing the fire, its intensity, and its eternal nature. The disbelievers had mocked these warnings, asking when they would come to pass. Now the warning is fulfilled, and the mockery is replaced with horror. The verse is brief but devastating, serving as the ultimate proof that Allah’s promise is true. The mental state of the condemned at this moment is unimaginable. They realize that every word of the Quran, every admonition from the prophets, was the absolute truth. Their previous laughter and arrogance now count for nothing. This verse also serves as a powerful reminder to the believers of the urgency of faith. The Hell that was warned of is not a myth or a metaphor; it is a created reality that one must strive to avoid. The connection to the earlier verses is clear: the disbelievers had rejected the Straight Path, followed Satan, and failed to use their intellect, and the natural consequence of those choices is now physically in front of them. The verse leaves no room for ambiguity: the warning was real, and now so is the punishment. This moment is the severest form of regret, a regret that the Quran asks us to experience in our hearts now, through belief in the unseen, so that we may be saved from experiencing it then as condemned reality.
64 | Burn in it Today for your disbelief. | The command to enter Hell is given with uncompromising directness. The word “Burn” (iṣlaw) is an imperative, but it is not a command that can be obeyed or disobeyed; it is a decree of fate being pronounced. The addition of “Today” (al-yawm) emphasizes that this is the Day of Recompense, the day when accounts are settled. The temporal marker contrasts with their days in the world when they were given respite and opportunity. Those days are gone forever. The reason for this punishment is stated plainly: “for your disbelief” (bimā kuntum takfurūn). This is a crucial point of justice. The punishment is not arbitrary or merely because Allah willed it without cause; it is a direct consequence of their own chosen action—their persistent kufr. Kufr here encompasses their denial of Allah’s oneness, their rejection of the messengers, their mockery of the resurrection, and their ingratitude for divine blessings. The clarity of this causal link upholds the principle stated earlier that no soul is wronged. They are burning because of what they used to do. The command is also a manifestation of Allah’s wrath against those who arrogantly defied Him. While Allah is the Most Merciful, His mercy is for those who sought it in the world. Those who die upon disbelief have placed themselves beyond the reach of mercy by their own choice. The verse is profoundly terrifying, but it also serves a merciful purpose for those who hear it in this life. It is a warning that strips away complacency and urges the listener to flee from disbelief and its causes. It reminds the believer to hold fast to faith and to fear a Day when such a command might be directed at anyone who neglected their duty to their Lord.
65 | On this Day We will seal their mouths, their hands will speak to Us, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit. | The scene of judgment reveals yet another profound dimension of divine justice. The disbelievers, now facing the reality of Hell, might attempt to deny their sins, just as they did in the world. But on this Day, Allah will “seal their mouths.” Their ability to speak and offer false excuses will be taken away. They will not be able to lie, argue, or plead innocence, because the ultimate witnesses will be brought forth—their own body parts. Their hands will speak, and their feet will testify to what they used to commit. This is not metaphorical; it is a literal miraculous event where Allah grants speech to the limbs that once performed the deeds. The hands that stole, struck unjustly, or signed false documents will recount their actions. The feet that walked to forbidden places or stood in defiance of truth will bear witness. This scene is both terrifying and perfectly just. It silences any protest from the sinner, because the testimony comes from within their own body, not from external witnesses who could be accused of bias. It also demonstrates that sins leave an imprint on the limbs, an imprint that will be articulated on the Day of Judgment. For the believer, this verse instills a deep sense of mindfulness. Knowing that one’s own hands and feet might testify should profoundly influence behavior. It encourages the use of one’s limbs in acts of obedience: praying, giving charity, walking to the mosque, so that on that Day, one’s body parts will testify in one’s favour. The verse also shows that no sin is truly hidden; even if one escapes worldly detection, the record is preserved within one’s own physical being by the All-Powerful Creator.
66 | Had We willed, We could have easily blinded their eyes, so they would struggle to find their way. How then could they see? | The surah shifts back to the worldly life, reminding the Quraysh of Allah’s absolute power over them, which should serve as a warning. “Had We willed, We could have easily blinded their eyes” (ʿalā aʿyunihim) refers to the power to strike them with immediate physical blindness. The phrase “easily” is not in the text but captured in the meaning: for Allah, it is a matter of simple will. The result would be that they would “struggle to find their way” (fastabaqū aṣ-ṣirāṭ), meaning they would rush and stumble, trying to traverse the paths they once knew, but they would be unable to see. The question “How then could they see?” emphasizes that once Allah removes sight, no one can restore it. The physical blindness is a metaphor for spiritual blindness, but the literal sense is also intended as a threat. The Quraysh who walked confidently through the mountain passes of Makkah, proud of their knowledge of the routes, could be rendered helpless in an instant. This verse reminds them that their eyesight is a loan from Allah, which He can reclaim at any moment. Their failure to use their eyes to see the signs of Allah in creation and revelation could result in the removal of that blessing altogether. The lesson extends to all people: the faculties we take for granted are divine gifts. If we use them in disobedience, we risk losing them, either literally or through the loss of their spiritual benefit. The verse also ties back to the earlier image of barriers being placed before and behind the disbelievers; here, the blindness is another form of that seal. The urgency is to use one’s sight to recognize the truth before the will of Allah decrees otherwise.
67 | And had We willed, We could have transfigured them on the spot, so they could neither progress forward nor turn back. | The warning escalates from blindness to total physical immobilization and disfigurement. “Transfigured them on the spot” (la-masakhnāhum ʿalā makānatihim) means that Allah could transform their forms into something else—such as stones or apes—right where they stand, as a punishment. This is not without precedent; the Quran mentions earlier nations that were physically transformed for their transgressions, such as the Sabbath-breakers who were turned into apes. The phrase “so they could neither progress forward nor turn back” describes the state of complete paralysis and helplessness that would follow. They would be stuck in their places, unable to advance toward any goal or retreat to safety. This is a terrifying image of divine power. The Meccans who arrogantly moved about their business, thinking themselves masters of their destiny, are reminded that their very ability to move is a gift from Allah, revocable in an instant. The verse also symbolizes the spiritual state of the disbelievers: they are already transfigured in a moral sense, unable to move forward toward faith or turn back from their evil ways. The warning is merciful in its essence; it is given in the world so that people might repent before such a punishment befalls them. The fact that they were still walking, talking, and conducting trade was a sign of Allah’s forbearance, not of His inability to punish. This should inspire the believer to constantly thank Allah for the ability to move and act, and to use that ability in His obedience before the day comes when it is too late.
68 | And whoever We grant a long life, We reverse them in development. Will they not then understand? | This verse presents a universal sign from human experience. It states a natural law: when a person is granted a long life, reaching advanced old age, Allah reverses them in creation. The physical and mental faculties that were once strong become weak. Strength turns to frailty, sharp memory to forgetfulness, youthfulness to senility. This process is a sign of the fleeting nature of worldly power and the certainty of the eventual end. Just as Allah can progress a person from weakness to strength and then back to weakness, He can easily bring about the greater transformation of resurrection. The question “Will they not then understand?” challenges the intellect. If they observe the inevitable decline of the human body, they should realize that this world is not a place of permanence. The One who can orchestrate this reversal in a single lifetime has the power to bring the dead back to life for judgment. The verse also serves as a reminder of human dignity and humility. The strongest among us will one day require the care we once gave to others. This should cultivate compassion and patience with the elderly. For the young, it is a warning not to be arrogant about their strength, for it is a temporary loan. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that respecting the elderly is part of glorifying Allah, because their white hairs are a sign of a life spent under Allah’s decree. The verse thus links biology to theology, using the observable process of aging as a proof for the unseen reality of resurrection and a call to humility and faith.
69 | We have not taught him poetry, nor is it fitting for him. This ˹Book˺ is only a Reminder and a clear Quran | The surah now directly addresses one of the Meccans’ key accusations: that the Prophet Muhammad was a poet and the Quran was poetry. Allah categorically denies this. “We have not taught him poetry” means that the Quran is not the product of poetic training or literary skill. The Prophet was unlettered; he had never composed a line of poetry. “Nor is it fitting for him” means that poetry, with its associations with exaggeration, imaginative falsehood, and emotional manipulation, does not befit the station of prophethood. This does not denigrate all poetry, for some poetry contains wisdom, but it distinguishes the Quran sharply from the craft of the poet. The statement then defines what the Quran truly is: “only a Reminder and a clear Quran.” The word “Reminder” (dhikr) means it is a message that awakens the human soul to what it already knows deep within—its duty to its Creator. It is a “clear Quran” (qurʾānin mubīn), meaning a recitation that makes truth manifest, distinguishing right from wrong with unmistakable clarity. The clarity of the Quran stands in stark contrast to the ambiguity of the soothsayers’ rhymes and the poets’ fancies. This verse was crucial in early Makkah because the pagan leaders were trying to classify the Prophet within a familiar, dismissible category. By denying poetry and asserting the Quran’s nature as a divine Reminder, Allah forces them to confront the message on its own terms. The verse also teaches believers to appreciate the Quran as something far above human literature; it is the speech of the Lord, bringing guidance and warning, not entertainment or aesthetic pleasure alone.
70 | to warn whoever is ˹truly˺ alive and fulfil the decree ˹of torment˺ against the disbelievers. | This verse states the dual purpose of the Quran. The first purpose is “to warn whoever is truly alive.” This does not refer to physical life, for the disbelievers were physically alive. It refers to spiritual life—a heart that is receptive, aware, and capable of being moved by the truth. A person whose heart is dead in heedlessness and sin does not benefit from warnings, just as a corpse does not hear the living. The warning only benefits those who have a living heart, one that can fear, love, and hope. This connects beautifully with the earlier descriptions of those who are heedless and those who follow the Reminder. The second purpose is “to fulfil the decree of torment against the disbelievers.” The revelation of the Quran serves as a proof for or against people. For those who reject it, it becomes the basis upon which the just decree of punishment is fulfilled. Their rejection after clear warning leaves them with no excuse, making the punishment fully deserved. This dual function of the Quran—a source of life for the believers and a documentation of guilt for the rejecters—highlights the immense responsibility that comes with hearing its message. It is not a neutral book; it either guides or it testifies against. The verse also reassures the Prophet: his duty is to deliver the warning. Those who respond are the truly alive, and those who persist in disbelief are simply fulfilling a decree that their own actions have made inevitable. The believer is urged to be among the truly alive, those whose hearts respond to the Quran with humility and acceptance, so that the Book becomes a source of mercy, not a proof of condemnation.
71 | Do they not see that We singlehandedly created for them, among other things, cattle which are under their control? | The surah returns to the signs of Allah’s power and mercy, this time focusing on domesticated animals. The question “Do they not see” calls the disbelievers to observe the obvious reality in their daily lives. The phrase “We singlehandedly created for them” (innā khalaqnā lahum) emphasizes that these animals are a direct creation of Allah, designed specifically for human benefit. The “singlehandedly” is rendered from the emphatic structure, highlighting that no one assisted Allah in this creation. “Among other things” (mimmā ʿamilat aydīnā) literally means “from what Our hands have made,” an expression of special honour and direct divine crafting. The cattle—camels, cows, sheep, and goats—are “under their control” (mālikūn). This means Allah has subjected these animals to human authority. Despite their size and strength, a human child can lead a large camel. This subjection is not a human achievement but a divine gift. The verse asks the Meccans to reflect on the livestock that were central to their economy and survival. The power to create such complex, beneficial creatures, and to make them subservient to humans, belongs to Allah alone. If He can create these animals and subject them, He can certainly subject creation to His will for the resurrection. The verse also implies a question of gratitude: who gave you these cattle? If you recognize that it is Allah, then why do you not worship Him alone? The sign is so close and familiar that it is often overlooked, but the Quran directs attention to it to awaken faith and thankfulness.
72 | And We have subjected these ˹animals˺ to them, so they may ride some and eat others. | The theme of subjection is made explicit. “We have subjected these animals to them” (dhallalnāhā lahum) means that Allah has made them tame, manageable, and suited to human needs. This is a specific act of divine taming. Wild animals of similar or smaller size would not submit to such service. The purpose is then detailed: “so they may ride some and eat others.” The camel, specifically, was the ship of the desert, used for riding and carrying heavy loads across vast distances. Without the camel, life and trade in Arabia would have been nearly impossible. Cows and oxen were used for plowing, sheep and goats for their meat, milk, and wool. The benefit of riding and eating are the two most apparent uses, but they stand for all the services animals provide. This verse reinforces the idea that human dominion over animals is not a right humans earned but a trust from Allah. The animals are a provision and a mercy. Reflecting on this, one should recognize the Giver and use the animals with kindness, as the Prophet taught. The verse also forms a logical link: the One who subjected these powerful beasts for your benefit can subject everything in the heavens and the earth to His command. The resurrection is merely another act of subjection. The sign of the cattle thus becomes a daily reminder of Allah’s power, mercy, and the certainty of His promise.
73 | And they derive from them other benefits and drinks. Will they not then give thanks? | The benefits of cattle are elaborated. Beyond riding and meat, there are “other benefits” (manāfiʿ) and “drinks” (mashārib). The benefits include their wool, fur, and hair, which provide clothing, tents, and furnishings. Their skins were used for water skins and containers. Their dung could be used as fuel. The drinks refer primarily to milk, a pure, wholesome beverage that emerges from between the animal’s waste and blood, as described in Sūrah an-Naḥl. Milk was a staple of the Arab diet and a symbol of pure, easy sustenance. The verse then poses the same powerful question: “Will they not then give thanks?” After listing blessing after blessing, the Quran points to the human tendency to enjoy the gifts while ignoring the Giver. True thankfulness is not just verbal but practical: it means using these blessings in obedience to Allah and directing worship to Him alone. The disbelievers, who benefited from their cattle daily yet worshipped idols, were the epitome of ingratitude. This verse teaches the believer to develop a habit of seeing blessings as signs, and to respond with gratitude that is expressed in worship, charity, and sharing the bounty with others. The sequence of signs—from the dead earth to the cattle—is all building a cumulative case for the existence, oneness, and mercy of the Creator, which logically demands a response of faith and gratitude. The failure to give thanks is a fundamental flaw that leads to the decay of the soul and the invitation of divine punishment.
74 | Still they have taken other gods besides Allah, hoping to be helped ˹by them˺. | Despite the overwhelming signs of Allah’s creative power and mercy, the disbelievers persist in their greatest error: shirk. “Still they have taken” (ittakhadhū) implies a deliberate act of adoption. They have chosen for themselves other gods besides Allah. The phrase “hoping to be helped” (laʿallahum yunṣarūn) reveals their motivation. They turned to idols, saints, or other deities because they hoped for assistance, protection, or intercession. This hope is tragically misplaced. The verse exposes the irrationality of polytheism: after showing that Allah alone created the earth, the heavens, and the cattle, and that He alone can give life, provide, and protect, how can one turn to powerless objects for help? The disbelievers hoped that their gods would aid them in worldly matters or intercede for them in the Hereafter. The Quran systematically demolishes this hope, showing that these false gods are incapable of any help. The verse also highlights a common human psychology: in times of fear or need, people seek a powerful protector. The disbelievers, lacking faith in the unseen power of Allah, manufactured visible, tangible protectors. The believer is called to place hope and reliance solely upon Allah, for He is the only true Helper. The verse serves as a mirror for all people: what are we hoping in? Is our hope in our wealth, our status, our connections, or in Allah alone? False hopes lead to ultimate disappointment.
75 | They cannot help the pagans, even though they serve the idols as dedicated guards. | The helplessness of the false gods is now declared with absolute clarity. “They cannot help them” (lā yastaṭīʿūna naṣrahum) means these idols have no power whatsoever to grant victory, protection, or assistance. They are lifeless, deaf, and dumb. The irony is then heightened: “even though they serve the idols as dedicated guards.” The disbelievers are not mere casual worshippers; they are a “ready army” (jundun muḥḍarūn) in the service of their gods. They fiercely defend their idols, fight for them, and stand guard over them. Yet these idols, for whom they are willing to sacrifice their lives, cannot lift a finger to help them in return. This is the height of folly. A human being, who is alive and capable, serves a stone that is incapable, and imagines that this relationship will bring benefit. The verse paints a picture of tragic devotion: an army of worshippers surrounding lifeless objects, unable to receive any aid from their deities. On the Day of Judgment, these false gods will disown their worshippers, and the worshippers will realize the complete futility of their devotion. The lesson extends beyond stone idols to anything that is worshipped besides Allah, including money, power, or ideologies. People dedicate their lives to these things, yet those things cannot save them from the smallest calamity, let alone from eternal punishment. True help and protection come only from Allah. The believer should ensure that their ultimate loyalty, service, and dedication are directed to the One who can actually help them in this life and the next.
76 | So do not let their words grieve you ˹O Prophet˺. Indeed, We ˹fully˺ know what they conceal and what they reveal. | After exposing the futility of the disbelievers’ beliefs, Allah turns to console the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The rejection, mockery, and insults of the Quraysh had weighed heavily on his heart. The command “do not let their words grieve you” (lā yaḥzunka qawluhum) addresses this emotional burden directly. The grief of the Prophet was not for himself but for his people who were heading toward destruction, and out of his deep desire for their guidance. Allah tells him not to let their words cause him sorrow, because their rejection is not due to any fault in him or his message, but due to their own corruption. The foundation for this comfort is: “Indeed, We fully know what they conceal and what they reveal.” Allah’s knowledge encompasses everything. He knows the secret plots they hatch in their private meetings, the doubts they hide in their chests, and the mockery they utter openly. Nothing escapes Him. Because Allah is fully aware, He is also fully in control. Their schemes will not succeed, and they will be dealt with justly. The Prophet’s job is not to worry about the outcome but to trust in the All-Knowing Lord who sees all and will settle all accounts. This verse is a profound source of comfort for every believer who faces mockery or oppression for their faith. The words of the rejecters may sting, but one should not let them cause lasting grief, because the matter is in Allah’s hands, and He knows the full picture. The believer can rest their heart in the certainty that no enemy action is hidden from the Lord of the worlds.
77 | Do people not see that We have created them from a sperm-drop, then—behold!—they openly challenge ˹Us˺? | The surah now delivers a powerful argument for the resurrection based on the very origin of humanity. “Do people not see” is an appeal to reflect on a fact that everyone acknowledges. The phrase “We have created them from a sperm-drop” (nuṭfah) refers to the initial stage of human creation from a humble, seemingly insignificant fluid. This is a biological fact that the Quran pointed to long before modern science confirmed the details of embryology. The fluid is weak, common, and devoid of any sign of the complex human being it will become. The phrase “then—behold!—they openly challenge Us” contrasts the humble origin with the arrogant behavior of the disbelievers. The word “behold” (fa-idhā) indicates the surprising transformation: from that drop emerges a fully formed human being who then has the audacity to dispute with his Creator, deny His power, and question the resurrection. The term “openly challenge” (khaṣīmun mubīn) literally means a fierce, obvious disputant. The disbeliever argues against the truth, forgetting his own lowly beginning. The verse shames the human being into humility. If Allah could create you from a mere drop of fluid, why do you find it strange that He can recreate you from bones and dust? The initial creation is the greater proof and the more complex process. This argument is repeated throughout the Quran, and it appeals directly to reason. The believer is reminded to maintain humility, knowing that their origin is from a despised fluid, and their honour comes only from Allah’s grace and guidance. The verse also serves as a warning: do not be among those who forget their own creation and then argue against their Maker.
78 | And they argue with Us—forgetting they were created—saying, “Who will give life to decayed bones?” | The specific argument of the disbelievers is now quoted. They present a question they think is a knockout punch against the idea of resurrection: “Who will give life to decayed bones?” The story behind this verse, as mentioned in many tafsirs, is that Ubayy ibn Khalaf, a staunch enemy of the Prophet, came to him with a decayed, crumbling bone in his hand, crushing it and scattering its dust into the air. He asked mockingly, “O Muhammad, do you claim that your Lord will bring this back to life after it has become like this?” The verse captures the essence of this challenge. The disbelievers could not conceive that lifeless, rotted bones could be reconstituted into a living being. Their imagination was limited by their earthly experience. The phrase “forgetting they were created” is the key response. In their argument, they disregarded their own origin. The One who created them initially from nothing can certainly recreate them. Their very existence as arguing, thinking beings is a living proof against their own argument. The verse illustrates how disbelief narrows human vision, causing a person to fixate on secondary problems (“how can old bones live?”) while ignoring the primary miracle (their own creation). This question is not posed as a sincere scientific inquiry but as a sarcastic denial of divine power. The Quran takes this objection seriously and will answer it directly in the next verse with the most logical and compelling response.
79 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time, for He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of every created being.” | The Prophet is instructed to give a direct and definitive answer to the mockery about decayed bones. The answer relies on two fundamental divine attributes: the power of initial creation and the comprehensiveness of divine knowledge. First, “They will be revived by the One Who produced them the first time.” This is the argument from the greater to the lesser. The initial creation of a human being from non-existence, and then from a drop of fluid into a complex living organism, is far more impressive than reassembling bones that already existed. Any reasonable person admits the first creation; therefore, they should have no difficulty admitting the second. The One who did it the first time can do it again, and it is even easier for Him, as another verse says, “It is He Who begins the creation and then repeats it, and that is easier for Him” (30:27). Second, “for He has perfect knowledge of every created being.” This answers the concern about scattered particles. Even if the bones have decayed into dust and been dispersed across the earth, eaten by animals, or dissolved in the ocean, Allah’s knowledge encompasses every atom. He knows precisely where each part of every individual is. There is no loss or confusion in His knowledge. The reassembly is not a problem of information or power for the All-Knowing, All-Powerful Creator. This verse provides a complete logical and theological answer to the doubts about resurrection. It offers the believer unshakeable certainty and a clear argument to present to those who question. It also elevates the mind from materialistic thinking to an appreciation of the limitless knowledge and power of Allah.
80 | ˹He is the One˺ Who gives you fire from green trees, and —behold!—you kindle ˹fire˺ from them. | The surah presents another tangible, everyday proof of the resurrection, drawn from the Bedouin experience. The verse refers to the trees known as markh and ʿafār, which the Arabs used for fire-making. When two green, fresh branches of these trees were rubbed together, they would produce sparks and ignite a flame. This was a well-known phenomenon: fire, which is hot and dry, emerging from green wood, which is cold and moist. The contrast between the green tree and the fire it produces is so striking that it was used as a proverb for opposites. Allah draws attention to this: “He is the One Who gives you fire from green trees.” The living, verdant tree contains within it the potential for fire, a fact that points to the hidden properties placed in creation by the Creator. The phrase “behold!—you kindle fire from them” indicates that this is a daily, observable miracle. If Allah can bring forth fire from a green tree, a thing from its opposite, then He can certainly bring forth life from dead bones. The power to combine opposites and to store energy in a living plant is a sign of the One who is not constrained by the laws He created. He who made the green tree a source of fire can make the dead bones a source of life. The verse challenges the empirical, skeptical mindset: you witness a miraculous extraction every time you light a fire, yet you deny the greater extraction of life from death. This proof is simple, powerful, and grounded in the immediate environment of the audience. It leaves no room for denial except from arrogance. The believer is reminded that the entire natural world is filled with signs that testify to the truth of the resurrection, if only people would reflect.
81 | Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not ˹easily˺ resurrect these ˹deniers˺?” Yes ˹He can˺! For He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing. | This verse delivers the final, conclusive proof in the series of arguments for the resurrection. It takes the form of a rhetorical question that leaves no room for doubt. “Can the One Who created the heavens and the earth not easily resurrect these deniers?” The argument here scales up from the previous proofs. The earlier verses spoke of the creation of humans from a sperm-drop and the bringing of fire from green trees; now the horizon is expanded to the largest conceivable scale. The heavens and the earth, with all their immensity, complexity, and majesty, are the greatest manifestations of Allah’s creative power known to human beings. If Allah originated this entire universe without any prior model or material, how could He be incapable of reassembling human bones and bringing the dead back to life? The implied answer is clear: the greater creation testifies to the ease of the lesser recreation. The word “easily” is not in the Arabic text but is a necessary clarification of the meaning, because for the Almighty, nothing is difficult. This question also directly addresses the root cause of disbelief in the resurrection. The disbelievers imagined that a scattered, decayed body was beyond recovery because they measured divine power by their own limited standards. The verse destroys this fallacy by pointing them to the limitless power that holds up the sky and spreads out the earth. The emphatic response “Yes He can!” (balā) is a powerful affirmation that cuts off any argument. The verse then grounds this power in two divine names: “For He is the Master Creator, All-Knowing.” The name “Master Creator” (al-Khallāq) is an intensive form, indicating the One who creates again and again, on a vast scale, with perfect mastery. The creation of the heavens and the earth is just one aspect of His continuous, endless creation. The name “All-Knowing” (al-ʿAlīm) addresses the supposed difficulty of gathering the scattered particles. The Master Creator is also the All-Knowing; He does not lose track of any atom in His creation. Every bone, every cell, every trace of every human being is fully known to Him. Thus, the combination of perfect power and perfect knowledge makes the resurrection not only possible but inevitable. For the believer, this verse inspires awe and trust. The same Master Creator who fashioned the cosmos will faithfully and easily bring His servants back to life for judgment.
82 | All it takes, when He wills something ˹to be˺, is simply to say to it: “Be!” And it is! | Having established that resurrection is easy for the One who created the heavens and the earth, this verse now reveals the breathtakingly simple mechanism of divine creation. “All it takes, when He wills something to be, is simply to say to it: ‘Be!’ And it is!” The phrase “All it takes” translates the Arabic innamā, which indicates restriction and exclusivity. There is no other process, no labour, no need for tools, materials, or time. The divine will directly translates into existence through the single command “Be” (kun). This command is not a physical sound but the expression of Allah’s absolute will and decree. When He wills a thing, its existence becomes inevitable in that very instant. The phrase “And it is” (fa-yakūn) underscores the immediacy and certainty of the result. This verse is one of the most powerful statements in the Quran about the nature of divine power. It explains how the vast universe came into being from nothing: by a single word of command. It also explains how the resurrection will occur. There is no need for a lengthy biological process of regrowth; the dead will simply be commanded to rise, and they will rise. This answers the mockery of the disbelievers about decayed bones in the most profound way: the same command that brought the first creation into existence will bring about the second. The verse also has profound implications for the believer’s understanding of Allah’s will in daily life. When a believer supplicates, they know that if Allah wills a matter, nothing can prevent it, and He can bring it about with ease. However, this verse does not negate the wisdom behind Allah’s use of means and gradual processes in the world; rather, it states that Allah is not bound by those means. He uses them by His wisdom, but He can also dispense with them by His power. The verse is a direct comfort for those who suffer under oppression or illness, for it reminds them that their Lord can change any situation in an instant, with a single word. It also serves as a warning: the punishment can come just as suddenly, by that same command. The verse thus combines awe, hope, and fear, drawing the heart to complete reliance on the One whose command is absolute.
83 | So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is the authority over all things, and to Whom ˹alone˺ you will ˹all˺ be returned. | The surah concludes with a magnificent verse of glorification that ties together all its major themes. “So glory be to the One in Whose Hands is the authority over all things.” The word “glory be” (subḥān) declares Allah’s absolute perfection, transcendence, and freedom from any flaw, partner, or deficiency. It is an exclamation of praise and wonder. The surah has presented numerous signs—from the dead earth coming to life, to the celestial bodies in their orbits, to the cattle, the ship, the fire from green trees, and the resurrection of bones. All of these point to the perfection and power of the Creator, and the appropriate response is to glorify Him. The description “in Whose Hands is the authority over all things” is especially significant. The “Hand” is an expression of power, possession, and control. The plural form “Hands” may imply the fullness of authority and the comprehensive nature of His dominion. The “authority” (malakūt) refers to the ultimate kingship and governance over the entire universe. Every atom, every soul, every system is under His absolute control. The surah began with the disjointed letters and an oath on the wise Quran, and it now ends with a declaration of the sovereignty of the One who revealed it. The final clause, “and to Whom alone you will all be returned,” brings the listener back to the central reality of the surah: the return to Allah. After all the proofs of resurrection and the accounts of previous nations, the inevitable conclusion is that every human being will stand before this King. The return is “to Him alone,” meaning there is no escape, no other destination, and no one else to turn to. The surah, which was a wake-up call to the heedless, ends by placing the reader directly before their Lord, reminding them of the absolute authority that will encompass them on the Day of Return. This verse is a prayer, a meditation, and a warning all in one. The believer says these words, glorifying the Lord who holds everything in His hands, and feels both immense peace and profound responsibility. The surah’s journey from the disjointed letters to the glorification of the Lord of all authority is now complete, leaving the heart filled with awe and the mind convinced of the truth of resurrection.
