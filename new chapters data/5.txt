1 | O believers! Honour your obligations. All grazing livestock has been made lawful to you—except what is hereby announced to you and hunting while on pilgrimage. Indeed, Allah commands what He wills. | This opening verse sets the tone for the entire surah and, in many ways, for the complete code of conduct a believer must live by. The address "O believers" immediately draws a circle of faith, speaking directly to those who have committed themselves to Allah and His Messenger. The command "Honour your obligations" translates the profound Arabic term *al-‘uqūd*, which linguistically points to binding contracts, covenants, and solemn pledges. This is not merely about fulfilling casual promises. It encompasses the covenant of faith itself, the natural contract between the Creator and the creation, and the social contracts that hold a community together, such as marriage vows, business agreements, and political treaties. The verse connects the spiritual with the social, teaching that faith cannot be separated from integrity in daily dealings. Immediately after this sweeping command, the verse transitions to a specific ruling: "All grazing livestock has been made lawful to you." The word "grazing livestock," *bahīmat al-an‘ām*, refers specifically to camels, cattle, sheep, and goats. By declaring these lawful, the Quran corrects the superstitions of pre-Islamic Arabia, where certain animals were self-imposed as sacred or forbidden based on pagan customs. The verse then introduces a crucial exception, "except what is hereby announced to you," directing the listener's attention forward to the detailed prohibitions that will be recited later in this very surah, such as carrion, blood, and the flesh of swine. A second exception is immediately added: "and hunting while on pilgrimage." This establishes the sanctified state of *ihram*, where a pilgrim abstains from specific otherwise-halal acts as an act of devotion. The concluding statement, "Indeed, Allah commands what He wills," roots all these rulings in divine wisdom. It reminds the believer that obedience is not based on our complete understanding of the command, but on our recognition of the Commander’s absolute authority and perfect knowledge.
2 | O believers! Do not violate Allah’s rituals ˹of pilgrimage˺, the sacred months, the sacrificial animals, the ˹offerings decorated with˺ garlands, nor those ˹pilgrims˺ on their way to the Sacred House seeking their Lord’s bounty and pleasure. When pilgrimage has ended, you are allowed to hunt. Do not let the hatred of a people who once barred you from the Sacred Mosque provoke you to transgress. Cooperate with one another in goodness and righteousness, and do not cooperate in sin and transgression. And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is severe in punishment. | The address "O believers" continues the flow of legislation by safeguarding the sanctities of the religion, particularly those connected to the pilgrimage. The command "Do not violate Allah’s rituals" introduces the concept of *sha‘ā’ir Allāh*, the visible signs and rites that honour Him. The verse then specifies what these rituals include: "the sacred months," referring to the four months in which fighting was traditionally forbidden, a prohibition that Islam reinforced to ensure safe passage for pilgrims; "the sacrificial animals," which are the livestock driven to the Kaaba to be offered for the sake of Allah; and "the offerings decorated with garlands," a practice where animals designated for sacrifice were marked with distinctive collars so that all who saw them would recognise their sanctity and not interfere with them. The mention of "those pilgrims on their way to the Sacred House seeking their Lord’s bounty and pleasure" broadens the protection to include the people themselves, safeguarding them from any kind of harassment or assault during their spiritual journey. The verse then prepares the listener for a transition back to normal life with the phrase, "When pilgrimage has ended, you are allowed to hunt," lifting the restriction mentioned in the previous verse. Yet the heart of the moral teaching in this verse lies in its next command. The early Muslims were deeply wounded by the memory of being barred from the Sacred Mosque by the Quraysh at Hudaybiyyah. A natural human response would be a desire for revenge. The Quran speaks directly to this feeling: "Do not let the hatred of a people who once barred you from the Sacred Mosque provoke you to transgress." This is a monumental principle of justice; personal grievances, no matter how justified, can never be a license to violate divine law. Hatred must not blind one to fairness. The verse then presents a comprehensive ethical formula: "Cooperate with one another in goodness and righteousness." This is the command to build a society based on mutual assistance in everything that pleases Allah. Its direct counterpart follows immediately: "and do not cooperate in sin and transgression." A believer is not only an individual who avoids evil but a person who refuses to be an accessory to it. The passage concludes by anchoring these social laws in a deep consciousness of accountability: "And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is severe in punishment."
3 | Forbidden to you are carrion, blood, and swine; what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah; what is killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or by being gored to death; what is partly eaten by a predator unless you slaughter it; and what is sacrificed on altars. You are also forbidden to draw lots for decisions. This is all evil. Today the disbelievers have given up all hope of ˹undermining˺ your faith. So do not fear them; fear Me! Today I have perfected your faith for you, completed My favour upon you, and chosen Islam as your way. But whoever is compelled by extreme hunger— not intending to sin—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | This verse provides a detailed catalogue of dietary prohibitions, and each item carries profound spiritual and physical wisdom. The list begins with "carrion," the animal that dies of itself, because consuming it is degrading to human dignity and poses a health risk. Then, "blood," the liquid life force, is forbidden, as its consumption was a pagan practice and it is a medium for disease. "Swine" is categorically prohibited, its flesh being considered intrinsically impure. The fourth item, "what is slaughtered in the name of any other than Allah," hits at the very core of monotheism; an animal is a provision from the Creator, and invoking a false god’s name at the moment of slaughter is an act of worship to another. The verse then displays divine mercy for the animal and the consumer by listing methods of killing that cause prolonged suffering or prevent proper blood drainage: "what is killed by strangling, beating, a fall, or by being gored to death." However, a window of leniency is immediately opened: "what is partly eaten by a predator unless you slaughter it." If you reach the animal while it still possesses a flicker of life and you perform the correct Islamic slaughter, the meat becomes lawful, demonstrating that law is not just about rules but about realistic situations. The prohibition then returns to a ritualistic sin: "and what is sacrificed on altars," referring to the stone idols that dotted the pre-Islamic landscape. The verse also prohibits seeking decisions through the casting of arrows, a form of gambling that abdicates human reason and trust in Allah to mere chance. A sudden and majestic shift in address occurs with the word "Today," which refers to the Farewell Pilgrimage. The phrase "The disbelievers have given up all hope of undermining your faith" is a divine comfort, a declaration that Islam had become an enduring reality. The verse reaches its spiritual climax in one of the most celebrated revelations of the Quran: "Today I have perfected your faith for you, completed My favour upon you, and chosen Islam as your way." This signifies the completion of the legislative mission. The verse then ends as it began, with mercy: "whoever is compelled by extreme hunger—not intending to sin—then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful," proving that the preservation of life overrides the dietary prohibition in moments of absolute necessity.
4 | They ask you, ˹O Prophet,˺ what is permissible for them ˹to eat˺. Say, “What is good and lawful. Also what is caught by your hunting animals and birds of prey which you have trained as instructed by Allah. So eat what they catch for you, but mention the Name of Allah over it ˹first˺.” And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning. | The verse turns to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, as the divinely appointed source of guidance, with the phrase "They ask you, O Prophet." This formula is used frequently in the Quran where the community seeks clarity on matters of practice, showing the living, dynamic relationship between revelation and the early Muslim society. The question here is about the limits of what is permissible to eat. The divine response begins with a broad, beautiful principle: "Say, 'What is good and lawful.'" The term "good," translating *tayyibāt*, refers not just to that which is legally halal, but to that which is pure, wholesome, pleasant, and naturally appealing to a sound human disposition. Islam does not only restrict; it designates the vast world of good and pure things as the default. The verse then addresses a more technical question concerning hunting. It explains that the catch of trained hunting animals is permissible. The phrase "your hunting animals and birds of prey which you have trained as instructed by Allah" sets a clear condition for this lawfulness. The animal must be a trained hunter, meaning it obeys commands, does not eat from the prey itself, and holds the game for its master. This training is described as being "as instructed by Allah," meaning it is a skill inspired by the knowledge that God has taught humanity. This transforms the act of training a dog or a falcon from a mere worldly activity into an act that can align with divine will. The instruction follows, "So eat what they catch for you," but it is bound by a crucial spiritual condition: "but mention the Name of Allah over it first." This connects back to the earlier prohibition of eating that which is slaughtered in other than Allah's name. The hunter must be conscious of the Creator at the moment of sending the animal, uttering "Bismillah." The verse then seals the instruction with a reminder of the swiftness of divine accountability: "And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is swift in reckoning." This serves as a warning against taking these rulings lightly or seeking loopholes, reminding the believer that a record is kept of every action and intention.
5 | Today all good, pure foods have been made lawful for you. Similarly, the food of the People of the Book is permissible for you and yours is permissible for them. And ˹permissible for you in marriage˺ are chaste believing women as well as chaste women of those given the Scripture before you—as long as you pay them their dowries in wedlock, neither fornicating nor taking them as mistresses. And whoever rejects the faith, all their good deeds will be void ˹in this life˺ and in the Hereafter they will be among the losers. | This verse expands the circle of lawful social interaction for the Muslim community, emphasising the theme of divine grace and facilitation. It begins once more with the triumphal "Today," linking it to the perfection of religion mentioned two verses earlier. The declaration that "all good, pure foods have been made lawful for you" reaffirms the general principle that was stated to be asked about, sweeping away the excessive and superstitious restrictions that had been placed by pre-Islamic customs. The verse then builds a bridge of practical coexistence with the Jewish and Christian communities. The ruling "the food of the People of the Book is permissible for you and yours is permissible for them" is highly significant. The food referred to here is specifically the meat they have slaughtered according to their own scriptural traditions. By sharing a table, social bonds are strengthened, and the doors of dawah, of invitation to Islam, are opened through neighbourly love and hospitality. From the permissibility of food, the verse ascends to the sacred bond of family, which is an even deeper form of social sharing. It states that "chaste believing women" are lawful in marriage, and similarly "chaste women of those given the Scripture before you." This permission to marry a Jewish or Christian woman comes with a profound condition and a clear objective. The condition is "as long as you pay them their dowries in wedlock." The dowry is not a purchase price but a gift of honour that signifies the seriousness of the contract. The objective is stated in the phrase "neither fornicating nor taking them as mistresses." The marriage must be a proper, public union of responsibility, not a secret or temporary arrangement for mere physical gratification. This protects the woman’s honour and secures the lineage of any children. The verse concludes with a stark warning that ties all these practical rulings back to the bedrock of faith: "And whoever rejects the faith, all their good deeds will be void." This serves as a balance, warning that the permissibility of eating with or marrying People of the Book should never be mistaken for a blurring of the theological line between Iman and Kufr. Good deeds without faith are ultimately a lost investment.
6 | O believers! When you rise up for prayer, wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows, wipe your heads, and wash your feet to the ankles. And if you are in a state of ˹full˺ impurity, then take a full bath. But if you are ill, on a journey, or have relieved yourselves, or have been intimate with your wives and cannot find water, then purify yourselves with clean earth by wiping your faces and hands. It is not Allah’s Will to burden you, but to purify you and complete His favour upon you, so perhaps you will be grateful. | Here the Quran moves from the purification of one’s food and social life to the purification of one’s body for the act of worship. The direct address "O believers" introduces the obligation of wudu, or ablution, a prerequisite for the daily prayers that are a believer’s constant meeting with the Lord. The process is described with precise physicality: "wash your faces and your hands up to the elbows, wipe your heads, and wash your feet to the ankles." This ritual washing of the exposed limbs, which are most prone to dust and the grime of daily toil, is not merely a physical cleansing. It is a symbolic and spiritual preparation, a shedding of worldly preoccupations before standing in the divine presence. The verse then transitions from the minor state of impurity, which requires wudu, to the major state. The instruction "if you are in a state of full impurity, then take a full bath" refers to the state of janabah, which necessitates a complete washing of the body, signifying a full re-entry into a state of ritual purity after intimate relations or other causes. The law is immediately softened by mercy. The verse addresses human vulnerability with the profound alternate system of tayammum. "But if you are ill, on a journey, or have relieved yourselves, or have been intimate with your wives and cannot find water, then purify yourselves with clean earth." The inability to use water, whether due to its scarcity or a medical reason, does not exclude one from worship. The human being, who was first created from dust, turns to the pure, clean surface of the earth. The act of wiping the face and hands with dust is a substitute that honours the intention to be pure even when the means are lacking. The final part of the verse cuts to the very heart of the Sharia’s purpose: "It is not Allah’s Will to burden you, but to purify you and complete His favour upon you, so perhaps you will be grateful." God does not delight in our hardship. The rules are designed to uplift and cleanse, and the presence of merciful alternatives is a favour meant to spark a state of perpetual gratitude in the heart of the worshipper.
7 | Remember Allah’s favour upon you and the covenant He made with you when you said, “We hear and obey.” And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah knows best what is ˹hidden˺ in the heart. | The verse turns from the physical act of ablution to a spiritual remembrance, calling the believers to recognise their own history and commitment. The command "Remember Allah’s favour upon you" invites reflection on the greatest blessing bestowed upon a community: divine guidance itself. The favour is immediately connected to "the covenant He made with you." This points towards a defining moment in the life of the early Muslim community, particularly the pledges of Aqabah which the Ansar of Medina made with the Prophet before his migration. More broadly, it encompasses the primordial covenant of all souls who testified to Allah’s lordship, and the covenant of faith that every Muslim enters into. The response of the believers is captured in a phrase of profound submission: "We hear and obey." This is the perfect expression of the Muslim’s relationship with God. It is not a blind obedience but one rooted in an attentive listening that immediately translates into action. There is no gap between hearing the command and the resolve to obey it, no negotiation, no hesitation. This small phrase is a profound diagnostic of the heart’s state; it reflects a complete trust in the wisdom of the Commander. However, the verse immediately introduces a note of divine caution. After the outward pledge, the command returns: "And be mindful of Allah." This continuous consciousness, or taqwa, is the soul of the covenant. The verse closes with a statement that probes the deepest recesses of the individual: "Surely Allah knows best what is hidden in the heart." The pledge "we hear and obey" can be uttered by a tongue that conceals a contrary resolve. The verse thus serves as both a comfort and a warning. It is a comfort for the sincere who may worry if their internal struggle is seen, for Allah knows the true faith hiding there. It is a warning for the hypocrite who thinks their secret rebellion is hidden, for no heartbeat is silent to the Creator. This connects organically to the preceding ablution verses, as the purification of the body is now shown to be intimately linked to the purification of the heart’s intentions.
8 | O believers! Stand firm for Allah and bear true testimony. Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice. Be just! That is closer to righteousness. And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is All-Aware of what you do. | This verse is one of the most powerful social commands in the Quran, establishing the absolute impartiality required of a faith community. The call "O believers" prepares the listener for a weighty obligation. The instruction "Stand firm for Allah" translates the word *qawwāmīn*, meaning to be rigorously steadfast, to be those who stand upright and persistently maintain a duty. It is not a passive standing but an active, challenging role. The purpose of this upright standing is immediately defined: "and bear true testimony." A believer is to be a witness to truth, whose testimony is shaped not by relationships, emotions, or social pressures, but solely by the reality of a situation as it stands before Allah. The next sentence directly addresses a universal human failing and links back to the prohibition in verse two. The verse acknowledges that the believers had real enemies: "Do not let the hatred of a people lead you to injustice." The historical context involves the deep grievances against the Quraysh who had persecuted the Muslims and barred them from the Sacred Mosque. The soul might whisper that an enemy does not deserve fair treatment. The Quran smashes this logic of tribal vengeance. Hatred, no matter how intensely felt or justified by past wrongs, can never be a reason to distort justice. Because if you do so, you are not punishing them for their sin; you are sinning yourself. The command is then stated in its simplest and most magnificent form: "Be just!" This single word, *i‘dilū*, is a pillar of the Islamic social order. It is then explained logically: "That is closer to righteousness." Justice is not merely a nice virtue; it is the path that leads to taqwa. The verse culminates with the reminder, "And be mindful of Allah. Surely Allah is All-Aware of what you do," a short sentence that acts as the ultimate deterrent against secret bias. A human judge may see your surface-level fairness and be fooled, but the One who is All-Aware sees the invisible tilt of your heart.
9 | Allah has promised those who believe and do good ˹His˺ forgiveness and a great reward. | After the stern warnings about injustice and the call to bear true witness, the Quran’s characteristic style immediately pivots to hope and mercy. This short, rhythmic verse presents a divine promise, and its beauty lies in its simplicity and comprehensiveness. The subject is "Allah," and the action is "has promised." A promise from the Almighty is not a mere possibility; it is a guaranteed reality, for God never breaks His word. The recipients of this beautiful promise are described with a dual condition: "those who believe and do good." This is the Quranic balance in its most concentrated form. The first condition, "believe," is an internal state of the heart, a genuine conviction in the oneness of God and the truth of His message. It is the root, but a root by itself is not a tree. The second condition, "do good," translating the term *as-sālihāt*, which implies righteous, reforming deeds that set things right, is the visible fruit. It serves as the evidence of the claim to belief. The promise itself consists of two parts, each corresponding to a deep human need. The first is "forgiveness." Every human being carries a record of shortcomings, sins, and moments of weakness. The deepest fear is that these past stains will define one’s final destiny. Allah’s promise begins here, with the assurance that the slate can be wiped clean for those who combine faith with action, for their good deeds demonstrate the sincerity of their repentance and their commitment to a new path. The second part of the promise is "a great reward." Once the sins are forgiven, the person is not left in a neutral, empty state. They are elevated to a magnificent station of divine generosity. This "great reward" is Jannah, a place of eternal bliss where every desire is fulfilled and the greatest reward is the vision of the Lord Himself. The verse bridges the commands of social justice in the previous verse with the eternal outcome, reminding the believer that the duty to be just, even towards those one hates, is not just a worldly social contract. It is an investment that will yield a magnificent, great return from the One who sees all actions.
10 | As for those who disbelieve and deny Our signs, they are the residents of the Hellfire. | This verse stands as the stark counterbalance to the preceding promise, completing a pair that together defines the consequences of human choice. The Quran frequently juxtaposes the fate of the two groups to clarify the absolute difference in their endings, allowing the message to resonate through contrast. The verse begins with "As for those who disbelieve," focusing on a fundamental position of the heart, the rejection of faith in God and His Messenger. This state of disbelief is immediately followed and sharpened by the phrase "and deny Our signs." The "signs" here represent the comprehensive evidence that Allah has placed for humanity. This includes the cosmic signs of the natural world, the sun, moon, stars, and mountains, that logically point to a Creator. It includes the historical signs, the ruins of past civilisations that destroyed themselves through their own rebellion. Most directly, it includes the recited signs, the verses of the Quran itself, which claim divine origin by their very linguistic and conceptual power. To "deny" these signs is not simply an intellectual oversight; it is an active turning away, a refusal to engage with truth that has been made clear. The destination for such people is stated plainly: "they are the residents of the Hellfire." The term "residents" translates *ashāb*, which implies a close, inseparable association, a belonging that makes it their permanent abode. This verse does not present Hell as a temporary detention centre but as the final, chosen home for those who made their disbelief a core part of their identity. However, the reading of this verse within the context of the surah must be nuanced by the many verses that precede and follow it, which speak about repentance and divine mercy. This verse serves a clear psychological and educational purpose within the surah’s argument. After commanding the believers to stand firmly for justice, even in the face of their own hatred for an enemy, the verse implicitly asks: is it not better to be just and be promised forgiveness and a great reward, than to let your hatred push you to transgression and risk a fate as terrifying as the Fire? It ties the immediacy of moral behaviour directly to the certainty of the Afterlife.
11 | O believers! Remember Allah’s favour upon you: when a people sought to harm you, but He held their hands back from you. Be mindful of Allah. And in Allah let the believers put their trust. | The address "O believers" invites the Muslim community to a moment of historical reflection that is intended to solidify their present trust in God. The command is to "Remember Allah’s favour upon you," an instruction that shifts the gaze from the abstract covenant mentioned earlier in the surah to a tangible, lived moment of divine intervention. The specific favour is then revealed: "when a people sought to harm you, but He held their hands back from you." The majority of scholars of tafsir connect this reference to a specific assassination plot. It is reported that the tribe of Banu An-Nadir, a Jewish tribe near Medina, invited the Prophet to a meeting with the secret intention of dropping a millstone on him from a rooftop. The Prophet, through divine revelation, was made aware of the plot and withdrew unharmed. Another context points to the incident of the Bedouin who drew his sword on the sleeping Prophet, only to be struck by a sudden fear when the Prophet awoke calmly and asked, "Who will protect you from me?" Either way, the message is clear: the believers were physically vulnerable, a hostile hand was extended to harm them, but an unseen force intervened. This memory was crucial for the fledgling community. They were a small minority surrounded by powerful foes. The verse is retraining their psychology away from a realistic fear of a stronger enemy towards a realistic awe of their Protector. Immediately following the reminder, the verse issues the central command of this surah: "Be mindful of Allah." This is the appropriate response to a remembered favour. To have taqwa is to live in a constant state of awareness that the same God who protected you then is watching your actions now. The verse then concludes with a profound principle of spiritual psychology: "And in Allah let the believers put their trust." Trust, or tawakkul, is not passive resignation. It is the action of the heart that occurs after all physical and strategic means have been exhausted. The knowledge that Allah held the enemy’s hand back once without any human military power is the ultimate evidence that He can and will do so again. This grounds all future striving in a foundation of calm reliance on the One who is truly in control.
12 | Allah made a covenant with the Children of Israel and appointed twelve leaders from among them and ˹then˺ said, “I am truly with you. If you establish prayer, pay alms-tax, believe in My messengers, support them, and lend to Allah a good loan, I will certainly forgive your sins and admit you into Gardens under which rivers flow. And whoever among you disbelieves afterwards has truly strayed from the Right Way.” | The surah shifts its narrative lens from the immediate experience of the Muslim community in Medina to a broader historical lesson drawn from the history of the Children of Israel. This serves the Quranic method of using past patterns to illuminate present realities. The verse states that "Allah made a covenant with the Children of Israel," affirming a historical fact of divine selection and responsibility. As part of this covenant, He "appointed twelve leaders from among them." These were the chieftains, one from each of the twelve tribes, tasked with maintaining order and piety among their people, a mirror to the leadership structure Prophet Muhammad was establishing in his own community. The content of the divine promise is then presented as a direct quote from Allah: "I am truly with you." This is the ultimate guarantee of success, the presence of the Almighty. However, this presence is conditional, built upon a framework of deeds. The condition is a five-part programme that integrates personal worship and social justice: "establish prayer," the direct link to God; "pay alms-tax," the link to the poor and the community; "believe in My messengers," which mandates a chain of belief that closes no doors; "support them," demanding active loyalty, not passive belief; and "lend to Allah a good loan," a beautiful metaphor for voluntary charity spent in God’s cause with a pure heart, viewing it as a loan that the Most Generous will repay manifold. The reward for fulfilling these terms is staggering: "I will certainly forgive your sins," the removal of fear, "and admit you into Gardens under which rivers flow," the granting of eternal pleasure. Yet the verse ends with a clear warning: "And whoever among you disbelieves afterwards has truly strayed from the Right Way." This narrative is not merely a story. It is a direct message to the Muslims, who were also given a prophet, a scripture, and a law. The framework of prayer, zakah, believing in all prophets, supporting the final Messenger, and spending in God’s path is precisely the Muslim covenant. The warning at the end serves to teach that election by God is not a racial privilege but a test of obedience.
13 | But for breaking their covenant, We condemned them and hardened their hearts. They distorted the words of the Scripture and neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold. You ˹O Prophet˺ will always find deceit on their part, except for a few. But pardon them and bear with them. Indeed, Allah loves the good-doers. | This verse directly confronts the consequences of the broken covenant described in the previous verse, moving from the promise to the historical reality. It begins with the sharp conjunction "But," signalling a turn towards betrayal. The cause is stated bluntly: "for breaking their covenant." The result was twofold. First, "We condemned them," meaning they were expelled from the spiritual rank and protection that their ancestors had earned. Second, "and hardened their hearts." This phrase requires deep theological understanding. The hardening of the heart was a punishment for their persistent, chosen rebellion. It was not an arbitrary, unprovoked curse; it was the natural seal placed upon a heart that consistently chose transgression over truth until the ability to discern good from evil atrophied and died. The outward symptoms of this hardened heart are then detailed: "They distorted the words of the Scripture." This refers to the intellectual crime of altering the Torah, either by twisting the meanings of its verses or by physically changing its text, a charge that has been witnessed historically. Furthermore, they "neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold," meaning they became selective observers of religion, adopting what pleased their desires and discarding what demanded sacrifice. The verse then turns directly to the Prophet, peace be upon him, with a sobering revelation about his own experience: "You, O Prophet, will always find deceit on their part, except for a few." This is a prediction and a consolation. It informs him that the breach of trust is not an anomaly but a deeply ingrained pattern that he will repeatedly witness in his dealings with some of them. Yet, it is also a recognition of a faithful minority who remain true to their covenant, preventing a blanket condemnation of an entire people. The divine command in response is a lesson of profound ethical restraint: "But pardon them and bear with them." The Prophet is instructed to turn away from immediate retaliation, to show patient forbearance and forgiveness. The motivation for this high road is stated at the end: "Indeed, Allah loves the good-doers." Forgiveness, in this context, is not a sign of weakness. It is a characteristic of the Muhsinun, those who excel in goodness.
14 | And from those who say, “We are Christians,” We took their covenant, but they neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold. So We let hostility and enmity arise between them until the Day of Judgment, and soon Allah will inform them of all they have done. | The surah now turns from the covenant with the Children of Israel to the covenant with the followers of Christ, creating a parallel historical analysis. The verse specifically identifies the group by their own claim: "those who say, 'We are Christians.'" This phrasing is precise; it acknowledges their self-identification without the Quran itself needing to validate the theological basis of the name. The verse then reveals that a similar divine agreement was struck: "We took their covenant." The core essence of this covenant, like that of the Israelites, was to hold fast to the scripture given to them, the Gospel, and to recognise and follow the final prophet when he appeared, as Jesus himself had foretold. However, the identical failure is recorded: "but they neglected a portion of what they had been commanded to uphold." This neglect refers most prominently to the doctrine of monotheism, which they compromised through the introduction of the Trinity, and to the commands of the Gospel regarding the coming of the final Messenger, prophecies which they reinterpreted or concealed. The punishment for this collective neglect is presented in a form dramatically different from a thunderbolt or an earthquake. It is a punishment of social and spiritual disintegration: "So We let hostility and enmity arise between them until the Day of Judgment." Here, divine justice works by removing the unifying bond of truth from a people who abandoned it. When the core of a religion is corrupted, its capacity to unify hearts is lost. Sects multiply, each denouncing the other as heretical, and the community becomes a battlefield of mutual recrimination. This condition is not a temporary state; it is decreed to persist "until the Day of Judgment," a historical reality that remains visible in the endless denominational fragmentations of the Christian world. Yet the verse ends with a note of ultimate accountability, not human judgment: "and soon Allah will inform them of all they have done." This serves as a closure to the historical analysis, placing the final verdict in the hands of God. It warns the Muslim readers who are witnessing this narrative that the consequences of dividing and neglecting the divine message are catastrophic for any community.
15 | O People of the Book! Now Our Messenger has come to you, revealing much of what you have hidden of the Scriptures and disregarding much. There certainly has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book | The address shifts dramatically, turning directly to the very communities whose history was just critiqued. The call "O People of the Book" is an invitation grounded in their own belief in revelation. The announcement is momentous: "Now Our Messenger has come to you." The word "Now" imparts a sense of urgency and historical culmination. The function of this Messenger, Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, in relation to their own scriptures is then described with two balanced clauses. First, he is "revealing much of what you have hidden of the Scriptures." The Quran, through divine revelation, exposes the truths that the scholars had buried under centuries of interpretation, such as the prophecies of the final Prophet or the pure monotheism of Christ. It brings these secrets into the open, holding the scripture-bearing communities accountable to their own books. Second, he is "disregarding much." This is a profound mercy. The verse then reaches a climax with a declaration of what this Messenger brought: "There certainly has come to you from Allah a light and a clear Book." The "light" is a beautiful metaphor for the Prophet himself, whose character, life, and teachings illuminated the darkness of a world lost in idolatry and scriptural confusion. This verse masterfully balances warning and invitation, telling the People of the Book that their past deceptions have been uncovered, but only so that they might walk into the light that now stands brightly before them.

It is guidance to the path that leads to safety from Allah’s punishment. It is the path of inner peace, freedom from the anxieties of a life without meaning. It is the comprehensive term for Islam itself, the balanced way that avoids every excess and neglect. The verse thus maps out the entire journey of a soul, from seeking God’s pleasure, through the guiding text, into the state of peace, towards the final path of uprightness.

The Quran addresses the belief that emerged among some Christians that Jesus was not merely a prophet or a messenger but was God incarnate. The verse labels this statement as kufr, a rejection of the fundamental truth of monotheism. It is not a judgment on the moral character of Christians, many of whom are praised elsewhere in this very surah, but on a specific theological proposition that contradicts the core message of every prophet. The virgin birth of Jesus is not a proof of his divinity, as some claimed, but a magnificent display of God’s limitless power to create in any mode He wills, without the need for a father, which is just as much a creation as the creation of Adam without a mother or a father.

This was not necessarily a literal claim of physical sonship for all of them, but a claim of an exclusive, unbreakable filial relationship that guaranteed salvation regardless of their deeds. Many Jews held that they were God’s chosen children, and if punished in Hell, it would be only for a few days. Many Christians held that their faith in Christ’s sacrifice made them beloved in a way that rendered their sins inconsequential. The verse concludes by reiterating the ultimate source of authority and the ultimate destination: "To Allah alone belongs the kingdom... And to Him is the final return." All false claims of privilege will vanish when everyone, Jew, Christian, and Muslim, stands alone before the One King for judgment.
19 | O People of the Book! The call "O People of the Book" brings them once more to the stand of accountability. The announcement is a repetition, but with a crucial addition: "Our Messenger has indeed come to you, making things clear to you." The Prophet’s mission is described with the verb "making things clear," which implies that he is not bringing a novel, strange religion, but is clarifying the obscured truths of their own tradition and resolving the differences they had fallen into. The historical context is then added as the central point of the argument: "after an interval between the messengers." This refers to the period of fatrah, the roughly six-century gap between the ascension of Jesus and the advent of Muhammad, peace be upon them both. During this long silence, no major prophet had been sent to the central stage of human civilisation. The divine purpose behind this timing is then revealed: "so you do not say, 'There has never come to us a deliverer of good news or a warner.'" The verse silences a potential protest on the Day of Judgment. The People of the Book could have argued, "We were not sent a messenger after Jesus to correct the deviations that crept in. The revelations were corrupted, and we had no new warner to alert us." The coming of the Prophet after this long break specifically strips them of this defence. He is described with the two fundamental prophetic titles: "a deliverer of good news," bashīr, who gives glad tidings of eternal bliss to the obedient, and "a warner," nadhīr, who cautions of a terrible punishment for the disobedient. The verse then caps the argument with the affirmation: "And Allah is Most Capable of everything." This is a quiet but powerful warning. To reject the Prophet now, after the excuse has been removed, is to face the One whose power is limitless, the One who has just demonstrated His power by sending a final messenger with a preserved book after centuries of silence, closing the chapter of human history with a clear, universal call.
20 | And ˹remember˺ when Moses said to his people, “O my people! Remember Allah’s favours upon you when He raised prophets from among you, made you sovereign, and gave you what He had never given anyone in the world. | The surah now transitions from direct theological address to a historical narrative, selecting a story that provides a profound practical lesson for the Muslim community on the cusp of struggle. The verse begins with the familiar Quranic command "And remember," calling the Prophet and his followers to bring a past scene to life in their minds because it mirrors their own. The scene opens with "when Moses said to his people," immediately establishing the parallel. The Prophet Muhammad is now standing before his people in Medina, just as Moses stood before the Children of Israel. Moses’ speech is a motivational address, beginning with the affectionate call, "O my people!" This is the tone of a sincere, loving leader, not a tyrant. The core of his message is a call to gratitude and courage: "Remember Allah’s favours upon you." This is the same strategy seen earlier in this surah, where the Muslims were repeatedly told to "remember Allah’s favour upon you." For a community facing a daunting task, the first step is to fill the heart with the memory of past blessings, for this generates the confidence that the One who blessed before can empower again. Moses then lists three specific divine favours. First, "He raised prophets from among you." This was a unique honour for the Israelites, producing a magnificent chain of spiritual guides who lived among them, speaking their language and understanding their struggles. Second, "made you sovereign." This refers to their transformation from a slave nation under Pharaoh’s boot to a free, dignified people with their own law and land under kings like David and Solomon. Third, "and gave you what He had never given anyone in the world." This refers to the immense spiritual and material blessings unique to their time, including the direct parting of the sea, the manna and quails from heaven, and the intimate communication on Mount Sinai. The lesson for the Muslims is transparent. They too have been given a Prophet from among themselves. They too have been lifted from the humiliation of tribal warfare to the dignity of a unified Ummah. And they too have been given a gift unparalleled in history: the Quran itself. Moses’ speech is meant to be their speech, and the call to enter a holy land is about to follow.
21 | O my people! Enter the Holy Land which Allah has destined for you ˹to enter˺. And do not turn back or else you will become losers. | Moses continues his impassioned address to the Israelites, building on the remembrance of divine favours. The command is direct and momentous: “Enter the Holy Land.” This refers to the land of Palestine, specifically Jerusalem and its surroundings, which had been promised to the descendants of Abraham. The word “destined” in the translation captures the term *kataba*, which linguistically means “written.” Allah had written this destiny for them, ordaining it as a place of settlement and worship, but this divine decree was conditional upon their active obedience and willingness to strive for it. The command therefore demolishes the notion of a passive reliance on destiny. It is not enough that God has written something good for a community; they must rise and walk into that promise with their own feet, facing its challenges. The second part of the verse is a severe warning: “And do not turn back or else you will become losers.” Turning back, in this context, refers to retreating from the battlefield of obedience out of fear of the enemy. It is a psychological and physical flight that reveals a deep failure of trust in God’s promise. The consequence of such a retreat is explicitly labelled as “losers.” This is not a loss of land or wealth, but a comprehensive state of failure in this world and the next. For the Muslim community in Medina, who were about to face major military tests and who would one day be called to spread the message, this verse was a direct parallel. They too had been given a divine mission and a covenant. The lesson was clear: the sacred mission cannot be abandoned at the first sight of a powerful adversary. When God has decreed a duty, the only path that avoids loss is to move forward with courage. Fear of the enemy's power betrays a heart that has forgotten the power of the One who made the promise. This verse thus serves as a timeless criterion for any generation of believers called to a difficult collective duty, reminding them that retreat from God’s command, when the path is clear, is the very definition of spiritual bankruptcy.

However, they add a condition that exposes their passive, conditional obedience: “until they leave.” This is a condition they know is unrealistic. They too faced “enormously powerful people” in the Quraysh and the Byzantine and Persian empires. The verse serves as a permanent diagnosis of a diseased mindset in any believing community, a mindset that magnifies the strength of the opponent and minimises the power of the Lord, thereby paralysing all action and reducing faith to a mere theory that never touches the battlefield of life.

The phrase “who had been blessed by Allah” indicates they possessed a special gift of insight, firmness, and wisdom. The key condition for this victory is then stated: “Put your trust in Allah if you are truly believers.” Trust, or tawakkul, is the active state of the heart that combines taking all worldly means with a complete reliance on God for the outcome. The statement “if you are truly believers” is a sharp rebuke. It ties the very authenticity of their claim to faith to their willingness to act and trust. This teaches that belief is not a static identity but a dynamic force that manifests as courage in the face of danger. For the Muslims, this was a direct lesson: the consultation of the righteous few who remind the community of God’s power and the necessity of trust must be heeded, and true iman is proven not in times of safety but in the moments that demand stepping through the gate.

For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a direct consolation. He too would witness his followers waver and some hypocrites refuse the call. The lesson was that a prophet’s duty is to convey, strive, and then entrust the final judgment to God. The prayer of Moses becomes a model for the believer who has done all they can: turn to Allah, acknowledge human limits, and leave the fate of the rebellious to the All-Wise Judge.

This line is a crucial lesson in emotional and spiritual boundaries. For the Muslim community, the story ends with a clear moral: the consequence of refusing to struggle in God’s path, of prioritising safety over obedience, is not immediate annihilation but a prolonged, wasted existence of spiritual and existential wandering, a life barred from the true blessings and victories God had in store for them.
27 | Relate to them in truth ˹O Prophet˺ the story of Adam’s two sons—how each offered a sacrifice: Abel’s offering was accepted while Cain’s was not. So Cain threatened, “I will kill you!” His brother replied, “Allah only accepts ˹the offering˺ of the sincerely devout. | The narrative lens now shifts from the collective story of the Israelites to the very first human story of envy and murder, which serves as a timeless archetype of the human condition. The command “Relate to them in truth, O Prophet,” instructs Muhammad, peace be upon him, to recite this story to his people with absolute precision, for it contains essential wisdom for the community. The story begins with Adam’s two sons, Abel and Cain, each offering a sacrifice. The core of the event is that “Abel’s offering was accepted while Cain’s was not.” The Quran does not detail the sacrifices here, but authentic traditions explain that Abel, a shepherd, offered his finest, healthiest animal, while Cain, a farmer, offered some of his worst produce. Their hearts were different, and thus the divine acceptance was different. The reaction of Cain is immediate and violent: he “threatened, ‘I will kill you!’” The first murder in human history was not over wealth or territory but over religious envy—a sick heart that could not bear to see his brother favoured by God. This is a powerful warning to all believers that a soul lacking sincerity may turn on those who are more pious, accusing them of hypocrisy or self-righteousness, when in reality the problem is a corrupt inner state. Abel’s response is a masterpiece of righteous composure. He says, “Allah only accepts the offering of the sincerely devout.” He identifies the root cause with clarity: his brother’s rejection was not a matter of ritual but a matter of the heart’s orientation. The word *muttaqīn*, translated as “sincerely devout,” refers to those who possess taqwa, a God-consciousness that purifies intention. Abel’s reply is not a boast about his own piety; it is a theological explanation meant to direct his brother’s anger towards self-reform, not murder. The story thus teaches that the correct response to being surpassed in virtue is introspection, not aggression, and that the foundation of every accepted deed is the quality of taqwa residing in the heart.
28 | If you raise your hand to kill me, I will not raise mine to kill you, because I fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds. | This verse continues Abel’s dignified and principled response to his brother’s murderous threat, presenting one of the most powerful declarations of moral restraint in scripture. Abel is fully aware of the danger he faces; Cain’s hand is poised to strike. Yet he states, “If you raise your hand to kill me, I will not raise mine to kill you.” This is not a statement of physical inability or cowardice. Abel is not saying he lacks the strength or the right to defend his life. Rather, he is making a conscious, ethical choice to not enter into a spiral of retaliation. He refuses to become a murderer even in the act of self-defence, because killing his brother would make him the same kind of transgressor. This sets an extraordinarily high moral standard, one that is not legislated as a general rule—Islamic law later permits self-defence—but is presented here as a personal, saintly virtue in a specific context. The reason for this choice follows immediately: “because I fear Allah—the Lord of all worlds.” The fear of Allah, this profound taqwa, completely overrides the instinct for vengeance. Abel’s vision of the world is not limited to this fleeting life. He sees beyond the immediate threat, connecting his action to the Lord of all worlds, the Master of every creation. His fear of God’s punishment and his hope for God’s pleasure are stronger than his fear of Cain’s hand. This teaches a profound lesson about the hierarchy of fears in a believer’s heart. When the fear of the Creator is truly alive, the fear of any created being shrinks to insignificance. Abel becomes the archetype of the peaceful believer, the one who absorbs violence rather than propagates it, whose faith is so deep that he entrusts his very life to divine justice. This spirit is reflected in numerous hadiths that praise the believer who controls anger and refrains from strife, even when he is in the right, leaving his ultimate vindication to God.
29 | I want to let you bear your sin against me along with your other sins, then you will be one of those destined to the Fire. And that is the reward of the wrongdoers. | Abel’s speech reaches its conclusion with a statement that reveals not a passive submission to death, but a profound understanding of divine justice and an unexpected form of care for his own soul. He says, “I want to let you bear your sin against me along with your other sins.” The phrase “I want” shows that Abel is not merely resigned to his fate but actively wills it for a higher purpose. He does not desire his brother’s destruction out of hatred; he is laying out the stark reality of what Cain’s action will bring upon himself. By murdering him, Cain will not only carry the sin of the murder but will also add that to his “other sins,” meaning the earlier sins of envy, a rejected offering, and a corrupted heart. Abel is essentially saying, “My blood will be a burden around your neck on the Day of Judgment.” The result is then stated with clarity: “then you will be one of those destined to the Fire.” Abel is not making a judgment from his own ego; he is stating the divine law as revealed to him. The act of murder, especially of a righteous believer, is a sin that leads directly to Hellfire unless met with sincere repentance. The final sentence, “And that is the reward of the wrongdoers,” universalises the principle. It is not just Cain who receives such a punishment; every wrongdoer who transgresses against others will face the same crushing justice. Abel’s words serve as a mirror for all tyrants and murderers. They may succeed in spilling blood in this world, but they are bankrupting their own Hereafter in a transaction of ultimate loss. For the believer who reads this verse, it inspires a sombre reflection: the one who refrains from retaliation is not weak; he is ensuring that he stands on the Day of Judgment with a clean record, while his oppressor appears laden with the sins he committed. It is the ultimate victory of the victim, a victory that is not seen by the eyes of this world but is eternal in the next.
30 | Yet Cain convinced himself to kill his brother, so he killed him—becoming a loser. | After the brilliant and truthful exhortation of Abel, the Quran records the tragic climax with a devastating brevity. The phrase “Yet Cain convinced himself” translates the Arabic *fa-ṭawwa‘at lahu nafsuhu*, which literally means his soul made the act easy and agreeable for him. This is the terrifying anatomy of sin. The human soul, when left unchecked by taqwa, slowly rationalises the worst actions until they appear not only acceptable but desirable. Cain’s initial envy brewed into anger, his anger was not checked by his brother’s wisdom, and now his own inner whispers have decorated the heinous act of fratricide as a justified solution to his wounded pride. There is no external devil mentioned here; the focus is on the internal enemy, the nafs, which can become a greater enemy than any external shaytan. The verse then states the grim action simply: “so he killed him.” The Quran does not linger on the gruesome details of the first murder. The starkness of the statement itself conveys the horror. In one moment of uncontrolled rage, the sacred bond of brotherhood was shattered, and a human soul, innocent of any crime, was sent to meet its Lord. Immediately, the divine verdict is pronounced: “becoming a loser.” The loss here is absolute and total. He lost his brother, his innocence, his peace of mind, his relationship with his Lord, and his Hereafter. In a worldly sense, he might have thought he had won by eliminating the source of his envy, but the Quranic reality flips this perception completely. He is the *khāsir*, the one in a state of utter bankruptcy. This is the final destination of every person who lets their envy and anger dictate their actions. The verse serves as a timeless warning that the one who transgresses against another human being ultimately destroys nothing but their own soul. The loss in the divine ledger is total, even if the worldly ledger shows a temporary, blood-stained gain.
31 | Then Allah sent a crow digging ˹a grave˺ in the ground ˹for a dead crow˺, in order to show him how to bury the corpse of his brother. He cried, “Alas! Have I ˹even˺ failed to be like this crow and bury the corpse of my brother?” So he became regretful. | After the horrific act of murder, the narrative takes a darkly ironic and deeply instructive turn, detailing the immediate aftermath that compounds Cain’s misery. The verse states that “Allah sent a crow digging a grave in the ground.” The crow is an animal of lowly status, a scavenger. God inspired this crow to scratch at the earth to bury another dead crow. The purpose was “to show him how to bury the corpse of his brother.” This is a moment of profound humiliation for the human being who had been honoured with knowledge. Cain, a son of a prophet, had just committed the first murder but had absolutely no idea what to do with the body. He was carrying his brother’s corpse on his back, confused and haunted. The fact that he needed a bird to teach him the most basic act of covering the dead revealed the utter regression of his intelligence caused by his sin. Seeing the crow’s action, he cried out in anguish, “Alas! Have I even failed to be like this crow and bury the corpse of my brother?” This exclamation is a confession of his own inferiority in that moment. A creature of instinct displayed more practical wisdom than the human who had let his passion destroy his reason. He was not like the crow; he was lower, for the crow disposed of the dead with dignity while he had caused the death. The verse ends with a note on his emotional state: “So he became regretful.” However, the scholars of tafsir note that this regret, or *nadamah*, was not the regret of sincere repentance that wipes out sin. It was the regret of a criminal who is sorry for the consequences of his crime, the inconvenience and the humiliation, not a remorse that turns back to God with a broken heart seeking forgiveness. There is no record of Cain repenting. His regret was the bitter fruit of guilt, a fire that burns in the heart but does not illuminate the path back to the Lord.
32 | That is why We ordained for the Children of Israel that whoever takes a life—unless as a punishment for murder or mischief in the land—it will be as if they killed all of humanity; and whoever saves a life, it will be as if they saved all of humanity. ˹Although˺ Our messengers already came to them with clear proofs, many of them still transgressed afterwards through the land. | This verse transitions from the narrative of Cain and Abel to a grand universal principle, which is the direct legal and moral lesson derived from that first murder. The opening phrase “That is why” connects the decree directly to the story. Because a single act of envy led to the first unjust killing, Allah ordained a law that magnifies the weight of human life to an almost incomprehensible degree. The law was specifically written for the Children of Israel, but its principle is enshrined in Islamic ethics as well. The decree states that “whoever takes a life—unless as a punishment for murder or mischief in the land—it will be as if they killed all of humanity.” The unjust killing of one soul is equated with the murder of all people. This is not a mere exaggeration; it is a profound ethical truth. The person who kills one innocent human being demonstrates a willingness to violate the sanctity of life itself. They have shredded the universal respect for human existence, and if left to their own devices, their disregard for life could extend to many. It is the principle of the sanctity of life in its most powerful expression. The only exceptions are clearly defined: lawful retribution for murder, and punishment for “mischief in the land,” which scholars interpret as crimes that destabilise society, such as terrorism and highway robbery. The opposite side of the coin is equally powerful: “whoever saves a life, it will be as if they saved all of humanity.” Saving a life here refers to any act that prevents a wrongful death—rescuing someone from danger, advocating for a wrongly accused person, or even refraining from killing when one has the power to do so. The verse then adds a sorrowful historical note: “Although Our messengers already came to them with clear proofs, many of them still transgressed afterwards through the land.” This indicates that even with this sacred law revealed, the Israelites, like many later communities, still violated it, shedding blood and causing corruption, proving that laws alone are not enough without hearts filled with taqwa.
33 | Indeed, the penalty for those who wage war against Allah and His Messenger and spread mischief in the land is death, crucifixion, cutting off their hands and feet on opposite sides, or exile from the land. This ˹penalty˺ is a disgrace for them in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter. | This verse reveals the severe worldly punishment for a specific category of criminals who combine terrorism, apostasy, and banditry. The crime is defined as “waging war against Allah and His Messenger and spreading mischief in the land.” This refers to acts of armed aggression that terrorise a peaceful society, such as highway robbery, murder, and destabilising the state that implements divine law. The verse is often called the verse of *hirābah*. The prescribed punishments are listed, and scholars have extensively discussed their application based on the severity of the crime. The options are “death” for those who kill and terrorise, “crucifixion” after execution as a public deterrent for those who combine murder with robbery, “cutting off their hands and feet on opposite sides” for those who engage in armed robbery without taking a life, and “exile from the land” for those who cause terror but stop short of these acts. The range of penalties gives the Islamic judge discretion to match the punishment to the crime. The purpose is explicitly stated as a “disgrace for them in this world,” meaning a public humiliation that serves as a deterrent and an affirmation of the state’s authority to protect its citizens. However, the verse does not stop at the worldly punishment. It adds the terrifying spiritual consequence: “and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.” This means that even if a criminal is executed, the worldly punishment does not expiate the sin before God, unless they repented before being apprehended, as the next verse clarifies. The severity of this law is a direct extension of the sanctity of life principle in the previous verse. Because human life is so valuable, those who shamelessly violate it and disrupt the peace of society face the sternest possible recompense in this life and the next, establishing a climate of safety for the common people.
34 | As for those who repent before you seize them, then know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | Immediately after the harsh and detailed punishment for hirabah, the Quran opens a wide door of divine mercy, demonstrating that the law’s ultimate goal is not vengeance but the reformation of society and the individual. The condition for this escape from the worldly punishment is clear and time-bound: “those who repent before you seize them.” Repentance must occur before the criminal is captured by the authorities. If they turn themselves in, overcome by remorse and a genuine desire to reform, the prescribed worldly punishment for the crime of waging war against the society is dropped. This is a powerful legal and spiritual incentive. It encourages the criminal to abandon their path of violence and surrender peacefully, knowing that they can find complete amnesty from the state’s penalty. This fosters a practical mechanism for reducing violence without a protracted manhunt. However, the phrase “before you seize them” is crucial. Once the criminal is captured, the door for amnesty from the worldly punishment closes, because at that point surrender is no longer a voluntary act of repentance but a consequence of being overpowered. The verse then highlights the divine attributes that ground this leniency: “then know that Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The guarantee of forgiveness for the one who repents is absolute, because these two names of God envelop the sinner in a promise of pardon. This does not necessarily mean they are absolved from the rights of their victims; many scholars state that the victims’ rights, such as blood money, remain intact and must be fulfilled. But the state’s right to punish is waived. The sequence of these two verses is a profound illustration of the balance between justice and mercy in Islamic law. The severity of the previous verse protects society, while the mercy of this verse protects the soul of the sinner who desires to change. It tells every human being that no matter how grave the crime, as long as the heart is still beating and the person is free to choose, the path back to God is open.
35 | O believers! Be mindful of Allah and seek what brings you closer to Him and struggle in His Way, so you may be successful. | After the intense legal passages dealing with murder, terrorism, and theft, the Quran once again addresses the believers with a comprehensive call to spiritual and practical action, providing the formula for true success. The verse opens with the core command of the faith: “O believers! Be mindful of Allah.” This is the foundational taqwa, the constant awareness that acts as an internal moral compass. Immediately, a second command is added, which explains how to achieve and deepen that taqwa: “and seek what brings you closer to Him.” The word for “seek” is *ibtighā'*, implying active, persistent searching. The “what brings you closer” translates *al-wasīlah*, a term that encompasses every permissible means that elevates one’s rank before God. This includes obligatory acts of worship, abundant voluntary prayers, charity, seeking beneficial knowledge, maintaining family ties, and serving the creation. It is a call to be proactive in one’s spiritual life, not merely to avoid sin but to aggressively pursue the practices and stations that draw the divine love. The third command forms a triad of personal piety, devotional pursuit, and societal struggle: “and struggle in His Way.” The term *jāhidū* is used, the root word of jihad, which encompasses every form of striving for God’s cause, from the internal struggle against one’s ego to the external struggle of defending the community and conveying the message. This struggle is the testing ground of the faith. The verse ends with the goal of all these actions: “so you may be successful.” The word *tufliḥūn*, from *falāḥ*, denotes the ultimate, comprehensive success that is not just material prosperity but the eternal well-being of the soul. The sequence is beautiful: taqwa is the inner light, seeking wasilah is the active devotional life, jihad is the external proof of sincerity, and falāḥ is the promised fruit of this complete programme. This verse gives the Muslim a concise roadmap for a life that leads to true victory.
36 | As for the disbelievers, even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over ˹and offer it all˺ to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Judgment, it would never be accepted from them. And they will suffer a painful punishment. | The surah now shifts back to the fate of those who reject faith, painting a vivid and terrifying picture of the Day of Judgment that serves as a stark contrast to the "success" promised to the believers. It begins with "As for the disbelievers," immediately defining the group whose destiny is about to be described. The hypothetical scenario presented is one of absolute material wealth: "even if they were to possess everything in the world twice over." This is a rhetorical device that pushes the imagination to its limits. Imagine a person who owns not just the entire earth's riches—all the gold, oil, lands, and treasures—but double that amount. Such wealth, in worldly terms, would be sufficient to buy anything a human heart could desire. The verse then places this unimaginable fortune in the context of the Afterlife: "and offer it all to ransom themselves from the punishment of the Day of Judgment." In this world, a wealthy person can often buy their way out of legal trouble. The Quran shatters this illusion. On that Day, there is no currency, no negotiation, and no bail. The divine verdict is stated with crushing finality: "it would never be accepted from them." The rejection is absolute. Their fate is sealed not by a lack of resources but by the nature of that Day, where the only acceptable currency is a sound heart, faith, and righteous deeds. The verse concludes with the sentence: "And they will suffer a painful punishment." The word "painful," translating *alīm*, indicates a pain that permeates the very core of one's being, a suffering that is both physical and spiritual. This verse is meant to shake the conscience of the listener. It warns the believers against being impressed by the material wealth of those who reject God, for on the Day of Judgment, that wealth will be less than worthless. It also instills a deep gratitude for the promise of forgiveness given to the believers, a gift that all the treasures of the earth could never purchase.
37 | They will be desperate to get out of the Fire but they will never be able to. And they will suffer an everlasting punishment. | This verse adds further terrifying details to the fate of the disbelievers, moving from the Day of Judgment’s courtroom to the actual experience within Hell. The psychological state of the damned is captured in the phrase "They will be desperate to get out of the Fire." The desperation is an intense, panicked craving, a desire for escape that overwhelms every other thought. When a person is trapped in a worldly fire, every fibre of their being screams to flee the flames. This verse magnifies that instinct to an eternal scale. They will wish for death, they will wish for oblivion, they will wish for any exit. However, the divine will is unalterable: "but they will never be able to." Their powerlessness is absolute. There is no eventual release, no parole, no annihilation of the self. Their wish is perpetually denied, adding a profound psychological torment to the physical pain. The verse then concludes with the definitive statement: "And they will suffer an everlasting punishment." The word "everlasting" translates *muqīm*, meaning established, permanent, and continuous without interruption. This affirms the Islamic doctrine that Hell is an eternal abode for those who die upon disbelief. This teaching is not a matter of divine cruelty but of divine justice, as they had been given a lifetime of opportunities, clear signs, and warnings, yet they persistently rejected the truth and would have remained in that state had they lived forever. The sequence of verses 36 and 37 serves a clear function in the surah. After detailing the punishments for worldly crimes like hirabah and theft, the Quran reminds the listener that these punishments, however severe, are finite and can even be nullified by repentance. The punishment of the disbelievers, however, is a stark reminder that the ultimate catastrophe is not a worldly penalty but the eternal loss of the Hereafter. This contrast is designed to reorder the believer’s priorities and to instill a healthy fear that propels one toward the success mentioned in verse 35.
38 | As for male and female thieves, cut off their hands for what they have done—a deterrent from Allah. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise. | The surah returns to the realm of criminal law, having established the grand ethical principles about the sanctity of life and the need for societal peace. This verse addresses the crime of theft, a violation of property rights that erodes economic trust and security. It begins by explicitly mentioning both genders: "As for male and female thieves." This is a profound statement of legal equality, ensuring that a woman's punishment for theft is the same as a man's, unlike the pre-Islamic customs that often treated women with harsher or more lenient standards arbitrarily. The prescribed punishment is stated with stark clarity: "cut off their hands." The Arabic word *yad* refers to the hand, and the juristic consensus is that for a first-time offence meeting all strict conditions, the right hand is amputated from the wrist. The condition for this punishment is that the theft must involve a minimum value of stolen goods, taken from a secure location, without any plausible claim of ownership or need-driven justification. This is not a punishment for a hungry person stealing food out of necessity, which is excused by the law of necessity. The rationale is immediately stated: "for what they have done—a deterrent from Allah." The word "deterrent," *nakālan*, implies an exemplary punishment that serves to prevent others from committing the same crime. It is a measure that protects the property of the entire community by making the cost of theft so severe. However, the verse closes with two divine names that frame the entire ruling: "And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise." The name "Almighty" indicates that He has the authority to enact such a law, and no human sentimentality can override it. The name "All-Wise" is crucial; it affirms that this punishment, which may seem harsh to modern sensibilities that prioritise the criminal's comfort over the victim's security, is rooted in a perfect wisdom that understands human nature, the need for a safe society, and the ultimate balance of justice and mercy. The wisdom is seen in the high standards of evidence required, the detailed conditions for amputation, and the provision for repentance in the next verse.
39 | But whoever repents after their wrongdoing and mends their ways, Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness. Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | Following the same merciful pattern seen after the punishment for hirabah, this verse opens the door of divine forgiveness for the thief who reforms, though it does not explicitly cancel the worldly punishment if the crime has already been proven and the punishment is due. The verse states, "But whoever repents after their wrongdoing and mends their ways." True repentance here is defined by two elements: remorse for the past sin and a concrete, visible amendment of behaviour. The thief must not only feel sorry but must also return the stolen property to its rightful owner if possible, and change their lifestyle to one of honesty and integrity. This act of genuine, transformative repentance triggers a magnificent divine promise: "Allah will surely turn to them in forgiveness." The term *yatūbu*, from *tawbah*, means that Allah Himself turns towards the servant with His mercy and pardon. It is a beautiful image of a reciprocal relationship: the servant turns away from sin, and the Lord turns towards the sinner with forgiveness. The certainty of this promise is emphasised with the word "surely." Then the verse concludes with the deeply comforting pair of divine names: "Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful." The word "All-Forgiving" (*Ghafūr*) means He covers the sin and wipes its record clean, while "Most Merciful" (*Raḥīm*) means He bestows blessings and love upon the repentant soul. This verse, when read with the previous one, perfectly illustrates the Islamic balance. The law on earth serves to protect society and deter crime through a visible, severe punishment. The law of the Hereafter serves to redeem the individual soul that genuinely changes. No matter how grave the sin—whether terrorism, murder, or theft—the penitent heart that turns to its Creator before death finds an ocean of mercy. This prevents the criminal from falling into despair, assuring them that their past does not permanently define them as long as they perform a sincere tawbah that mends their relationship with God and with the creation they have wronged.
40 | Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah ˹alone˺? He punishes whoever He wills and forgives whoever He wills. And Allah is Most Capable of everything. | This verse serves as a majestic conclusion to the extended legal discourse, bringing the reader’s attention back to the absolute sovereignty and authority of the Lawgiver. It begins with a rhetorical question: "Do you not know that the kingdom of the heavens and the earth belongs to Allah alone?" This is a gentle rebuke aimed at awakening the innate knowledge that every soul possesses about God’s absolute ownership. Everything in the universe, from the vast galaxies to the cells in a thief's hand, belongs to Him. The laws He decrees are not the impositions of an outsider but the rulings of the true Sovereign over His own dominion. This realization is meant to foster a deep submission to the rulings that preceded it. Why should a human being object to the punishment for theft or for waging war, when the One who commanded it owns the very hand being cut and the life being taken? The verse then declares a principle that governs the operation of this divine kingdom: "He punishes whoever He wills and forgives whoever He wills." This statement is not a license for arbitrary will devoid of wisdom, as the names Al-Hakīm (All-Wise) and Al-‘Adl (The Just) are fundamental. Rather, it means that no one can force Allah’s hand. His punishment and forgiveness are not subject to external approval. He punishes those who meet the conditions for His punishment according to His perfect knowledge, and He forgives those who meet the conditions for His mercy. It silences the human arrogance that questions why certain sins are met with certain penalties, reminding the believer that the criteria belong to the All-Knowing. The verse ends with the comprehensive affirmation: "And Allah is Most Capable of everything." His capability encompasses the enforcement of His laws, the resurrection of the dead for judgment, and the execution of His perfect will in both worlds. This final note is a warning and a comfort: a warning to those who think they can evade the divine law, and a comfort to those who trust that the Almighty is fully able to grant them the success and forgiveness He promised in the earlier verses.
41 | O Messenger! Do not grieve for those who race to disbelieve—those who say, “We believe” with their tongues, but their hearts are in disbelief. Nor those among the Jews who eagerly listen to lies, attentive to those who are too arrogant to come to you. They distort the Scripture, taking rulings out of context, then say, “If this is the ruling you get from Muhammad, accept it. If not, beware!” Whoever Allah allows to be deluded, you can never be of any help to them against Allah. It is not Allah’s Will to purify their hearts. For them there is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter. | This verse is a direct consolation to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, as he faced the coordinated opposition of hypocrites and certain Jewish tribes in Medina. The address “O Messenger” is intimate, and the command “Do not grieve” acknowledges the profound sorrow a sincere prophet feels when people deliberately choose disbelief despite clear evidence. The first group described are those who “race to disbelieve,” a phrase that conveys their eagerness and competitive haste in rejecting the truth. Outwardly, they say “We believe” with their tongues, but Allah exposes the reality: “their hearts are in disbelief.” This is the classic definition of nifaq, hypocrisy, where the external declaration masks an internal void. The Prophet is then alerted to another group, “those among the Jews who eagerly listen to lies.” These were Jewish leaders and individuals who did not come with a sincere desire for guidance but sought out falsehood and slander against the Prophet to spread it. They were “attentive to those who are too arrogant to come to you,” meaning they acted as spies and informants for other Jews who refused even to attend the Prophet’s gatherings. Their distortion of the Scripture is a specific historical crime. They would “distort the Scripture, taking rulings out of context.” A notorious example involved the penalty for adultery. The Torah prescribed stoning for the married adulterer, but the Jewish elite had replaced it with a lighter punishment of flogging and blackening the face. When they brought a case to the Prophet, hoping he would rule lightly, he asked what the Torah said. They concealed the stoning verse. When a learned convert, Abdullah ibn Salam, revealed the true verse, the Prophet ordered the stoning, demonstrating that his judgment was aligned with the original, uncorrupted Torah. The Jewish tactic was to then say, “If this is the ruling you get from Muhammad, accept it. If not, beware!” They would shop for verdicts, accepting only what aligned with their desires. The verse then declares a divine law: “Whoever Allah allows to be deluded, you can never be of any help to them against Allah.” The Prophet’s role is to convey; he cannot force guidance into a heart that Allah has left astray due to persistent rebellion. “It is not Allah’s Will to purify their hearts” does not mean Allah is unjust, but that their hearts have become so corrupted by their own choices that purification is no longer sought or granted. Their fate is sealed: “disgrace in this world” through exposure and defeat, and a “tremendous punishment in the Hereafter.” This verse instructs every caller to truth that grief for the obstinate should not paralyze them; the results belong to God, and the stubbornly rebellious have chosen their own ruin.
42 | They eagerly listen to falsehood and consume forbidden gain. So if they come to you O Prophet, either judge between them or turn away from them. If you turn away from them, they cannot harm you whatsoever. But if you judge between them, then do so with justice. Surely Allah loves those who are just. | The verse continues the exposé of the corrupt Jewish leaders’ behaviour, listing two defining vices. “They eagerly listen to falsehood” means they are addicted to lies, whether it is slander against the Prophet or fabricated interpretations of their own scripture. The second vice is that they “consume forbidden gain.” The Arabic term here is *suht*, which refers specifically to ill-gotten wealth, most commonly bribery. These leaders would take bribes to alter judgments or to hide the truth of the Torah. In a famous hadith, the Prophet said, “The curse of Allah is upon the one who gives a bribe and the one who takes it in matters of judgment.” This consumption of illicit money had poisoned their spiritual integrity. Having established their moral bankruptcy, the verse provides the Prophet with a strategic judicial option. When such people come seeking his judgment in their internal disputes, he is given a choice: “either judge between them or turn away from them.” This is a remarkable legal and diplomatic provision. It shows that an Islamic state is not obligated to adjudicate every case brought by non-Muslim citizens if there is evidence of bad faith or manipulation; the community can govern its own internal religious and social affairs. The Prophet is assured, “If you turn away from them, they cannot harm you whatsoever,” negating any political pressure they might try to exert. However, if he chooses to judge, the divine command is absolute: “then do so with justice.” Even though they are known liars and consumers of bribery, the Prophet must not allow their character to prejudice his ruling. The judgment must be based strictly on the evidence and the law, whether that be the Islamic Sharia or their own Torah if he chooses to apply it. The verse seals this principle with the divine attribute of love: “Surely Allah loves those who are just.” Justice, *qist*, is a core attribute of the believer, a quality that must shine especially when dealing with those who are themselves unjust. This verse beautifully balances practical wisdom with an uncompromising ethical standard, teaching that the most effective way to deal with a corrupt opponent is to meet their scheming with a justice that is beyond reproach.
43 | But why do they come to you for judgment when they already have the Torah containing Allah’s judgment, then they turn away after all? They are not true believers. | This verse is a rhetorical question that exposes the deep contradiction and hypocrisy of those Jewish leaders who sought the Prophet’s judgment. The question “But why do they come to you for judgment” arises naturally from the previous verse’s permission for the Prophet to judge or turn away. It calls out the absurdity of their situation. They possessed the Torah, which they themselves claimed to be divine revelation, and it “contained Allah’s judgment.” The Torah had clear rulings on matters like adultery, theft, and dietary laws. Why then would they abandon their own holy book and come to a prophet they claimed not to believe in? The answer is exposed in their subsequent action: “then they turn away after all.” They would come only if they thought they could get a more lenient verdict than what their own scripture demanded. If the Prophet ruled against their desires, they would reject his judgment and walk away, revealing that their quest was never for truth but for convenience. The famous incident of the adulterous couple illustrates this perfectly. The Jewish leaders brought the case to the Prophet hoping he would issue a light sentence that would allow them to avoid the harshness of the Torah’s stoning penalty, which they had already abandoned in practice. When the Prophet, by divine guidance, ruled according to the true Torah, they were exposed and humiliated. The verse concludes with the damning verdict: “They are not true believers.” This statement means they are not genuine believers in the Torah they profess to follow, because a true follower of the Torah would submit to its judgments wholeheartedly, just as a true follower of the Quran submits to its rulings without picking and choosing. The verse therefore establishes a timeless principle: selective obedience to divine law is a mark of disbelief, regardless of the scripture one claims to follow. It is a sharp warning to all communities that the claim of faith is void if it is contingent on personal desire aligning with the law, rather than the law shaping one’s desires.
44 | Indeed, We revealed the Torah, containing guidance and light, by which the prophets, who submitted themselves to Allah, made judgments for Jews. So too did the rabbis and scholars judge according to Allah’s Book, with which they were entrusted and of which they were made keepers. So do not fear the people; fear Me! Nor trade my revelations for a fleeting gain. And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are truly the disbelievers. | The Quran now provides a magnificent vindication of the Torah’s original purity and the high status of those who upheld it, before contrasting it with the corruption of later generations. The verse begins by affirming that the Torah was indeed a divinely revealed scripture “containing guidance and light.” The guidance is the path to righteousness, and the light is the illumination that dispels ignorance. The phrase “by which the prophets, who submitted themselves to Allah, made judgments for Jews” is significant. The prophets were Muslim in the universal sense, completely submitted to God, and they governed the Jewish people by the Torah. This shows that all prophets, including those after Moses like David, Solomon, and Jesus, judged the Israelites by the Torah until the coming of new scriptures. The role of the scholarly class is then highlighted: “So too did the rabbis and scholars judge according to Allah’s Book.” The rabbis and learned men were the inheritors of the prophetic mission, entrusted with preserving the scripture and adjudicating by its laws. They were “entrusted” with it and “made keepers,” a responsibility of unimaginable weight. At this point, the verse shifts to a direct, thunderous command: “So do not fear the people; fear Me!” This addresses those scholars and leaders who, in later times, were tempted to conceal or alter the law out of fear of powerful members of their community. This is precisely what happened when they hid the stoning penalty to avoid social pressure. They traded the truth for social comfort. The command is followed by the prohibition: “Nor trade my revelations for a fleeting gain.” This refers to the bribes and material advantages they accepted in exchange for distorting judgments. The verse then delivers the famous and often-misunderstood verdict: “And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are truly the disbelievers.” The renowned companion Ibn Abbas explained that this refers to a disbelief that is less than major disbelief, a rejection or replacement of the law that does not necessarily expel one from Islam unless accompanied by a denial of its divine origin. The verse, in its context, primarily targets the Jewish leaders who substituted Allah’s law with man-made alternatives while knowing the truth. It stands as a severe warning to any individual or authority who consciously replaces divine legislation with something else, declaring it better or more suitable.
45 | We ordained for them in the Torah, “A life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear, a tooth for a tooth—and for wounds equal retaliation.” But whoever waives it charitably, it will be atonement for them. And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are truly the wrongdoers. | This verse zooms into a specific legal prescription from the Torah, the law of Qisas, or equal retaliation, which Islam also upholds with merciful modifications. The ordinance is listed in precise physical detail: “A life for a life, an eye for an eye, a nose for a nose, an ear for an ear, a tooth for a tooth.” This principle establishes a strict proportionality in justice. It is not a savage call for vengeance but a precisely measured limitation. Before this, feuding tribes would kill dozens for a single life; the law of Qisas restricted retaliation to the exact equivalent of the harm, thereby curbing excessive violence and providing a basis for social order. The verse then also mentions “and for wounds equal retaliation,” extending the principle to all forms of bodily injury. Immediately after establishing the right to retaliation, the Quran introduces a spiritual and ethical elevation: “But whoever waives it charitably, it will be atonement for them.” The victim or their family is given the choice to forgo retaliation, either by forgiving the injury completely without compensation or by accepting monetary blood money, diyah. The act of charitable waiver is described as “atonement,” meaning it expiates the forgiving person’s own sins with God. This is a profound moral incentive. The law of retaliation is there to protect lives and deter aggression, but the door of forgiveness is opened wide to encourage a higher spiritual station, where the victim overcomes their anger for the sake of divine reward. This directly mirrors the Islamic principle enshrined in Surah Al-Baqarah, where retaliation is prescribed but forgiveness is declared the better path. The verse concludes with the second category of divine warning: “And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are truly the wrongdoers.” The word used here is *zālimūn*, wrongdoers or oppressors. While the previous verse called them disbelievers for rejecting the law, this verse calls them wrongdoers for violating the rights of the victims by not applying the just law of equality. The subtle shift in wording indicates that ignoring God’s revealed judgment is simultaneously an act of disbelief, injustice, and rebellion, each term capturing a different dimension of the crime.
46 | Then in the footsteps of the prophets, We sent Jesus, son of Mary, confirming the Torah revealed before him. And We gave him the Gospel containing guidance and light and confirming what was revealed in the Torah—a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing. | The surah now traces the unbroken chain of revelation, moving from Moses and the Torah to Jesus and the Gospel, to establish the continuity of divine guidance and to set the stage for the final revelation. The phrase “in the footsteps of the prophets” is a beautiful image of Jesus walking the path paved by the great prophets before him, such as David, Solomon, Isaiah, and John the Baptist. He did not come to erase their legacy but to follow and complete it. The verse asserts that Jesus, the son of Mary, was sent “confirming the Torah revealed before him.” His mission was not to abolish the Mosaic law but to bear witness to its original truth and to correct the distortions that had crept in through rabbinical traditions. His moral and spiritual teachings were an extension of the Torah’s core monotheism and ethical demands. The verse then declares, “And We gave him the Gospel containing guidance and light.” The Gospel, or Injil, was not a book of new law replacing the Torah entirely; it was a spiritual supplement that illuminated the heart and guided to a purer devotion. The description of the Gospel as “confirming what was revealed in the Torah” reinforces the idea that the original scriptures were a harmonious set, all pointing to the same God and the same moral universe. However, the Gospel was tailored to its audience, tempering some of the Torah’s rigorous laws while intensifying the call to inner purity and God-consciousness. The verse concludes that the Gospel was “a guide and a lesson to the God-fearing.” The people of taqwa are the ones who truly benefit from scripture. It is a guide to practical righteousness and a “lesson,” or admonition, that awakens the soul. By presenting this lineage, the Quran demolishes the idea that Jesus came with a brand new religion disconnected from Abrahamic monotheism. He was a link in the same golden chain. This verse, therefore, functions as both an affirmation of Christian scripture in its original, uncorrupted form, and a preparation for the next verse’s command that the Christians should judge by their own Gospel, which in fact contains prophecies and directives that align with the coming of the final Messenger.
47 | So let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed in it. And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are truly the rebellious. | This verse is a direct address to the Christian community, both at the time of the Prophet and as a principle for all time. The command “So let the people of the Gospel judge by what Allah has revealed in it” is deeply significant. The Quran does not tell the Christians to abandon the Gospel immediately in every detail of their daily life; instead, it challenges them to be true to their own scripture. If they sincerely judged by the authentic Gospel, they would find within it the clear teachings of pure monotheism, the human nature of Jesus as a messenger, and the glad tidings of a final prophet to come, Ahmad or the Paraclete. Their own book, when read without the lens of later Trinitarian doctrines, would guide them to the truth of Islam. The verse thus sets a standard of internal consistency: before they can even begin to reject the Quran, they must first honestly apply the teachings of the book they already profess to believe in. The failure to do so is then categorized with the third and final label in this series: “And those who do not judge by what Allah has revealed are truly the rebellious.” In verse 44, those who abandoned the Torah were called disbelievers. In verse 45, they were called wrongdoers. Here, with regard to the Gospel, they are called *fāsiqūn*, the rebellious. This term indicates a deliberate stepping outside the bounds of obedience, a moral and spiritual anarchy where one knows the command but willfully defies it. The rebellious person is not necessarily an outright denier of the scripture’s divine origin but is one who treats its commands as optional, bending them to fit personal desires and cultural pressures. The historical Christian departure from the Gospel’s monotheism and law is a case study in such rebellion. This triple classification—disbeliever, wrongdoer, rebellious—is a masterful Quranic method of layering the condemnation, each term highlighting a different angle of the same catastrophic error: the substitution of divine authority with human whim. The verse is a call to integrity, urging every community to examine whether they are truly living by the light they have already received, before a brighter light arrives to test them further.
48 | We have revealed to you O Prophet this Book with the truth, as a confirmation of previous Scriptures and a supreme authority on them. So judge between them by what Allah has revealed, and do not follow their desires over the truth that has come to you. To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life. If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given each of you. So compete with one another in doing good. To Allah you will all return, then He will inform you of the truth regarding your differences. | This magnificent verse encapsulates the entire Quranic philosophy of religious pluralism, divine law, and human accountability. It begins by addressing the Prophet, affirming that the Quran is revealed “with the truth,” meaning its entire content is an undeniable reality that dispels all falsehood. Its relationship to the previous scriptures is defined with two terms. First, it is “a confirmation of previous Scriptures,” authenticating the original Torah and Gospel and their core message. Second, it is “a supreme authority on them,” translating the powerful word *muhaymin*. This means the Quran is a guardian, a watcher, and a criterion over all prior revelations. It preserves what is eternally true in them, clarifies what was distorted, and abrogates what was temporary. Based on this supreme standard, the Prophet is commanded: “So judge between them by what Allah has revealed, and do not follow their desires.” The Quran is the final reference point. The verse then reveals a profound divine wisdom that explains why different religious communities exist. “To each of you We have ordained a code of law and a way of life.” The word *shir‘ah* refers to a specific legal path, and *minhāj* is a broader way of life. This acknowledges that the specific legal details of Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad’s messages differed, even though the foundational faith was the same. This diversity was not a divine mistake but a deliberate test: “If Allah had willed, He would have made you one community, but His Will is to test you with what He has given each of you.” The different laws are a means of testing obedience, humility, and recognition of truth when the final and most complete law arrives. Since the test is active, the verse gives the instruction for righteous competition: “So compete with one another in doing good.” The different communities are not to compete in hatred or war but in virtuous deeds. The end of all diversity is union before the One Judge: “To Allah you will all return, then He will inform you of the truth regarding your differences.” The final judgment belongs to God alone, which relieves believers of the burden of ultimate adjudication while motivating them to live their own law with excellence.
49 | And judge between them O Prophet by what Allah has revealed, and do not follow their desires. And beware, so they do not lure you away from some of what Allah has revealed to you. If they turn away from Allah’s judgment, then know that it is Allah’s Will to repay them for some of their sins, and that many people are indeed rebellious. | This verse repeats and intensifies the command to judge by divine revelation, emphasizing the gravity of the prophetic responsibility and the subtlety of the danger posed by those who oppose the truth. The command “And judge between them, O Prophet, by what Allah has revealed” is a direct reiteration from the previous verse, indicating its central importance. The prohibition that follows, “and do not follow their desires,” is the negative boundary. The desires of the People of the Book, and of any group, often pull in the direction of leniency when it suits their own interests and severity when it targets their enemies. The true judge must be blind to these pressures. The verse then adds a chilling warning: “And beware, so they do not lure you away from some of what Allah has revealed to you.” The verb “lure” suggests a gradual, almost imperceptible seduction. The enemies of truth might not ask the Prophet to abandon his entire mission at once; they might begin with small compromises, a slight softening of a ruling here, a convenient interpretation there. The divine warning is that even a prophet must be on guard against this slippery slope. The verse thus teaches that the threat of corruption is not just a sudden, open invitation to disbelief, but a subtle erosion of principles. The next sentence addresses the inevitable rejection that the Prophet will face: “If they turn away from Allah’s judgment, then know that it is Allah’s Will to repay them for some of their sins.” Their turning away is not a sign that the truth is weak, but a fulfillment of a divine decree that punishes them for their prior sins. The phrase “some of their sins” indicates that the punishment for every sin is not all given in this world; some are stored for the Hereafter, while worldly afflictions serve as a partial recompense or a warning. The verse concludes with the observation, “and that many people are indeed rebellious.” This universalizes the situation, reminding the believer that the rebellion against divine law is a widespread human affliction, not an anomaly. The verse as a whole is a manual for ethical leadership: unwavering commitment to revealed law, resistance to manipulative temptation, and a psychological understanding that rejection is often the consequence of the rejector’s own spiritual sickness.
50 | Is it the judgment of pre-Islamic ignorance they seek? Who could be a better judge than Allah for people of sure faith? | This verse is a rhetorical crescendo that condemns any preference for man-made systems over divine legislation, contrasting the light of revelation with the darkness of jahiliyyah. The question “Is it the judgment of pre-Islamic ignorance they seek?” uses the powerful term *hukm al-jāhiliyyah*, the rule of ignorance. Jahiliyyah was the state of the Arabs before Islam, a time not defined by a lack of intelligence but by a lack of divine guidance, where tribal customs, brute force, and arbitrary desires reigned supreme. By framing the rejection of the Prophet’s judgment as a desire to return to jahiliyyah, the verse draws a sharp line: there is no neutral ground between God’s law and the law of ignorance. Every legal or moral system that does not derive its authority from the One God is essentially a modern form of the same jahiliyyah. This was a direct challenge to the hypocrites and the Jewish tribes who would only accept a ruling if it matched their whims, which is the very essence of pre-Islamic tribalism. The second half of the verse asks a question that requires no answer: “Who could be a better judge than Allah for people of sure faith?” The word “better” is *aḥsan*, implying both superior justice and superior beauty. Allah’s judgment is perfectly just, perfectly wise, and perfectly merciful. For the people of “sure faith,” or *yūqinūn*, those whose conviction is deep and unshakable, this truth is self-evident. Their certainty in God’s wisdom allows them to submit even when the logic behind a particular ruling is not clear to them. The verse implicitly declares that any hesitation to fully accept divine law is a symptom of a deficiency in one’s certainty. This short but powerful verse remains a constant mirror for Muslim societies. Whenever a community begins to feel that laws derived from human ideologies, whether secularism or nationalism, are more modern or more just than the Sharia, this verse rings out, asking them if they are truly seeking the judgment of ignorance and if they have lost the sure faith that recognizes no judge can be better than Allah.
51 | O believers! Take neither Jews nor Christians as guardians—they are guardians of each other. Whoever does so will be counted as one of them. Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people. | This verse addresses a critical socio-political boundary for the early Muslim community in Medina, a community that was still fragile and surrounded by powerful tribal and religious groups. The address “O believers” identifies the audience. The prohibition is against taking the Jews and Christians as *awliyā’*, a word translated here as “guardians” but encompassing a range of close alliances: protectors, intimate friends, confidants whose loyalty supersedes the bond of faith. The verse does not forbid basic kindness, fair dealing, or even eating with them, as earlier verses in this surah explicitly permitted eating their food and marrying their chaste women. Rather, it forbids a level of alliance that would compromise the community’s independence or secrets. The historical context involved the hypocrites and some weak Muslims who were turning to the Jewish tribes of Medina for protection and strategic partnership out of fear that the Muslim state might fail. The reason given is that “they are guardians of each other.” The Jews and Christians, despite their theological differences, shared a common scriptural heritage and would unite in their opposition to the final message that exposed their deviations. This is a sociological observation, not a racial slur. The warning is severe: “Whoever does so will be counted as one of them.” This means that a Muslim who gives their ultimate loyalty and identity to a non-Muslim bloc in a way that undermines the Muslim community has, in a profound spiritual sense, joined that group, sharing their destiny in this world and the next. This is because actions of the heart, like misplaced loyalty, can erode faith to the point of nullification. The verse ends with the divine principle, “Surely Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people.” The act of seeking protectors among those who oppose the faith is a form of zulm, a major wrong against one’s own soul and the community. Allah’s guidance is withdrawn from such people, leaving them to wander in the confusion of their divided loyalties. This verse is a timeless instruction about the importance of communal solidarity and the hierarchy of allegiances, where the bond of faith must always take precedence over all other social or political ties.
52 | You see those with sickness in their hearts racing for their guardianship, saying in justification, “We fear a turn of fortune will strike us.” But perhaps Allah will bring about your victory or another favour by His command, and they will regret what they have hidden in their hearts. | The verse paints a vivid psychological portrait of the hypocrites, the “those with sickness in their hearts.” The sickness is a chronic doubt, a spiritual disease that makes them see the world purely through the lens of material power and political calculation. The Prophet is told, “You see them racing for their guardianship.” The word “racing” conveys a desperate eagerness to secure the protection of the Jews and other non-Muslim powers in Medina. Their internal justification is exposed: “We fear a turn of fortune will strike us.” This reveals their complete lack of trust in Allah’s promise. They viewed the conflict between the Muslims and their opponents as a purely worldly contest. If the Muslims were to lose, they wanted to be on the winning side, with their pre-secured alliances saving them. This is the logic of the opportunist who has no conviction in the truth’s ultimate victory. The verse then counters their materialistic analysis with a divine “perhaps,” which, when spoken by Allah, is a certainty. “But perhaps Allah will bring about your victory or another favour by His command.” Victory here refers to the conquest of Mecca or a military triumph that would leave the hypocrites’ protectors powerless. “Another favour” could be a treaty, a political advantage, or even the death of the false allies. When this happens, the hypocrites will “regret what they have hidden in their hearts.” Their secret hopes for the Muslim failure, their inner allegiance to the disbelievers, and their false oaths will all be exposed, and they will be left in a state of profound humiliation. The regret is not the virtuous regret of repentance but the bitter remorse of a failed strategy. This verse serves as a timeless warning to those who, within the Muslim community, prioritize political expediency over principle, always hedging their bets with worldly powers because they lack certainty that Allah’s plan will prevail. It is a call to purify the heart of the disease of fear and to place complete trust in the ultimate triumph of the truth.
53 | Only then, the believers will ask one another, “Are these the ones who swore solemn oaths by Allah that they were with you?” Their deeds have been in vain, so they have become losers. | The verse transports the listener to the moment after the divine favour of victory has been granted, revealing the reaction of the sincere believers as they witness the exposure of the hypocrites. The phrase “Only then” links this scene directly to the preceding promise of victory or a divine favour. When that event occurs, the believers will turn to each other in astonishment and ask, “Are these the ones who swore solemn oaths by Allah that they were with you?” The question is rhetorical, expressing a profound shock at the scale of the deception. The hypocrites had repeatedly given their most sacred oaths, invoking Allah’s name, to prove their loyalty and to assure the believers that their allegiance was with the Muslim community. The oath by Allah was the highest form of assurance in that society, yet it was a veil for pure treachery. The exposure of their true loyalties in the moment of triumph strips away that veil completely. The verse then pronounces the divine judgment on their entire spiritual enterprise: “Their deeds have been in vain, so they have become losers.” All the actions they might have considered as good deeds—prayers, acts of charity, or participation in battles—were rendered null and void because they were built on a foundation of hypocrisy. An act without sincere faith is like a magnificent structure built on a sinkhole; it will collapse and yield nothing on the Day of Judgment. The term “losers” is the ultimate Quranic description of a failed existence. They lost the trust of the believers, the protection of their false allies, and their own souls in the Hereafter. This verse provides immense psychological comfort to a beleaguered community of sincere believers. It assures them that the truth of a person’s claims will eventually be tested and revealed by circumstances. No amount of slick oaths can permanently mask a diseased heart. The day of clarity will come, and on that day, the faithful will be vindicated, while the double-faced will stand utterly bankrupt.
54 | O believers! Whoever among you abandons their faith, Allah will replace them with others who love Him and are loved by Him. They will be humble with the believers but firm towards the disbelievers, struggling in the Way of Allah; fearing no blame from anyone. This is the favour of Allah. He grants it to whoever He wills. And Allah is All-Bountiful, All-Knowing. | This verse is both a warning and a glorious prophecy that provides profound assurance about the future of the faith. It begins by addressing the reality of human weakness: “Whoever among you abandons their faith.” Apostasy is a possibility, and the verse does not shy away from acknowledging that some who claim to be believers may turn back. However, the divine response to such abandonment is not the collapse of Islam but its renewal. The statement “Allah will replace them with others” is a direct declaration that the cause of truth is not dependent on any single group, tribe, or generation. If the current bearers of the faith fail in their duty, God will bring forth a new people. The description of this new people is one of the most beautiful and comprehensive portraits of a believer in the entire Quran. First, “they love Him and are loved by Him.” This is the vertical, spiritual core. Their motivation is not fear of hell or desire for paradise alone, but a mutual love between the servant and the Lord, the highest possible station. Second, “they will be humble with the believers.” Among their brothers and sisters, they are soft, compassionate, and free from arrogance. Third, “but firm towards the disbelievers.” The word for firm is *a‘izzah*, which carries the meaning of strong, dignified, and uncompromising when it comes to principles. This balance of humility and strength is the hallmark of the true Muslim character. Fourth, they are “struggling in the Way of Allah,” actively engaged in jihad with their wealth and lives. Fifth, they possess a rare moral courage: “fearing no blame from anyone.” In their commitment to God’s commands, they are not swayed by the criticism, mockery, or pressure of society. This is the divine favour, granted freely to whom He wills. Many scholars see the fulfillment of this prophecy in the companions of the Prophet, in the armies that conquered the apostates under Abu Bakr, or in the subsequent generations of revivalists. The verse is an eternal source of hope, promising that the faith will always have guardians raised by God Himself.
55 | Your only guardians are Allah, His Messenger, and fellow believers— who establish prayer and pay alms-tax with humility. | After the prohibition of taking Jews and Christians as intimate allies and the promise of a new, faithful community, this verse defines the true circle of guardianship and loyalty for the Muslim. The statement “Your only guardians are” is emphatic, restricting the bond of deep, committed alliance to three entities. The first is “Allah.” The ultimate protector, guide, and friend of the believer is the Creator Himself. This is a metaphysical allegiance that reorients the heart away from dependence on any created power. The second is “His Messenger.” Obedience to and love for the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, is the practical channel through which the divine will is known and implemented. The third is “fellow believers,” but this category is immediately qualified by a descriptive clause that identifies the kind of believers worthy of such close alliance: “who establish prayer and pay alms-tax with humility.” The mention of prayer and alms encapsulates the twin duties of the faith: devotion to God and service to humanity. The phrase “with humility” translates the Arabic *wa hum rāki‘ūn*, which literally means “while they are bowing.” This image of paying the alms-tax while in a state of bowing, or being from among those who regularly bow in prayer, signifies that their charity and social responsibility are performed in a state of deep spiritual submission. Some reports in exegesis mention that this verse was revealed when Ali, may God be pleased with him, gave his ring in charity to a beggar while he was bowing in prayer. While many Sunni scholars acknowledge this as a specific instance and a virtue of Ali, they also hold that the verse’s general meaning applies to all believers who combine the ritual devotion of prayer with the social duty of charity, all done in a spirit of humility before God. The verse thus draws a clear boundary: the community of faith is a family of mutual protection and support, defined not by race, tribe, or political party, but by sincere worship and charitable deeds. The true Muslims are guardians of one another under the supreme guardianship of God and His Messenger.
56 | Whoever allies themselves with Allah, His Messenger, and fellow believers, then it is certainly Allah’s party that will prevail. | This verse is a declaration of the ultimate outcome for those who choose their loyalties correctly. It serves as the conclusion and the promise that seals the preceding instructions on guardianship. The phrase “Whoever allies themselves with” uses the same root, *yatawalla*, that appeared in the earlier prohibition of taking Jews and Christians as guardians. Here, the positive alliance is clearly defined: with Allah, with the Messenger, and with the believers. This is the act of entering into the divine camp, of making a conscious choice to place one’s trust, one’s secrets, and one’s security within the fortress of faith. The result of such a choice is stated with absolute certainty: “then it is certainly Allah’s party that will prevail.” The term “Allah’s party,” *hizb Allāh*, is a powerful political and spiritual metaphor. In a tribal society where a person’s entire safety depended on the strength of their clan, the Quran declares that the ultimate party, the one whose victory is guaranteed, is not a tribe of Quraysh or a confederation of Medinan Jews, but the group whose banner is monotheism and whose allegiance is to the Lord of the Worlds. The word “prevail,” *ghālibūn*, indicates a definitive, overcoming victory. This victory can manifest in many forms: sometimes as a worldly political or military triumph, sometimes as the moral and spiritual victory of a martyr whose blood inspires generations, and ultimately as the eternal victory of Paradise. The verse functions as a powerful psychological weapon against the fear that drove the hypocrites to seek false guardians. While they were “racing” to secure their safety with the powerful tribes of the disbelievers, fearing a turn of fortune, the Quran assures the faithful that fortune itself is in the hands of their party’s leader, the Almighty. This is not a call to arrogance, but to a deep, calm confidence. The believer can be poor, outnumbered, and besieged, yet the promise stands: the party of God is the one that will see final success. It is a promise that has echoed through centuries, sustaining every generation of believers facing formidable odds.
57 | O believers! Do not seek the guardianship of those given the Scripture before you and the disbelievers who have made your faith a mockery and amusement. And be mindful of Allah if you are truly believers. | This verse returns to the theme of loyalty, adding a specific category of disbelievers who must be completely excluded from intimate alliance. The address “O believers” frames the command as a test of faith. The prohibition is against seeking the guardianship of two groups: “those given the Scripture before you,” meaning the Jews and Christians, and “the disbelievers,” referring to the polytheists of Arabia. However, these groups are not described by their mere religious identity but by a specific, hostile action: they “have made your faith a mockery and amusement.” This provides the operative reason for the prohibition. It is not a blanket ban on civil interaction with any non-Muslim, but a protective measure against those who actively ridicule and belittle the religion. To take such people as intimate confidants and protectors while they despise the very core of your identity is a form of self-betrayal. It compromises the dignity of the believer and weakens the psychological strength of the community. The verse warns against seeking closeness with those who look down upon your sacred beliefs, as this will gradually erode your own reverence for them. The command is followed by the typical Quranic anchor: “And be mindful of Allah if you are truly believers.” The call to taqwa, God-consciousness, is the internal shield. If a person truly possesses this awareness, they will be naturally repelled by the idea of making a mockery of their own faith by befriending its mockers. The phrase “if you are truly believers” is a gentle but firm challenge. It pushes the listeners to examine the authenticity of their faith, because true iman cannot coexist with a cozy alliance with those who treat the religion as a joke. This verse teaches a crucial lesson about maintaining a community’s moral and psychological boundaries. While Islam commands justice, kindness, and fair dealing with all peaceful people, it also requires a sense of honor and a clear-eyed recognition that deep, trusting alliances cannot be formed with those who hold the believer’s most cherished truths in contempt.
58 | When you call to prayer, they mock it in amusement. This is because they are a people without understanding. | This verse provides a concrete example of the mockery that justifies the prohibition of seeking their guardianship in the previous verse. The specific act highlighted is the response to the call to prayer, the adhan. The adhan is the public proclamation of monotheism, a call that rings out five times a day, declaring “Allah is the Greatest” and “There is no god but Allah.” It is the audible heartbeat of a Muslim community. The verse states that when this call is made, “they mock it in amusement.” The People of the Book and the polytheists would hear the phrases of the adhan and turn them into jokes, mimicking the caller, making snide comments about the words, or ridiculing the very concept of a public call to worship. The act of prayer itself, with its bowing and prostrating, was a source of amusement for those who were steeped in arrogance. The Quran diagnoses the root cause of this behavior: “This is because they are a people without understanding.” The word for “understanding” is *‘aql*, the intellect or the faculty of reason. This is a profound diagnosis. Mockery of divine rituals is not a sign of intellectual sophistication; it is a symptom of a profound cognitive and spiritual failure. A truly intelligent person, upon hearing that God is the Greatest, would pause and reflect on the truth of that statement. To laugh at it is to prove that one’s faculty of reason has been overridden by ego and prejudice. The verse also implicitly consoles the believers. When their most sacred symbols are ridiculed, they might feel a sense of shame or anger. The Quran tells them that the ridicule is not a reflection of a flaw in their rituals but of a defect in the mockers’ intellects. They lack the depth to comprehend the majesty of what is being proclaimed. This teaches the believer to respond to mockery not with a shaken faith but with pity for the one whose understanding is so veiled. It also reinforces the command of the previous verse: it is folly to seek the intimate guardianship of a people whose spiritual blindness leads them to laugh at the very call that leads to eternal success.
59 | Say, O Prophet, “O People of the Book! Do you resent us only because we believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed before—while most of you are rebellious?” | This verse instructs the Prophet to directly address the People of the Book with a probing, soul-searching question that exposes the true, unspoken reason for their hostility. The rhetorical question “Do you resent us only because we believe in Allah and what has been revealed to us and what was revealed before?” cuts to the heart of the matter. The Muslims believed in the God of Abraham, in the Torah, and in the Gospel, affirming the very scriptures the Jews and Christians claimed to follow. They did not come with a new God or a denial of the old prophets. So what could possibly justify the resentment, the hatred, and the plotting against the Muslim community? The phrase “only because” suggests that the Muslims’ comprehensive belief system was, in fact, the sole reason for the animosity. The resentment was not provoked by any material aggression or injustice from the Muslims. It was a pure, ideological envy. The Muslims believed in all the prophets, forming a complete chain of divine messengers, while the Jews rejected Jesus and Muhammad, and the Christians rejected Muhammad. The Muslims’ inclusive faith was a silent judgment on their partial, fragmented belief. The verse then adds a devastating parenthetical observation: “while most of you are rebellious.” The word for rebellious is *fāsiqūn*, those who have stepped outside the bounds of obedience. The question implies that their resentment stems from their rebellion. A rebellious heart cannot stand the sight of a soul at peace in its submission. The pure monotheism and the complete acceptance of all prophets that characterized the Muslim community acted as a mirror, reflecting their own disobedience. This verse is a masterpiece of da‘wah psychology. Instead of responding to hostility with equal hostility, it hands them a question that forces introspection. It challenges them to search their hearts and see that the problem is not with the message of Islam, which is a logical continuation and perfection of their own tradition, but with their own stubborn refusal to submit to the full scope of divine guidance. It leaves the listener with only two options: to admit the irrationality of their resentment and reconsider, or to continue in open rebellion.
60 | Say, O Prophet, “Shall I inform you of those who deserve a worse punishment from Allah than the rebellious? It is those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure—some being reduced to apes and pigs and worshippers of false gods. These are far worse in rank and farther astray from the Right Way.” | This verse is a sharp and intense divine rebuttal that follows the previous question. The Prophet is told to ask, “Shall I inform you of those who deserve a worse punishment from Allah?” This rhetorical setup immediately captures attention, implying that the rebellious People of the Book who resent the believers are not the worst case; there is a category even more deserving of divine wrath. The description that follows is terrifying: “those who earned Allah’s condemnation and displeasure.” This refers to some among the forefathers of the Jews who violated the Sabbath and other divine commands in the most brazen manner. The phrase “some being reduced to apes and pigs” recounts a historical punishment. In Surah Al-Baqarah and Surah Al-A‘raf, the Quran tells the story of a community of Israelites who were commanded not to fish on the Sabbath but were tested by an abundance of fish. They transgressed, and as a punishment, Allah transformed them into apes and pigs, a physical deformation that mirrored their spiritual debasement. This was a specific historical event, not a statement about the intrinsic nature of any contemporary people. The verse also mentions “worshippers of false gods,” meaning those who served the *tāghūt*, the idols and tyrants. The verse then declares, “These are far worse in rank and farther astray from the Right Way.” The rebellious People of the Book who merely resent the believers are on a bad path, but those who merited divine metamorphosis and served idols are in an even more terrible spiritual condition. This serves two functions. First, it is a stark warning to the contemporary opponents that their rebellion, if unchecked, could lead them down a path to an even more severe divine punishment. Second, it consoles the believers by putting the opposition in perspective; the enmity they face is from a people whose ancestors had a history of earning Allah’s condemnation through terrible sins. The ultimate message is a call to fear the God who can punish in ways beyond human imagination, and to repent before the rebellion of the heart leads to a fate far worse than worldly disgrace.
61 | When they come to you ˹believers˺ they say, “We believe.” But they are committed to disbelief when they enter and when they leave. And Allah knows what they hide. | This verse continues the detailed portrait of the hypocrites from among the Jewish tribes in Medina, exposing the profound duplicity that characterised their interaction with the Muslim community. The phrase “When they come to you, believers” describes their physical approach to the gatherings of the Prophet and his companions. Upon entering this sacred space, their tongues utter the words, “We believe.” This is a verbal claim of faith, a public declaration meant to secure the social and political benefits of being considered part of the Muslim ummah. However, the verse immediately shatters this outward profession by revealing their inner state: “But they are committed to disbelief when they enter and when they leave.” The Arabic construction here, using the word *dakhalū*, indicates that they are already immersed in a state of kufr when they arrive, and they remain in that same state when they depart. The encounter with the believers and the hearing of the Quran changes nothing in their hearts. They came as disbelievers and they leave as disbelievers, their outer confession being nothing more than a theatrical performance. This is a terrifying diagnosis of a heart that has become immune to the truth; it can sit in the company of the Prophet, hear the words of God, and still walk away completely unmoved because its inner commitment to rejection is absolute. The verse then seals this exposure with the statement, “And Allah knows what they hide.” This is both a comfort to the believers, who might be deceived by the hypocrites’ apparent friendliness, and a dire warning to the hypocrites themselves. They may conceal their mockery, their secret alliances with the enemies of Islam, and their true theological positions, but the One before whom all secrets are laid bare is fully aware. This knowledge is not passive; it is the prelude to a just reckoning. The lesson for all believers is the immense value of sincerity, ikhlas. A person can be physically present in the most blessed gatherings, yet gain nothing because the heart is absent or, worse, hostile. The verse prompts a regular self-examination: when I enter the company of the faithful or the house of God, and when I leave, has my state of faith been increased, or have I merely performed a social ritual while my heart remained locked in its own world of disobedience and doubt?
62 | You see many of them racing towards sin, transgression, and consumption of forbidden gain. Evil indeed are their actions! | After exposing the hidden disbelief of the hypocrites, this verse turns to their visible, outward behaviour, which provides the evidence of their inner corruption. The Prophet is addressed, and through him every observer: “You see many of them racing towards sin, transgression, and consumption of forbidden gain.” The verb “racing” is highly significant. It indicates not a reluctant fall into error, but an eager, competitive haste to commit wrongdoing. They are not merely sinners; they are athletes of sin, striving to outdo one another in wickedness. The three categories of their vice are then listed. “Sin” here is *ithm*, the general category of any deed that violates divine law and harms the soul. “Transgression” is *‘udwān*, the act of overstepping limits, of being aggressive and unjust towards others, a direct violation of the command to cooperate in goodness and not in sin. The third and most specific is “consumption of forbidden gain,” translating *suht*, which, as explained earlier in the surah, refers primarily to bribery, usury, and any wealth acquired through utterly corrupt means, such as taking money to alter a judgment. This greed was a hallmark of the leadership that had lost all fear of God. The verse ends with the divine verdict: “Evil indeed are their actions!” This is not a human judgment but God’s own assessment of their deeds. It teaches the believer to develop a moral clarity that is not swayed by the apparent worldliness or sophistication of such people. Their cleverness, their wealth amassed through suht, and their political manoeuvring are all, in the final analysis, nothing but evil. The verse also serves as a warning about the social environment. When a community sees its members racing towards sin and no one stops them, it is a sign of deep sickness. It calls the believers to be a people who race towards forgiveness and good deeds, as described elsewhere in the Quran, rather than towards the things that earn divine condemnation. The visible conduct of a person or a group is a reliable indicator of the state of their heart, and the actions of these hypocrites, a frantic scramble for sinful profit and bribery, revealed the emptiness of their claim to faith.
63 | Why do their rabbis and scholars not forbid them from saying what is sinful and consuming what is unlawful? Evil indeed is their inaction! | The Quran now turns its scrutiny from the sinful masses to the religious leadership, exposing a failure that is even more catastrophic than the sins themselves. The question is a sharp rebuke: “Why do their rabbis and scholars not forbid them?” The rabbis, *rabbāniyyūn*, and the scholars, *aḥbār*, were the custodians of the Torah, the spiritual guides entrusted with teaching and correcting the community. They possessed the knowledge of what God had prohibited. They could see their people racing towards sinful speech, such as mocking the Prophet and distorting scripture, and towards suht, the consumption of bribery and usury. Yet they remained silent. The verse demands an answer for this silence. This is a foundational principle in Islam: the obligation of enjoining good and forbidding evil, *al-amr bil-ma‘rūf wa-n-nahy ‘ani-l-munkar*, is not merely an individual virtue but a collective responsibility. When those with knowledge and authority fail to speak out against clear violations of divine law, they become complicit in the sin. Their silence is not neutral; it is a form of approval that allows evil to spread and corrupt the entire society. The verse then pronounces a separate condemnation specifically on their inaction: “Evil indeed is their inaction!” The Arabic word used is *mā kānū yaṣna‘ūn*, which refers to their habitual practice, their “crafting” of deeds. Their inaction is their main product, their defining work, and it is declared to be evil. A hadith of the Prophet, peace be upon him, powerfully illustrates this principle: “When people see an oppressor and do not restrain him, it is likely that Allah will punish them all with a widespread punishment.” The failure of the scholars is often the first step towards the downfall of a nation. This verse is a direct address to every Muslim scholar, teacher, and community leader. It is a warning that knowledge is a trust, and the one who sees a sin being committed openly and does not raise a voice of correction out of fear, laziness, or a desire to maintain worldly status, has betrayed that trust. The silence of the learned is the funeral prayer of a righteous society.
64 | ˹Some among˺ the Jews said, “Allah is tight-fisted.” May their fists be tied and they be condemned for what they said. Rather, He is open-handed, giving freely as He pleases. That which has been revealed to you ˹O Prophet˺ from your Lord will only cause many of them to increase in wickedness and disbelief. The historical context, as reported by commentators, is that some Jewish leaders, when the verse “Who will lend Allah a good loan?” was revealed, mockingly said, “Allah is poor and we are rich,” or, as this verse states, “Allah is tight-fisted,” implying that God withholds provision from them, and thus they cannot be expected to spend in charity. The immediate divine reaction is a curse: “May their fists be tied and they be condemned for what they said.” The punishment mirrors the sin; because they attributed stinginess to the Most Generous, their own hands are symbolically tied in miserliness and they are distanced from God’s mercy. The verse then corrects the blasphemy with the beautiful truth: “Rather, He is open-handed, giving freely as He pleases.” The Arabic term *mabsūṭatān*, translated as “open-handed,” literally means “spread out,” a vivid image of unrestricted, abundant giving without limit. The verse then delivers a tragic prophecy about the effect of the Quran on such people: “That which has been revealed to you from your Lord will only cause many of them to increase in wickedness and disbelief.” The same revelation that heals and guides the sincere heart becomes a poison that increases the disease in a jealous, arrogant heart. Next, the verse proclaims a divinely imposed social curse: “We have stirred among them hostility and hatred until the Day of Judgment.” Because they rejected the unifying truth, they were left to the chaos of their own tribal and sectarian divisions, a state of perpetual internal conflict. The verse then gives a powerful assurance of divine protection against their military schemes: “Whenever they kindle the fire of war, Allah puts it out.” Every plot and alliance against the Prophet was foiled by God’s intervention. The final note condemns their ultimate activity: “they strive to spread corruption in the land. And Allah does not like corruptors.” The verse thus offers a complete cycle of their sin, punishment, God’s nature, the paradoxical effect of truth, and the ultimate failure of their conspiracies.
65 | Had the People of the Book only been faithful and mindful ˹of Allah˺, We would have certainly absolved them of their sins and admitted them into the Gardens of Bliss. | This short but profoundly merciful verse opens a window of hope immediately after the severe condemnations of blasphemy and corruption. It presents a conditional statement that reveals the heart of divine mercy and the true purpose of God’s dealings with humanity. The condition is placed on “the People of the Book,” the Jews and Christians, the very groups whose leadership had just been described with such dark imagery. They are not eternally condemned by race; their fate is tied to their response. The condition is that they become “faithful and mindful of Allah.” The word “faithful,” *āmanū*, means to embrace the full faith, which after the coming of the Prophet Muhammad necessarily includes believing in him and the Quran. The phrase “mindful of Allah” translates *ittaqaw*, meaning they would live with a constant, active consciousness of God, avoiding His prohibitions and fulfilling His commands. Had they done this, two magnificent divine acts would have followed. First, “We would have certainly absolved them of their sins.” The word *kaffarnā* means to cover, blot out, and expiate completely. Every sin from their past, including the blasphemies and the distortions, would have been erased as if they had never existed. This is the tremendous promise of forgiveness that awaits every sincere repentant, regardless of the depth of their prior transgression. Second, “and admitted them into the Gardens of Bliss.” The Gardens are not merely ordinary paradises; they are *Jannāt an-Na‘īm*, the Gardens of pure, untainted delight and ease, the highest levels of eternal pleasure where every desire is fulfilled and every sorrow is absent. This verse thus demonstrates that God’s door is never shut. The very people whose beliefs and actions have been scrutinised and condemned are simultaneously being invited to a complete transformation that would change their eternal destiny from the Fire to the highest Bliss. It is a profound lesson in balancing fear with hope: the warnings are severe because the danger is real, but the invitation remains open because the Creator is, above all, the All-Forgiving and Most Merciful. No past is too stained to be purified by a sincere return.
66 | And had they observed the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord, they would have been overwhelmed with provisions from above and below. Some among them are upright, yet many do nothing but evil. | This verse extends the conditional promise of the previous one, moving from the spiritual reward of forgiveness and Paradise to the tangible, worldly consequences of true adherence to divine guidance. The condition now is specifically about the scriptures: “had they observed the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord.” The “what has been revealed to them” is understood as the Quran, which is addressed to all humanity and is the final fulfillment of the scriptural promise. True observance is not merely a ritualistic reading but a comprehensive implementation of the guidance contained in these books, which would naturally lead the sincere follower to recognise and accept the final message. The result of this observance is described in breathtaking terms: “they would have been overwhelmed with provisions from above and below.” The phrase “from above” refers to the blessings of the sky: abundant rain in its proper seasons, spiritual illumination, and the descent of divine mercy. “From below” refers to the blessings of the earth: fertile soil, abundant crops, mineral wealth, and general economic prosperity. This teaches a direct correlation between a society’s collective righteousness and its material well-being. When a people live by God’s law, the natural world, which is also subject to God’s command, cooperates with them. This is not a “prosperity gospel” in the simplistic sense, but a divine principle repeatedly affirmed in the Quran. However, the verse immediately balances this ideal picture with a realistic assessment of the present reality: “Some among them are upright.” This is a crucial recognition that generalisation is not absolute. Among the People of the Book, there was a minority that genuinely sought truth, who were just and humble, like Abdullah ibn Salam and the Negus of Abyssinia. But then the verse concludes with the sad reality: “yet many do nothing but evil.” The majority, due to their rejection and corruption, had made evil their only occupation. This verse teaches the Muslim community the vital link between obedience and provision, and it also commands fairness in judgment, acknowledging the righteous minority while not being naive about the corrupted state of the majority.
67 | O Messenger! Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord. If you do not, then you have not delivered His message. Allah will certainly protect you from the people. Indeed, Allah does not guide the people who disbelieve. | This verse is a direct, powerful mandate to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, and through him to every caller to truth until the end of time. The address “O Messenger” singles him out for the weightiest of tasks. The command is absolute: “Convey everything revealed to you from your Lord.” The Arabic term *balligh* means to deliver completely, without omission, distortion, or concealment. The Prophet is not allowed to hide any part of the revelation, even if that part might provoke the anger, mockery, or opposition of the powerful. Some reports suggest that the Prophet, aware of the intense hostility from the Jews and hypocrites as detailed in the preceding verses, might have felt a natural human hesitation to deliver certain rulings that would be particularly difficult for them to hear, such as the exposure of their scriptural distortions or the condemnation of their theological errors. This verse orders him to suppress that hesitation entirely. The severity of the obligation is then emphasised with a striking statement: “If you do not, then you have not delivered His message.” This does not suggest that the Prophet ever failed in his duty, but it underscores the principle that the messenger’s entire mission hinges on complete, fearless proclamation. A partial delivery is a nullification of the prophethood itself. Immediately following this command, a divine guarantee of protection is given: “Allah will certainly protect you from the people.” This was a specific promise to the Prophet that he would not be assassinated before his mission was complete. After this verse was revealed, it is reported that the Prophet dismissed his personal guards, saying, “Leave me, for my Lord has protected me.” This assurance is for every dai‘i: the one who delivers the truth faithfully is under a divine shield, and no harm can befall them except what Allah permits for a greater good. The verse concludes, “Indeed, Allah does not guide the people who disbelieve,” meaning that the Prophet’s job is to convey, not to convert. The hearts that remain in disbelief despite the clear message are beyond his responsibility; their guidance is a matter for God alone.
68 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “O People of the Book! You have nothing to stand on unless you observe the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to you from your Lord.” And your Lord’s revelation to you ˹O Prophet˺ will only cause many of them to increase in wickedness and disbelief. To claim adherence to Moses and Jesus while rejecting Muhammad is a self-contradiction, because Moses and Jesus, in their authentic teachings, commanded the worship of one God and foretold the seal of the prophets. Thus, their very salvation, according to their own books, depends on their acceptance of the Quran. The verse then revisits the tragic reality of their response: “And your Lord’s revelation to you, O Prophet, will only cause many of them to increase in wickedness and disbelief.” The very truth that should have been their salvation becomes a means of their spiritual destruction because they meet it with envy and arrogance instead of humility. As in verse 64, this is a law of spiritual psychology: the same sun that softens wax hardens clay. Finally, the Prophet is consoled, “So do not grieve for the people who disbelieve.” This is a direct echo of earlier consolations in the surah. The Prophet’s heart was heavy with the desire for their guidance. The command “do not grieve” is a liberation from an emotional burden that is not his to carry. He has delivered the message, established the clear proof, and the consequences of rejection are now between them and their Creator. This is a lesson for every believer who feels sorrow over the misguidance of loved ones: the grief must not become a paralysing despair, and the responsibility ends where the clear delivery of truth ends.
69 | Indeed, the believers, Jews, Sabians and Christians—whoever ˹truly˺ believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good, there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve. | This is one of the most famous yet often misunderstood verses of the Quran, and it must be understood within its clear context in Al-Ma'idah. The verse lists four categories: “the believers,” meaning the Muslims; “Jews”; “Sabians,” a group often described as monotheistic followers of a prophetic tradition prior to Islam; and “Christians.” The key to unlocking the verse is the conditional clause that follows: “whoever truly believes in Allah and the Last Day and does good.” The verse does not say that anyone who calls themselves a Jew or a Christian is automatically saved. It says that among all these groups, those individuals who possess true, authentic faith in God and the Last Day, combined with righteous deeds, are the ones who will be saved. Before the coming of the Prophet Muhammad, a Jew who sincerely followed the uncorrupted Torah, or a Christian who sincerely followed the authentic Gospel that preached pure monotheism, would be on the path of salvation. However, after the advent of the final Prophet, the very definition of “truly believes in Allah” must include belief in all His messengers, including Muhammad, peace be upon him. A person today who is aware of the Prophet’s message and rejects him cannot be described as having true faith in God, because they have rejected the very messenger and book that God sent to guide them. The great companion Ibn Abbas clarified that when Surah Al-Baqarah’s similar verse was revealed, it was later explained and contextualised by verses like “Whoever seeks a religion other than Islam, it will never be accepted from him.” This verse, therefore, is not a charter for religious pluralism where all paths are equally valid. It is a profound statement of divine justice: God judges individuals according to their sincere response to the truth that reached them. The reward for such sincere believers, from any age, is complete peace: “no fear for them, nor will they grieve.” They will be spared the terror of the Day of Judgment and the sorrow of regret. The verse is a call to all humanity that the essential formula for salvation is unwavering monotheism, eschatological accountability, and virtuous action, a formula that is completely and perfectly embodied in Islam.
70 | Indeed, We took a covenant from the Children of Israel and sent them messengers. Whenever a messenger came to them with what they did not desire, they denied some and killed others. | After the universal statement of salvation’s conditions, the Quran returns to the historical narrative of the Children of Israel to provide a concrete example of a people who broke the covenant and failed the test of responding to divine truth. The verse begins with the reminder, “Indeed, We took a covenant from the Children of Israel and sent them messengers.” This covenant was the solemn pledge to worship God alone, obey His law, and follow the prophets He would send. God, in His mercy, did not leave them with just a book; He continuously sent a stream of messengers to revive their faith, clarify their law, and warn them against deviation. However, the response to these divine favours is tragic: “Whenever a messenger came to them with what they did not desire, they denied some and killed others.” The phrase “with what they did not desire” is the psychological key to their rebellion. The prophets did not come to affirm their whims; they came with the truth, which often clashed with their material interests, their pride, and their deeply ingrained habits. When the message contradicted their desires, instead of subjugating their egos to the truth, they judged the truth by their egos. The result was a bifurcated atrocity. “They denied some” refers to those prophets they rejected as liars, refusing to accept their prophethood. “And killed others” is the chilling escalation. They murdered prophets like Zechariah and John the Baptist, and they attempted to kill Jesus. This is the ultimate crime against divine guidance. The verse is a warning to the Muslim community not to repeat this pattern. The Quran and the Sunnah will also bring teachings that challenge the desires of the lower self. The true believer is the one who submits when the revelation comes, even if it is difficult, while the loser follows the path of the Israelites, accepting only what suits their convenience and rejecting, or even violently opposing, what does not. It is a lesson that the criterion of truth is not its alignment with human desire, but its divine origin.

This punishment can be understood as both worldly chastisement and the eternal punishment of the Hereafter. This verse, combined with the previous one, confronts the two primary theological pillars of historical Christian orthodoxy. It demonstrates that the Islamic critique is not a superficial polemic but a direct engagement with specific, defined doctrines. The lesson for the Muslim is the absolute importance of preserving the purity of tawḥīd in one’s belief and language, always resisting any subtle blurring of the line between the Creator and the most honoured of His creations.

The rhetorical question, “Will they not turn to Allah in repentance and seek His forgiveness?” is an expression of divine longing for the return of the sinners. The name Ar-Raḥīm means His mercy is perpetual and flowing, enveloping the repentant sinner with love and care, not just a cold legal pardon. This verse is a masterpiece of da‘wah. It follows the Quranic method of using the threat of punishment to break the ego’s resistance, and then immediately applying the balm of mercy to attract the now-softened heart. It shows that no human being, no matter how deeply entrenched in false theology, is beyond the reach of divine forgiveness if they take the step of sincere repentance. For the Muslim reader, it cultivates a hopeful outlook towards all people, reminding them that the greatest sinners of yesterday can become the greatest saints of tomorrow through the miracle of tawbah.

The question “How can you worship besides Allah those who can neither harm nor benefit you?” is a challenge to the very purpose of worship. Worship, at its core, is an act of seeking benefit and protection from harm. A rational person directs their worship, their ultimate hope and fear, only towards a being that possesses the power to grant benefit and avert harm. The idols worshipped by the pagans, and the human beings like Jesus and Mary worshipped by the Christians, in their own earthly existence could not control their own destiny. Jesus could not prevent the plot of the Jews who sought to crucify him, nor could he provide sustenance for himself without God’s will. So why would a human being devote their ultimate devotion to another creature who is, in terms of independent power, completely impotent? The second half of the verse provides the positive alternative, highlighting the attributes that make God alone worthy of worship: “And Allah alone is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” The name “All-Hearing” means He hears every prayer, every cry for help, every whispered secret, no matter the language or the number of simultaneous supplicants. The idols and the saints in their graves hear nothing. The name “All-Knowing” means He is fully aware of every need, every pain, and every intention, even before it is expressed. If they do not stop saying this, those who disbelieve among them will be afflicted with a painful punishment. | Continuing the refutation of Christian theology, this verse targets the second major doctrinal error: the concept of the Trinity. The phrase “Allah is one in a Trinity” captures the contradictory essence of the doctrine as it is often professed, the belief in three co-equal persons comprising one God. The Quran’s verdict is the same as before: they “have certainly fallen into disbelief.” The grammar here, “one of three” or “a third of three,” points to the specific formula of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and the idea that Jesus is God or part of the Godhead. The counter-argument is presented with absolute clarity in the very next sentence: “There is only One God.” This is the entire message of all prophets in one short sentence. The Arabic *ilāhun wāḥid* emphasizes absolute oneness, a being who is indivisible, without partner, without component parts, and without equal. The Trinitarian concept, no matter how philosophically sophisticated its explanations attempt to be, fundamentally contradicts this unyielding monotheism. It divides the divine essence in a way that revelation never sanctioned. The verse then delivers a stark ultimatum and a conditional warning: “If they do not stop saying this, those who disbelieve among them will be afflicted with a painful punishment.” The door of repentance is still open. The phrase “if they do not stop” shows that God is giving them a chance to recant their blasphemous statement. The warning of a painful punishment refers to both the worldly consequences of their deviation and the eternal torment of the Hereafter. The qualifying phrase “those who disbelieve among them” is significant. It acknowledges the diversity within the Christian community; there were, and are, some who do not hold the extreme Trinitarian view that compromises pure monotheism, like the Arians or the early Jewish Christian sects. The punishment is specifically for those who, after the truth is made clear, persist in this declaration that elevates a prophet to divinity. The verse is a call to intellectual honesty and a return to the simple, uncompromising worship of the one true God.
74 | Will they not turn to Allah in repentance and seek His forgiveness? And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | After the severe warning of a painful punishment for the Trinitarian belief, the Quran immediately, in its characteristic style of balancing warning with mercy, opens the widest possible door for redemption. The verse is a rhetorical question infused with divine longing for the repentance of His creation: “Will they not turn to Allah in repentance and seek His forgiveness?” The question implies astonishment at their hesitation. What logical reason could there be to delay turning back to God when the punishment is so terrible and the offer of forgiveness is so immense? The word “turn” is the essence of *tawbah*, a complete pivot of the soul away from the wrong belief and back to its Creator. It is not merely a verbal admission of error but a profound change in the heart and mind, leading to a declaration of pure monotheism. “Seek His forgiveness” emphasizes the active pleading for the specific sin of shirk to be pardoned. The verse then anchors this call in two of God’s most beautiful names, which serve as the guarantee of the promise: “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The name *Ghafūr* indicates that He covers and wipes away the sin completely, even the sin of equating a partner with Him. The name *Raḥīm* indicates that He envelops the repentant sinner with His special, sustaining mercy that grants them a place in His love. This verse single-handedly shatters the pessimistic notion that a person who has fallen into theologically mistaken beliefs about God is forever doomed. It directly addresses the Christians of that time, and by extension all who have deviated into Trinitarian or other non-monotheistic beliefs, with a clear message: the way back is open, and the One you have wronged by your misrepresentation is precisely the One who will eagerly forgive you if you come back. The only condition is genuine, sincere repentance before the moment of death. The verse is a shining beacon of hope and a powerful tool of dawah, inviting people to leave behind complicated, inherited dogmas for the simple, merciful embrace of the One God.
75 | The Messiah, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger. ˹Many˺ messengers had ˹come and˺ gone before him. His mother was a woman of truth. They both ate food. See how We make the signs clear to them, yet see how they are deluded ˹from the truth˺! | After inviting them to repentance, the Quran dismantles the theological basis for the divinity of Jesus by presenting a simple, human portrait of him and his mother. The verse begins with the definitive Islamic statement on Jesus: “The Messiah, son of Mary, was no more than a messenger.” The phrase “no more than a messenger” is not a demotion but a precise placement. Being a messenger of God is the highest possible human rank, but it is a rank of servitude, not lordship. The verse immediately adds a historical fact to shatter any claim of uniqueness that could justify divinity: “Many messengers had come and gone before him.” Jesus was not the first, nor was he the last before Muhammad. He stands in a long, illustrious line of mortal human beings chosen to deliver a message. If prophethood implied divinity, then Adam, Noah, Abraham, and Moses would all have a claim, yet no one argues for their divinity. The verse then mentions his mother, Mary, and makes a point about both of them: “His mother was a woman of truth. They both ate food.” Mary is honored with the beautiful title “a woman of truth,” *ṣiddīqah*, signifying her complete sincerity and faith. She was a righteous human, not a goddess. The most devastatingly simple argument follows: “They both ate food.” Eating food is a sign of human need, a dependence on external sustenance to survive. The One who is truly God is Al-Samad, the Self-Sufficient, who neither eats nor drinks nor needs anything. The fact that Jesus required food, digested it, and was sustained by it is the clearest possible proof of his pure, dependent humanity. The verse concludes with a note of divine exasperation and amazement: “See how We make the signs clear to them, yet see how they are deluded from the truth!” The signs are rationally clear, logically laid out, and simple enough for a child to understand, yet the human mind, when tangled in inherited dogma, can be utterly deluded, turning away from the obvious truth.
76 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “How can you worship besides Allah those who can neither harm nor benefit you? And Allah ˹alone˺ is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” | This verse is a direct logical challenge to the idolaters and those who deify saints, prophets, or angels, presenting the pure rational basis of Islamic monotheism. The Prophet is instructed to ask a question that appeals to the most basic human instinct of self-preservation and benefit-seeking: “How can you worship besides Allah those who can neither harm nor benefit you?” The question exposes the absurdity of worshiping any entity other than the Creator. All worship, at its core, is an act of seeking benefit or warding off harm. The idols made of stone, the saints in their graves, and even the mightiest of prophets, including Jesus, are completely powerless to affect the universe in any way. Jesus himself, as the Quran states, could perform miracles only “by My Will,” as Allah clearly said. In his own human capacity, he possessed no independent power to hurt or help anyone, let alone the one who prays to him. If the object of worship cannot control the smallest atom of harm or benefit, then worshiping it is an irrational, self-defeating act. The contrast is then immediately drawn: “And Allah alone is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.” The two divine attributes chosen here are perfectly relevant to worship. He is the All-Hearing, so He hears every whispered supplication, every cry of distress, and every silent prayer of the heart. He is not a distant, deaf idol. He is the All-Knowing, so He knows exactly what you need, what will truly benefit you, and what harm you need to be protected from, even before you ask. By pairing the powerlessness of the false gods with the perfect knowledge and responsiveness of the true God, the verse provides a complete rational framework for why worship must be directed solely to the One who can actually hear and respond. It is a call to intellectual integrity in one's spiritual life.
77 | Say, “O People of the Book! Do not go to extremes in your faith beyond the truth, nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before ˹you˺. They misled many and strayed from the Right Way.” | This verse is a direct and loving warning to the Christians and Jews, encapsulating the central spiritual disease that led them away from pure monotheism. The command “Do not go to extremes in your faith beyond the truth” uses the word *taghlū*, which means to exceed the proper bounds, to be immoderate. In a religious context, it refers to exaggerating the status of a prophet or a saint. The Christians went to extremes by elevating Jesus, a blessed human messenger, to the status of God. The Jews went to extremes in the opposite direction, in their harshness and their rejection of Jesus and Muhammad. Both are deviations from the balanced truth that accords each being its proper place. The “truth” is the simple, unexaggerated reality: God is One, and the prophets are His honored servants. To go “beyond the truth” is to enter the realm of falsehood, no matter how pious the intention seems. The verse then warns against the root cause of this extremism: “nor follow the vain desires of those who went astray before you.” The “vain desires” are the speculative theological whims that had no basis in the authentic revelations. These included the Gnostic ideas, the pagan concepts of divine sonship, and the philosophical corruptions that infiltrated early Christian thought from the surrounding cultures. The people who introduced these ideas are described as those who “went astray before,” referring to the earlier generations of deviant thinkers whose names they may not even know, yet whose legacy of error they have blindly inherited. The result of following such desires and going to extremes is stated as a historical fact: “They misled many and strayed from the Right Way.” The path of extremism is not a victimless deviation; it leads the self astray and drags multitudes of others into the same darkness, all under the banner of a religion that was originally pure. The verse is a timeless call for the Muslims as well, who are warned against the same trap of exaggerating the status of the Prophet Muhammad or the righteous saints, a tendency that some sects have tragically fallen into, deviating from the pure, balanced monotheism of the Quran.
78 | The disbelievers among the Children of Israel were condemned in the revelations of David and Jesus, son of Mary. That was for their disobedience and violations. | The surah now connects the condemnation of the Israelites to the scriptures of their own most revered prophets, David and Jesus, demonstrating that the Quran’s critique is not a foreign assault but a confirmation of their own prophetic tradition. The verse states, “The disbelievers among the Children of Israel were condemned.” The qualification “among” is important, as it excludes the righteous minority. The condemnation is a divine curse, a being expelled from divine mercy. The astonishing point is that this curse was not first pronounced in the Quran; it was already “in the revelations of David and Jesus, son of Mary.” The Psalms of David, the Zabur, are filled with laments about the wickedness of the Israelites, their corruption of justice, and their straying from God’s path. The Psalms speak of the rebellious who do not know God and who eat up God’s people as they eat bread. Similarly, the Gospels depict Jesus delivering some of his harshest rebukes against the Pharisees and the religious elite of the Jews, calling them a brood of vipers, children of the devil, and whitened sepulchers. The Quran is merely restating a judgment that their own beloved prophets had already issued. The reason for this ancient and enduring condemnation is then given: “That was for their disobedience and violations.” Disobedience refers to their persistent refusal to follow the commands, and violations refer to their active transgression of the sacred boundaries, especially the Sabbath-breaking and the killing of prophets. This verse serves a powerful rhetorical purpose: it dismantles the claim that Muhammad was unfairly targeting the Jews. It shows that the prophetic tradition itself is a unified chain of moral accountability, and that the same Jews who revered David and Jesus were, in fact, the descendants of the very people those prophets condemned. It forces the listener to confront the uncomfortable continuity of their communal choices.
79 | They did not forbid one another from doing evil. Evil indeed was what they did! | This verse isolates one specific, catastrophic social sin that defined the condemned community and sealed their fate: the collapse of mutual moral responsibility. “They did not forbid one another from doing evil” is a diagnosis of a community that has lost its collective conscience. The verse refers to the practice of the Israelites, especially during the times of the later prophets, where sin was rampant and visible, yet those who were righteous or in positions of influence remained silent. This is the same critique leveled earlier in the surah at the rabbis and scholars who failed to forbid sin and the consumption of unlawful wealth. Here, it is expanded to the entire community. The act of commanding good and forbidding evil, *amr bil-ma‘ruf wa nahy ‘anil-munkar*, is an essential pillar of a healthy Ummah. When a society abandons this duty, sin normalizes. The first generation commits a sin with a sense of guilt; the next generation sees it with indifference; the third generation defends it as a cultural norm. The Israelites had reached the stage where they would witness their fellow tribesmen violating the Sabbath, engaging in usury, and distorting the law, and they would simply watch, or perhaps gently complain in private, but never take a public stand to forbid the action. The divine verdict on this passive community is devastating in its simplicity: “Evil indeed was what they did!” The word “evil” condemns not just the sins being committed, but the very act of their silence. Their inaction is itself classified as a foul deed. This verse is a dire warning to the Muslim Ummah. A community that loses the courage to speak against public sin, that prioritizes personal comfort over communal correction, is a community that has internalized the same fatal disease that brought down the Children of Israel. The believer is not an island; silence in the face of evil is complicity that invites the wrath of God upon the whole society.
80 | You see many of them taking the disbelievers as allies. Truly wicked are their misdeeds, which have earned them Allah’s wrath. And they will be in everlasting torment. | The verse returns to the theme of political and spiritual alliances, providing a final damning observation of the behavior of some among the People of the Book, specifically the Jewish tribes of Medina. “You see many of them taking the disbelievers as allies.” Here, the “disbelievers” refer to the pagan idolaters of the Quraysh and other Arab tribes who were actively at war with the Muslim state. The Jewish tribes, who possessed a scripture and should have been the natural allies of a monotheistic prophet, instead formed political and military pacts with the polytheists, who were the most ardent enemies of monotheism. They chose the worshippers of idols over the worshippers of the One God. The reason was purely tribal and materialistic: they hated the Prophet for challenging their local authority and for not being from the Jewish lineage, so they allied with any party that promised them a share of the spoils if the Muslims were defeated. The verse pronounces a severe judgment on this choice: “Truly wicked are their misdeeds.” This is the evil, the *bi’sa*, of their souls made manifest in their foreign policy. The word “misdeeds” refers to these alliances of betrayal. The consequence of these misdeeds is twofold. First, they are the actions “which have earned them Allah’s wrath.” The divine anger is not an arbitrary emotion but the just response of a holy God to those who, despite knowledge, actively side with falsehood against truth. They purchased the temporary friendship of the pagans with the permanent enmity of God. The second, ultimate consequence is stated as a matter of fact: “And they will be in everlasting torment.” This is the final destination of those who die upon this path. The phrase “everlasting torment” leaves no room for the false hope that the punishment of Hell is a temporary purgatorial state for such people. The verse serves as a powerful political theology lesson for the Muslims: the bond of faith is the only true foundation for a deep alliance. To side with the enemies of God against the community of believers, out of ethnic loyalty or worldly gain, is to share in their destiny of divine wrath and eternal ruin.
81 | Had they believed in Allah, the Prophet, and what has been revealed to him, they would have never taken those ˹pagans˺ as allies. But most of them are rebellious. | This verse delivers the definitive diagnosis of the political betrayal described in the previous verse, linking the alliance with disbelievers directly to a failure of faith. The hypothetical structure “Had they believed in Allah, the Prophet, and what has been revealed to him” sets up the complete, integrated belief that is the hallmark of a true Muslim. The verse mentions three interconnected objects of faith: Allah, the Prophet Muhammad, and the Quran. Belief in the Prophet is inseparable from belief in God, because he is the one who conveys God’s message. The phrase “what has been revealed to him” refers to the Quran, the final and preserved scripture. This comprehensive belief would have acted as an unbreachable barrier against forming protective alliances with the polytheist enemies of Islam. A heart that genuinely believes that Allah is the All-Powerful and that the Prophet is His true messenger would not place its ultimate trust and strategic partnership in the hands of those who are actively fighting against that God and that messenger. To do so is a practical denial of the faith one claims to profess. The verse states categorically, “they would have never taken those pagans as allies.” The word “never” conveys an absolute incompatibility. True iman and a reliance on the enemies of the faith cannot coexist in the same heart. The verse then concludes with the realistic assessment, “But most of them are rebellious.” The word “rebellious” translates *fāsiqūn*, those who have stepped outside the bounds of obedience and faith. This indicates that their problem is not a lack of information or a nuanced political calculus; it is a deep-seated moral and spiritual rebellion against the truth they recognize. For the Muslim community, this verse establishes a timeless principle: the quality of one’s faith is directly reflected in one’s political and social allegiances. A weakening of trust in God and His Messenger inevitably leads to a search for security from worldly powers, even those that oppose the faith. The verse calls for an alignment where the heart’s belief and the public alliance are in complete harmony under the banner of divine loyalty.
82 | You will surely find the most bitter towards the believers to be the Jews and polytheists and the most gracious to be those who call themselves Christian. That is because there are priests and monks among them and because they are not arrogant. | This verse presents a fascinating sociological observation and a divine prediction about the relative attitudes of different religious groups towards the early Muslim community. The Prophet is told, “You will surely find the most bitter towards the believers to be the Jews and polytheists.” The Jews, many of whom possessed knowledge of scripture and had expectations of a final prophet, were often the most virulently opposed when that prophet came from the line of Ishmael rather than Isaac. Their enmity was driven by envy, a sense of being supplanted, and a rigid ethnic pride. The polytheists, who had no scriptural background, opposed the Muslims because Islam shattered their ancestral idolatry and their social power structure built around the Kaaba. In stark contrast, the verse states, “and the most gracious to be those who call themselves Christian.” The word “gracious” translates *mawaddah*, meaning affectionate love and warm friendship. The early Muslims experienced this directly. When the companions were persecuted in Mecca, the Prophet sent a group of them to seek refuge with the Christian Negus of Abyssinia. The Negus received them with honour, wept when he heard the Quranic description of Jesus, and provided them with safety and protection. This act of Christian hospitality became a living proof of this verse. The verse then provides the reasons for this graciousness: “That is because there are priests and monks among them and because they are not arrogant.” The presence of ascetics, devoted worshippers, and learned men who had dedicated their lives to God cultivated a spirit of humility in some of them. This humility made them more receptive to recognizing truth and respecting the devotion of others. The verse does not blanket-endorse Christian theology, which is refuted elsewhere, but acknowledges a praiseworthy quality: a lack of arrogance that softens the heart. It is a profound lesson that humility can be a bridge to recognizing truth, while envy and pride, as seen in some Jews and polytheists, create an impenetrable wall of bitterness.

The act of listening is not passive; it is an attentive, open-hearted receiving of the divine message. The effect is immediate and dramatic: “you see their eyes overflowing with tears.” The phrase “overflowing” translates *tafīḍu*, indicating a welling up of tears that cannot be contained, streaming down their faces. This is not a calculated emotional display but an involuntary physical response of a soul touched by truth. The cause of their tears is identified as “recognizing the truth.” This is the key; their hearts are not encountering a foreign message but a familiar one, the same pure monotheism that Jesus preached. They see the Quran as the fulfillment and clarification of their own tradition, not its negation. Their emotional response is then paired with a verbal declaration: “They say, ‘Our Lord! We believe, so count us among the witnesses.’” The direct address to “Our Lord” shows an immediate, intimate turning to God. Their declaration of faith is not just an intellectual assent but a prayer for inclusion. The “witnesses” are those who testify to the truth, who bear witness that God is One and that Muhammad is His messenger. This verse was revealed, according to many commentators, in connection with the arrival of a delegation of Christians from Abyssinia or Syria who listened to the Prophet recite and were so moved that they immediately embraced Islam. The scene serves as a timeless testament to the power of the Quran to penetrate a sincere heart, a heart that is not hardened by arrogance or political interest. It also provides a powerful model of dawah: presenting the Quran with sincerity can unlock a response that no amount of argumentation can achieve.

And we long for our Lord to include us in the company of the righteous.” | This verse continues the speech of the tearful Christians, providing the logical and emotional reasoning behind their immediate acceptance of Islam. The rhetorical question, “Why should we not believe in Allah and the truth that has come to us?” is a beautiful expression of the natural and inevitable response of a sound intellect and a pure heart to divine truth. For them, the barriers that kept others from believing—arrogance, tribal pride, material interest—simply do not exist. The truth has presented itself clearly, and the only rational, natural thing to do is to accept it. They see no valid reason to hesitate, to negotiate, or to delay. This phrase is also a subtle critique of those among the People of the Book who, seeing the same truth, turned away. The implication is clear: once the truth is recognized, unbelief becomes an irrational, unjustifiable act. The second part of their statement reveals a profound spiritual ambition: “And we long for our Lord to include us in the company of the righteous.” The verb “long for” translates *naṭma‘u*, which conveys a sense of eager hope, a craving for a divine favour that one does not take for granted. They do not boastfully declare that they are already righteous; instead, they humbly aspire to be counted among them. “The righteous,” *al-ṣāliḥīn*, are the highest rank of believers, the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the sincere worshippers whose lives are perfectly aligned with God’s will. To be in their company is the ultimate success. This demonstrates that their conversion was not a shallow emotional moment but a deep, transformative faith that immediately reoriented their ultimate goals. They were no longer content with mere salvation; they sought the highest levels of Paradise in the companionship of the best of creation. The verse teaches every believer to couple their declaration of faith with a humble, urgent longing for the highest spiritual stations, recognizing that entry into Islam is the first step of a journey whose destination is the company of the truly righteous.
85 | So Allah will reward them for what they said with Gardens under which rivers flow, to stay there forever. And that is the reward of the good-doers. | This verse provides the divine response and the eternal reward promised to the sincere Christians who recognized the truth and spoke the words recorded in the previous two verses. The structure is one of immediate and magnificent recompense: “So Allah will reward them for what they said.” The phrase “for what they said” explicitly connects the reward to their verbal testimony of faith, their tears, and their humble longing. This shows the immense value of a sincere word spoken in submission to God; a single moment of genuine conversion can erase a lifetime of error and open the door to an eternity of bliss. The reward is described as “Gardens under which rivers flow.” This is the classic Quranic image of Paradise, a place of perpetual shade, beauty, and lush greenery, where water, the symbol of life and purity, flows effortlessly beneath the palaces and trees. The phrase “to stay there forever” emphasizes the absolute permanence of this gift. There is no death, no eviction, and no diminishing of its pleasures. This is the ultimate security that contrasts sharply with the fleeting and treacherous alliances of this world that the hypocrites and the rebellious Jews sought. The verse then declares, “And that is the reward of the good-doers.” The word “good-doers” is *muḥsinīn*, derived from *iḥsān*, which is the highest station of faith: to worship Allah as if you see Him. Those who recognized the truth when it came and embraced it without hesitation are counted among the muḥsinīn. This verse is a powerful message of mercy and hope. It demonstrates that no matter what a person’s previous background was—even if they were from a community that held incorrect theological doctrines—a sincere response to the truth when it is clearly presented can instantly elevate them to the ranks of the good-doers, securing for them the eternal Gardens of Paradise.
86 | As for those who disbelieve and reject Our signs, they will be the residents of the Hellfire. | The Quran returns to the principle of contrast, immediately following the beautiful promise to the sincere believers with the stark warning to those who take the opposite path. This verse serves as the balance to the scale. “As for those who disbelieve and reject Our signs” describes a twofold crime. Disbelief, or kufr, is the state of the heart that covers and conceals the truth. Rejecting the signs, *āyāt*, is the external manifestation of that disbelief. The signs include the verses of the Quran that were recited to them, the moral and rational proofs embedded in the scripture, and the historical evidence of God’s dealings with previous nations. To reject them after they have been made clear is an act of defiance against the Creator. The consequence is given without embellishment: “they will be the residents of the Hellfire.” The term “residents,” *aṣḥāb*, implies an intimate association, a belonging that makes it their permanent home. There is no temporary stay and no eventual release for those who die in this state of rejection. This verse, when read in sequence with the previous one, creates a powerful motivational binary: a few sincere words of faith lead to eternal Gardens, while a state of disbelief and a life of rejecting signs lead to eternal Fire. The starkness of the presentation is meant to shake the listener awake. It is a call to make a decisive choice, because the middle ground does not exist. The verse also serves as a warning to the Muslim community itself: the same fate awaits any among them who, after knowing the truth, fall into disbelief and reject the signs. The special mercy shown to the converted Christians should not breed complacency, for the criteria of divine judgment are universal and based on one’s final state before God.
87 | O believers! Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you, and do not transgress. Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors. | The surah now turns to a different type of transgression, one that can arise from an excessive, misguided desire for piety. This verse addresses the believers directly, correcting a tendency that had emerged among some of the companions. The command “Do not forbid the good things which Allah has made lawful for you” was revealed in response to a specific incident. A group of companions, in their zeal to purify themselves and attain a higher spiritual station, contemplated extreme forms of asceticism. Some said they would fast every day without break, others said they would pray all night without sleep, and some even considered castrating themselves to avoid the temptations of desire. The Prophet, peace be upon him, upon hearing this, firmly corrected them, saying, “I am the most God-fearing among you, yet I fast and break my fast, I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. Whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me.” The verse, therefore, is a fundamental charter of balance in Islam. God has, in His wisdom, made certain things lawful, and these are “good things,” *ṭayyibāt*, pure and beneficial. To declare them forbidden is to usurp a divine prerogative and to imply that God’s law is not sufficient for spiritual growth. The second command, “and do not transgress,” is a warning against the opposite extreme: using the lawful things in a way that violates the boundaries, leading to wastefulness, gluttony, or obsession. The verse concludes, “Indeed, Allah does not like transgressors.” The path of Islam is the middle way between the excess of hedonism, which transgresses by seeking pleasure in the unlawful, and the excess of asceticism, which transgresses by forbidding the lawful. The true servant accepts God’s permissions with gratitude and His prohibitions with submission, finding the perfect balance that nourishes both body and soul.
88 | Eat of the good, lawful things provided to you by Allah. And be mindful of Allah in Whom you believe. | This verse is a succinct and beautiful summary of the Islamic ethic of consumption, directly following the command not to forbid what is lawful. The positive command is “Eat of the good, lawful things provided to you by Allah.” The word “eat” is a specific, concrete example that represents all forms of enjoying God’s provisions. The two conditions for what is consumed are “good” and “lawful.” The term “good,” *ṭayyib*, refers to the quality of the food itself: it must be pure, wholesome, and appetising to the sound human nature. The term “lawful,” *ḥalāl*, refers to its legal status: it must be acquired and prepared in a manner that God has permitted. The verse emphasizes that these provisions are “provided to you by Allah.” This simple phrase instills a profound sense of gratitude. The food on the plate is not merely the result of one’s salary or labour; it is a direct provision from the Creator, who sustained the seed in the earth, sent the rain, and granted the strength to work. Eating, then, becomes an act of worship when done with this awareness. The second half of the verse connects consumption to the heart: “And be mindful of Allah in Whom you believe.” The mindfulness, or taqwa, is to be conscious that the Provider is watching how His provision is used. This taqwa manifests in practical ways: not eating excessively, ensuring the food is purely halal, beginning with the name of Allah, and ending with praise. The phrase “in Whom you believe” is a reminder that the claim of faith is directly tied to the behavior around the dinner table. A believer cannot claim to believe in the All-Provider while consuming what is forbidden, or while being ungrateful for the blessings. The verse ties together the physical and the spiritual, the worldly and the otherworldly, teaching that the simple act of eating a meal is an opportunity to demonstrate gratitude, obedience, and the mindfulness that proves the sincerity of one’s faith.
89 | Allah will not call you to account for your thoughtless oaths, but He will hold you accountable for deliberate oaths. The penalty for a broken oath is to feed ten poor people from what you normally feed your own family, or to clothe them, or to free a bondsperson. But if none of this is affordable, then you must fast three days. This is the penalty for breaking your oaths. So be mindful of your oaths. This is how Allah makes things clear to you, so perhaps you will be grateful. | This verse is a comprehensive legal ruling on the expiation, or kaffarah, for breaking an oath, a subject introduced among the laws of lawful and unlawful things. It begins by distinguishing between two types of oaths. “Allah will not call you to account for your thoughtless oaths,” meaning the casual statements people make in daily speech, such as “No, by Allah” or “Yes, by Allah,” without a serious intention to bind themselves to a commitment. These are forgiven, and there is no sin or penalty for them. Then, “He will hold you accountable for deliberate oaths,” which are the solemn pledges made with full intention, where a person swears to do or abstain from something and then breaks that oath. This is a serious matter that requires expiation. The penalty is then laid out in a clear order of preference. The first and primary option is “to feed ten poor people from what you normally feed your own family.” The jurists explain this as providing a full meal of the average quality that one feeds one’s own household to ten needy individuals. The second equivalent option is “to clothe them,” meaning to provide each with a garment that covers them adequately for prayer. The third option is “to free a bondsperson,” which was a means of gradually emancipating slaves in the early Islamic period. These three options represent a graduated act of charity, transforming the sin of breaking an oath into an act of social goodness. The verse then provides a concession for those who genuinely lack the financial means to do any of these: “But if none of this is affordable, then you must fast three days.” The three days of fasting are a spiritual discipline that compensates for the material inability. The verse concludes by emphasising the seriousness of the matter: “So be mindful of your oaths.” This means to avoid making oaths lightly, and if one makes a binding oath, to strive to fulfill it, or if breaking it is the better course, to do so and then perform the expiation. The final statement, “This is how Allah makes things clear to you, so perhaps you will be grateful,” ties the entire ruling to gratitude for a law that is clear, practical, and merciful.
90 | O believers! Intoxicants, gambling, idols, and drawing lots for decisions are all evil of Satan’s handiwork. So shun them so you may be successful. | This verse delivers a decisive, comprehensive prohibition of four major practices that plagued pre-Islamic Arabian society, framing them as a unified front of satanic evil. The address “O believers” prepares the listener for a serious, identity-defining command. The list begins with “intoxicants,” translating *khamr*, which originally referred to wine but, by scholarly consensus, covers all substances that cloud and befog the intellect. The prohibition came in stages; earlier verses warned of some harm, and this verse provides the final, absolute ban. The second item is “gambling,” *maysir*, which was a popular pre-Islamic practice of dividing a slaughtered camel into parts and distributing them by chance through the drawing of arrows, often leading to the ruin of the poor. The third is “idols,” *anṣāb*, the stone altars upon which sacrifices were offered to false gods. The fourth is “drawing lots for decisions,” *azlām*, the practice of seeking knowledge of the unseen or making important life choices by throwing arrows with written instructions, a form of divination. The verse declares that all these are “evil of Satan’s handiwork.” This phrase, *rijs*, meaning filth and impurity, categorizes them as spiritually, morally, and mentally polluting. They are directly attributed to Satan’s design. Satan’s goal is to ensnare humans through them. The command that follows is emphatic: “So shun them.” The word “shun,” *ijtanibū*, is stronger than a simple prohibition; it means to keep far away, to have nothing to do with them, to treat them as something to be avoided with distaste. The purpose clause is “so you may be successful,” *tufliḥūn*. True success, the ultimate falāḥ, cannot coexist with indulgence in these satanic traps. The verse links spiritual and worldly success directly to the sober, clear-minded, and pure lifestyle that avoids these vices. It is a call to a radical break with the corruptions of the past, a purification of the individual and society from the tools that Satan uses to create animosity and distract from the remembrance of God.
91 | Satan’s plan is to stir up hostility and hatred between you with intoxicants and gambling and to prevent you from remembering Allah and praying. Will you not then abstain? | The Quran continues with an incisive analysis of the psychology and social consequences of the two most common and destructive of the prohibited acts: intoxicants and gambling. The verse reveals the hidden agenda, “Satan’s plan,” which operates through these seemingly pleasurable activities. The first objective of Satan’s plan is “to stir up hostility and hatred between you.” This is a profound insight. Under the influence of intoxicants, a person’s inhibitions are lowered, leading to arguments, insults, and physical violence that can sever lifelong friendships and tear apart families. In gambling, the nature of the zero-sum game—one person’s win is another’s loss—breeds deep resentment, envy, and enmity between players. What was meant as a social pastime becomes a factory of hatred. The second objective is even more devastating: “and to prevent you from remembering Allah and praying.” The ultimate aim of Satan is to sever the connection between the servant and their Lord. An intoxicated person cannot pray, as their mental faculties are compromised, and the state of addiction leads to a general neglect of worship. A gambler’s heart is obsessed with the next bet, the loss, or the win, leaving no space for the peaceful remembrance of Allah. These two activities build a wall between the soul and its Creator, which is Satan’s greatest victory. After exposing this sinister plan, the verse closes with a powerful rhetorical question: “Will you not then abstain?” This is the final, decisive push. After understanding the enmity, the distraction from prayer, and the satanic origin of these practices, what rational person, what sincere believer, would continue to engage in them? The question “Will you not then abstain?” is an appeal to the intellect, the fitrah, and the faith of the listener. It leaves the matter with a final, resounding call to stop, a call that history records was immediately heeded by the companions of the Prophet, who poured their wine into the streets of Medina.
92 | Obey Allah and obey the Messenger and beware! But if you turn away, then know that Our Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺ clearly. | After the powerful prohibition of intoxicants and gambling, the verse reinforces the obligation of obedience and clarifies the limits of prophetic responsibility. “Obey Allah and obey the Messenger” is a dual command that underpins the entire structure of Islamic law. Obedience to Allah is achieved by following the Quran; obedience to the Messenger is achieved by following his authentic Sunnah, which is the authoritative explanation and implementation of the Quran. The repetition of the verb “obey” for the Messenger emphasizes that his commands have independent authority derived from Allah. The command to “beware” is a sharp warning against disobedience and against thinking that one can selectively follow what pleases one’s desires. The verse then addresses the reality of human free will and the potential for rejection: “But if you turn away.” This phrase acknowledges that some will hear all the clear proofs and still choose to turn their backs on the divine command, preferring their addictions and worldly habits. In that case, the Prophet is given a clear instruction: “then know that Our Messenger’s duty is only to deliver the message clearly.” The Prophet’s job is not to force faith or obedience; it is to convey the message in a manner that is perfectly clear, leaving no excuse for ignorance. Once the delivery is accomplished, the accountability shifts entirely to the individual who turns away. This verse was a great consolation to the Prophet, peace be upon him, who deeply grieved when his people rejected the truth. It liberated him from the burden of an impossible task—controlling the choices of others—and focused him on the task he could perform: conveying with clarity. It is a timeless reminder to every caller to Islam that their success is not measured by the number of people who follow them but by the faithfulness with which they deliver the message. The results belong to God alone, and the turning away of the rebellious does not diminish the truth of the message in the slightest.
93 | There is no blame on those who believe and do good for what they had consumed before ˹the prohibition˺, as long as they fear Allah, have faith, and do what is good; then they believe and act virtuously, then become fully mindful ˹of Allah˺ and do righteous deeds. For Allah loves the good-doers. | This verse beautifully captures the divine mercy and the principle of non-retroactivity of law, specifically addressing the anxiety of some companions who had consumed intoxicants or engaged in gambling before the prohibition was revealed. It opens with a comforting reassurance: “There is no blame on those who believe and do good for what they had consumed before the prohibition.” Those who had partaken in these acts in the past, before the divine command came, are not held accountable for them. God’s law does not punish retroactively. However, the verse does not stop at a mere amnesty for the past; it lays out a progressive framework of spiritual growth that transforms the believer into a person who is completely protected from returning to such vices. The progression is described in careful stages. The first stage is “as long as they fear Allah, have faith, and do what is good.” This is the entry level of a conscious, believing, and active Muslim life. The verse then elevates: “then they believe and act virtuously.” This is a deepening and reaffirmation of faith and a commitment to virtuous, or *taqwā*-centred, actions. The final stage is “then become fully mindful of Allah and do righteous deeds.” This is the station of *iḥsān*, where a person’s mindfulness, or *ittaqaw*, reaches a level of full, all-encompassing consciousness, and their deeds are elevated to the highest standard of *iḥsān*, doing what is excellent and beautiful. The ones who reach this station are described in the closing sentence: “For Allah loves the good-doers.” The *muḥsinīn* are the beloved of Allah. The verse demonstrates that the journey from being a consumer of khamr to becoming a *muḥsin* is a journey of spiritual intensification. The prohibition was not just a legal ban; it was an invitation to a higher state of being, a life of such profound mindfulness and virtuous action that the old, satanic habits become not only forbidden but unthinkable, replaced by a love for what Allah loves.
94 | O believers! Allah will surely test you with game within the reach of your hands and your spears to distinguish those who fear Him in secret. Whoever transgresses from now on will suffer a painful punishment. | The surah now transitions back to the laws of pilgrimage, specifically addressing a test that was placed upon the believers during their state of ihram. The verse opens with the address “O believers” and a clear statement of divine purpose: “Allah will surely test you with game within the reach of your hands and your spears.” This refers to the experience of the companions during the Hudaybiyyah expedition or another pilgrimage, where wild game animals, such as small birds, rabbits, and deer, would come so close to the pilgrims that they could be easily caught by hand (“the reach of your hands”) or killed with a short throw of a spear (“your spears”). The abundance and tameness of the game was a direct test from Allah. The pilgrims were in the state of ihram, where hunting is strictly forbidden. The test was to see whether their fear of God would overcome their natural desire for the easy prey. The purpose of this test is explicitly stated: “to distinguish those who fear Him in secret.” This is a profound phrase. The fear of God “in secret” refers to the taqwa that operates when no human eye is watching. There was no game warden patrolling the desert to fine a pilgrim who surreptitiously hunted. The only deterrent was the internal, unseen consciousness of Allah. This test was designed to expose the difference between the one who obeys only under social pressure and the one who obeys out of a deep, personal conviction that Allah is watching. The verse then delivers a severe warning: “Whoever transgresses from now on will suffer a painful punishment.” The phrase “from now on” indicates that this warning comes after the law has been clearly established. The punishment could be the specific expiation detailed in the next verse, as well as a spiritual punishment and a painful consequence in the Hereafter. This verse teaches the immense value of integrity, the type of faith that functions when one is alone and unobserved, which is the truest measure of taqwa.
95 | O believers! Do not kill game while on pilgrimage. Whoever kills game intentionally must compensate by offering its equivalence—as judged by two just men among you—to be offered at the Sacred House, or by feeding the needy, or by fasting so that they may taste the consequences of their violations. Allah has forgiven what has been done. But those who persist will be punished by Allah. And Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment. | This verse lays out the detailed law of compensation, or *fidyah*, for the pilgrim who violates the prohibition against hunting in the state of ihram. The initial command, “Do not kill game while on pilgrimage,” is a clear, direct prohibition. The verse then addresses the one who breaks this rule: “Whoever kills game intentionally must compensate.” The word “intentionally” covers both deliberate hunting and a knowing act that kills the game, as opposed to a genuine, unavoidable accident. The compensation is three-tiered, providing flexibility while ensuring the pilgrim feels the weight of their transgression. The first and ideal option is “to offer its equivalence—as judged by two just men among you—to be offered at the Sacred House.” This means the pilgrim must select a domestic animal that is equivalent in size and value to the wild game they killed. For example, an ostrich might be compensated with a camel, a gazelle with a goat. This judgment of equivalence is to be made by two trustworthy, knowledgeable Muslims. The animal is then brought to the Haram, the sacred precinct of Mecca, and sacrificed there, its meat distributed to the poor. The second option, “or by feeding the needy,” involves calculating the value of the equivalent animal and using that money to purchase food that is distributed to the poor of the Haram. The third option is for those who lack the financial means: “or by fasting so that they may taste the consequences of their violations.” The number of fasts is typically calculated as one day for each needy person that the food would have fed. The phrase “so that they may taste the consequences of their violations” reveals the philosophy behind the ruling: it is not just a fine but an experiential lesson in the seriousness of disobeying a divine command. The verse then offers the mercy of amnesty for past mistakes: “Allah has forgiven what has been done.” What occurred in ignorance before the ruling was made known is pardoned. However, a sharp warning follows: “But those who persist will be punished by Allah. And Allah is Almighty, capable of punishment.” Persistence in wilful violation after the law is clear invites the divine wrath of the Almighty.
96 | It is lawful for you to hunt and eat seafood, as a provision for you and for travellers. But hunting on land is forbidden to you while on pilgrimage. Be mindful of Allah to Whom you all will be gathered. | This verse provides a crucial exception and clarification to the prohibition of hunting while in ihram, addressing a practical necessity of the pilgrim. The verse begins with a grant of permission: “It is lawful for you to hunt and eat seafood.” The Arabic phrase *ṣayd al-baḥr* literally means the “game of the sea.” According to the majority of scholars, this refers to all sea creatures that are caught by any means, whether they are fish or not, and whether they are caught alive or found dead on the shore. The permission extends to both the “hunting” and the “eating” of seafood, confirming that the act of catching sea life, which is technically a form of hunting, is exempted from the ihram prohibition. The wisdom behind this is then explained: “as a provision for you and for travellers.” Seafood is a readily available source of sustenance, particularly for those on a long journey like the pilgrimage. The pilgrim, who might be traveling through coastal areas or desert routes where dried fish is a staple provision, needs this lawful source of food, as land hunting is forbidden. The verse then reiterates the general rule to avoid confusion: “But hunting on land is forbidden to you while on pilgrimage.” The prohibition on land game remains firmly in place. The state of ihram is a state of peace and non-aggression even towards the animals of the land. The verse concludes by tying the physical regulation to the spiritual reality: “Be mindful of Allah to Whom you all will be gathered.” The observance of these rules of hunting and abstention during pilgrimage is an act of taqwa, a mindfulness of the One before whom all humanity will stand for judgment. The gathering of humanity on the plains of Arafat is a microcosm of the Day of Judgment. Observing these sacred limits is a training for that ultimate gathering. The verse teaches that the dietary and hunting laws of pilgrimage are not arbitrary rules but spiritual disciplines that instill God-consciousness and remind the believer of the final return to the Lord.
97 | Allah has made the Ka’bah—the Sacred House—a sanctuary of well-being for all people, along with the sacred months, the sacrificial animals, and the ˹offerings decorated with˺ garlands. All this so you may know that Allah knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and that He has ˹perfect˺ knowledge of everything. | This verse offers a magnificent reflection on the divine wisdom behind the sanctities of the pilgrimage, tying the physical symbols to the metaphysical reality of God’s all-encompassing knowledge. The verse states, “Allah has made the Ka’bah—the Sacred House—a sanctuary of well-being for all people.” The Ka’bah is described as a *qiyāman*, a source of stability and well-being for humanity. It is the spiritual centre, a place where disputes are ceased, security is guaranteed, and prayers are answered. Its sacredness benefits not just Muslims but all people, as it was a place of gathering and a hub of trade and safety even in pre-Islamic times, and its blessings continue to overflow. The verse then extends this sacred status to other associated elements: “along with the sacred months, the sacrificial animals, and the offerings decorated with garlands.” The sacred months—Dhul-Qa‘dah, Dhul-Hijjah, Muharram, and Rajab—are periods of divinely mandated peace where warfare is prohibited, allowing safe passage for pilgrims. The sacrificial animals and those adorned with garlands are a visible sign of the pilgrim’s devotion and are themselves protected from harm. All these are institutions designed for the collective well-being of society. The verse then gives the ultimate purpose of these sanctities: “All this so you may know that Allah knows whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and that He has perfect knowledge of everything.” The establishment of these sacred symbols and laws points to the Legislator’s perfect knowledge. He knows the human need for a safe, centralised place of worship; He knows the need for seasons of enforced peace in a violent world; He knows the spiritual benefit of the rituals of sacrifice. The precision and wisdom of these laws testify that the One who ordained them is the Knower of the unseen, the One who perfectly understands the human condition and has provided exactly what is needed for its material and spiritual stability. The verse thus elevates the rituals from mere tradition to proofs of divine omniscience.
98 | Know that Allah is severe in punishment and that He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful. | This short, powerful verse serves as a theological keystone, balancing the two fundamental attributes of God that govern the human relationship with the divine, especially in the context of the laws and sacred limits that have been detailed. The first command is “Know that Allah is severe in punishment.” This knowledge is not meant to be a distant intellectual fact but a living, active awareness that influences behaviour. The severity of punishment, *shadīd al-‘iqāb*, is a reality that must be known and internalised. For those who wilfully violate the sacred limits, who mock the rituals, who hunt during ihram with impunity, or who reject the signs, the punishment is not a light slap; it is a crushing, severe reality, manifested in this world and, infinitely more so, in the Hereafter. This knowledge produces a healthy fear that serves as a powerful deterrent against transgression. Immediately, the verse pairs this with the second command: “and that He is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.” The transition is swift and deliberate, preventing the soul from falling into despair. The same God whose punishment is severe is also the God whose forgiveness is immense and whose mercy is all-encompassing. For the sinner who repents, for the pilgrim who errs and seeks pardon, for the one who sinned out of ignorance and then turns back, the door of Al-Ghafūr and Ar-Raḥīm is wide open. The combination of these two attributes in a single verse encapsulates the entire Islamic spiritual path: walking towards God on a path bordered by hope and fear, never feeling so safe as to become complacent, and never feeling so lost as to give up. The verse also reminds the believer that the rules of pilgrimage and the dietary laws just discussed are not merely dry regulations but are undergirded by the reality of a God who will hold people accountable and who will also forgive the penitent. It is a verse that should shape the Muslim’s internal emotional landscape at all times.
99 | The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver ˹the message˺. And Allah ˹fully˺ knows what you reveal and what you conceal. | This verse reiterates a central theme of the surah, underscoring the limits of prophetic responsibility and the comprehensive knowledge of God. “The Messenger’s duty is only to deliver the message” is a statement of liberation for the Prophet and a clarification for the community. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, has fulfilled his role by conveying the complete message, warning of the punishment, giving glad tidings of mercy, and establishing the laws. He is not a guardian over people’s hearts, nor is he responsible for forcing them to believe. This is a direct refutation of any tendency to elevate the Prophet to a station where he is seen as a being who can grant salvation or forgiveness independently. His duty is the delivery, pure and clear. The second half of the verse shifts the focus to God: “And Allah fully knows what you reveal and what you conceal.” This statement has a dual function. For the sincere believer, it is a comfort: God knows the hidden faith in the heart, the silent struggles, and the secret good deeds that no human sees, and He will reward them accordingly. For the hypocrite and the transgressor, it is a dire warning: God knows the hidden mockery, the secret violations of the sacred limits, the bribes taken in dark rooms, and the oaths broken without any human witness. Nothing escapes His knowledge. The verse thus establishes a complete system of accountability. The Messenger delivers the external law and the clear message, establishing the public standard. God monitors the internal and the external reality, knowing both the public confession and the secret rebellion. On the Day of Judgment, the Messenger will testify that he delivered the message, and God will judge based on His perfect knowledge of every soul’s response to that message.
100 | Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Good and evil are not equal, though you may be dazzled by the abundance of evil. So be mindful of Allah, O people of reason, so you may be successful.” | This verse is a profound ethical and spiritual principle, a call to not be deceived by the apparent prosperity of wrongdoing. The Prophet is instructed to make a declaration that corrects a fundamental human misperception: “Good and evil are not equal.” This is an absolute statement of value. In the divine scale, a small amount of lawful, pure, and good—whether in wealth, status, or pleasure—is infinitely superior to an abundant amount of the impure and evil. The two are not in the same category and cannot be compared. The verse then identifies the psychological trap that leads people to devalue this truth: “though you may be dazzled by the abundance of evil.” The word “dazzled,” *yu‘jibuka*, means to be impressed and amazed. A person sees the immense wealth of the corrupt, the widespread indulgence in intoxicants and gambling, the glittering prosperity of societies that have abandoned divine law, and their hearts may waver. They may wonder, “If evil is so bad, why do its practitioners seem to thrive and fill the earth?” The verse answers this doubt directly. The abundance and apparent success of evil is a temporary illusion, a test that dazzles the eye but has no weight in the scales of the Hereafter. The command that follows is the antidote to this deception: “So be mindful of Allah, O people of reason, so you may be successful.” The call to “people of reason,” *ulī al-albāb*, is significant. It is the people of intellect, those who see beyond the surface and understand the true, eternal calculus, who are able to resist the dazzling effect of evil’s abundance. Their mindfulness, or taqwa, is the filter that allows them to see the intrinsic ugliness of evil and the intrinsic beauty of good, irrespective of their worldly quantities. The promise for such people is “so you may be successful,” the ultimate falāḥ, which is the true and lasting success that makes all worldly dazzle fade into insignificance.
101 | O believers! Do not ask about any matter which, if made clear to you, may disturb you. But if you inquire about what is being revealed in the Quran, it will be made clear to you. Allah has forgiven what was done ˹in the past˺. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing. | This verse presents a crucial etiquette of dealing with revelation, addressing a tendency among some companions to ask excessive, unnecessary questions that could lead to undue hardship. The address “O believers” sets the stage for a lesson in trusting divine wisdom. The command “Do not ask about any matter which, if made clear to you, may disturb you” refers to inquiries that delve into matters best left unspecified. The Prophet, peace be upon him, once warned, “The greatest criminal among the Muslims is the one who asks about something that was not prohibited, but it becomes prohibited because of his asking.” The historical context includes instances where people asked overly detailed questions about pilgrimage rituals or lineage, only to receive answers that added obligations or exposed uncomfortable truths. The verse teaches the believer to accept the broad, merciful silence of the law on certain matters rather than forcing a specificity that would cause distress. However, the verse immediately balances this prohibition: “But if you inquire about what is being revealed in the Quran, it will be made clear to you.” The Quran itself is a book of clarity; sincere questions about its meanings, its ethics, and its guidance are welcomed and answered. The key distinction is between a question that seeks genuine understanding and a question that is merely nitpicking, testing, or trying to create new restrictions out of excessive zeal or argumentativeness. The verse then offers a merciful reassurance: “Allah has forgiven what was done in the past.” Some of these annoying questions had already been asked before this warning was revealed; Allah pardons those past instances. The verse concludes with two divine names perfectly suited to the context: “And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Forbearing.” Al-Ghafūr forgives the past errors, while Al-Ḥalīm, the Most Forbearing, does not hasten to punish the questioners for their impertinence. This verse cultivates a disciplined, trusting mind that knows when to ask and when to remain silent, trusting that the silence of the Sharia is a mercy, not an oversight.
102 | Some people before you asked such questions then denied their answers. | This verse provides a historical warning that reinforces the previous command, drawing a direct parallel to the fate of earlier nations, specifically the Israelites. The phrase “Some people before you” refers to the communities that received prophets and scriptures before the advent of Islam, such as the followers of Moses and Jesus. They “asked such questions,” meaning they posed the same kind of excessive, nitpicking, argumentative, and test-filled queries. They demanded to know intricate, unnecessary details of their laws and then, when the answers were given and the obligations were laid upon them, they “denied their answers.” This denial was not necessarily an intellectual rejection that the answer came from God; it was a practical refusal to obey, a turning away from the newly-clarified duty. The classic example is the story of the cow in Surah Al-Baqarah, where the Israelites were commanded to sacrifice a cow. They could have sacrificed any cow, but they kept asking more and more specific questions—about its age, its color, its characteristics—until the matter became extremely difficult for them, and they almost did not do it. Their excessive questioning led to a constriction of what God had left mercifully broad. This verse serves as a mirror for the Muslim community. It warns them not to repeat the mistakes of the Children of Israel, who turned the flexible spaces of divine law into rigid, difficult obligations through their own argumentative nature, and then still failed to honour them. The lesson is profound: the health of a religious community is often preserved by not digging too deeply into matters where God has chosen to remain silent. Such silence is a vast mercy, and poking at it with incessant questioning can transform it into a burden. The believer’s motto is the famous supplication: “We hear and obey,” trusting that the Lawgiver knows best where to specify and where to leave things general.
103 | Allah has never ordained the ˹so-called˺ baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm camels. But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah, and most of them lack understanding. | This verse tackles a specific set of superstitious practices from pre-Islamic Arabia, where the pagan Arabs would dedicate certain camels to their idols and impose upon themselves restrictions that had no divine sanction. The four categories—baḥîrah, sâ'ibah, waṣîlah, and ḥâm—were types of camels that were set free, forbidden to be milked, ridden, or slaughtered, based on superstitions about their offspring or their perceived sacredness. The verse declares emphatically, “Allah has never ordained” these practices. This is a direct refutation of the pagan claim that such restrictions were part of their religious devotion. By forbidding what God did not forbid, they were assuming a divine authority and creating a law that had no basis in revelation. The verse then identifies the source of this practice: “But the disbelievers just fabricate lies about Allah.” They attributed these laws to God, claiming that He had sanctioned these dedications, when in reality they were following the customs of their forefathers or the whisperings of their own desires. The phrase “most of them lack understanding” delivers the diagnosis. Their superstitions were not based on intellectual reasoning or true divine guidance; they were the product of blind imitation and a failure to use the mind God had given them to distinguish between true religion and man-made fabrication. This verse was a key part of Islam’s broader project of dismantling the entire superstitious structure of jahiliyyah, replacing it with a simple, rational, and divinely-revealed code of law. It teaches the universal lesson that not everything that parades as religion or piety is from God; humans have a constant tendency to invent restrictions and rituals and then falsely attribute them to the divine. True faith is found in the clear, unadulterated revelation, and the silence of revelation on a matter is just as binding as its explicit commands.
104 | When it is said to them, “Come to Allah’s revelations and to the Messenger,” they reply, “What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us.” ˹Would they still do so,˺ even if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance? | This verse exposes the root cause of the rejection of truth by the pagan Arabs and, by extension, by any people who cling to ancestral tradition in the face of divine revelation. The call is a simple, open invitation: “Come to Allah’s revelations and to the Messenger.” This is the universal invitation to submit to the Quran and the Sunnah, to enter into a faith based on divine evidence rather than human custom. The response of the disbelievers is captured in a single, devastating sentence that summarises the attitude of blind traditionalism: “What we found our forefathers practicing is good enough for us.” This is the argument of someone who has no rational or revelatory proof for their position. They appeal not to logic, not to scripture, not to the welfare of the soul, but simply to the inertia of the past. The fact that their ancestors did it is, to them, sufficient justification to continue it. The Quran then demolishes this argument with a rhetorical question that exposes its absurdity: “Would they still do so, even if their forefathers had absolutely no knowledge or guidance?” The question forces the listener to consider the possibility that their ancestors were profoundly wrong. If the forefathers were ignorant, if they had no true guidance from God, then to follow them is not an act of filial piety but a willful dive into the same darkness. Blind allegiance to a misguided past is a prison that keeps the soul chained. This verse is a timeless call to intellectual and spiritual maturity. It demands that every human being examine their beliefs, not on the basis of what their family, tribe, or culture hands down to them, but on the basis of the truth that stands on clear proofs. Faith must be a personal choice anchored in conviction, not an inherited heirloom accepted without question. The believer is the one who has the courage to break from a misguided past and walk towards the light, even if that means standing alone against the current of their entire society.
105 | O believers! You are accountable only for yourselves. It will not harm you if someone chooses to deviate—as long as you are ˹rightly˺ guided. To Allah you will all return, and He will inform you of what you used to do. | This verse, when read correctly within its context, is a powerful declaration of personal moral responsibility and a clarification of the limits of the duty of enjoining good and forbidding evil. The address “O believers” highlights that the verse is about the internal dynamics of the community. “You are accountable only for yourselves” means that a person’s primary and most direct responsibility is their own soul. On the Day of Judgment, no soul will bear the sin of another. The verse then adds a phrase that is often misunderstood and misused: “It will not harm you if someone chooses to deviate.” Some have misinterpreted this as a license to abandon the duty of calling others to good, thinking, “I am guided, and the misguidance of others does not affect me.” However, the verse itself provides the essential condition that nullifies this misinterpretation: “as long as you are rightly guided.” Part of being rightly guided is fulfilling the command to enjoin good and forbid evil. A person who witnesses evil and remains silent, when they have the ability to speak, is not in a state of complete right guidance; their silence is a form of deviation. The great companion Abu Bakr, may God be pleased with him, clarified this when he heard people misusing this verse. He said, “O people, you recite this verse and misinterpret it. I heard the Messenger of God say, 'If people see an oppressor and do not restrain him, it is likely that Allah will punish them all.'” Thus, the verse does not cancel the obligation of mutual correction. It means that after you have done your duty of advising, warning, and trying to correct, if the other person still stubbornly refuses to change, their deviation will not harm your salvation, provided you have fulfilled your responsibility. The verse concludes by reminding all of the ultimate return: “To Allah you will all return, and He will inform you of what you used to do.” The final judgment belongs to God, who will perfectly distinguish between the one who tried to reform and the one who stubbornly deviated.

When the formal legal process is corrupted by false witnesses, the law provides a remedial oath that allows the truth to reassert itself. The entire sequence shows a divine wisdom that understands the complexities of travel, the difficulty of finding ideal witnesses, and the ever-present human potential for greed, and then provides a step-by-step mechanism that balances the need for flexibility with an unyielding commitment to the truth.
108 | In this way it is more likely that witnesses will give true testimony or else fear that their oaths could be refuted by those of the heirs. Be mindful of Allah and obey. The verse thus connects the meticulous civil law to the ultimate spiritual reality: the law is a tool, but its success depends on the taqwa of the heart, and its abuse leads to divine abandonment.
