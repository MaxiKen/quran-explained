1 | I do swear by the Day of Judgment! | This surah opens with a thunderous oath that immediately seizes the attention of the listener. "I do swear by the Day of Judgment!" The Arabic "la uqsimu bi yawm al-qiyamah" uses the emphatic "la" to affirm the oath with absolute certainty. Some scholars explain that the "la" is an extra particle of emphasis, not a negation, meaning "Indeed, I swear." Others say it means "Nay," as if responding to an unspoken denial. The oath is by the Day of Qiyamah itself, the day of rising, the day of standing before the Lord of the worlds. When Allah swears by something, He is drawing attention to its immense importance and its absolute reality. The Day of Judgment is the central event that the disbelievers of Mecca denied. By swearing by it, Allah is declaring that it is as real as the sun and moon, as real as the earth beneath their feet. An oath in human communication is used to convince a skeptical listener. Here, the Divine employs it to break through the hardened shell of denial. The Day of Judgment is not just any event; it is the Day when the entire cosmic order will be overturned, when the dead will rise, and when every soul will face the consequences of its deeds. The surah that follows this oath will paint a vivid picture of that Day and refute the arguments of those who consider it impossible. The oath also serves as a declaration of the Quran's core message. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was sent as a warner, and the warning revolves around this Day. By beginning with this oath, the surah aligns the listener's focus onto the ultimate reality. The very first word of the surah, often translated as "I swear," is a powerful verbal act that establishes the courtroom of divine justice. The listener is called to witness that the trial is about to begin, and the evidence will be presented. This oath is the gavel that calls the court of the heart to order.
2 | And I do swear by the self-reproaching soul! | The second oath is of a deeply personal and introspective nature: "And I do swear by the self-reproaching soul!" The Arabic "la uqsimu bi al-nafs al-lawwamah" introduces a second witness to the truth of the resurrection. The "nafs lawwamah" is the soul that blames itself, that feels remorse, that engages in self-criticism. This is the conscience. Psychologically, it is the inner voice that accuses a person after he commits a wrong. It is the soul that is in a state of constant moral tension, oscillating between good and evil, but never fully at peace with its sins. Commentators have explained that this is the soul of the believer, which reproaches itself for its shortcomings, always striving to be better. Some say it is the soul that blames itself on the Day of Judgment for its negligence. Others say it is the universal human conscience, which testifies to the existence of a moral law and, therefore, a Lawgiver. The oath by the self-reproaching soul is a profound argument for the Hereafter. Why does the human being feel guilt? Why does the conscience torment a person for a deed that no one else saw? If death were the end and there were no judgment, the pangs of conscience would be an inexplicable evolutionary cruelty. The very existence of the reproaching soul is a sign that there is a higher court where justice will be served. The oath connects the external cosmic event of the Day of Judgment with the internal human reality of conscience. The two are linked: the Day of Judgment is the objective manifestation of the justice that the soul intuitively expects. The reproaching soul is the internal witness, and the Day of Judgment is the external witness. By swearing by both, Allah is telling the human being: look within yourself and look at the universe; both testify to the same truth. For the believer, this oath brings comfort. The inner struggle against the self is not meaningless; it is a sign of a living faith that will be rewarded. The reproach is a divine gift that keeps the soul humble and seeking forgiveness.
3 | Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones? | After the two powerful oaths, the surah confronts the central doubt of the pagan Arabs: "Do people think We cannot reassemble their bones?" The Arabic "ayahsabu al-insanu allan najma'a 'izamah" expresses astonishment at such an idea. The word "ahsaba" means to calculate, to reckon, to think. The "insan" (human being) here primarily refers to the disbeliever who denies the resurrection. His doubt is presented in the form of a rhetorical question. He sees dead bodies decompose, bones scatter and turn to dust over time, and he concludes that it is impossible for them to be gathered back together. The specific mention of "bones" is significant because bones are the hardest part of the body, the framework that lasts the longest after death. The Arabs would sometimes carry bones and ask, "Who can revive these decayed bones?" The question mocks their limited reasoning. They measure the power of the Creator by the standards of their own weakness. A human being cannot reassemble scattered dust, so he assumes the Creator cannot either. This is the fallacy of anthropomorphism in reverse. The verse exposes the arrogance of this assumption. The word "najma'a" (we gather) emphasizes the precision and completeness of the reassembly. It is not a random collection but a purposeful gathering of every single bone, even those that were dispersed across the earth, eaten by animals, or dissolved in the sea. The verse prepares the mind for the answer that follows. It is a direct challenge to the materialist worldview that limits reality to what is observable and humanly possible. The question is as relevant today as it was then. Modern man, with his scientific achievements, may still scoff at the idea of bodily resurrection. But the Quran asks: is the One who created the bones in the first place, from a drop of fluid, incapable of recreating them? The question is left hanging, creating suspense for the powerful response.
4 | Yes ˹indeed˺! We are ˹most˺ capable of restoring ˹even˺ their very fingertips. | The response to the doubt is emphatic and overwhelming: "Yes indeed! We are most capable of restoring even their very fingertips." The Arabic "bala qadireena 'ala an nusawwiya bananah" uses "bala" which is a strong affirmative particle that means "Yes, on the contrary!" It rejects their assumption completely. Then comes the declaration of power: "qadireena" (We are perfectly capable). The word "qadir" denotes the One who has the power and the will to do whatever He pleases. Nothing can stand before His decree. The phrase "restore even their very fingertips" is a stunningly precise detail. The "banan" are the tips of the fingers, the smallest and most intricate parts. They include the unique fingerprints that distinguish every single human being who has ever lived. Allah is not only able to reassemble the large bones, the skull, and the ribcage; He is able to recreate the most minute details, the very ridges of the fingertips. This verse, revealed 1400 years ago, contains a remarkable insight. Fingerprints are unique; no two individuals, even identical twins, share the same fingerprint pattern. Modern forensic science uses fingerprints as the definitive proof of identity. Allah is stating that He will reconstruct every individual with such precision that even their unique fingerprints will be restored. This is a powerful argument against those who think resurrection is a vague, generic reassembly. It will be a perfect, individual recreation. The mention of fingertips also carries a symbolic meaning. The hands and fingers are the instruments of human action. They are used for good and for evil. On the Day of Judgment, these very fingers will testify against their owner. The precise restoration of the fingertips implies the precise recording and recompense of every deed they performed. The verse is a breathtaking combination of physical and spiritual truth. It leaves no room for doubt: if Allah can recreate the finest details, what of the larger parts? The argument is complete and devastating to the skeptic. The same Lord who designed the intricate loops and whorls on the tip of a finger in the womb can surely repeat that design at the resurrection.
5 | Still people want to deny what is yet to come, | Despite the compelling argument, the surah exposes the stubborn reality of the human condition: "Still people want to deny what is yet to come." The Arabic "bal yureedu al-insanu li yafjura amamah" carries a deeper nuance. The word "yafjura" comes from "fujur," which means immorality, licentiousness, and transgression. The phrase "amamah" means what is in front of him, his future, the Day of Judgment. The verse reveals that the denial of the Hereafter is not primarily an intellectual problem but a moral one. The human being does not deny the resurrection because of a lack of evidence; he denies it because he "wants" (yureedu) to continue living a life of unrestrained sin. The prospect of judgment and accountability is inconvenient. It threatens his freedom to pursue his desires without limits. So he convinces himself that there is no reckoning, or he questions it endlessly to justify his heedlessness. This is a profound psychological diagnosis. The denial creates a comfortable bubble where the human being can be his own god, answering to no one. The verse strips away the facade of intellectual skepticism and reveals the corrupt intention beneath. The "what is yet to come" is the entire sequence of death, resurrection, judgment, and the eternal afterlife. The disbeliever wants to deny this whole chain so that he can live for the moment. The verse is a warning to every person to examine their own heart. When one finds resistance to the truth of the Hereafter, one should ask: is it because the evidence is insufficient, or is it because I want to cling to a sin? The honest answer often reveals the latter. This verse also explains why the Quraysh leaders, despite recognizing the eloquence and power of the Quran, still rejected it. They did not want to give up their status, their wealth, and their pagan freedoms. The verse is a mirror that forces the reader to confront the true motives behind doubt. The word "still" (bal) indicates a turning away from the clear proof just given. The proof of the fingertips is irrefutable, but the corrupted will ignores it. This is the tragedy of the disbeliever.
6 | asking ˹mockingly˺, “When is this Day of Judgment?” | The inner desire to deny manifests outwardly in a specific behavior: "asking mockingly, 'When is this Day of Judgment?'" The Arabic "yas'alu ayyana yawm al-qiyamah" captures the tone of sarcastic interrogation. The question "When?" is not a genuine search for knowledge. It is a rhetorical weapon meant to ridicule and dismiss. By asking for the exact timing, the disbeliever implies that the Day is so remote or so uncertain that it can be safely ignored. If the Prophet cannot provide a date and time, then surely, they think, the whole thing is a myth. This was the constant refrain of the Quraysh, as seen in previous surahs like Al-Mulk and Al-Ma'arij. The question is loaded with arrogance. It assumes that the speaker has the right to demand a schedule from the Creator. The verse exposes the childish nature of this challenge. A student who mocks the teacher's warning about an exam by shouting, "When is it? What hour? What minute?" does not disprove the exam; he merely reveals his own insolence. The Quran does not answer the "when" question with a date because the wisdom of concealing the Hour is part of the test. The uncertainty is meant to keep humanity vigilant. The mockery is a defense mechanism. By turning the warning into a joke, the disbeliever relieves the psychological pressure of its implications. The verse also connects to the oath at the beginning. Allah swears by the Day of Judgment, and here the denier asks mockingly about it. The contrast is stark. The Lord of the universe takes an oath by its reality, while the petty human scoffs and asks for its timetable. This verse is a lesson for believers not to be drawn into such futile debates. The question is not sincere, so it does not deserve a direct answer. Instead, the surah will continue to describe the events of that Day, ignoring the mockery and reinforcing the certainty. The asker will get his answer, not in words, but in the terrifying experience when the Day actually arrives.
7 | But when the sight is stunned, | Instead of giving a date, the surah provides a vivid preview of the Day's events. "But when the sight is stunned." The Arabic "fa-idha bariqa al-basar" means when the vision is dazzled, confounded, and struck with terror. The verb "bariqa" can also be read as "baraqa," meaning to flash like lightning. The eyes of the entire creation will be fixed in a state of shock, unable to blink, unable to look away from the horrors unfolding. This is the moment when the Trumpet is blown and the cosmic order begins to collapse. The physical reaction of the eye is a window to the soul's terror. The sight, which was once so bold in looking at forbidden things and so dismissive in its glances at the truth, is now paralyzed. The verse captures the immediate, instinctive response of the human body to overwhelming trauma. There is no time to think, no time to prepare. The stunning of the sight is the first blow. The word "fa-idha" (but when) indicates a sudden, unexpected occurrence. The Day they mocked and demanded to see is now upon them, and it begins with their very senses being overwhelmed. This description is part of the Quran's method of making the unseen a present reality. The listener is invited to imagine that moment, to feel the shock in his own eyes. The stunning of the sight also signifies the removal of the veil. In the world, the disbelievers were blind to the truth. On that Day, they will see everything clearly, but the clarity will be too much to bear. Their sight will be stunned by the very things they denied. The verse serves as a warning that the Day will not be a leisurely intellectual discussion; it will be a sensory and psychological onslaught that no one can withstand without divine protection. The believer is encouraged to lower his gaze from sin now so that his sight is not confounded with terror then.
8 | and the moon is dimmed, | The cosmic signs of the Day intensify with the darkening of the moon. "And the moon is dimmed." The Arabic "wa khasafa al-qamar" means the moon is eclipsed, its light is extinguished, it disappears into darkness. The moon, which is a symbol of beauty, romance, and the marking of months, will lose its light permanently. This is not a temporary lunar eclipse that lasts for a few hours; it is the final, irreversible extinction of its glow. The moon was worshipped by some ancient peoples, including the pagan Arabs of certain tribes, as a deity. Its dimming is a declaration of its servitude and its mortality. The universe is not eternal; the lights of the sky are not gods. They are creations that will be switched off by their Creator. The dimming of the moon also represents the collapse of the order that humans rely upon. The night, governed by the moon, was a time of rest and a sign of the regularity of the cosmos. When it fades, all sense of normalcy vanishes. The verse echoes the scenes from earlier surahs like Al-Ma'arij, where the sky becomes like molten brass, and At-Takwir, where the sun is folded up and the stars fall. Here, the focus is on the moon, the gentle night-lamp, turning dark. The psychological impact on the human beings witnessing this would be indescribable. The familiar, comforting presence of the moon is gone. The darkness is absolute. The verse creates a sense of dread and dislocation. The world that the deniers thought was permanent is falling apart before their very eyes. The dimming of the moon is a sign that the time for action is over. The soft light that guided travelers at night is no more; the only light that matters now is the light of faith, which the disbelievers lack. They are plunged into an outer darkness that matches their inner darkness. The verse is a powerful call to reflect on the transient nature of creation and to seek the light of guidance before the permanent darkness falls.
9 | and the sun and the moon are brought together, | The cosmic chaos reaches a new, incomprehensible peak: "and the sun and the moon are brought together." The Arabic "wa jumi'a al-shamsu wa al-qamar" means the two great luminaries are joined. This is one of the most astonishing signs of the Day of Judgment. In the normal order of the universe, the sun and the moon are in different orbits, appearing at different times. The sun rules the day, the moon rules the night. They never meet. Their separation is a fundamental law of nature. On the Day of Judgment, this law will be abolished. The two will be brought together. Commentators have explained this in various ways: they will be fused together, or they will rise from the west together, or their lights will be extinguished and they will be rolled up as one. Regardless of the precise mechanics, the image is one of the complete dissolution of the current cosmic structure. The sun and the moon, which were worshipped by many as gods, will be seen as helpless creations dragged before their Creator. The joining together also signifies the end of time as we know it. The alternation of night and day, which measures our lives, will cease. There will be no more tomorrows. The verse is a direct answer to the mockery of the disbelievers. They asked, "When?" The answer is: when the very sun and moon you rely on are scrambled together. Their coming together is a visual, terrifying proof that the One who separated them can also reunite them. For the believer, this verse is a reminder of the absolute power of Allah. He is the One who created the sun and moon and set them in their courses with mathematical precision. He is also the One who can uncreate that order in an instant. The laws of physics are not autonomous; they are sustained by His command. When He wills, they collapse. The verse inspires a profound awe and a sense of the fragility of the cosmos. The world that seems so solid is held together by a thread, and that thread is the will of the Almighty.
10 | on that Day one will cry, “Where is the escape?” | With the cosmic order shattered and the terror mounting, the human reaction is one of desperate panic. "On that Day one will cry, 'Where is the escape?'" The Arabic "yaqulu al-insanu yawma'idhin ayna al-mafar" captures the frantic search for a way out. The "insan" here is the disbeliever, the one who denied and mocked. His arrogant questions are gone, replaced by a single, terrified plea. The word "mafar" means a place of flight, a refuge, an escape route. He looks left and right, forward and backward, for any exit from this overwhelming reality. But there is none. The question "Where?" implies that he expects to find something. In the world, he could always run away. He could avoid the truth by keeping busy, by distracting himself with entertainment, by surrounding himself with like-minded deniers. On that Day, all those escape hatches are sealed. The verse highlights the profound change in the attitude of the disbeliever. Before, he asked "When?" with sarcasm. Now, he asks "Where?" with terror. The two questions bracket his journey from arrogance to despair. The word "ayna" (where) is a question of place, but the Day of Judgment is not a place from which one can flee; it is the arena of the entire universe. There is no "outside." The cry is a cry of realization. He finally understands that what he denied is true, and his only instinct is the age-old human instinct of flight. But the flight of the zebra from the lion, described in the previous surah, was a metaphor for his worldly rejection. Now the real Lion has arrived, and there is no savanna to run across. The verse is a stark warning. It asks the listener: if you were in that situation, what would your answer be? The believer, who has made Allah his refuge in this world, will find that Allah is his refuge on that Day. The one who sought escape from the truth in the world will find no escape from its consequences. The cry is answered immediately in the next verse with crushing finality.
11 | But no! There will be no refuge. | The desperate cry for escape is met with a blunt, absolute negation. "But no! There will be no refuge." The Arabic "kalla la wazar" uses the strong particle "kalla" to reject the very idea of escape. "La wazar" means there is no shelter, no mountain fortress, no safe haven. The word "wazar" originally refers to a mountain stronghold where people would seek protection from enemies. In the rugged terrain of Arabia, a high, inaccessible mountain was the ultimate refuge. On the Day of Judgment, even the mountains will be flattened into shifting sand, as described in Al-Ma'arij. There will be no high ground, no cave, no bunker. The negation is comprehensive. The verse strips away every illusion of safety. The disbeliever might have thought his wealth, his tribe, his idols would protect him. Here, the answer is a single word: "no refuge." The finality is terrifying. This is the moment when all hope of avoidance dies. The verse also serves as a linguistic and psychological mirror to the previous cry. The cry was a long, frantic question; the answer is a short, sharp, definitive statement. The contrast emphasizes the hopelessness. The verse is not only about the external physical reality; it is about the internal state of the soul. The soul that did not make Allah its refuge in the world will find no refuge anywhere. The Prophet, peace be upon him, often sought refuge in Allah from the trials of the grave and the Fire. He taught the believers to say, "I seek refuge in You from You." This is the true refuge. Those who took it will be sheltered; those who mocked it will be exposed. The verse is a crucial turning point in the surah. It closes the door on the possibility of flight and forces the attention toward the only direction that remains: facing the Lord. The "no" is not just an answer to the question; it is the final verdict on a life spent running away from the truth. The disbeliever is now cornered by his own deeds, and the path leads only forward, to the tribunal of his Lord.
12 | On that Day all will end up before your Lord. | With the escape routes blocked, the surah declares the inevitable destination: "On that Day all will end up before your Lord." The Arabic "ila rabbika yawma'idhin al-mustaqarr" means to your Lord alone is the final place of settlement, the ultimate endpoint. The word "mustaqarr" comes from "istiqrar," meaning stability, settlement, a place where one comes to rest after a journey. The journey of life, with all its twists and turns, its flights and its pursuits, ends here. There is no further movement. The soul is brought to its final station before its Creator. The phrase "your Lord" (rabbika) is addressed to the Prophet, peace be upon him, and through him to every listener. It is a deeply personal address. The Lord who created, sustained, and guided is the same Lord before whom all will stand. The use of "rabb" (Lord) rather than a name of majesty like "Al-Jabbar" (the Compeller) carries a nuance of intimate authority. This is the Lord who nurtured you, and to Him you return. The verse contrasts the frantic movement of the previous scenes—the stunned sight, the dimming moon, the panicked cry—with the utter stillness of this final standing. The "mustaqarr" is a place of no more motion, no more change, just the reality of being in the presence of the Divine. For the believers, this verse holds a promise of coming home. The Lord they loved and worshipped in the unseen is now before them. For the disbelievers, it is a sentence of confrontation. The Lord they ignored and denied is now inescapably present. The verse reframes the entire narrative: the cosmic collapse was not an end in itself; it was the clearing of the stage for the main event, the meeting with the Lord. The destination is the same for all, but the experience of that destination differs vastly depending on what one brings. The verse brings a sense of closure to the chaos and a focus on the central relationship of existence.
13 | All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind. | On that Day of final settlement, the full record of every human being's life will be laid bare. "All will then be informed of what they have sent forth and left behind." The Arabic "yunabba'u al-insanu yawma'idhin bima qaddama wa akhkhar" is a comprehensive statement of accountability. The "insan" (human being) will be told, notified, made fully aware of two categories of deeds. "Ma qaddama" refers to what he has sent forth: the actions, words, and intentions that preceded him into the afterlife. These are the deeds that have already been recorded and whose consequences are now unfolding. "Ma akhkhar" refers to what he left behind: the ongoing effects of his life, the good or evil he set in motion, the legacy of his example. This includes knowledge he taught, wealth he spent or hoarded, children he raised, institutions he built, or traditions he started. It also includes the deeds he delayed and never performed, the repentance he postponed. The human being will be informed of it all, not because Allah needs to be told, but because the human being needs to see and acknowledge the complete picture. The veil of forgetfulness and self-justification will be removed. The verse is a profound meditation on the long shadow of a single life. A person may think his deeds are done when he dies, but their ripples continue. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that when a person dies, his deeds cease except for three: ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, and a righteous child who prays for him. These are part of what he "left behind." Conversely, a person who initiated evil will bear the burden of all who follow that evil. The verse calls the believer to audit his life daily: what am I sending forth today, and what am I leaving behind? It instills a sense of urgency and forward-thinking. The Day when all will be informed is the Day when all excuses evaporate. The only response will be the stark recognition of one's own record. This verse is the core of the surah's message: the resurrection is for judgment, and the judgment is precise and all-encompassing.
14 | In fact, people will testify against their own souls, | The surah intensifies the scene of accountability by revealing that the ultimate witness is internal. "In fact, people will testify against their own souls." The Arabic "bal al-insanu 'ala nafsihi baseerah" can be understood as: the human being will be a clear proof against himself, or he will have insight into his own self. The word "baseerah" means clear evidence, insight, or inner vision. On that Day, the human being will not need external witnesses to convict him. His own limbs, his own memory, his own conscience will rise up as a witness. The skin, the tongue, the hands will speak, as described in other surahs. The inner reality of the person will be exposed, and he will see himself with a clarity that was impossible in the world. In the world, the ego (nafs) constantly justifies itself, blames others, and minimizes its sins. On that Day, that protective shell will be shattered. The human being will look at his own record and recognize it fully. He will be his own judge in the presence of the Ultimate Judge. This is the ultimate humiliation for the arrogant: they are forced to condemn themselves. The verse also contains a profound psychological truth. Deep down, every person knows his own state. The conscience, the self-reproaching soul mentioned in the oath, is a constant witness. On the Day of Judgment, that inner knowledge will become an explicit, undeniable testimony. The veil of self-deception will be burned away. The "baseerah" is the piercing insight that no longer flinches from the truth. The verse is both a warning and a call to self-reflection now. If a person practices honest self-accounting in this world, the testimony of that Day will be a moment of relief rather than shock. The Prophet, peace be upon him, advised, "Take account of yourselves before you are taken to account." The verse encourages the believer to develop this baseerah in the world, to see his own faults clearly and rectify them, so that the testimony on that Day becomes a testimony of repentance and forgiveness rather than condemnation.
15 | despite the excuses they come up with. | The self-testimony of the human being is given despite any attempts to justify or deflect. "Despite the excuses they come up with." The Arabic "wa law alqa ma'adheerah" means even if he throws forth his excuses, his justifications, his apologies. The word "ma'adheer" is the plural of "ma'dhirah," meaning an excuse or a plea. On that Day, the human being might still try to offer explanations. He might say, "I was misled by my leaders," or "My environment made me do it," or "I didn't know." But these excuses will be cast aside, rejected. The inner testimony is so clear and overwhelming that no external argument can override it. The verse indicates that the excuses will be presented, but they will not be accepted. The Arabic construction implies a futile act: he throws his excuses, but they fall flat. This is the death of the last hope of the condemned. In human courts, a skilled lawyer can create reasonable doubt. In the divine court, there is no doubt, and the soul's own baseerah is the prosecutor, the witness, and the jury. The verse also teaches that the habit of making excuses in this world is a dangerous one. The person who constantly justifies his sins and blames others is training himself for a pattern that will fail him miserably on that Day. The believer is the one who stops making excuses and starts making amends. The acknowledgment of sin without excuse is the path to forgiveness. The verse connects back to the self-reproaching soul. The nafs lawwamah is the soul that blames itself now, in this world, sparing itself the futile excuses then. The disbeliever, who silenced his conscience and surrounded himself with justifications, will find those justifications evaporate. The verse is a powerful conclusion to this section. It paints a complete picture of the judgment: the destination is the Lord, the information is total, the witness is oneself, and the excuses are useless. The only thing that remains is the sentence, whether it is mercy or punishment.
16 | Do not rush your tongue trying to memorize ˹a revelation of˺ the Quran. | In the midst of this powerful discourse on the Day of Judgment, the surah offers a tender interlude directed to the Prophet, peace be upon him. "Do not rush your tongue trying to memorize a revelation of the Quran." The Arabic "la tuharrik bihi lisanaka li ta'jala bih" literally means "do not move your tongue with it to hasten with it." This verse addresses a specific, beautiful aspect of the Prophet's experience during the reception of revelation. According to authentic hadith in Bukhari, narrated by Ibn Abbas, when Jibril would bring a new passage of the Quran, the Prophet, out of intense love and fear of forgetting, would try to repeat the words immediately, moving his tongue rapidly along with the angel. He would struggle to memorize it on the spot, even before Jibril had finished reciting. This was a sign of his deep devotion and his anxiety to preserve every single letter. Allah, in His mercy, gently instructs him to relax, to be still, and to listen. The command is not a rebuke but a reassurance. It tells the Prophet that he does not need to strain himself. The preservation of the Quran is not his burden to bear; it is Allah's guarantee. The verse reveals the human side of the Prophet, his fear, his eagerness, his intense sense of responsibility. It also teaches a profound lesson about the reception of divine knowledge: one must approach it with calmness and trust, not with anxious haste. The student of the Quran is taught to be patient, to listen deeply, and to let the words settle in the heart before trying to reproduce them. The rush to master everything immediately can lead to confusion and exhaustion. The verse is a balm for those who fear they cannot memorize or learn. The ultimate responsibility for the Quran's preservation lies with its Revealer. This interlude within the surah also contrasts with the rushing and fleeing of the disbelievers. The Prophet's rush was out of love for the Quran; their rush was away from it. Yet even the Prophet's love needed to be channeled into calm trust. The verse is a moment of profound intimacy between Allah and His Messenger, witnessed by the reader, inviting us into that space of trust and stillness.
17 | It is certainly upon Us to ˹make you˺ memorize and recite it. | The reassurance is now made explicit and emphatic. "It is certainly upon Us to make you memorize and recite it." The Arabic "inna 'alayna jam'ahu wa qur'anah" uses the emphatic particle "inna" and the preposition "'ala" (upon Us), indicating a binding divine commitment. The phrase "jam'ahu" means "its collection," which encompasses gathering it in the Prophet's heart, preserving its words, and ensuring that it is not lost. "Qur'anahu" means "its recitation," ensuring that the Prophet can recite it flawlessly, with the correct pronunciation and sequence. Allah is taking full responsibility for the entire process. The Prophet does not need to fret or exhaust himself. The One who revealed it will preserve it. This verse is one of the strongest guarantees of the Quran's textual integrity. It is not merely a promise to the Prophet; it is a declaration that the Quran we have today is the same Quran that was revealed, collected, and recited under divine supervision. The verse also teaches the proper attitude of a servant entrusted with a divine task. The servant exerts effort, but his ultimate reliance is on Allah. The Prophet would continue to engage with the Quran intensely, but his heart was at peace because the outcome was guaranteed. For the believers, this verse is a source of immense comfort. When one struggles to memorize a surah or to understand a passage, one should remember that it is Allah who facilitates the Quran for remembrance. The burden is not on the weak human memory alone; it is supported by the divine will. The verse also highlights the miracle of the Quran's oral preservation. Millions of Muslims, from the Prophet's time until today, have memorized the entire Quran. This living chain of memorizers is the fulfillment of Allah's promise to collect and recite it. The verse is a testament to the unique status of the Quran among all scriptures.
18 | So once We have recited a revelation ˹through Gabriel˺, follow its recitation ˹closely˺. | The practical instruction follows the promise. "So once We have recited a revelation through Gabriel, follow its recitation closely." The Arabic "fa-idha qara'nahu fattabi' qur'anah" means that when Jibril, representing the divine "We," recites the revelation, the Prophet should listen attentively and then, after the recitation is complete, recite it himself. The command "fattabi'" means to follow, to go behind. The Prophet is to let Jibril lead, and he follows. This defines the order of the transmission. The angel recites from the Preserved Tablet, and the Prophet listens and then repeats. There is no room for alteration, insertion, or omission. The Prophet is the perfect receiver and transmitter. This process ensured the accuracy of every letter. The verse also teaches the etiquette of learning. The student must first listen to the teacher, absorb the knowledge correctly, and then reproduce it. The haste that the Prophet initially felt was a natural human impulse, but the divine training refined it into a disciplined, calm reception. The phrase "follow its recitation" also implies a lifelong commitment. The Prophet was not just to memorize the words but to follow the Quran in his life, to embody its teachings. The recitation is not merely a vocal act; it is a path to be walked. The verse links the preservation of the text with the preservation of the meaning through practice. The believer is instructed to do the same: to learn the Quran, recite it properly, and then follow its guidance. The sequence is listen, recite, and act. The verse is a masterclass in the methodology of Quranic transmission. It assures the Prophet that the entire process is under control, and it invites the believer to enter the chain of transmission with the same trust and discipline. The focus shifts from the Prophet's anxious tongue to the steady rhythm of divine instruction.
19 | Then it is surely upon Us to make it clear ˹to you˺. | The final stage of the divine guarantee is the explanation. "Then it is surely upon Us to make it clear to you." The Arabic "thumma inna 'alayna bayanah" means that beyond memorization and recitation, Allah takes responsibility for clarifying the meanings, the rulings, the implications, the abrogations, and the contexts of the Quran. The word "bayan" means explanation, elucidation, making something manifest. The Prophet might receive a verse whose full understanding he does not grasp immediately. This verse promises that Allah will provide that understanding over time, through further revelation, through inspiration, or through the unfolding of events. The Prophet's Sunnah, his statements, actions, and tacit approvals, are the manifestation of this divine "bayan." The Quran often gives principles, and the Prophet's life gives the detailed application. This verse is a crucial source of the authority of the Sunnah. The explanation of the Quran was divinely guided. The Prophet did not interpret the Book from his own whims; his interpretations were part of the "bayan" that Allah had promised. The verse also provides comfort to the believers who struggle to understand certain passages. It reminds them that the Quran is ultimately explained by its Author, and the sincere seeker will be granted insight. The sequence is beautiful: collection in the heart, recitation on the tongue, and clarification in the mind and life. The three stages cover the complete preservation and transmission of the divine message. The interlude that began with the Prophet's rushing tongue ends with the comprehensive assurance that everything—memory, recitation, meaning—is handled by Allah. The Prophet is now ready to continue his mission with absolute confidence. The verse also serves as a transitional point, bringing the focus back to the main theme of the surah, which is the denial of the Hereafter and the consequences thereof. The clarity of the Quran's message leaves no excuse for those who reject it.
20 | But no! In fact, you love this fleeting world, | The surah now returns to the deniers with a sharp diagnosis. "But no! In fact, you love this fleeting world." The Arabic "kalla bal tuhibboona al-'ajilah" uses "kalla" to rebuke and "bal" to turn to the real truth they are avoiding. The "you" here is plural, addressing the Quraysh and by extension all who share their attitude. The root of their denial of the Hereafter is diagnosed as the excessive love of the dunya, the fleeting, immediate world. The word "'ajilah" comes from "'ajil," meaning that which is hasty, that which comes quickly and passes quickly. The worldly life is characterized by its transience. It offers immediate pleasures and immediate gains, while the Hereafter is delayed. The disbelievers are those who are impatient, who cannot delay gratification. They are attached to the cash, not the credit. This love of the fleeting world explains their mockery of the Day of Judgment, their demand for its immediate arrival, and their turning away from the reminder. The love is not a passive fondness; it is an active, consuming passion that drives their decisions. They live for wealth, status, and sensual pleasures. The idea of a Hereafter threatens the primacy of these pursuits, so they push it away. The verse echoes the description of the human being in Al-Ma'arij as being created impatient, loving the good that comes quickly. This is the default human state, which only prayer and faith can cure. The verse is a mirror for every soul. It asks: do I love this fleeting world more than the everlasting Hereafter? The honest answer for many is a struggle. The believer is not one who has no love for the world, but one who subordinates that love to the love of Allah and the Hereafter. The verse is a call to reorder one's priorities. The "no" at the beginning rejects the false claims of the disbelievers that they are sincere seekers of truth. The truth is that their desires have blinded them.
21 | and neglect the Hereafter. | This verse completes the diagnosis of the human spiritual condition that began in the previous verse. After stating that the deniers love this fleeting world, Allah declares that they “neglect the Hereafter.” The Arabic word for neglect here carries a sense of leaving something behind deliberately, of abandoning it and turning away from it. The two verses together paint a complete picture: the heart is attached to what is immediate and visible, and consequently it is detached from what is to come. The love of the worldly life and the neglect of the Hereafter are two sides of the same coin. One cannot fill the heart with love for this transient world and still have room for the concern about the eternal life. The neglect of the Hereafter manifests as a failure to prepare for it, a lack of reflection upon its realities, an absence of fear and hope regarding its outcomes, and ultimately a life lived as if death and judgment will never arrive. The verse explains the root cause of the disbelief, the mockery, and the denial of resurrection described earlier in this surah and the previous ones. The disbelievers are not primarily in doubt because of a lack of intellectual evidence. Rather, their hearts are so immersed in the pleasures and pursuits of this world that they have pushed the thought of the Hereafter to the margins of their consciousness. It is a psychological and spiritual neglect, not an intellectual one. The pairing of love and neglect is profoundly instructive. Human nature abhors a vacuum. If the love of the eternal does not occupy the heart, the love of the temporal will flood in and take over. The verse is a call to examine one’s own priorities. Does the weekly prayer, the daily remembrance, the preparation for death, take second place to the endless chase for money, status, and entertainment? The neglect of the Hereafter is not a passive oversight; it is an active choice repeated daily. The surah is building toward the contrasting images of faces that will be bright and faces that will be gloomy, which are the direct results of where the heart placed its love.
22 | On that Day ˹some˺ faces will be bright, | Having described the two opposing attitudes in the world, the surah now unveils the results in the Hereafter. “On that Day some faces will be bright.” The Arabic “wujuhun yawma’idhin nadirah” refers to faces that are radiant, glowing with light, freshness, and beauty. The word “nadirah” implies a lush, blooming quality, like a flourishing garden. It is the face of someone who is profoundly happy, at peace, and honored. The brightness is not merely a physical glow; it is an outward manifestation of inner purity, faith, and the good news of salvation. In the world, these faces may have been tired from night prayers, dusty from poverty, or marked with the traces of tears shed in fear of Allah. But on that Day, they will shine like the full moon. This brightness is a reward for their love of the Hereafter over the fleeting world. They neglected the temporary pleasures and sought the eternal, so Allah grants them an eternal radiance. The verse also serves as a powerful visual contrast. The listener is invited to imagine the scene: a vast plain of resurrection, and among the multitudes, some faces are illuminated with a light that sets them apart. This light is described elsewhere as the light of faith that will walk before them and on their right, as they cross the Bridge over Hell. The brightness is also a sign of recognition and honor; the angels and the prophets will know them by this light. For the believers who hear this verse, it is a source of immense hope. It tells them that every hardship endured for the sake of Allah, every night spent in prayer, every act of charity done in secret, is accumulating as light for that Day. The verse calls the believer to live in such a way that his face will be among the bright ones, not through cosmetics or worldly beauty, but through the inner light of iman.
23 | looking at their Lord. | This verse is one of the greatest and most beautiful promises in the entire Quran, and it explains the ultimate source of the brightness on those faces: “looking at their Lord.” The Arabic “ila rabbiha nadhirah” means they will gaze upon their Lord. The word “nadhirah” comes from “nazar,” meaning to look, and the construction implies a continuous, pleasant, expectant gaze. The believers in Paradise will be granted the supreme honor of seeing Allah, the Exalted. This is the beatific vision, the greatest reward of Paradise, surpassing all other pleasures. The Prophet, peace be upon him, confirmed this in authentic hadith, stating that the believers will see their Lord as clearly as they see the full moon on a cloudless night, without any doubt or difficulty. This vision will be a source of such overwhelming joy and delight that all the other pleasures of the Gardens will pale in comparison. When the believers gaze upon the Face of their Creator, they will forget every hardship they ever endured. The verse refutes those who claim that Allah cannot be seen. The wording is clear: the faces are looking at their Lord. The preposition “ila” (toward) indicates direction. The early Muslims, the Ahl al-Sunnah, firmly believed in the reality of this vision without asking how it occurs or imagining any physical resemblance to creation. The verse also provides a profound contrast with the previous warnings. The deniers neglected the Hereafter and loved the world; now, in the Hereafter, they will be veiled from seeing Allah, which is the greatest deprivation. The believers, who loved Allah and yearned to meet Him, are granted the vision. The word “nadhirah” also carries a sense of freshness and radiance, which ties back to the brightness in the previous verse. The light on their faces is a reflection of the Light they are beholding. The faces are bright because they are looking at their Lord. The verse is an invitation to live for that moment. Every act of worship, every restraint from sin, is a step closer to the vision of the Most Merciful. The heart that truly believes in this promise will not be overly attached to the glitter of this world, for it awaits a far greater sight.
24 | And ˹other˺ faces will be gloomy, | The surah now presents the stark opposite. “And other faces will be gloomy.” The Arabic “wa wujuhun yawma’idhin basirah” means faces that are dark, scowling, frowning, and grim. The word “basirah” comes from “busur,” which describes a face that is contracted, harsh, and expressing intense distress. These are the faces of the deniers, the ones who loved the fleeting world and neglected the Hereafter. Their inner state of terror, regret, and despair is now printed on their features. There is no light, no hope, no beauty. The gloom is the external manifestation of the darkness of disbelief and sin that filled their hearts. Just as the believers’ faces are bright with the reflection of the divine vision, the disbelievers’ faces are dark with the anticipation of punishment and the veil from Allah. The verse creates a powerful binary. On that Day, there will be no neutral faces. Every face will be either bright or gloomy. The dividing line is faith and deeds. The gloom is not merely a temporary emotion; it is a permanent mark of their wretched state. In the world, they might have walked with pride and smiled with arrogance, but on that Day, their faces will betray their true condition. The verse is a warning to those who wear a mask of happiness in this world while their hearts are empty of faith. The mask will fall, and the reality will appear. For the believer, this verse instills a healthy fear and a deep gratitude. It is a reminder to seek the radiance of faith and to avoid the deeds that cause the face to darken. The mention of gloomy faces immediately leads to the description of what they are anticipating, which is the subject of the next verse. The contrast is complete: brightness and vision for one group, gloom and impending doom for the other.
25 | anticipating something devastating to befall them. | The surah now reveals what is causing the gloom on those faces: they are “anticipating something devastating to befall them.” The Arabic “tadhunnu an yuf‘ala biha faqirah” means they are certain, they expect with dread, that a catastrophic, spine-breaking punishment is about to be inflicted upon them. The word “faqirah” comes from “faqr,” meaning to break the vertebrae of the back. It describes a calamity so severe that it snaps the spine. The faces are not just sad; they are frozen in the horrified expectation of an imminent, crushing disaster. They know, with absolute certainty, that they are about to be thrown into the Fire, and the sheer terror of that approaching doom is already written on their faces. This is the state of the criminal who has been sentenced and is awaiting the executioner. Every second is an eternity of dread. The word “tadhunnu” here means certainty, not mere suspicion, because on that Day, all doubts are removed. They see the angels of punishment, they see Hell, and they know their destination. The anticipation itself is a punishment. The contrast with the believers is profound. The believers are looking at their Lord with radiant faces, in a state of peaceful bliss. The disbelievers are looking at the approaching punishment with gloomy faces, in a state of spine-breaking fear. The verse encapsulates the essence of the Day of Judgment: it is the day of final separation. The one who lives in this world fearing the punishment of the Hereafter, as described in Al-Ma‘arij, is saved from this terror. The one who lived secure and mocking is now shattered by fear. The “devastating” thing is exactly what the prophets warned about, what the Quran described, what they denied and laughed at. Now it is no longer a distant threat; it is a present, overwhelming reality. The verse ends the general description of the Day and leads into the specific moment of death, where the transition to this gloomy state begins. It is a call to take the warning seriously before the moment of anticipation becomes the moment of infliction.
26 | But no! ˹Beware of the day˺ when the soul reaches the collar bone ˹as it leaves˺, | The surah now shifts to a terrifyingly intimate scene: the moment of death for a denier. “But no! Beware of the day when the soul reaches the collar bone as it leaves.” The Arabic “kalla idha balaghat al-taraqee” uses “kalla” to jolt the listener out of any remaining heedlessness. The soul, at the time of death, rises up through the body and reaches the “taraqee,” which are the bones of the upper chest, the collarbones. This is the final stage of the soul’s extraction. The imagery is graphic and visceral. The person is on his deathbed, and the soul is being pulled out. The family stands around helplessly. The “collar bone” signifies the point of no return; the soul is at the exit door of the body. The verse does not say “when a person dies,” it zooms in on that precise, agonizing moment. This is the moment that every human being will experience. For the denier, this moment is filled with terror because he knows what awaits him. The angels of death come, and he sees his destination. The surah is inviting the listener to imagine that moment now, while there is still time to prepare. The phrase “as it leaves” clarifies that the soul is in the process of departing. This is the moment when the worldly life is definitively over, and the Hereafter is about to begin. All the wealth, the children, the status that the denier loved are now completely useless. They cannot stop the soul from reaching the collarbone. The verse is a powerful meditation on mortality. It reminds the believer that every soul will one day reach that point. The difference is what the soul carries with it—faith or disbelief, good deeds or sins. The denier who loved the fleeting world will face this moment with horror, while the believer who longed for the meeting with his Lord will face it with peace and longing. The verse is the beginning of a dramatic narrative of death, leading to the shroud and the final march to judgment.
27 | and it will be said, “Is there any healer ˹who can save this life˺?” | As the soul reaches the collarbone and the person is in the throes of death, the verse describes the desperate reaction of those around him. “And it will be said, ‘Is there any healer who can save this life?’” The Arabic “wa qeela man raq” means someone asks, “Who is a raqi?” The word “raqi” comes from “ruqyah,” which is incantation, healing by spiritual means, or a skilled physician who can administer a cure. The people around the dying person are in a state of panic. They are looking left and right for anyone who can perform a miracle, who can recite something, who can do anything to stop the soul from departing. Some commentators say it is the angels who say this, meaning they ask, “Who can ascend with this soul and take it up?” But the predominant interpretation is that it is the grieving relatives and friends, crying out in desperation. They seek a healer, a savior, but there is none. The angel of death does not wait. The appointed time, which was delayed and denied, has now arrived. The question “Is there any healer?” echoes the helplessness of human power in the face of divine decree. The best doctors, the most powerful medicines, the most recited spells cannot push back the soul once it has reached the collarbone. The scene exposes the futility of all worldly attachments. The denier who relied on his wealth, his tribe, his influence, now lies dying, and none of those can offer a “raqi” who can save him. The verse also carries a subtle irony. The disbelievers used to mock the Prophet and seek refuge in their idols and their magicians. Now, at the moment of truth, they are asking for a healer, but the only true Healer is Allah, whom they rejected. The call for a healer goes unanswered. This verse is a stark reminder that death is the one appointment no one can cancel or reschedule. The believer is taught to prepare the “ruqyah” of faith and good deeds in advance, so that at the moment of death, instead of panic, there are angels with glad tidings.
28 | And the dying person realizes it is ˹their˺ time to depart, | At that same moment, the veil is lifted from the dying person’s own consciousness. “And the dying person realizes it is their time to depart.” The Arabic “wa dhanna annahu al-firaq” means he is certain that this is the separation. The word “dhanna” here indicates conviction, not guesswork. The dying denier now knows with absolute certainty that he is leaving the world. The “firaq” is the parting from his wealth, his family, his body, and his earthly life. This realization is the culmination of his worldly experience. For his entire life, he denied the Hereafter and lived as if he would never die. Now, the truth slaps him in the face. The separation is final. He is crossing a border from which there is no return. The phrase “it is their time to depart” captures the profound loneliness of death. No companion can come along. The person is separated from everything he loved and hoarded. The verse exposes the psychological state of the denier in that moment. It is not a peaceful acceptance but a horrified recognition. The certainty that he mocked has now become his own immediate reality. The “firaq” is also the separation from the false hopes he harbored. He may have thought that his idols would intercede, or that death was the end, or that he would somehow escape. All those illusions are now shattered. He realizes he is departing to face the consequences of his deeds. This verse is a powerful tool for self-reflection. It asks the listener to imagine that very moment of realization. What will it feel like to know that your time is up, that the chapter of worldly action is closed forever, and that you are now entering the chapter of eternal consequence? The believer uses this reflection to stay vigilant and to hate the idea of being unprepared when the realization dawns. The surah continues to describe the final steps of the departure.
29 | and ˹then˺ their feet are tied together ˹in a shroud˺. | The final physical act of the worldly life is described with a stark, grim image: “and then their feet are tied together in a shroud.” The Arabic “wal-taffat al-saqu bil-saq” literally means “the shin is wrapped around the shin” or “one leg is joined to the other.” This is a description of the moment when the corpse is prepared for burial. The legs are straightened and wrapped together in the shroud. The body that once walked arrogantly, that rushed toward the Prophet in mockery, that kicked and strutted with pride, is now a motionless, bound object being readied for the grave. Some commentators also interpret this phrase as the joining of the last agony of this world with the first agony of the Hereafter, meaning that one suffering is wrapped upon another, with no respite. Others say it refers to the two legs of the soul and the body being separated but joined in the experience of death. The most common and powerful interpretation, however, remains the binding of the feet in the burial shroud. It is the ultimate humiliation for the arrogant denier. The man who had abundant wealth, who wore fine clothes, who enjoyed a smoothed path in life, is now reduced to a wrapped corpse, his feet tied. This is the inevitable end of every human being, believer and disbeliever alike, but the context here specifically highlights it as a stage in the transition to punishment for the denier. The verse strips away all the glamour of the worldly life and shows its final scene. It is a profound reminder of human frailty. The believer accepts this end with dignity, knowing that the grave is a garden of Paradise or a pit of Hell depending on one’s deeds. The denier, who never prepared, faces it with nothing but the terror of what follows. The binding of the feet also signifies the complete helplessness. The legs that could have walked to the mosque, walked to help the poor, walked to visit the sick, are now bound. The opportunity for voluntary movement is over.
30 | On that day they will be driven to your Lord ˹alone˺. | The journey of the denier continues from the deathbed and the shroud to the final destination. “On that day they will be driven to your Lord alone.” The Arabic “ila rabbika yawma’idhin al-masaq” means the driving, the herding, is toward your Lord. The word “masaq” implies being driven like cattle, forcefully and without choice. The angels will drive the soul, and later the resurrected body, toward the judgment of Allah. There is no escape route, no detour, no hiding place. The phrase “your Lord” is addressed to the Prophet, but it also applies to every human being—each person will be driven to his Lord. The mention of “Lord” here is significant because it reminds the listener that the destination is not an impersonal court but the presence of the Creator, the Sustainer, the One who was worshiped or rejected. The deniers will stand before the very Lord they turned away from. The word “alone” indicates that there will be no helpers, no intercessors to divert the path. The driving is direct and singular in its goal. The verse echoes the earlier statements that the wicked will have no refuge and no escape. The shroud and the binding of the feet are the prelude to this forceful march. In the world, the denier walked freely, choosing his own paths of misguidance. Now, he is a prisoner being transferred to the supreme court. The verse is a solemn conclusion to the scene of death. It answers the rhetorical question asked earlier by the deniers: “When is this Day of Judgment?” It tells them that the Day begins at death, with the driving to the Lord. For the believer, this verse is a reminder that the journey to Allah is inevitable. The difference is that the believer comes to his Lord willingly, through a life of worship, and his meeting is a joyous reunion. The denier is dragged there against his will, and his meeting is a horrifying confrontation. The surah has now taken the listener from the moment of death to the threshold of judgment.
31 | This denier neither believed nor prayed, | The surah now provides a summary of the denier’s life that led to such a horrific end. “This denier neither believed nor prayed.” The Arabic “fa la saddaqa wa la salla” links back to the previous verses with a particle of consequence. So, this is the person who ended up being driven to his Lord. His first crime is that he did not “saddaqa”—he did not accept the truth, he did not confirm the message, he did not believe. This is the root sin, the rejection of faith itself. The second crime, mentioned alongside and immediately after, is that he did not “salla”—he did not pray. The pairing of belief and prayer is foundational. Belief is the inner conviction, and prayer is the outer manifestation of that conviction. The one who does not pray has given evidence that his belief is either absent or fatally weak. As in the confession of the damned in the previous surah, the neglect of prayer is the first specific deed mentioned. It is the line that separates the Muslim from the non-Muslim in practice. The denier’s failure to pray is not a small oversight; it is a conscious refusal to bow to his Creator. The verse uses the past tense, looking back at the life of this individual from the perspective of the Hereafter. His life is now being summarized in these two damning negations: he did not believe, and he did not pray. Everything else that he did—his wealth, his status, his family—is irrelevant. These are the only two things that mattered in the end. The verse is a chilling warning to anyone who claims to have faith but neglects the prayers. It calls for an urgent examination of one’s own consistency. The denier’s path to Hell began with these two omissions. The surah will further elaborate on his character, but the foundation of his ruin is now clearly laid bare.
32 | but persisted in denial and turned away, | The description of the denier’s life continues with his general attitude: “but persisted in denial and turned away.” The Arabic “wa lakin kadh-dhaba wa tawalla” shows that he did not merely disbelieve passively; he actively called the truth a lie. The word “kadh-dhaba” implies a vigorous, repeated denial. He accused the Prophet of falsehood, he belied the Quran, he rejected the Day of Judgment. This was his verbal and intellectual stance. Then “tawalla” means he turned his back, he walked away physically and spiritually. He did not just disagree; he withdrew, he boycotted, he showed aversion. This is exactly the behavior described in the previous surahs: the arrogant turning away, the frowning and scowling, the flight from the reminder. The combination of “persisted in denial” and “turned away” captures the complete posture of the disbeliever. His mind is set against the truth, and his body follows. There is no openness, no willingness to consider. The verse teaches that disbelief is not merely a lack of information; it is an active state of rejection and aversion. The denier is not a seeker who couldn’t find the truth; he is a refuser who saw the truth and ran from it. This attitude is the opposite of the believer, who says, “We hear and we obey,” and who turns toward Allah in repentance and worship. The verse also connects to the earlier description of the people of Hell, who admitted they indulged in falsehood along with others. This denier is a typical example. The surah is building a case, showing that the terrible end at death and the gloomy faces on the Day of Judgment are the direct result of a life lived in denial and turning away. It is a warning that the direction one turns in this life determines the direction one is driven in the next.
33 | then went to their own people, walking boastfully. | The portrait of the denier concludes with a vivid description of his worldly demeanor: “then went to their own people, walking boastfully.” The Arabic “thumma dhahaba ila ahlihi yatamatta” means after rejecting the truth and turning away, he went back to his family, his tribe, his companions, strutting and swaggering with pride. The word “yatamatta” comes from “matya,” which relates to a camel’s gait. It describes a person walking with an affected, arrogant, self-satisfied swagger, swinging his shoulders, taking pride in his rejection. He is not ashamed of his disbelief; he flaunts it. He returns to his people as if he has achieved a victory, having dismissed the Prophet’s message. His people are like-minded deniers, and they reinforce his arrogance. The scene is a powerful contrast to the believer who hears the truth, humbles himself, and walks away with a bowed head, pondering. The denier walks as if he owns the earth, forgetting that the same feet will one day be bound in a shroud. The verse exposes the psychology of the arrogant elite. Their public swagger is a performance to maintain status and to intimidate the believers. They want everyone to see that they are unfazed by the warning. But this boastful walk is a delusion. The surah has already shown where that walk ends: at the collarbone, then in the shroud, then being driven to the Lord. The contrast between the boastful walk in the world and the humiliating march to judgment is devastating. The verse also reminds the believers not to be impressed by the swagger of the disbelievers. Their arrogance is a symptom of their disease, not a sign of strength. The real strength is in the humility of the one who bows to his Creator. The phrase “to their own people” highlights the importance of social environment. The denier surrounds himself with those who applaud his disbelief, creating an echo chamber that seals his fate. The believer is called to choose his company wisely, surrounding himself with those who encourage humility and faith, not arrogance and rejection.
34 | Woe to you, and more woe! | After narrating the life and death of the denier, the surah turns to directly address him with a thunderous threat. “Woe to you, and more woe!” The Arabic “awla laka fa-awla” is a phrase of severe threat and imminent destruction. It literally means “nearer to you, and nearer,” implying that a great evil is closing in, that destruction is drawing closer and closer. Some commentators explain it as “Woe to you, then woe to you,” a repeated curse. Others say it means “Hell is nearer to you, and nearer.” The repetition intensifies the threat. The denier who walked boastfully is now being told that the very thing he mocked is creeping toward him. Every step of his boastful walk is a step closer to the abyss. The phrase is directed at the individual denier, but it encompasses all who share his characteristics. It is a chilling moment in the surah. The tone shifts from narrative to direct, confrontational address. The listener is made to feel that these words are aimed at him if he recognizes the traits of the denier in himself. The repetition “awla fa-awla” creates a sense of an echo, as if the threat is bouncing off the walls of the denier’s consciousness. It is a divine warning that leaves no room for ambiguity. The verse is a verbal strike, a slap of reality to wake up the heedless. The woe is not just a temporary misfortune; it is the eternal woe of Hellfire. The word encompasses all the torments described earlier: the scorching fire, the shackles, the choking food, the gloomy face, the spine-breaking punishment. All of that is “awla”—it is right there, approaching fast. The believer reads this verse with a sense of gratitude that he is not among those to whom it is addressed, and with a renewed fear that he must never become like them. The verse is a sharp, unforgettable warning.
35 | Again, woe to you, and even more woe! | The surah amplifies the threat even further with a second, intensified repetition. “Again, woe to you, and even more woe!” The Arabic “thumma awla laka fa-awla” uses “thumma” to indicate a successive, piled-on woe. It is not just one woe; it is woe upon woe upon woe. The first threat was terrible, but the second is even more emphatic. This repetition is rare in the Quran and signifies the exceptional severity of the case. The denier described in these verses—the one who neither believed nor prayed, who belied and turned away, who walked boastfully—has reached a depth of wretchedness that merits this quadruple warning. The rhetorical effect is overwhelming. The listener feels the weight of divine anger pressing down. The verse leaves no room for the denier to dismiss the warning as a generic statement. It is personal, repeated, and escalating. Some scholars have noted that this is one of the most severe threats in the Quran, directed at a specific type of individual: the arrogant, wealthy, prayer-abandoning denier. The repetition also serves as a mercy, because it is a warning before the punishment. Even now, after this thunderous threat, the door of repentance is still open for the living. The verse is a final alarm bell. If a person hears “woe to you” repeated four times and still does not change his ways, what else will move him? The “woe” is a prediction, not a wish. It is the statement of a fact in the divine knowledge. For the believer, this verse is a source of profound reflection. It makes him cling to prayer and humility, lest he ever develop the slightest trace of the denier’s arrogance. The surah has now completed its direct warning to the individual denier, and it will shift to a broader argument for the resurrection.
36 | Do people think they will be left without purpose? | The surah now transitions from the specific threat to a universal, philosophical argument for the resurrection. “Do people think they will be left without purpose?” The Arabic “ayahsabu al-insanu an yutraka sudan” asks a rhetorical question that strikes at the core of human arrogance. “Sudan” means aimless, neglected, left to wander without any accountability, without any command or prohibition, without any ultimate judgment. It describes a state of being utterly free of responsibility, like a camel left to graze with no shepherd. The question implies that such a thought is absurd and insulting to the Creator. Would Allah, who created the heavens and the earth with such precision, who formed the human being in stages, simply abandon His creation to eat, drink, and die like animals? The human being is “al-insan,” the species known for its intellect and moral capacity. To think that such a creature would be left without purpose is a failure of basic reasoning. The verse challenges the materialist worldview that sees life as a meaningless accident. It asserts that the very existence of human consciousness, morality, and the longing for justice point to a higher purpose and an eventual judgment. The disbelievers’ denial of the resurrection is rooted in this false assumption: that they are their own masters, left to do as they please without consequence. The surah refutes this by appealing to the wisdom of Allah. A creator who designs a magnificent machine does not leave it to rust without a manual or a purpose. Every prophet was sent to inform humanity that they are not “sudan,” that they have a purpose, and that they will be held accountable. The verse is a powerful call to reflect on the meaning of life. It tells the believer to live with purpose and to prepare for the meeting with the One who gave that purpose. The next verses will provide the biological evidence for this purpose by reminding the human being of his humble origin.
37 | Were they not ˹once˺ a sperm-drop emitted? | The surah now presents the first proof of resurrection: the very origin of human life. “Were they not once a sperm-drop emitted?” The Arabic “alam yaku nutfatan min maniyyin yumna” reminds the human being of his most humiliating stage. The “nutfah” is a tiny drop of fluid, a microscopic entity. “Maniyyin” is the seminal fluid that is “yumna”—emitted, ejaculated, poured forth. The human being who now walks boastfully, who denies the resurrection, who thinks he is a great and independent being, was once that insignificant drop. This is a fact that no one can deny. The verse uses it as a devastating argument against arrogance. How can a creature whose origin is so lowly and base question the power of the One who created him from that drop? If Allah could bring a fully formed, intelligent, seeing, hearing human being from a fluid that is almost nothing, then bringing him back to life after death is an easier task in comparison. The logic is irrefutable. The word “yumna” meaning emitted also emphasizes the passive, involuntary nature of the beginning. The human being did not choose to be created; he was the result of a biological process designed and executed by Allah. The verse strips away all pretensions of self-made grandeur. Every person, regardless of wealth or status, shares this same humble origin. The denier who looks down on others should remember that he began as a despised drop. The argument from creation is used throughout the Quran. It appeals to the rational mind. The one who can create from nothing can certainly recreate. The verse also connects to the earlier warning: the boastful walker, the prayer-abandoning denier, was once just a sperm-drop. How did he become so arrogant? The reminder is a mercy to bring him back to humility and to acceptance of the truth.
38 | Then they became a clinging clot ˹of blood˺, then He developed and perfected their form, | The verse continues the stages of human creation, adding detail and wonder. “Then they became a clinging clot of blood, then He developed and perfected their form.” The Arabic “thumma kana ‘alaqatan fa-khalaqa fa-sawwa” takes the listener on a journey. After the sperm-drop, the next stage is the “‘alaqah,” a leech-like, clinging clot that attaches to the uterine wall. This is a stage of utter dependence and primitive structure. Then “fa-khalaqa” means He created, shaped, and formed the being. He gave it proportions, organs, limbs. Then “fa-sawwa” means He perfected, balanced, and fashioned the form in the best shape. The process moves from a single cell to a complex organism with systems, senses, and a unique face and fingerprints. The verbs are all in the third person singular, referring to Allah alone. No one else intervened. This gradual, meticulous development is a proof of the Creator’s wisdom, knowledge, and power. The verse marvels at the human creation as a sign. Every human being passes through these stages in the womb, completely unaware, utterly vulnerable. The same power that orchestrated this symphony of development can surely orchestrate the resurrection. In fact, the resurrection is described in the Quran as an easier feat than the original creation. The mention of “perfected their form” is also a subtle answer to the question of whether the exact same body will be resurrected. The One who perfected the form the first time knows every detail and can perfectly reconstruct it, even the very fingertips, as mentioned earlier. The verse inspires awe and gratitude in the believer. He looks at himself and sees the trace of the divine Artist. The denier should feel the weight of this evidence and realize how irrational his denial is. The journey from a sperm-drop to a perfected human is a daily miracle that is ignored. The surah forces the listener to stop and think.
39 | producing from it both sexes, male and female. | The final detail of the creation argument is the emergence of gender. “Producing from it both sexes, male and female.” The Arabic “fa-ja‘ala minhu al-zawjayn al-dhakara wa al-untha” means from that originally single, undifferentiated drop, Allah made the two paired types: the male and the female. This is a profound biological truth. The sperm-drop does not contain a fully formed miniature human; it is the combination of the male and female gametes, and the determination of the sex is part of the divine creative process. The verse highlights the wonder of reproduction. From a single, homogeneous fluid, two vastly different beings can emerge, each with distinct reproductive organs, physiological characteristics, and psychological dispositions. The male and female are “zawjayn,” a pair, designed to complement each other and continue the species. This is another sign of the Creator’s design. The verse refutes those who attribute creation to nature or chance. The consistent production of male and female, the precise balance of sexes in the population, is a sign of intelligent purpose, not randomness. The mention of “from it” (minhu) referring to the sperm-drop, emphasizes the humble origin. The mighty human male, with all his strength, and the beautiful human female, with all her grace, both came from the same base fluid. There is no room for gender-based arrogance. Both are equally the creation of Allah from a lowly origin. The verse also serves as a bridge to the final argument: if Allah can do all this, can He not bring the dead back to life? The creation of the sexes is one of the great signs that are repeatedly mentioned in the Quran. It is a call to reflect on the obvious, daily miracle of birth. Every newborn child is a proof that the Creator is not incapable of resurrection. The denier is left with no intellectual escape. The evidence is in his own body, in his own origin, in the very fact that he exists as a male or female.
40 | Is such ˹a Creator˺ unable to bring the dead back to life? | The surah concludes its argument with a powerful, rhetorical question that is the climax of the entire discourse. “Is such a Creator unable to bring the dead back to life?” The Arabic “alaysa dhalika bi-qadirin ‘ala an yuhyiya al-mawta” literally means “Is not that (Creator) powerful over giving life to the dead?” The demonstrative pronoun “dhalika” (that) refers to the Creator who has just been described: the One who created from a sperm-drop, who developed the clinging clot, who perfected the form, who made the two sexes. The question appeals directly to the intellect and the innate human sense of logic. If someone can build a complex machine from raw materials, no one would doubt that he can repair it if it breaks. If Allah can create a human being from a drop of fluid, how can anyone doubt His ability to recreate that human being after death? The word “qadir” indicates complete, unrestricted power. The question is rhetorical, expecting only the answer “Of course He is able.” The verse does not end with a statement but with a question to engage the mind actively. The listener is forced to answer silently, and in doing so, he affirms the very truth he might have been denying. This is the Quranic method of persuasion at its finest. The surah, which began with the oath by the Day of Judgment and the self-reproaching soul, now ends with the logical conclusion that the Day is not only possible but inevitable, given the power of the Creator. The question also carries a moral weight. If the Creator is able to resurrect, then He will resurrect, and the accountability that the deniers mocked is a certainty. The verse is a call to prepare. The one who believes in the resurrection will live a life that reflects that belief. The one who denies it is living in a dangerous delusion. The surah’s final note is one of hope and warning, leaving the listener with the clear message: the Creator who gave you life once will certainly give it again, so live accordingly.
