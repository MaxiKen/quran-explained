1 | Competition for more ˹gains˺ diverts you ˹from Allah˺, | After the surah of the Striking Disaster placed the reality of the Hereafter directly before the soul, this surah turns to the root disease that makes a person’s scale light on that Day. The verse opens with a sharp diagnosis: “Competition for more gains diverts you from Allah.” The Arabic term “at-takâthur” goes far beyond simply wanting more. It is the frantic race to outdo others in wealth, children, status, possessions, or followers, a rivalry that becomes an obsession consuming the mind and heart. The word paints the picture of two parties each striving to say “I have more than you,” so that life turns into a scoreboard of accumulation. The verb “diverts” translates “alhâkum,” which comes from lahw, meaning entertainment or distraction that makes one oblivious to what truly matters. The root carries the sense of something that occupies you so completely that you forget the purpose of your existence. And what is it that you forget? The translation adds “from Allah” to make explicit what the Arabic implies: that this diversion pulls you away from the remembrance of Allah, from preparing for the meeting with Him, and from the deeds that give weight to the scale. This is not a distraction that merely wastes an hour; it is a lifelong obsession that continues relentlessly. The Prophet ﷺ once warned, “If the son of Adam had a valley full of gold, he would desire to have two valleys. Nothing will fill his mouth except the dust of the grave.” This hadith is a perfect commentary on takâthur, showing that the greed for more is insatiable and ceases only when a person is buried. In the Meccan context, the Quraysh prided themselves on their caravans, their large clans, and their tribal superiority. They competed in boasting about ancestors and riches, and this competition consumed their gatherings. Some reports indicate that the surah was revealed when two tribes from the Ansâr, Banû Ḥârithah and Banû al-Ḥârith, boasted about their numbers and even visited the graveyard to count their dead ancestors, each claiming greater numbers. The verse thus applies to any rivalry that distracts the human being from his ultimate return to Allah. It is a merciful jolt: look at what has taken hold of your life, look at what has stolen your attention from the Eternal.
2 | until you end up in ˹your˺ graves. | The diversion described in the first verse is not a passing phase; it has a terrifying endpoint: “until you end up in your graves.” The Arabic says “ḥattâ zurtum al-maqâbir,” which literally reads “until you visit the graves.” The word “zurtum” comes from ziyârah, a visit, but here it carries a double meaning that makes the verse even more piercing. On one level, it describes the final outcome: you remain preoccupied with rivalry and accumulation until death overtakes you, and you are placed in the graves as a visitor who will then depart to the Hereafter. Every grave is a temporary lodging; the dead are visitors waiting for the Day of Resurrection. This meaning alone is enough to cut through the illusions of worldly permanence. The one who spent his life chasing more is finally stopped by the walls of the grave, where his wealth and boasting companions can no longer help him. On another level, the verse refers to the actual practice of the boastful tribes who would go to the graves of their forefathers to count them and compete in ancestral glory. They were literally visiting the graveyards to fuel their takâthur. The verse tells them that the graves they visit for boasting are the very destination that awaits them, and there the competition will end in silence and regret. The transition from the diversion to the grave is swift, as if to say that death arrives without warning, cutting off the race before any prize is truly claimed. The Prophet ﷺ said, “Remember frequently the destroyer of pleasures: death.” This verse serves exactly that function. The grave is the door to the barzakh, the intermediate realm where the soul will either experience a window into Paradise or a pit of torment, and none of the competed-for possessions accompany the deceased except three things, as the hadith states: ongoing charity, beneficial knowledge, and a righteous child who prays for him. The rivalry for more produces none of these unless it is channeled into righteousness. Instead, it leaves a person empty-handed, buried in the dust, while the real assets are the deeds that were neglected during the mad race. The phrase “your graves” personalizes the warning; each listener must face their own appointment with the soil.
3 | But no! You will soon come to know. | The Quran here interrupts the flow with a severe and abrupt rebuke: “But no! You will soon come to know.” The word “kallâ” is a forceful negation, a repelling of the heedless state described in the previous verses. It is as if to say: stop, what you are doing is utterly wrong, and the reality you are ignoring will soon become painfully clear to you. The expression “you will soon come to know” (sawfa ta‘lamûn) is a threat wrapped in a promise of future certainty. The knowledge spoken of here is not intellectual information but a direct, experiential realization of the consequences of their competition. What exactly will they come to know? The answer is left hanging in the air, allowing the mind to imagine the horrors of the grave, the terror of the Resurrection, the severity of the Judgment, and the scorching Fire that was just described in the previous surah. This elliptical threat is a classical Arabic rhetorical device that makes the warning more frightening because the listener’s imagination can paint scenes more terrifying than any explicit description. Some scholars explain that the first “soon” in this verse refers to the knowledge that comes at the moment of death, when the veils are lifted and the dying person sees the angels and either the glad tidings of Paradise or the punishment of the grave. The dying person then “knows” with a certainty that no longer allows for any action or change. This makes the threat particularly sharp because death can come at any moment, making this “soon” frighteningly close for every human being. The verse is a call to wake up before it is too late, before knowledge becomes an irrevocable verdict rather than a guide to repentance. The suddenness of “kallâ” also breaks the rhythm of the surah, just as death breaks the rhythm of worldly life. It grabs the listener who might have been nodding along in abstract agreement and shakes them into a personal confrontation with their own choices. The verse does not say “the disbelievers will soon know”; it says “you will soon come to know,” making the threat direct and inescapable.
4 | Again, no! You will soon come to know. | The repetition of the same words in this verse intensifies the warning to a degree that leaves no room for escape. “Again, no! You will soon come to know.” The doubling of “kallâ” and the threat of knowing serves to emphasize that this is an absolute, unchangeable reality. Rhetorically, repetition in the Quran is never mere redundancy; it is a way of driving a point deep into the heart. Just as a parent might repeat a warning to a child who is not paying attention, Allah repeats the threat to jolt the heedless soul out of its slumber. Many scholars of tafsir have explained that the two occurrences refer to two distinct stages of knowledge. The first “you will come to know” refers to the knowledge that strikes at death or in the grave, when the soul begins to experience the first payment of its deeds. The second “you will come to know” refers to the knowledge that arrives on the Day of Resurrection when the full reckoning is unveiled, when the person sees the Fire with the eye of certainty and is questioned about every pleasure. This layering of warnings reflects the Quran’s method of building psychological pressure to inspire urgency. It also mirrors the earlier structure of Sûrah Al-Qâri‘ah, where the question about the Striking Disaster was repeated to magnify its importance. Here, the threat is repeated to show that the diversion of takâthur is so deadly that it deserves a double warning. The believer who hears this verse should feel as though a second alarm bell is ringing, making it impossible to ignore. If the first “soon” could be rationalized away, the second confirms that there is no escape. The repetition also serves as a mercy; it gives another chance to listen, another moment to reflect. It is as if the Quran is saying: I have told you once, and now I tell you again, do not let this competition swallow your soul. The verse also sets up the conditional statement that follows, contrasting the hypothetical “if you knew with certainty” with the certain knowledge that will come too late. The repeated “no!” acts as a barrier between the world of heedlessness and the world of imminent accountability.
5 | Indeed, if you were to know ˹your fate˺ with certainty, ˹you would have acted differently˺. | This verse introduces a powerful conditional sentence that drives the warning to its logical conclusion. It says: “Indeed, if you were to know your fate with certainty, you would have acted differently.” The translation expands the Arabic “‘ilma al-yaqeen” (the knowledge of certainty) to “your fate with certainty” to make the implication clear: this knowledge is about the ultimate consequences of your actions, the reality of the Hereafter that you are neglecting. The verse presents a hypothetical scenario that exposes the root cause of heedlessness. If these people possessed certainty (yaqeen), a knowledge so firm that it dispels all doubt and becomes the driving force of their lives, they would never have allowed competition for worldly gains to divert them. But they lack this certainty; their knowledge of the Hereafter, if they have any, is a superficial acknowledgment that does not translate into action. The classical scholars outline three ascending levels of certainty found in this surah and elsewhere. The first level is ‘ilm al-yaqeen, knowledge based on authentic report and sound reasoning—the certainty a person gains from hearing the truth and believing it. The second level is ‘ayn al-yaqeen, the eye of certainty, which comes from seeing with one’s own eyes, mentioned later in verse seven. The third is haqq al-yaqeen, the absolute truth of direct experience, which is the reality of entering Paradise or Hell. This verse laments that if only people had that first level of genuine certainty, they would shun the rivalry that leads to ruin. The implied answer to the conditional is left to the listener’s conscience: but you do not know with certainty, and that is why you continue to compete. The translation adds “you would have acted differently” to complete the meaning, and indeed this is the understood apodosis: you would have turned away from frivolous competition and devoted yourselves to what saves you. The verse is a profound call to seek yaqeen through reflection on the signs of Allah and the truth of revelation, so that certainty enters the heart before it is too late. It reminds us that every sin and every act of heedlessness is ultimately the fruit of a weakness in our certainty about the Day of Judgment.
6 | ˹But˺ you will surely see the Hellfire. | Having laid out the hypothetical of what certainty would produce, the verse now moves to the stark reality: “But you will surely see the Hellfire.” The translation inserts “But” to contrast with the unrealized wish of the previous verse. The Arabic opens with the emphatic particle “la” attached to the verb “tarawunna,” creating a forceful oath-like statement: you shall certainly, without any doubt, see the Blazing Fire. This seeing is not optional and not reserved for some distant possibility; it is an inevitable encounter that every single human being will experience. The Prophet ﷺ explained that everyone will see Hell on the Day of Resurrection when the bridge (ṣirâṭ) is placed over it, and all of humanity will pass over it. The righteous will cross safely at speeds according to their deeds, while the wicked will slip and fall into it. Thus, even the believers will see Hell with their own eyes as they cross above it, a terrifying sight that will make the hearts of the prophets cry out, “O Allah, save us, save us.” The use of the future “will surely see” brings the listener from the abstract knowledge discussed earlier into the realm of sensory experience. The veils that currently hide the unseen world will be torn away, and the Hellfire that was described in words will become a visible, audible, terrifying reality. This seeing is the first stage of the direct encounter with the consequences of one’s choices. The verse thus serves as a bridge between the intellectual warning and the palpable confrontation. The emphatic certainty of the statement erases any room for doubt or hope that perhaps the warnings are metaphorical. It ties back directly to the closing of Sûrah Al-Qâri‘ah where Hell was described as a scorching Fire; now the listener is told that they will personally see that Fire with their own gaze. The psychological impact is immense. The Quran is not merely saying “Hell exists”; it is saying “you will see it.” This personalizes the threat and makes it a matter of individual destiny, urging the listener to live in a way that makes the sight of Hell a passing moment of gratitude for salvation rather than an entrance into eternal torment.
7 | Again, you will surely see it with the eye of certainty. | The threat is intensified once more through repetition, now coupled with a new phrase that escalates the level of encounter. “Again, you will surely see it with the eye of certainty.” The Arabic “thumma latarawunnahâ ‘ayna al-yaqeen” employs the word “‘ayn,” meaning the physical eye. This is the second level of certainty, the certainty that comes from direct, unmediated vision. The earlier verse spoke of seeing in general; this verse specifies that the seeing will be with the very organ of sight, a personal, undeniable visual experience. The repetition also parallels the double warning earlier in the surah, creating a symmetrical structure that reinforces the message. The first seeing might be understood as the general sight of Hell from afar or as the initial glimpse, while the second seeing with the “eye of certainty” indicates a close-up, detailed, and prolonged observation that leaves absolutely no doubt. Some scholars explain that the first seeing refers to when the Fire is brought forth on the Day of Resurrection, visible to all, and the second seeing refers to the moment the disbelievers are cast into it and stare at its horrors from within. For the believers, this eye of certainty will be the sight that causes them to praise Allah for His mercy, as they witness the punishment they have been saved from. For the heedless, it will be the horrific confirmation that what they were warned about is absolutely true, and now it is too late. The term “eye of certainty” also contrasts with the “knowledge of certainty” mentioned earlier. The surah thus traces the tragic journey of the soul from possible knowledge that could have led to action, to the missed opportunity, and then to the enforced certainty of sight that cannot change one’s destiny. This progression is deeply sobering. It teaches that the window for benefiting from certainty is in this life, through faith in the unseen. Once the unseen becomes seen, the test is over. The verse stands as a final, thundering declaration that the reality of Hell is not a matter for philosophical debate but an object that will be visibly presented before every human eye.
8 | Then, on that Day, you will definitely be questioned about ˹your worldly˺ pleasures. | The surah closes with a verse that ties all the preceding warnings directly to the daily actions of every human being. “Then, on that Day, you will definitely be questioned about your worldly pleasures.” The word “thumma” (then) indicates a sequence: after you see the Hellfire with the eye of certainty, the questioning will begin. And the subject of the questioning will be the “na‘eem,” the pleasures and blessings that you enjoyed in the worldly life. The competition for more gains, which began the surah, is now revealed as the very matter that will be investigated. Every moment of ease, every delicious meal, every cool drink, every comfortable home, every healthy day, and every joyful experience will be brought forth for accounting. The Prophet ﷺ elaborated on this in a profound hadith narrated by Abû Hurayrah, where after the revelation of this verse, the companions asked, “O Messenger of Allah, which pleasures? The two black things (dates and water) are our only pleasures, and our swords are on our shoulders (i.e., we are in a state of struggle).” The Prophet ﷺ replied, “Indeed, that will be so,” meaning even those simple pleasures will be questioned. Another narration states that the first thing a servant will be questioned about from the pleasures of the world is being asked, “Did We not give you a healthy body and quench your thirst with cool water?” The questioning about pleasures is not limited to luxuries; it encompasses every blessing, from the gift of eyesight to the shade of a tree. The key issue is not the enjoyment itself but gratitude and responsibility. Did you acknowledge the Giver of the blessing? Did you use it in obedience or in sin? Did you give the rights associated with it, such as charity from wealth and using strength for good? The verse transforms the very concept of worldly enjoyment from a right into a trust that will be audited. This is the ultimate answer to the takâthur that opened the surah: the more you acquire, the more you will be asked about. The verse urges the believer to pause before every pleasure and ask, “How will I answer my Lord about this?” It turns even a sip of water into a moment of conscious gratitude and accountability. Thus the surah concludes with a question that should echo in the heart of every listener long after the recitation ends, a question that makes every blessing a bridge to remembrance or a witness against the heedless.
