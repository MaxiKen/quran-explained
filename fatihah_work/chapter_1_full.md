## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:1

> In the Name of Allah—the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful.

**Commentary:**

The Fātiḥah — the "Opener," the *Umm al-Kitāb*, the Mother of the Book — begins as it shall be invoked by the servant at every threshold of worship for the rest of the servant's life: with the *Basmalah*, *Bismillāh al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*, "In the Name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful." No sūrah of the Qur'an opens the door of its recitation without this formula except one (Sūrah al-Tawbah, 9), and its omission there is itself a statement the mufassirūn have pondered for a thousand years. The Basmalah is a gateway: before any act, before any word of this Book is read, the reciter seals his intention to the One who sent it down. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, in a hadith transmitted by Abū Dāwūd and Ibn Mājah, "Every important matter that is not begun with the mention of Allah is severed [of blessing]" — and in the variant reported in the collections, "is cut off and devoid of blessing." To begin with the Name is to acknowledge that nothing proceeds from one's own power but only from Allah (الله), that one's tongue and hand are instruments of a Will greater than one's own. This is why the Basmalah is recited before the daily actions of the Muslim's life — eating, drinking, entering a home, mounting a journey — so that the Name of Allah (الله) is never absent from the corners of an ordinary day.

The opening preposition *bi-* (with/by means of) deserves attention. The Basmalah does not say "in the existence of Allah" but "in the Name of Allah" — it seeks the *instrument* of the divine Name as the means of the act that follows. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early exegetes explained that the servant says "in the name of Allah" as a seeking of help and blessing (*istiʿānah* and *tabarruk*) through the remembrance of His Name, not through the essence itself, which no creature can reach. The Name is the bridge between the creature and the Creator: the servant cannot grasp the Essence of Allah (الله), so he grasps His Name, and through that Name he approaches Him. The grammarians of Basra and Kūfah debated what is hidden after the *bā* — is it *aqraʾu* (I recite), *arīdu* (I intend), or *atabarraku* (I seek blessing)? The most comprehensive understanding, held by Sībawayh's school, is that the suppressed verb is general: "I begin, by the Name of Allah…" — so that every action is begun in His Name and for His sake. The Basmalah, then, is a declaration of the priority of Allah (الله) over every human undertaking: the Name comes before the deed, just as knowledge of the Commander must precede obedience to the command.

The three Names chosen here are not arbitrary. *Allāh* is the personal Name, *al-ism al-aʿẓam*, the proper noun of the Divine Essence, the name that gathers within it all the attributes of perfection and majesty; the later theologians said that whereas the other names describe Allah (الله), this Name denotes Allah (الله). It is the Name that the tongues of all creation, the Arabic of the Qur'an in particular, reserve exclusively for the one true God — the *Ilāh* — and the Qur'an confirms this exclusivity when it demands, "So know that there is no deity worthy of worship except Allah" (47:19). Upon this Name the whole sentence turns, and it is the pivot upon which the entire Fātiḥah, and indeed the entire Qur'an, revolves.

The two adjectives that follow derive from a single root, *r-ḥ-m*, the root of *raḥmah* (mercy, compassion, tenderness). *Al-Raḥmān* is the intensely and continuously Merciful — a form (*faʿlān*) that, in Arabic, denotes intensity and plenitude of the attribute; it is used in the Qur'an almost exclusively of Allah (الله) and carries the sense of mercy that embraces all of creation without distinction, the mercy by which the rain falls on the just and the unjust alike. *Al-Raḥīm* is the ever-compassionate — the *faʿīl* form denoting a constant, abiding quality — and is frequently understood, following the mufassirūn and the scholars of the creed, as the specific mercy that Allah (الله) bestows upon the believers in particular. The classical exegetes distilled the difference into a famous dictum: *al-Raḥmān* is the mercy that encompasses all things, as the Qur'an says, "My mercy encompasses all things" (7:156); *al-Raḥīm* is the mercy reserved for those who are grateful and follow the guidance. Al-Qurṭubī narrates that the Prophet (ﷺ) said, "Al-Raḥmān is the Name that is specific to Allah alone; not a single human is described as Raḥmān, whereas people can be described as Raḥīm." Thus the first Name of the opening verse testifies to the *general* mercy that sustains the whole cosmos, and the second to the *particular* mercy that saves the believers — and both are placed before the servant, so that every reading of this sūrah begins and ends in the embrace of a mercy that never fails.

The status of the Basmalah within the Qur'an is itself a matter the scholars have weighed. It is undoubtedly part of a verse of the Qur'an at Sūrah al-Naml 27:30 — "Indeed, it is from Solomon, and indeed, it is: 'In the Name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful.'" Whether it is a verse of every sūrah at its head, as the reciters of Madinah and Kūfah held and as is reflected in the mushaf of the majority, or a mere divider and invocation between the sūrahs, as a minority held, the practical reality is the same for the believer: it is recited aloud at the head of the Fātiḥah in prayer by the reciters of most of the world, and its recitation is a sunnah before the reading of every sūrah. The Prophet (ﷺ) is reported in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim to have recited "Bismillāhi al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm" aloud in prayer, and in another hadith the Prophet (ﷺ) said: "Whoever is granted the mercy of saying the Basmalah, and every matter begun with it is blessed." The placement of this verse at the head of the Mother of the Book is a divine ordering: the servant approaches Allah (الله) only through His mercy, and the reciter who begins with these Names is, from the very first breath, taught that the entire relationship between the creature and the Creator — from the opening of the Book to the opening of every deed — is grounded not in fear and dread alone, but in a mercy that precedes even the command and the prohibition.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:2

> All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds,

**Commentary:**

Having opened with the Name, the sūrah immediately moves to the second great utterance of the servant: *al-ḥamdu lillāhi Rabb al-ʿālamīn*, "All praise is for Allah, Lord of all the worlds." The movement is deliberate and profound. The Basmalah taught the servant whose Name he begins with; this verse teaches what that Being deserves — *ḥamd*, praise. Praise is not merely thanks; the scholars of Arabic distinguish *ḥamd* (praise) from *shukr* (thanksgiving). *Shukr* is gratitude for a benefit received, and is the response of one who has been given something. *Ḥamd* is praise and commendation for excellence and perfection of attributes, whether or not a benefit has reached the praiser; it is praise for what the praised one *is*, not merely for what he has *given*. The *ḥamd* of this verse is therefore unconditional and absolute — Allah (الله) is praised for His very Being, for His mercy, His power, His knowledge, His justice, regardless of what He grants to the one who praises. Ibn al-Qayyim observed that *ḥamd* is rooted in the perfection of the praised one, while *shukr* is rooted in the benefit conferred, and that the Prophet (ﷺ) praised Allah (الله) at the beginning of his sermons with the *ḥamd* that his Lord deserves.

The definite article in *al-ḥamd* ("the praise," "all praise") is for *istighrāq* — it encompasses every praise, all praise in its totality. And the phrase *al-ḥamd lillāh* restricts that totality to Allah (الله) alone: every act of praise, in every language, from every tongue, is due ultimately to Him, because He is the origin of every excellence. When a human being is praised for wisdom or generosity or beauty, the praise is really a fragment of the praise due to the One who bestowed those qualities, for "all praise is for Allah." The Prophet (ﷺ) said, "Purity is half of faith, and al-ḥamdu lillāh fills the scale" (reported by Muslim), teaching that the praise of Allah (الله) is among the heaviest of deeds upon the balance of the Hereafter. He also said, in a hadith that the scholars have treasured, "The most beloved of speech to Allah is four: subḥānallāh, wal-ḥamdu lillāh, wa lā ilāha illallāh, wallāhu akbar" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim).

Then comes the first attribute of Allah (الله) named in this sūrah: *Rabb al-ʿālamīn*, the Lord of all the worlds. The word *Rabb* in Arabic denotes the master, the owner, the one who cherishes and brings up, the one who sustains and nurtures and governs. It comes from the root *r-b-b* meaning to nurture, to bring to completion, to develop. The *Rabb* is not merely a creator who made the world and left it; He is the constant, ongoing sustainer who raises creation from one stage of perfection to the next, who provides for every creature its portion, who administers the cosmos from moment to moment. The Qur'an affirms this continuous governance: "Indeed, your Lord is Allah, who created the heavens and the earth in six days and then established Himself above the Throne, arranging every matter" (10:3). The *Rabb* of this verse is therefore not a distant deity of the deists — a first cause who wound the clock and withdrew — but the intimate, ever-present Sustainer to whom every atom of existence is indebted at every instant.

*Al-ʿālamīn*, "the worlds," is a plural of *ʿālam*, which the exegetes define broadly: the worlds of the angels, of the humans, of the jinn, of the heavens, of the earth, of every species and every realm of existence. Ibn ʿAbbās and the early mufassirūn explained that *ʿālamīn* refers to all that exists other than Allah (الله) — every created thing, from the highest throne to the smallest particle, constitutes a "world." Al-Rāghib al-Iṣfahānī noted that *ʿālam* is derived from *ʿalāmah* (sign, marker), because every created world is a sign (*āyah*) pointing to its Creator. When the servant says "Lord of all the worlds," he confesses that his own world — with all its concerns, anxieties, and ambitions — is but one world among countless others, all of them under the same Rabb. This confession enlarges the heart: the One I worship is not merely the lord of my house, my nation, my century, but the Lord of every world that has ever existed or ever will, of realms beyond my imagination. The phrase is a lens of expansion; it cures the provincialism of the human spirit by placing it within the governance of an infinite Sustainer.

The *ḥamd* and the *Rabb* are joined here because gratitude and praise flow naturally from the recognition of nurture and provision. The child who has been raised and cared for by a loving parent naturally praises that parent; the creature who has been sustained, nurtured, and governed by the Rabb from the first moment of existence owes Him a debt of praise that can never be discharged. "And He gave you from all you asked of Him, and if you should count the favors of Allah, you could not enumerate them" (14:34). This verse, then, is the servant's acknowledgment of that debt: before he asks for anything, before he makes a single petition of the Fātiḥah, he first praises — because gratitude is the precondition of asking. The seeker who approaches a king with praise before petition is more likely to be granted his need; and the servant who opens his worship with *al-ḥamdulillāh* has placed himself in the posture most pleasing to the Rabb. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught that when the servant says in his prayer, "All praise is for Allah, Lord of all the worlds," Allah (الله) says, "My servant has praised Me" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, in the hadith of the division of the Fātiḥah between Allah and His servant), and the scholars have noted that the Fātiḥah is divided equally between the servant and the Lord, each verse of praise belonging to Allah and each verse of petition belonging to the servant — an arrangement that teaches that the worship which reaches Allah (الله) begins in praise and ends in need, and that the two cannot be separated.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:3

> the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful,

**Commentary:**

This verse repeats the two divine epithets already met in the Basmalah — *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm*, the Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful. The repetition is not redundancy; it is emphasis and placement with purpose. Having been told in the previous verse that Allah (الله) is the Rabb, the Lord and Sustainer of all the worlds, the servant might recoil in awe — a Lord who governs infinite worlds, whose power sustains the cosmos, whose reckoning is final. At precisely that moment of overwhelm, this verse interjects the twin Names of mercy: this Rabb, this Lord of all the worlds, is al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm, the intensely Merciful, the ever-Compassionate. Power alone terrifies; mercy alone sentimentalizes; but power joined to mercy is the God of Islam — majestic in His sovereignty, tender in His care. The scholars of the creed have pointed out that the entire opening of the Fātiḥah is a ladder of attributes arranged to draw the servant toward Allah (الله) without despair and without presumption: the Name (1:1), then praise and Rabb-hood (1:2), then mercy (1:3), then dominion over the Day of Judgment (1:4). Each rung builds on the last.

The pairing *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* after *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* answers a question the heart naturally asks: if Allah (الله) is the Lord of all the worlds and will judge all of them, can such a Sovereign be approached? Can this infinite Lord be petitioned by a weak, failing servant? The answer is the very next breath: yes, because He is al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm. Mercy is not an occasional mood of Allah (الله) but His abiding attribute, the one that He has written upon His own Self: "Your Lord has decreed upon Himself mercy" (6:54). The Prophet (ﷺ) taught, "Verily, Allah has one hundred mercies, of which He sent down one among the jinn, humans, and animals — and it is by this mercy that they show compassion to one another; and He reserved ninety-nine mercies for the Day of Resurrection" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). Even the compassion a mother feels for her child — the tenderness that the Prophet (ﷺ) invoked when he told the Companions, "Allah is more merciful to His servants than this mother is to her child" — is but a fraction of a single one of Allah's (الله) mercies.

The two Names also define the two modes of divine mercy in operation. *Al-Raḥmān* — that form of intensity — is the mercy that runs through all of creation indiscriminately: it is the mercy by which the sun rises over the believer and the disbeliever alike, by which rain falls upon the righteous and the wicked, by which every creature, even the most defiant, is fed and sheltered. This is the mercy of provision and sustenance that no one can escape, and it corresponds to the *Rabb* of the previous verse: the Lord sustains all His creatures with a universal mercy. *Al-Raḥīm* — the form of constancy — is the mercy of acceptance, guidance, and forgiveness that is specifically bestowed upon those who believe, repent, and obey: the mercy by which a repentant servant is forgiven, by which the believer is guided to the Straight Path, by which the faithful are admitted to Paradise. The Qur'an itself draws this distinction when it says, "My mercy encompasses all things, and I shall decree it for those who are God-fearing and give the zakāt and who believe in Our signs" (7:156) — encompassing mercy for all, decisive mercy for the believers.

The placement of these two Names directly after *Rabb al-ʿālamīn* is a constant in the Qur'an's opening and at the head of every sūrah. The divine pattern is to join lordship and mercy so that no servant ever conceives of the Lord as harsh. When the Qur'an recounts the stories of the messengers and the punishment of the defiant, it opens and closes the sūrahs with *al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm* so that judgment is never the final word. And for the reciter of the Fātiḥah, the effect is transformative: before he comes to the terror of the Day of Judgment in the next verse, he has already been anchored in the mercy of the Judge. The mufassirūn have noted that the repetition of these Names — twice in the opening verses of the sūrah, and then a third time in the servant's petition for the Straight Path is preceded by them — is a divine insistence that the servant approach the Lord of the Day of Judgment leaning upon His mercy, not fleeing from His power. The believer does not tremble before the Judge as before a merciless executioner, but as before a merciful Father whose justice is tempered by a mercy that He has written upon Himself. This is the profound reassurance of a single verse that, on its surface, seems merely to repeat the Basmalah but in fact serves as the hinge upon which the entire opening of the Fātiḥah turns.

The two Names also carry a practical counsel for the servant who would imitate the attributes of his Lord, as the Prophet (ﷺ) taught, "Adorn yourselves with the attributes of Allah" — an instruction understood within the bounds of what creatures can share of the divine qualities. If Allah (الله) is al-Raḥmān and al-Raḥīm, the Merciful and Compassionate, then the believer is called to be merciful and compassionate to Allah's (الله) creatures. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, "The merciful are shown mercy by the Most Merciful. Have mercy on those on the earth, and the One above the heavens will have mercy upon you" (reported by Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī). The believer who recites "al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm" in every rakʿah is being taught that the worship of the Merciful must be reflected in mercy toward His creatures — toward the poor, the orphan, the weak, the animal, and even the enemy. The attribute of mercy is thus not a passive ornament of theology but a living pattern of conduct, and the scholars have long noted the connection between this verse and the prophetic command to show compassion, so that the recitation of the divine mercy becomes the fountain of human mercy.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:4

> Master of the Day of Judgment.

**Commentary:**

The fourth verse completes the portrait of the One who is worshipped: *Māliki yawmi al-dīn*, "Master of the Day of Judgment." The two readings of the first word — *Māliki* (Master, Owner) and *Maliki* (King, Sovereign), both transmitted among the ten canonical reciters — enrich rather than divide the meaning. *Mālik* is the owner who has absolute disposal over what he possesses; *Malik* is the sovereign who exercises authority and commands. When combined, as the mufassirūn have done, they yield the fullest portrait: Allah (الله) is both the absolute Owner and the absolute Sovereign of the Day of Judgment — the day on which ownership and sovereignty over all things will be fully manifested, with no share and no partner and no rival. The Prophet (ﷺ) is reported to have said that the Qur'an contains a verse that is the greatest of all — Āyat al-Kursī (2:255) — and the scholars have noted how this verse gathers the divine attributes; but this verse of the Fātiḥah performs a similar gathering in miniature, bringing the whole drama of creation to a single point: the Day of Judgment.

The *Day of Judgment* — *yawm al-dīn* — is the day of recompense, the day of requital, the day when every deed, every intention, every hidden action will be repaid in full. The word *dīn* in Arabic carries a cluster of meanings that the Qur'an employs: religion (the dīn of submission), obedience, and judgment/recompense. In this verse it is the last sense that predominates: the day of *dīn* is the day of requital, the day when each soul is repaid for what it has earned. The Qur'an confirms this understanding elsewhere: "And what can make you know what is the Day of Recompense? Again, what can make you know what is the Day of Recompense? It is the Day when no soul will possess for another soul anything, and the command, that Day, is for Allah" (82:17–19). The day is called the day of *dīn* because on it the true religion, the true reckoning, and the true sovereignty of Allah (الله) are all revealed at once. The Prophet (ﷺ) said, "The clever one is he who takes account of himself and works for what is after death, and the incapable one is he who follows his desires and has false hopes in Allah" (reported by al-Tirmidhī), and the scholars of the Hereafter have urged that the believer live every day as though this verse were being recited against him, for "Master of the Day of Judgment" is a declaration that all accounts will be settled.

The placement of this verse between the mercy of 1:3 and the worship of 1:5 is carefully ordered. The servant has been told that his Rabb is al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm; now he is told that this same merciful Rabb is the Master of the Day of Judgment. Mercy does not cancel justice; justice does not cancel mercy. They coexist in the divine Being: He is merciful, and He is the Master of the day of requital. The Fātiḥah therefore guards against two errors at once. The first error is presumption — the belief that because Allah (الله) is merciful, one may sin freely without consequence. The verse crushes this by announcing a Day of Judgment over which the Master presides. The second error is despair — the belief that because there is a Day of Judgment and a reckoning, Allah (الله) is harsh and inaccessible. The preceding verse crushes this by announcing His mercy. Between the mercy and the judgment, the servant is positioned in the only posture that is safe: worship and petition, the very next verse.

The Day of Judgment is described in the Qur'an in language that should fix the heart: "That Day, people will depart separated, to be shown their deeds. Whoever does an atom's weight of good will see it, and whoever does an atom's weight of evil will see it" (99:6–8). The absolute equity of that day is the foundation of all justice in this world — if the Master of the Day of Judgment is just, then no injustice done in this world is final, and no act of goodness is lost. This is why the confession "Master of the Day of Judgment" is a source of profound comfort to the oppressed and a source of profound warning to the oppressor. The one who is wronged in this world knows that the Master of that day will restore every right; the one who wrongs knows that every wrong will be requited. The hadith qudsī reported in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim records the Prophet's (ﷺ) narration that on the Day of Resurrection Allah (الله) will say, "O My servants, I have forbidden wrong-doing for Myself, and I have made it forbidden among you, so do not wrong one another." The confession of this verse, then, is not a mere theological proposition but a lived orientation: the believer who recites "Master of the Day of Judgment" five times a day and every time he recites the Fātiḥah is constantly re-orienting his entire life toward a day of perfect equity, so that no injustice he commits and no injustice he suffers is ever seen as final.

The mufassirūn have also noted the singular and specific address: Allah (الله) is called Master of the Day of Judgment specifically, and not Master of the days, because the Day of Judgment is the day when His mastery will be uniquely and completely manifested — on that day "the sovereignty that day is truly for Allah, the Most Merciful" (25:26), and "whose is the dominion this Day? It is for Allah, the One, the Irresistible" (40:16). In this world, sovereignty is shared, apparent, and contested: tyrants rule, justice is delayed, and the wicked seem to triumph. But on the Day of Judgment, all false sovereignty is stripped away, and only the true Master remains. To recite this verse is therefore to declare one's allegiance to the only sovereignty that is real and permanent, and to loosen one's attachment to every earthly power that is fleeting and doomed. It is, in the words of the scholars, a daily oath of loyalty to the true King, taken against the background of all the false kings and claimants of this world.

The scholars have also drawn from this verse a profound lesson about the union of love and fear in the worship of Allah (الله). The heart that truly understands "Master of the Day of Judgment" is a heart that balances hope and awe — hope in the mercy of al-Raḥmān al-Raḥīm, awe before the judgment of the Mālik. Neither hope alone nor fear alone suffices; the Qur'an describes the believers as those who "call upon their Lord in fear and hope" (32:16). The recitation of this verse, coming as it does between the mercy of the preceding verses and the worship and petition of the following ones, is the divinely appointed balance: the believer stands before the merciful Judge, neither presumptuous because of the mercy nor despairing because of the judgment, but walking the middle way of the Straight Path that the sūrah is about to define. This is why the verse is indispensable to the prayer that follows it: the worship declared in the next verse — "You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help" — is meaningful only if it is offered to the One who is truly the Master of the Day of Judgment, the only Being whose approval ultimately matters, the only sovereignty that will survive the collapse of every earthly throne.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:5

> You ˹alone˺ we worship and You ˹alone˺ we ask for help.

**Commentary:**

With this verse the Fātiḥah turns from the description of Allah (الله) to the response of the servant. Having contemplated the Name, the praise, the Lordship, the mercy, and the judgment, the servant now makes his declaration: *Iyyāka naʿbudu wa iyyāka nastaʿīn*, "You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help." The Arabic begins with the object pronoun *iyyāka* — "You" — placed before the verb, and the scholars of the Arabic language have identified this fronting (*taqdīm*) as a device of *ḥaṣr*, of restriction and exclusivity. Had the verse said "we worship You," the emphasis would be ordinary; by saying "You [alone] we worship," the meaning is restricted: we worship none but You. The fronted object is not accidental but deliberate, and the mufassirūn have extracted from it the twin pillars of the Islamic creed: *tawḥīd al-ulūhiyyah* — the oneness of Allah (الله) in worship — and *tawḥīd al-rubūbiyyah* — the oneness of Allah (الله) in lordship. We worship none but Allah, and we seek help from none but Allah.

The two clauses are joined by *wa* (and), and the scholars have pondered their relationship. Some have read them as two aspects of a single state: worship is the goal, and seeking help is the means — we worship You, and we seek Your help in performing that very worship, for we cannot worship You by our own strength. Others have read them as a summary of the entire religion: *naʿbudu* (we worship) is the essence of the dīn, and *nastaʿīn* (we ask for help) is the recognition that worship is only possible through the help of the Worshipped. Ibn Taymiyyah, in his treatise on the servant's need of Allah (الله), made this verse the foundation of the entire spiritual path: "Iyyāka naʿbudu" answers the question of the *purpose* of life (to worship Allah alone), and "iyyāka nastaʿīn" answers the question of the *means* (through Allah's help alone). Every act of the servant, whether inward devotion or outward conduct, is subsumed under these two phrases: a right intention (worship) and a dependence upon Allah (seeking help). Whoever combines them has realized the essence of servitude, *ʿubūdiyyah*.

The word *naʿbudu* (we worship) comes from the root *ʿ-b-d*, which denotes submission, humility, and service. The ʿabd is the servant, and worship (ʿibādah) is the willing, humble, obedient service rendered to Allah (الله) out of love and veneration. The scholars of the creed have defined ʿibādah comprehensively: it is a name for everything that Allah (الله) loves and is pleased with, of words and deeds, outward and inward. It includes prayer, fasting, charity, pilgrimage, honesty, kindness, patience, gratitude, and even the eating of lawful food with the intention of strengthening oneself for obedience. The Qur'an states the purpose of creation in precisely this term: "And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me" (51:56). The first clause of this verse, then, is the servant's response to that stated purpose: "Yes, my Lord — it is You whom we worship."

The word *nastaʿīn* (we ask for help) comes from the root *ʿ-w-n*, and seeking help (istiʿānah) is the acknowledgment of need and dependence. The servant declares that he does not rely upon his own strength, his own knowledge, or his own power, but upon Allah (الله) alone. This is the refutation of the self-sufficiency that the Qur'an attributes to the heedless: "But man desires to persist in sin before him. He asks, 'When is the Day of Resurrection?'" (75:5–6) — the attitude of one who thinks himself independent of Allah (الله). In contrast, the servant of this verse leans wholly upon his Lord for every matter, great and small. The Prophet (ﷺ) taught a young Companion, "If you ask, ask of Allah; and if you seek help, seek help from Allah" (reported by al-Tirmidhī), and this counsel is the practical application of the second clause of the verse. The believer does not first exhaust his own devices and then turn to Allah (الله); rather, he begins with Allah (الله), knowing that every success, every breath, every victory is from Him. "And whoever relies upon Allah — then He is sufficient for him" (65:3).

The conjunction of worship and seeking help in a single verse, in the plural voice ("we worship," "we ask for help"), carries two further lessons. First, the plural indicates that the servant is not alone — he worships within a community of servants, the ummah, standing in rows of prayer, and the Fātiḥah is prayed in congregation. The "we" teaches that worship is not a solitary, individualistic affair but a communal reality; the believer is part of a body of worshippers. Second, the structure of the verse — a statement rather than a direct address of petition — teaches the manner of worship: before asking, the servant declares his allegiance. He does not barge into the presence of the King with his requests; he first declares "You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help," aligning his heart with his tongue, so that his subsequent petition (in the next verses) proceeds from a servant who has already surrendered his will.

The mufassirūn have therefore called this verse the *fāṣil* (the divider) of the sūrah: everything before it is about Allah (الله) — His Names, His praise, His attributes; everything after it is about the servant — his worship, his petition, his path. And the two halves are joined at this verse, for it is precisely in the act of exclusive worship and exclusive reliance that the servant passes from the contemplation of the Lord to the request for guidance, which is the subject of the concluding verses. There is a further subtlety the scholars have noted: the verse teaches the correct order of prayer and dependence. The servant first pledges worship (*naʿbudu*) and only then asks for help (*nastaʿīn*), because the request for help is made from within the posture of worship, not outside it. A servant who has not yet surrendered to his Lord has no right to ask His help; but a servant who declares exclusive worship has placed himself in the position where his Lord's help becomes his due. This is the deep logic of the verse and the reason it precedes the great petition of the Fātiḥah: "Guide us along the Straight Path." Only the one who has said, with a sincere heart, "You alone we worship and You alone we ask for help," is truly prepared to ask, and to be answered.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:6

> Guide us along the Straight Path,

**Commentary:**

Having declared exclusive worship and exclusive reliance in the previous verse, the servant now makes the first and greatest of his petitions: *Ihdinā al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm*, "Guide us along the Straight Path." The verb *ihdinā* (guide us) comes from the root *h-d-y*, which in Arabic denotes guidance, direction, and leading to a path. But the mufassirūn have distinguished two levels of guidance that the Qur'an employs with this root. The first is *hidāyat al-īrshād* — the guidance of direction and instruction, by which Allah (الله) shows humanity the way through the prophets, the scriptures, and reason; this is the guidance that is given to both the believer and the disbeliever, and it is what the Qur'an means when it says, "And We guided him to the way, whether he is grateful or ungrateful" (76:3). The second is *hidāyat al-tawfīq* — the guidance of success and enablement, by which Allah (الله) opens the heart, grants the ability to act, and keeps the believer firm upon the path; this is the guidance that only Allah (الله) bestows upon whom He wills, and it is this higher guidance that the servant seeks in this verse. When the believer says "Guide us," he is not merely asking for information about the path — he already knows the path — but for the strength, the constancy, the success, and the firmness to walk upon it until death. This is why the scholars have called this petition the daily renewal of the request for *thabāt*, for steadfastness upon the dīn.

The verse is cast in the imperative (the *amr*), but it is an imperative addressed to Allah (الله) by a servant — and an imperative of supplication, of *duʿāʾ*, not of command. The servant who says "Guide us" is not giving an order to his Lord but humbly requesting a favor, and the imperative form conveys urgency and need. There is a subtle and beautiful lesson here: even the act of asking for guidance is itself a form of guidance, for the servant who is able to ask Allah (الله) to guide him has already been guided to the asking. The Prophet (ﷺ) used to make the same petition constantly, and his prayers are filled with requests for guidance and steadfastness. He taught his Companions to say, "O Turner of the hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion," and he said, "The hearts of the children of Adam are all between the two fingers of the Most Merciful, and He turns them as He wills" (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim). The recitation of "Guide us along the Straight Path" five times and more every day is thus a recognition that the heart is in the hands of Allah (الله), and that guidance is not a one-time event but a daily, continuous need.

The *Straight Path* — *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* — is the recurring image of the Qur'an. The ṣirāṭ is the road, the way; al-mustaqīm is the straight, the upright, the one that does not deviate. The Qur'an describes the path of the believers: "And indeed, this is My straight path, so follow it, and do not follow other ways, for they will separate you from His path" (6:153). The mufassirūn have expounded on what the Straight Path is with a richness that has filled volumes. Ibn ʿAbbās and the Companions explained that the Straight Path is the path of the Prophet (ﷺ) and his companions — the path of Islam, the path of the Qur'an, the path of submission to Allah (الله) in belief, word, and deed. It is the path that leads to Allah (الله) and to Paradise, and it is straight because it has no crookedness, no deviation, no excess, and no shortfall. Al-Ṭabarī reports the interpretation that the ṣirāṭ is Islam, and that its straightness is the straightness of the dīn which balances between the extremes of exaggeration and negligence. Others have said the Straight Path is the path of the Prophet (ﷺ) and his companions (reported in the tafsīr of Ibn Kathīr from the early authorities), and the Prophet (ﷺ) is reported to have explained the Straight Path with the hadith of the way: he drew a line and said, "This is the path of Allah," then drew lines to its right and left and said, "These are the other paths, on each of which is a devil calling to it," and he recited, "And indeed, this is My straight path, so follow it, and do not follow other ways" (6:153).

The petition for guidance is placed before any other request because it is the root of every other good. If the servant is guided, he will be led to every virtue; if he is misguided, every endeavor will be in vain. The scholars have noted that the Fātiḥah, having begun with praise and having established exclusive worship and reliance, now asks for the one thing that encompasses all needs: guidance. Ibn al-Qayyim wrote at length that guidance is the greatest of Allah's (الله) favors, and that the Prophet (ﷺ) was commanded in the very first revelation to ask for more of it, for "increase in guidance" is the substance of the servant's daily bread. The verse uses the first-person plural ("guide us") because the servant prays not for himself alone but for the community of believers — he asks that his family, his fellow Muslims, and the whole ummah be guided, for the guidance of the individual is bound up with the guidance of the community.

The scholars have also drawn out the specific and comprehensive meaning of the petition by contrasting it with lesser requests. The servant does not ask merely for provision, or for health, or for victory, or for any of the goods of this world — he asks for guidance, because guidance is the key that unlocks every other good and the shield that guards against every harm. One who is guided will be guided to lawful provision, to sound health used rightly, to victory in what matters, and to contentment in every state. And the verse is phrased as a continuous, ongoing request ("guide us," not "guide us once") because the path is a road that must be walked every day, and the believer needs guidance at every step. The daily repetition of this verse in the obligatory prayers, in every rakʿah of every prayer, is the believer's constant confession that he is perpetually in need of divine direction — that he cannot guide himself, that his intellect and desire are insufficient, and that the only Guide who can lead him safely to the blessed end is Allah (الله) Himself, the Master of the Day of Judgment whose mercy he has already invoked. The petition, then, is the culmination of the servant's journey through the first half of the sūrah: from the Name, to praise, to mercy, to judgment, to worship — and now, leaning upon all of that, he asks for the one thing that will carry him through this world and into the next.

## Sūrah al-Fātiḥah 1:7

> the Path of those You have blessed—not those You are displeased with, or those who are astray.

**Commentary:**

The final verse of the Fātiḥah specifies the Straight Path by describing those who walk upon it and those who do not. *Ṣirāṭ alladhīna anʿamta ʿalayhim*, "the Path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor," — *ghayr al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim*, "not [the path] of those who have earned Your anger," — *wa lā al-ḍāllīn*, "nor of those who are astray." The verse begins with the word *ṣirāṭ* (path), grammatically in apposition to the *al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm* of the previous verse, so that the Straight Path is now defined not abstractly but by its travelers. The path is not a map of doctrines alone; it is a living road walked by a living people, and the servant asks to be placed among them.

The first category is *those upon whom Allah (الله) has bestowed favor* — *alladhīna anʿamta ʿalayhim*. The Qur'an elsewhere enumerates precisely who these people are, and the verse is decisive: "And whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger — those are with the ones upon whom Allah has bestowed favor of the prophets, the steadfast affirmers of truth, the martyrs, and the righteous. And excellent are those as companions" (4:69). The favored ones, then, are the prophets (al-nabiyyīn), the truthful (al-ṣiddīqīn), the martyrs (al-shuhadāʾ), and the righteous (al-ṣāliḥīn). This verse of the Fātiḥah is thus a petition to be counted among the company of the prophets and the righteous, the best of creation. The Prophet (ﷺ), in the hadith of the division of the Fātiḥah recorded in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, reported that when the servant recites, "Guide us to the Straight Path, the path of those upon whom You have bestowed favor…" Allah (الله) replies, "This is for My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks." The mention of the four ranks in 4:69 gives the servant a concrete picture of the path he seeks: he seeks to walk the path of the prophets who delivered the message, of the truthful who confirmed it, of the martyrs who died for it, and of the righteous who lived it.

The second and third categories are the two groups whose path the servant seeks to avoid. The first is *al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim* — those who have incurred Allah's (الله) anger. The mufassirūn are nearly unanimous, on the basis of the Prophet's (ﷺ) own explanation, that these are the Jews (al-Yahūd), for they were given the scripture and the truth but rejected it and killed the prophets and broke the covenant, and the Qur'an describes them as those who "incurred the anger of Allah" (3:112). The Prophet (ﷺ) is reported to have said, "al-maghḍūbi ʿalayhim are the Jews, and al-ḍāllīn are the Christians" (reported by al-Tirmidhī and al-Ḥākim). The second is *al-ḍāllīn* — those who are astray. The mufassirūn explain these as the Christians (al-Naṣārā), for they deviated from the truth not by denying it outright but by going to extremes — exalting a human being (ʿĪsā, peace be upon him) to the rank of divinity and thus straying from the balanced path. The distinction between the two is subtle and profound: the Jews are described as having incurred anger because they *knew* the truth and rejected it — their sin was one of the heart and of willful rebellion; the Christians are described as astray because they *erred* in their knowledge — their sin was one of misguided excess and deviation in belief. The Fātiḥah thus asks to be saved from the path of those who knew and rebelled, and from the path of those who strayed and exaggerated, and to be placed instead upon the balanced, straight path of those whom Allah (الله) has favored.

This division — known and confirmed by the Prophet's (ﷺ) own commentary — is not an exercise in sectarian denigration but a precise delineation of the two fundamental deviations from the Straight Path. Every person who strays from guidance does so in one of two ways: either by knowing the truth and rejecting it (the way of anger), or by seeking the truth and missing it through excess or error (the way of straying). The servant who recites this verse is, therefore, praying to be saved from both the arrogance of the one who knows and refuses, and the carelessness of the one who seeks but wanders. The scholars have noted the order: the servant asks to be guided, then specifies that he seeks the path of the favored, then guards against the path of the angered and the strayed. The verse functions as both a positive aspiration (to be with the prophets and the righteous) and a negative safeguard (to be saved from the two classes of the lost). It is a complete map of salvation and ruin in a single breath.

The mufassirūn have also pointed out that the verse is a perfect conclusion to the sūrah because it ties the servant's daily prayer to the entire history of revelation. The "favored ones" are the prophets and their followers of every age; the "angered" and the "astray" are those who opposed or corrupted the message of every age. When the servant recites this verse, he aligns himself with the long line of the prophets — from Ādam to Nūḥ to Ibrāhīm to Mūsā to ʿĪsā to Muḥammad (peace be upon them all) — and against every deviation that has ever arisen against them. This is why the Prophet (ﷺ) is reported to have said that the Fātiḥah is the greatest sūrah of the Qur'an (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, in the hadith of the greatest sūrah), and why it is the sūrah that Allah (الله) has divided between Himself and His servant, and why it is recited in every unit of every prayer.

The sūrah that began with the Name and the mercy and the praise and the judgment and the worship ends with the plea to be guided along the path of the favored and saved from the path of the angered and the astray — and the servant who recites it, in every rakʿah of every prayer, is thereby renewing, in a handful of verses, the whole of his relationship with his Lord: praise for what Allah (الله) is, worship of what He deserves, reliance upon His help, and a plea for the guidance that will carry him to the blessed company of the prophets and the righteous, saved from the two great deviations that have ever divided humanity. It is for this comprehensiveness that the scholars have called the Fātiḥah the Mother of the Book (*Umm al-Kitāb*), the seven oft-repeated verses (*al-sabʿ al-mathānī*), and the essence of the entire Qur'an, for it contains the praise of Allah, the acknowledgment of His Lordship and mercy and judgment, the declaration of exclusive worship and reliance, and the petition for the straight path — the whole of the dīn gathered into seven verses, opened by the Basmalah and closed by the prayer for guidance.
