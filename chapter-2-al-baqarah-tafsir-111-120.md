# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 111–120

This batch moves from human claims to divine reality, and the movement is the whole point of it. It opens with two communities each claiming Paradise as its own property, and the Qur'an answering with a single demand: produce your proof (111). Then the true criterion is stated — not membership but the submission of the whole self to God, joined to a life of excellence (112). Then the sectarian reflex in its purest form, and the observation that the people without scripture made exactly the same claim as the people with it (113). Then the gravity of preventing worship and destroying houses of worship (114). And then the surah lifts entirely above the disputes and gives the theology that settles them: to God belong all directions (115), He has no offspring (116), He is the originator who needs no child (117). From there it returns to the human refusal — the perennial demand for signs (118) — and closes with two verses of consolation to the Prophet ﷺ: you are not accountable for the outcome (119), and their approval was never attainable (120).

---

## Verse 111

**"The Jews and Christians each claim that none will enter Paradise except those of their own faith. These are their desires. Reply, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Show ˹me˺ your proof if what you say is true.”"**

In simple terms: they claimed that Paradise belongs exclusively to Jews or to Christians. The Qur'an calls this what it is — a wish — and demands the evidence.

*Wa qalu lan yadkhula al-jannata illa man kana hudan aw nasara* — "and they said: none will enter Paradise except one who is *hudan* or *nasara*." Note the word *hudan*. It is the term the Qur'an uses when quoting them in their own voice (the external term is *yahud*), and it comes from the same root as *alladhina hadu* at verse 62. The quotation reproduces their self-description, which is part of what makes the reported claim so precise: the people who say it are naming themselves in their own preferred terms while excluding everyone else.

A note on who "they" are. The reported saying contains both alternatives — Jews *or* Christians — which means the verse is quoting two communities at once, each making the same exclusive claim about itself. The commentaries understood it this way: the claim was made twice, in two directions, and the Qur'an combines them into one sentence because they are the same claim.

*Tilka amaniyyuhum* — "that is their wishful thinking." The word is *amaniyy*, from the identical root as the *amani* of verse 78, where the unlettered among them "know nothing of the Book except *amani*." There I noted that the commentators divided over whether *amani* meant wishful thinking or recitation, and that both readings are transmitted. Here the context settles it decisively in favour of "wishes": an exclusive claim about who enters Paradise, made without evidence, is a desire projected onto the scripture. The surah has closed its own loop — what verse 78 called wishful thinking, verse 111 shows in operation.

*Qul hatu burhanakum in kuntum sadiqin* — "Say: produce your proof, if you are truthful." *Hatu* is an imperative meaning bring, produce. *Burhan* is not a mere argument; it is a demonstration, a clear and decisive evidence — from a root meaning to be manifest and unmistakable.

And here is the methodological principle, which is one of the most important things in the Qur'an: **the burden of proof rests on whoever makes a claim about the unseen.** This is not a demand the Qur'an makes only of others. It makes it of itself at verse 23 — "then produce a surah the like thereof" — and it states the principle again at 21:24, "Say: bring your proof, if you should be truthful."

Now, an honesty that must be stated, because this verse is constantly quoted in interfaith settings in a way it will not bear.

**The Qur'an does not object here to exclusivism as such.** It objects to exclusivism without proof. Islam is itself exclusivist in the sense that 3:85 states plainly: "And whoever desires other than Islam as religion, never will it be accepted from him, and he in the Hereafter will be among the losers." The difference between that claim and the claim of this verse is not the shape of the claim. It is the *burhan*. The Muslim claim rests on a revelation that offers itself for examination; theirs, the verse says, rests on a wish. To read verse 111 as though the Qur'an were saying "all paths are equally valid" is to make it say something it nowhere says, and it does not survive reading three verses on to 3:85.

What the verse does establish is a discipline that applies to everyone, including Muslims: if you are going to claim that salvation runs through your group and not another, you must be able to produce the text that says so, and you must be honest about what it says.

For practice, the verse's instrument is the demand for evidence, and it is transferable without limit.

Apply it outward first. Any community that claims exclusive access to salvation must produce its warrant. The claim is not illegitimate in form. It is illegitimate in the absence of the proof, and the proof must be from revelation, not from sentiment, national feeling, ancestral loyalty, or the observation that the other group behaves badly.

Then apply it inward, which is where it bites. The Muslim version of this verse's error is alive and well: the claim that only one's own school of law, one's own movement, one's own strand of the tradition, one's own teacher's followers will be saved. Each of those claims is the claim of verse 111 with a different name attached, and each of them is answerable to the same demand — *hatu burhanakum*. The person who makes such a claim and cannot produce the text should not make it again.

And there is a third application that is quieter and more useful. Most people hold beliefs about their own standing that they have never examined. "I will be fine." "God knows my heart." "I have done more good than harm." Every one of those is a claim about the unseen, and this verse's question applies to each of them with undiminished force: from where? The question is not hostile. It is the most useful question anyone can be asked, because a claim that cannot be sourced can still be corrected before the day it matters.

## Verse 112

**"But no! Whoever submits themselves to Allah and does good will have their reward with their Lord. And there will be no fear for them, nor will they grieve."**

In simple terms: the truth is the opposite of what they claimed. Whoever surrenders his whole self to God and lives a life of excellence will have his reward with his Lord, and will be safe on the Day of Resurrection.

*Bala* — "Yes indeed / on the contrary." The particle that reverses a negation. Verse 111 reported their claim that no one enters Paradise except them; this verse opens with a single word that overturns it and replaces it with the real criterion.

*Man aslama wajhahu lillahi* — "whoever submits his **face** to Allah." Why the face, and not "himself"?

Because the face is the seat of a person's honour, of his visible identity, and of the direction he is facing. To submit the face is to submit the dignity, the self-presentation, and the orientation — the three things human beings protect most fiercely. The lexicographers and commentators noted that the face is named because it is the noblest part, so that naming it implies the whole.

And there is a second sense of *wajh* in Arabic that matters here and that the Qur'an will use deliberately later in this surah: *wajh* also means direction, aspect, the way a thing faces. That is the word at the centre of the qibla verses — "turn your face (*wajh*) toward the Sacred Mosque" (2:144, 2:149–150). So the phrase *aslama wajhahu* carries both: he has submitted his dignity, and he has turned his direction entirely toward God.

The word *aslama* itself should be read in its root sense here. Before it became the proper name of a religion, *islam* meant to surrender, to hand oneself over whole. The commentators discussed whether the word in this verse refers to the specific religion revealed to Muhammad ﷺ or to the general act of self-surrender, and the majority held that the wording is general while the question of what constitutes surrender after the Prophet's mission is settled by 3:85 and the hadith cited at verse 62. The general wording is deliberate, and it means the verse's promise is genuinely open to anyone who meets its two conditions.

*Wa huwa muhsinun* — "while he is a doer of good." *Muhsin* is the station of *ihsan* — the word the Prophet ﷺ defined in the hadith of Jibril as worshipping Allah as though you see Him, and if you do not see Him, then knowing that He sees you. It is the same word used at verse 58, where Allah promises to "increase the *muhsinin*." Note the structure of the verse: surrender **and** excellence. Neither alone. Surrender without excellence is a claim; excellence without surrender is a career.

*Fa-lahu ajruhu 'inda rabbihi* — "for him is his reward with his Lord." And *wa la khawfun 'alayhim wa la hum yahzanun* — "and no fear shall be upon them, nor shall they grieve." The same closing formula as verses 38 and 62: *khawf* is dread of what lies ahead, *huzn* is sorrow over what lies behind. Both directions of the heart are covered.

A small grammatical note worth making because it is characteristic of the Qur'an: the verse opens in the singular (*man aslama*, *lahu ajruhu*) and closes in the plural (*'alayhim*, *hum*). That is because *man*, "whoever," is singular in form but plural in sense. The shift is not carelessness; it is the grammar tracking the meaning.

For practice, this verse gives the criterion and the promise, and both are needed.

The criterion is a test that can be run in an afternoon. Not: what community do I belong to? Not: what do I call myself? Not: whose name is on my certificate? The question is double and it is behavioural: have I handed my whole self over — my dignity, my direction, the parts of me I would rather not surrender — and am I living that surrender *beautifully*, with the heart in it? Most people can answer both halves honestly, and the answers usually differ.

The second application is the direction, which is the *wajh*. Every person is facing somewhere. The face is turned toward something — a career, a person, an approval, a resentment, an ambition, a fear — and the turning is visible in how he spends his hours and what he cannot bear to lose. The verse asks: which way is your face pointed, and can you tell? That is the same question the surah will ask about the qibla in thirty verses, and it is worth noting that the Qur'an treats the bodily direction and the direction of the self as one question rather than two.

And the third is the hope, which is the reason this verse is here at all. It comes immediately after a verse that demolished two communities' claims to exclusive salvation, and what it replaces them with is not a third exclusive claim. It is an open condition, stated in terms anyone can meet: surrender, and do it well. There is no one reading this who is outside the offer.

## Verse 113

**"The Jews say, “The Christians have nothing to stand on” and the Christians say, “The Jews have nothing to stand on,” although both recite the Scriptures. And those ˹pagans˺ who have no knowledge say the same ˹about people of faith˺. Surely Allah will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their dispute."**

In simple terms: each of the two communities declared the other to have no foundation at all, despite both possessing revealed scripture — and the ignorant, who had no scripture, said exactly the same thing. Allah will judge between them.

*Wa qalat al-yahudu laysat al-nasara 'ala shay'in wa qalat al-nasara laysat al-yahudu 'ala shay'in* — "the Jews say the Christians are not upon anything, and the Christians say the Jews are not upon anything." Note the form of the claim. Neither group says "we are righter." Each says the other is *'ala shay'* — **upon nothing at all**. Total negation, not comparison. That is the signature of sectarianism in every age: not the assertion of a position but the annihilation of the other's.

*Wa hum yatluna al-kitab* — "while they recite the Book." This is the aggravating clause, and it is placed exactly where it will do the most damage. Both of them have revelation. Both of them read it. And each declares the other to have nothing. The possession of scripture did not moderate the claim; it was the setting in which the claim was made. The commentators were clear that this is what makes the statement indefensible — not ignorance, but recitation combined with total denial.

*Kadhalika qala alladhina la ya'lamuna mithla qawlihim* — "thus did those who do not know say the like of their saying." Who are *alladhina la ya'lamuna*, "those who do not know"? The majority of commentators identified them as the polytheist Arabs — people with no scripture at all — who made the same total denial of the believers. Other readings take them as the ignorant among the People of the Book, or as the ignorant generally. The dominant reading is the first, and it is the dominant reading because it produces the verse's most devastating observation:

**The people with revelation and the people without it made the identical claim.** Which means the claim was not produced by the revelation. It was produced by something in the claimant. If scripture generated the sectarian reflex, only the scriptured would have it. They do not have it exclusively. The idolaters, with no book at all, said the same thing in the same form, which proves that what is operating here is not doctrine but disposition.

That is the most important sentence in this verse, and it is worth stating plainly because it indicts every religious community including the Muslim one. Denying that another group is "upon anything" feels like fidelity. It is not fidelity. It is a human reflex that flourishes with or without a scripture, and the presence of the scripture only supplies it with vocabulary.

*Fa-Allahu yahkumu baynahum yawma al-qiyamati fima kanu fihi yakhtalifun* — "so Allah will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection concerning that over which they used to differ." Note what the verse does with the dispute. It does not adjudicate it. It does not tell you which of them was right. It assigns the adjudication to Allah and moves on.

That is the discipline, and it is the same discipline stated at 2:62 and at 22:17: "Indeed, those who have believed and those who were Jews and the Sabians and the Christians and the Magians and those who associated others with Allah — Allah will judge between them on the Day of Resurrection."

For practice, this verse is a mirror and most readers will not enjoy looking into it.

The first application is the distinction the verse draws between two things that are constantly confused: "I believe this is correct" and "you have nothing." The first is a position and can be argued. The second is a verdict on another person's standing before God, and no human being is authorised to deliver it. Almost all sectarian speech is the second wearing the costume of the first.

The second is the Muslim version, which is as old as the community and as current as this morning. Sunni and Shi'a declaring one another outside the fold. Schools of law declaring one another's followers misguided. Movements declaring one another innovators. Reformists and traditionalists each declaring the other to be upon nothing. The verse's diagnosis covers all of it, and it is unflattering: this is what people without scripture do, and we are doing it with scripture in our hands.

And the third is the remedy the verse itself supplies. *Allahu yahkumu baynahum* — Allah will judge between them. The person who has internalised that is free of the burden of settling other people's cases, and he is free of the exhausting work of maintaining the certainty that everyone outside his group is on nothing. That is not relativism. It is the recognition that there is a Judge, and that the office is not vacant and not yours.

## Verse 114

**"Who does more wrong than those who prevent Allah’s Name from being mentioned in His places of worship and strive to destroy them? Such people have no right to enter these places except with fear. For them there is disgrace in this world, and they will suffer a tremendous punishment in the Hereafter."**

In simple terms: no one is more unjust than someone who stops the name of God from being invoked in His houses of worship and works for their ruin. Such people have no right to enter them except in fear. Disgrace in this life, and a tremendous punishment in the next.

*Wa man azlamu mimman mana'a masajida Allahi an yudhkara fiha ismuhu* — "and who is more unjust than one who prevents the mosques of Allah from His name being mentioned in them?" *Azlamu* is the comparative of *zalim*: this is a rhetorical question to which the answer is "no one." The surah is placing this act at the extreme of injustice.

Which mosques? The commentaries transmitted two principal identifications, and they are not exclusive of one another.

The first is the Sacred Mosque in Makkah, and the prevention of the Prophet ﷺ and the believers from praying there — an obstruction that continued until the conquest of Makkah and is referenced at 48:25: "They are the ones who disbelieved and obstructed you from al-Masjid al-Haram while the offering was prevented from reaching its place of sacrifice."

The second is Bayt al-Maqdis in Jerusalem, and the destruction brought upon it — the transmitted reports connect this to the Babylonian destruction and, in some accounts, to the Roman one. The second identification draws support from the next clause.

*Wa sa'a fi kharabiha* — "and strove for their ruin." *Kharab* means physical desolation. Striving for ruin describes the demolition of buildings more naturally than the obstruction of worship, which is why some commentators preferred the Jerusalem identification, while others held that the Qur'an means both, and others took the verse as a general principle covering all houses of worship in every time and place. All three readings are held.

*Ula'ika ma kana lahum an yadkhuluha illa kha'ifin* — "those — it was not for them to enter them except in fear." There is a genuine interpretive question here that should be stated rather than smoothed over.

Some commentators read this as a statement of fact or a prediction: they will not enter these places except in a state of fear, because the believers will have authority over them. Others read it as a normative statement about their unworthiness: it is not fitting that such people should enter the houses of God except as terrified suppliants. And a number of jurists derived from it a legal ruling concerning the entry of non-Muslims into mosques — a question on which the schools differed, and on which the evidence is genuinely mixed.

The evidence on that question runs both ways, and it should be presented as running both ways. On the restrictive side, 9:28 prohibits the polytheists from approaching the Sacred Mosque after the year of the final pilgrimage, and some jurists extended a restriction to mosques generally or to the Hijaz. On the permissive side, the Sunnah records the Prophet ﷺ receiving the Christian delegation of Najran and hosting them in his mosque in Madinah, and records him having the captive Thumamah ibn Uthal tied to a pillar in the mosque before his release and conversion. On that basis the Hanafis permitted the entry of non-Muslims to mosques with permission; the Shafi'is permitted it except in the Sacred Mosque; the Malikis and Hanbalis held a range of positions including stricter ones.

The honest statement is that this is a disputed question in the schools, that the restriction on al-Masjid al-Haram rests on its own specific text, and that the practice of mosques welcoming non-Muslim visitors today rests on the Sunnah evidences above. It is not a question on which a single position should be dogmatically asserted outside its school, and no individual should treat it as settled in the direction of exclusion.

*Lahum fi al-dunya khizyun wa lahum fi al-akhirati 'adhabun 'azim* — "for them in this world is disgrace, and for them in the Hereafter is a tremendous punishment." *Khizy* is the same word used at verse 85 for the reward of those who believe in part of the Book: disgrace, exposure, shame. The punishment begins here.

Now the verse that must be read alongside this one, because without it the verse is regularly turned into something it is not.

"And were it not that Allah checks the people, some by means of others, there would have been demolished monasteries, churches, synagogues and mosques in which the name of Allah is much mentioned. And Allah will surely support those who support Him" (22:40).

That is the definitive Qur'anic text on the protection of houses of worship, and it names **monasteries, churches and synagogues** alongside mosques. The Islamic principle is not the protection of Muslim places of worship only. It is the protection of every place where the name of God is invoked, and the verses that establish it were revealed while the community was being fought. A reader who takes verse 114 as a warrant for attacking anyone's house of worship has taken the opposite of what the Qur'an teaches, because 22:40 was revealed to explain what the community was permitted to fight *for*.

For practice, three things follow.

The first is the gravity the verse assigns to this act. *Man azlamu* — who is more unjust? Nothing in the religion is described as a more extreme form of injustice than preventing the worship of God. That should recalibrate how a person thinks about the destruction of a church, a synagogue, a temple, a shrine or a mosque anywhere in the world. In the Qur'an's own scale, this is at the very top of the list of wrongs, not somewhere in the middle.

The second is that the obstruction is not always from outside. A house of worship can be prevented from fulfilling its purpose by the people who run it. The mosque that has become a faction's headquarters, where the name of God is mentioned but the mention has been hollowed out by politics, personality and money. The institution that drives people away from the worship of God by what it has become. The verse's indictment is of anyone who prevents the name of God from being mentioned, and it does not specify that the preventer must be an outsider.

And the third is the final clause — *illa kha'ifin*, except in awe. The proper state of a person entering a house of God is awe. Not familiarity, not ownership, not the relaxed confidence of a member in his club. Awe. That is a word for everyone who enters one, and it is worth asking whether it is still how you enter.

## Verse 115

**"To Allah belong the east and the west, so wherever you turn you are facing ˹towards˺ Allah. Surely Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: every direction belongs to God, so wherever you turn, you are turning toward Him — because He is not confined to any direction, and His knowledge and mercy encompass everything.

*Wa lillahi al-mashriqu wa al-maghribu* — "and to Allah belong the east and the west." The premise. Direction is His. Nothing about a compass point belongs to anyone else, and no direction confers proximity to Him or distance from Him.

*Fa-aynama tuwallu fa-thamma wajhu Allah* — "so wherever you turn, there is the Face of Allah."

Two things must be said about this clause, and the first is a warning.

**This verse does not abolish the qibla.** It is quoted constantly by people who believe it says "direction doesn't matter," and it says no such thing. The direction of the prayer is established explicitly later in this very surah: "So turn your face toward al-Masjid al-Haram. And wherever you [believers] are, turn your faces toward it" (2:144), repeated at 2:149–150. The qibla is prescribed, and it is one of the most visible obligations in the religion.

What this verse establishes is something different and deeper, and the commentators were precise about it. Its subject is the theology of direction, not the abolition of it.

The transmitted occasions cluster around cases where the direction could not be determined or did not apply: people who prayed at night or in cloud and discovered afterwards that they had faced the wrong way; people travelling, for whom the voluntary prayer may be performed on a mount facing whatever direction the animal faces; and generally, the case of a person who has no means of establishing the qibla. In all such cases the prayer is valid, because what is being sought is not a compass point but God, and God is not in a compass point.

And that is the second thing: **Allah is not located in a direction.** That is the theological heart of the verse. The clause does not mean that God's Face is physically present in every place. It means that a person who turns toward Him is not turning toward a location, because He is not in one. Had He been in the east, turning to the west would have been turning away from Him. Because He is not in any direction, every direction is a possible direction of worship, and the prescribed direction is prescribed for obedience and unity — not because of where God is.

*Wajh Allah*, the Face of Allah, is a divine attribute, and the treatment of it follows the same rule as the treatment of *istawa'* noted at verse 29. The position of the Salaf and of the majority of Ahl al-Sunnah is to affirm the attribute as real, in a manner befitting Allah's majesty, without asking how and without likening it to the attributes of created beings. A substantial body of later scholars interpreted it figuratively — as His essence, His pleasure, His self, or the direction of His acceptance. Both approaches are represented in the tradition, and this is not a question a verse commentary should settle by assertion. The practical content of the verse is unaffected by which reading a person holds: on either reading, God is not in a place, and the worshipper's turning is toward a Person and not a location.

The Sunnah that bears on this verse is clear and should be cited alongside it. The Prophet ﷺ said: "The earth has been made for me a place of prostration and a means of purification" (al-Bukhari and Muslim). And he is recorded as praying the supererogatory prayers on his camel, facing whatever direction it took him (al-Bukhari and Muslim).

*Inna Allaha wasi'un 'alim* — "indeed Allah is All-Encompassing, All-Knowing." *Wasi'* is one of the Beautiful Names: the Vast, the All-Encompassing, whose mercy, dominion and knowledge take everything in. The commentators connected it directly to the argument: because He is vast, He is not contained by a direction. And *'alim*: He knows the intention, the circumstance, and the constraint — which is exactly why the prayer of a person who could not determine the direction is accepted.

For practice, this verse is one of the great releases in the Qur'an, and it works on three levels.

The first is the practical ruling, which brings immediate relief to a lot of anxious people. If you are travelling, hospitalised, at sea, in a place with no way to determine the direction, and you pray after your best effort to establish it, your prayer is valid. The requirement is effort, not impossibility. Allah is *'alim* — He knows what you had to work with.

The second is the theology, which is the cure for a whole family of errors. God is not up, not down, not in a place, not in a direction, not spatially near to some people and far from others. Every crude picture of a localized deity is removed by this verse. And this is why the qibla is not idolatry: the Muslims face a building, and everyone can see that they do, and everyone should understand that they are not facing a building.

And the third is the deeper application, which is the one the verse is really for. The bodily direction is the expression; the heart's turning is the thing expressed. A person can face Makkah with his body and be turned elsewhere entirely with his heart — toward the person he wants to impress, the worry he is rehearsing, the calculation he is running. The direction of the body is fixed and easy. The direction of the self is the question, and this verse is where the surah first asks it — the same question it will ask again, at much greater length, when it comes to the change of the qibla.

## Verse 116

**"They say, “Allah has offspring.” Glory be to Him! In fact, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth—all are subject to His Will."**

In simple terms: they claim Allah has taken a child. Exalted is He above that. Everything in the heavens and the earth belongs to Him as possession, and everything is in submission to Him.

*Wa qalu ittakhadha Allahu waladan* — "and they say: Allah has taken a child." Who said this? The Qur'an attributes the claim to three different groups, and 9:30 specifies the first two: "The Jews say, 'Uzayr is the son of Allah,' and the Christians say, 'The Messiah is the son of Allah.'" The third are the polytheist Arabs, who called the angels the daughters of Allah — a claim the Qur'an rebukes with particular sharpness at 16:57, 37:149–153 and 43:16–19, where it points out that they were distressed by the birth of daughters to themselves while attributing daughters to God.

So verse 116 here is stated generally, and the specification comes elsewhere. It should be read as covering all three.

An honesty that must be stated here, because it is where Muslim and Christian readers most often talk past each other. The Qur'anic objection is to the claim that Allah *has offspring* — which on the Qur'an's reading implies need, dependence, physical begetting, and a partner sharing the divine nature. Historic Christian theology has itself distinguished the Sonship of Christ from crude physical generation, and has insisted that the doctrine is a metaphysical relation within the Trinity rather than a claim about biology. The Qur'an appears to be rejecting the claim as it understood it, and it is worth acknowledging plainly that the two traditions are genuinely and irreconcilably different on this point rather than pretending the difference is a misunderstanding that better explanation would dissolve. Muslims revere Jesus enormously — as noted at verse 87 — and the rejection of divine sonship is a rejection of a claim about God, not a diminution of Jesus.

*Subhanahu* — "Glory be to Him." This is the standard Qur'anic response to any statement unworthy of God, and the word is worth understanding. It comes from a root meaning to swim, to move swiftly and unencumbered through something. *Tasbih* is therefore declaring God free — letting Him swim clear — of every defect, limitation and association that anyone attributes to Him. It is not a vague exclamation of praise. It is a specific repudiation of a specific error, and the Qur'an places it precisely where the error is named.

*Bal lahu ma fi al-samawati wa al-ard* — "rather, to Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth." And here is the argument, compressed into a clause. Ownership refutes sonship. A child shares the nature of the parent and inherits from him; a possession does not. If everything in existence is His property, then nothing in existence stands in the relation of child to Him. There is no overlap between "what He owns" and "what He begot," because the two categories exclude one another.

*Kullun lahu qanitun* — "all are subservient to Him." *Qanit* means obedient, humble, standing in devoted submission. The majority of commentators read this as the subjection of the whole creation to His will — willingly, in the case of the angels and the believers, and by necessity in the case of everything else. The Qur'an states the same thing elsewhere: "And to Him submits whoever is in the heavens and the earth, willingly or by compulsion" (3:83), and "Do you not see that to Allah prostrates whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth" (22:18). The next verse reinforces this reading, since it describes creation as coming into being by a command.

For practice, three things.

The first is the *subhan* reflex, and it is worth acquiring deliberately. The Qur'an does not argue with every false claim at length; sometimes it repudiates and moves on, and *subhanahu* is the word it uses. A person who hears something unworthy attributed to God — a crude picture, a superstition, a claim that He needs something, a statement that He is like something — should have that word available. It is the shortest complete theology in the language.

The second is the argument from ownership, and it applies directly to the self. Everything you have been given is property, not offspring and not partner. Your children are not yours in the sense that God has a child; they are a trust. Your wealth, your achievements, your position, your talents — all of them are *lahu ma fi al-samawati wa al-ard*, among the things that belong to Him. The confusion between ownership and identification is the source of most human misery about what we have and might lose.

And the third is the *qanitun*. Everything in creation is in submission. The sun, the sea, the cell, the star — all of it obeys, necessarily. The human being is the one creature given the choice to obey *willingly* rather than by compulsion, and that is simultaneously his dignity and his danger. Every other thing in existence does what it was made to do. The question that this verse puts to a person is simple: which kind of submission is yours?

## Verse 117

**"˹He is˺ the Originator of the heavens and the earth! When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, “Be!” And it is!"**

In simple terms: He is the one who brought the heavens and the earth into being without any prior model. And when He wills something, He says only "Be," and it is.

*Badi'u al-samawati wa al-ard* — "the Originator of the heavens and the earth." *Badi'* comes from a root meaning to bring forth something entirely new, without precedent, without a model, without prior material. It is the same root as *bid'ah*, which is why the tradition is so severe about innovation in the religion: origination belongs to God, and the creature who originates in worship is claiming something that is not his.

And here is the argument of the verse, which follows directly on from verse 116. Verse 116 refuted the claim of offspring by ownership: everything is His property, and property is not children. Verse 117 refutes it by origination: **a being who creates from nothing, without a model, has no need of a child.**

That is because the point of a child, in the logic the Qur'an is addressing, is the perpetuation of a nature that would otherwise end. A mortal begets because he dies. A being who originates everything and who does not die has no need of that mechanism. The Qur'an states this argument explicitly at 6:101: "Originator of the heavens and the earth. How could He have a son when He has no consort, and He created all things, and He is, of all things, Knowing?"

*Wa idha qada amran fa-innama yaqulu lahu kun fa-yakun* — "and when He decrees a matter, He only says to it: Be! And it is."

The formula *kun fa-yakun* is one of the most famous in the Qur'an, and it carries three things. Creation requires no effort. Creation requires no material and no prior model. And creation requires no time — the *fa* of immediate sequence indicates that there is no interval between the command and the thing.

A clarification that should be stated so the formula is not misunderstood. The "saying" is not a utterance in letters and sounds, and "and it is" does not describe a temporal sequence in which God speaks and then, afterwards, the thing appears. The commentators were careful on this point: the expression conveys the immediacy, ease and completeness of the divine creating, not a process with stages in it.

Now, the cross-references here are unusually rich, and they are what make the Muslim position coherent rather than merely negative.

At 19:35 the identical sentence is used in the context of Jesus: "It is not [befitting] for Allah to take a son; exalted is He! When He decrees a matter, He only says to it, 'Be,' and it is." The argument used against divine sonship in general is the argument used against the sonship of Christ in particular.

And at 3:47 the same formula explains the virginal conception: Mary said, "My Lord, how will I have a son when no man has touched me?" and the answer was, "Such is Allah; He creates what He wills. When He has decreed a matter, He only says to it, 'Be,' and it is."

And at 3:59 the argument is made complete: "Indeed, the example of Jesus to Allah is like that of Adam. He created him from dust; then He said to him, 'Be,' and he was."

That last verse is the crux of the whole question, and it deserves to be stated plainly because it resolves something that puzzles people on both sides. Islam does not deny the miraculous birth of Jesus. It affirms it and goes further: it says the birth of Jesus was *more* remarkable than the usual account of a son of God would require, since he had no father — and yet it is still less remarkable than the creation of Adam, who had neither father nor mother. The logic is consistent: miraculous origin is not evidence of divinity. It is evidence of a Creator who creates by command, and Adam, who had no parents at all, is the stronger case rather than the weaker one.

For practice, two things carry.

The first is the argument itself, which is a complete answer to a question many people quietly carry. The assumption behind "God has a son" is that God needs something — a successor, a helper, a partner, a continuation. Origination removes the need. If He made everything by command, He lacks nothing, and a being who lacks nothing does not beget.

And the second is the *kun fa-yakun* as a source of hope, which is how the tradition has always used it. The thing you have concluded is impossible requires one command. Not a process, not a negotiation, not a gradual unlocking — one command. A person who has tried everything, calculated every path, and reached the end of what he can do has arrived exactly where this verse is addressed. *Badi'* means He is not bound to work from an existing model, which means He is not bound by your analysis of what is achievable.

The corollary is the one the tradition draws in the other direction: since origination is His, it is not yours. In worship, innovation is a theft. The root that names His creating-without-precedent is the root that forbids you to invent a way to Him that He did not lay down.

## Verse 118

**"Those who have no knowledge say, “If only Allah would speak to us or a sign would come to us!” The same was said by those who came before. Their hearts are all alike. Indeed, We have made the signs clear for people of sure faith."**

In simple terms: the ignorant demand that God speak to them directly or send them a sign. Previous generations said exactly the same thing. Their hearts are indistinguishable from one another. And the signs have been made clear — for those who seek with certainty.

*Wa qala alladhina la ya'lamuna* — "and those who do not know said." The same phrase used at verse 113, and the echo is deliberate: in both verses the description is applied to people making a claim they have no warrant for. The commentators identified them here as the polytheists of Makkah, who demanded miracles from the Prophet ﷺ.

*Lawla yukallimuna Allahu aw ta'tina ayatun* — "why does Allah not speak to us, or why does not a sign come to us?" The fullest catalogue of what they actually demanded is at 17:90–93 — that he cause a spring to gush from the ground, that he have a garden of palms and vines, that he make the sky fall on them in pieces, that he bring God and the angels before them, that he have a house of gold, that he ascend into the sky. The list is worth reading in full, because it shows the demands were not modest.

*Kadhalika qala alladhina min qablihim mithla qawlihim* — "thus said those before them the like of their saying." The demand is not new and not original. Every generation produces it.

And the verse that settles the question of whether the demand is made in good faith is 15:14–15: "And even if We opened to them a gate from the heaven and they continued therein to ascend, they would surely say, 'Our eyes have only been dazzled. Rather, we are a people affected by magic.'" The demand, if met, would be explained away. That is the proof that the demand is not about evidence.

*Tashabahat qulubuhum* — "their hearts resemble one another." Note the root. It is *sh-b-h*, the same root as the *mutashabihat*, the ambiguous verses of 2:7, and the same verb used at 2:70 when they said *inna al-baqara tashabaha 'alayna* — "the cows all look alike to us." Three times in this surah the same root appears, and each time it marks the same phenomenon: a confusion that is manufactured rather than discovered. At 2:70 they made the cows indistinguishable in order to delay. At 2:7 the deviant pursue what is ambiguous in order to evade. And here the hearts of every generation are indistinguishable from one another because the refusal is the same.

That is one of the surah's quiet threads, and it is worth seeing: the ambiguity is never in the thing. It is in the person.

*Qad bayyanna al-ayati li-qawmin yuqinun* — "We have certainly made the signs clear for a people who are certain."

This clause must be read carefully, because it can look circular. It does not mean the signs are clear only to those who already believe. The verb is *bayyanna*, "We have made clear" — the clarity is an accomplished fact about the signs, not a property of the observer. What the clause says is that the benefit of that clarity is realised by those in the state of *yaqin*.

And *yaqin* here is not prior belief. It is the disposition of a person who is genuinely looking rather than a person who is demanding. The distinction matters enormously. The person who says "show me and then I will believe" is not in the state of *yaqin*; he is in the state of *lawla*. The person in the state of *yaqin* examines what is already in front of him and finds it sufficient.

There is a further point here that is worth making directly, because it is the answer to the objection in its modern form. What these people were demanding was a **private, unduplicable, coercive** sign — something that would compel belief and that nobody else could examine. What they were given was a **public, examinable** sign: a text that anyone can read, test, check for consistency, and attempt to imitate. The Qur'an's own answer to the demand for signs is to point at itself: "And is it not sufficient for them that We revealed to you the Book which is recited to them?" (29:50–51).

That is a higher grade of evidence, not a lower one. A miracle witnessed by one person must be taken on that person's report. A text available to everyone can be examined by everyone, in every generation, by anyone willing to do the work. The demand for a private sign is, whatever else it is, a request for something less durable than what was given.

For practice, this verse is most useful as a self-diagnosis.

The first application is the test of the demand. When you find yourself saying "if only God would make this clear to me," ask what you have done with what is already clear. In almost every case the honest answer is that there is an unexamined text, an unread passage, an unmade decision — and the demand for a sign is a way of not attending to it. The verse does not say the signs are insufficient. It says they have been *made clear*, and it locates the failure in the heart.

The second is the uniformity of the objection. *Tashabahat qulubuhum*. Whatever objection you are hearing — or making — it has been made before, in the same form, by people in the same condition. That is oddly consoling for anyone engaged in inviting others: the objection is not a discovery about your argument. It is a recurring feature of the human response to being asked to change.

And the third is the *yaqin*. Certainty, in the Qur'an's usage, is not the absence of questions. It is the state of a person who is not using his questions as a place to stand. There is a difference between "help me understand this" and "I will not move until this is resolved," and the difference is visible in what the person is doing while he waits.

## Verse 119

**"We have surely sent you with the truth ˹O Prophet˺ as a deliverer of good news and a warner. And you will not be accountable for the residents of the Hellfire."**

In simple terms: We sent you with the truth, to bring good news and to warn — and you are not answerable for those who end up in the Fire.

*Inna arsalnaka bi-al-haqqi bashiran wa nadhiran* — "indeed We have sent you with the truth, as a bringer of glad tidings and a warner." This pairing is the standing description of the Prophet's function throughout the Qur'an — at 5:19, 11:2, 17:105, 18:56, 25:56, 34:28, 35:24, 48:8 and elsewhere. The two together are the religion in miniature. A message of only good news produces presumption; a message of only warning produces despair. The Prophet ﷺ was sent with both, in balance, to the same people, in the same breath.

That balance is a standing rebuke to every religious community since. The community that preaches only love and never judgement has half a religion. The community that preaches only judgement and never love has half a religion. The two halves were delivered by the same man to the same audience, and neither is optional.

*Wa la tus'alu 'an ashabi al-jahim* — "and you will not be asked about the companions of the Hellfire." A variant reading is transmitted with the verb in the prohibitive form, "and do not ask about the inhabitants of the Hellfire." The standard reading is the passive, "you will not be asked," and the meaning on either reading converges: the outcome is not laid at his door.

Now, why is this said, and why here?

Because the Prophet ﷺ grieved. The Qur'an says so in the plainest terms. "Then perhaps you would kill yourself through grief over them, [O Muhammad], if they do not believe in this message, [and] out of sorrow" (18:6). And: "Perhaps you would kill yourself with grief that they will not be believers" (26:3). And: "And We already know that your breast is constrained by what they say" (15:97).

This verse is consolation to a man who was wearing himself out over people's refusal. And the consolation is a statement about the structure of responsibility: he was sent to convey, not to convert. His office is *bayan* — making clear — and the response is not in his gift.

The Qur'an states this repeatedly and from every angle, and the texts should be collected because together they are one of the great releases in the religion:

"Not upon you, [O Muhammad], is their guidance, but Allah guides whom He wills" (2:272).
"Indeed, you do not guide whom you like, but Allah guides whom He wills" (28:56) — revealed concerning Abu Talib, the uncle who raised him and protected him, and whom he loved and could not bring to faith.
"So remind, [O Muhammad]; you are only a reminder. You are not over them a controller" (88:21–22).
"And We have not made you over them a guardian, nor are you over them a manager" (6:107).
"Then, [O Muhammad], would you compel the people in order that they become believers?" (10:99).

Two boundaries must be stated with this verse, and both are important.

The first is that release from accountability is not indifference. The Prophet ﷺ grieved, which means the grief is not a failure of trust; it is the natural cost of loving people who are refusing what you know they need. The verse does not tell him to stop caring. It tells him to stop carrying what is not his.

And the second is that this verse, and the phrase "companions of the Hellfire," authorises no one to identify any individual as one of them. The surah has been consistent on this from verse 24 onward, and the boundary was restated as recently as verse 113: judgment belongs to Allah, and the office is not vacant.

For practice, this is one of the most pastorally necessary verses in the whole surah, and it has three applications.

The first is for anyone engaged in calling others to the religion. Your task is to convey, clearly and well and with good character. It is not to produce the result. Most of the burnout in religious work comes from quietly assuming responsibility for outcomes that were never assigned to us — the convert who lapsed, the friend who never came, the congregation that did not change. This verse releases that. Convey, and leave the rest to the One who said it is not yours.

The second is for parents, which is where this verse does the most work in most lives. A parent is responsible for what he transmitted — the example, the instruction, the love, the consistency. He is not responsible for the adult child's choice. That distinction is the difference between a parent who prays and a parent who is destroyed, and the Qur'an draws it here and at 28:56, where the person the Prophet could not guide was his own beloved uncle.

And the third is for anyone who has watched someone he loves walk away. The grief is real, and the Qur'an records it in the Prophet ﷺ rather than treating it as a defect. But the case was never yours. You were sent to them with the truth, as someone who brings good news and warns — and you will not be asked about the outcome.

## Verse 120

**"Never will the Jews or Christians be pleased with you, until you follow their faith. Say, “Allah’s guidance is the only ˹true˺ guidance.” And if you were to follow their desires after ˹all˺ the knowledge that has come to you, there would be none to protect or help you against Allah."**

In simple terms: neither community will ever be satisfied with you unless you abandon your message and adopt theirs. Say: the guidance of God is the guidance. And were you to follow their desires after the knowledge that has reached you, you would have no protector and no helper against God.

*Wa lan tarda 'anka al-yahudu wa la al-nasara hatta tattabi'a millatahum* — "and never will the Jews be pleased with you, nor the Christians, until you follow their religion." *Lan* is the emphatic negation; *hatta* sets an unattainable condition. *Millah* is the religion as a communal path, the way of a people — a slightly different word from *din*, and the commentators drew the distinction.

A small textual note: the word is *millatahum*, singular with a plural suffix, which can be read as "the religion of each of them" — hence the rendering "their respective faith" — or as a shared way. Both readings are held.

The argument is stark and it is meant to be. There is no version of the message that would earn their approval, because the approval is conditional on the abandonment of the message. Therefore approval is not a strategy. It is a surrender with a fixed price, and the price is everything.

*Qul inna huda Allahi huwa al-huda* — "Say: indeed, the guidance of Allah is the guidance." Note the construction: *huwa* between two definite nouns is the pronoun of separation, and its function here is exclusivity. Not "Allah's guidance is guidance," but "Allah's guidance is **the** guidance" — everything else called guidance is not guidance in the proper sense.

That is an exclusivist statement and it should not be softened. But note precisely what it claims. It claims something about *guidance* — about the source of truth. It does not claim anything about the people who possess it, and it does not say that Jews and Christians are incapable of goodness, sincerity, or nearness to God by their own lights. Those are different claims, and the Qur'an makes the first and not the second.

**And a guardrail that must be stated, because this verse is quoted constantly as though it said something far broader than it does.** The verse describes a specific historical demand: that the Prophet ﷺ abandon the revelation he had been given. What is unattainable is their endorsement of *that*. It is not a statement that Jews and Christians can never be satisfied with Muslims, that good relations are impossible, or that coexistence is a fantasy. The Qur'an establishes the opposite for civil life in the clearest terms: "Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, Allah loves those who act justly" (60:8). What is irreconcilable is the truth claim. What is entirely possible, and commanded, is justice and kindness between people who disagree about it. Those two things are held together throughout the Qur'an and neither may be used to cancel the other.

*Wa la-ini ittaba'ta ahwa'ahum ba'da alladhi ja'aka min al-'ilmi* — "and if you were to follow their desires after what has come to you of knowledge." Note the word: *ahwa'*, desires — the plural of *hawa*, the same word used at verse 87 for the reason they rejected the messengers: "whenever a messenger came to you with what your souls did not desire." The surah has now used the same root for both halves of the exchange. They rejected because the message contradicted their desire, and they wanted him to follow their desire in turn. Everything in this passage comes back to the same word.

*Ba'da alladhi ja'aka min al-'ilmi* — "after what has come to you of knowledge." It is called **knowledge**, not opinion, not preference. And the aggravating factor is stated in that phrase: to follow desire is one thing; to follow it *after* knowledge has arrived is another.

A necessary note on the address. This is spoken to the Prophet ﷺ, and the condition is contrary to fact — he would never do it. The commentators explained the function: the Qur'an addresses the most protected person in order to warn those who are less protected. If the warning is addressed to him, it applies a fortiori to everyone else. The same device appears at 39:65: "And it was already revealed to you and to those before you that if you should associate [anything] with Allah, your work would surely become worthless."

*Ma laka min Allahi min waliyyin wa la nasir* — "you would have no protector from Allah and no helper." The same pair as verse 107, and the two verses frame the passage. At 107 the believer is told he *has* no guardian besides Allah — which is a release. Here the warning is that following desire would leave him with none at all — which is a threat. The same two words, used for consolation and for warning, because the reality described is the same: there is nowhere else to go.

For practice, this verse is one of the most liberating in the Qur'an for anyone holding a conviction in a hostile environment.

The first application is the end of approval-seeking. If the price of someone's approval is that you stop being who you are, then approval is not available and the pursuit of it is not a strategy — it is a slow surrender with a fixed cost. Most people spend years discovering this, and many never do. The verse tells you on the first page.

The second is the distinction it makes possible: irreconcilable claims, entirely possible relations. You can hold that your position is true and that the person who disagrees is owed justice, kindness, good neighbourliness and fair dealing. Those are not in tension. The tradition has always held both, and the Qur'an states both within a few verses of each other here.

And the third is *ba'da alladhi ja'aka min al-'ilmi* — after what has come to you of knowledge. That phrase applies to every reader of this surah. You have been given knowledge: about what is required, about what is forbidden, about the accounting, about the criterion. Following desire is one thing; following it after this is another, and the verse names the difference. The person who has heard the truth and follows his preference anyway is not in the position of the person who never heard. That is a heavy sentence, and it is also the reason this surah exists.

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses move from competing human claims to the divine reality that settles them, and then back down to the human refusal.

Two communities each claimed Paradise as their own, and the Qur'an answered with a demand for proof and named the claim for what it was — a wish (111). The real criterion was then stated in terms anyone can meet: the surrender of the whole self to God, joined to a life of excellence (112). Then the sectarian reflex was exposed in its pure form — each community declaring the other to be upon nothing while both recited scripture — with the devastating addition that the people without scripture made the identical claim, which proves the claim was never produced by the scripture (113). Then the extreme of injustice: preventing the name of God from being mentioned in His houses and working for their ruin (114).

And then the surah rises above the dispute entirely. Every direction belongs to God, so wherever you turn you are turning toward Him, because He is not in a direction (115). He has no offspring — glory be to Him — because everything in existence is His property, and property is not children (116). And He is the originator who creates without model or material, by a single command, and therefore has no need of a child at all (117).

Then back to the human refusal: the demand for signs, made by every generation in the same form, by hearts indistinguishable from one another, in the face of signs already made clear (118). And the closing consolation: you were sent with the truth, to give good news and to warn, and you are not accountable for the outcome (119). Their approval was never attainable except by surrender, and the guidance of God is the only guidance (120).

Three conclusions hold the batch together. First, every human claim in these verses is answered by a demand for evidence, and the demand is one the Qur'an makes of itself as well — so the principle is not a polemical weapon but a rule of method. Second, the theology at the centre is what dissolves the disputes: God is not located, not begotten, not in need, and origination belongs to Him alone, which means no community and no individual can claim Him by descent, direction, or inheritance. And third, the passage that opens with competing claims to Paradise closes by releasing the Prophet ﷺ — and every reader with him — from responsibility for who accepts and who refuses. The message is yours to convey. The outcome is not yours to carry.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where the commentators and jurists genuinely differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: which mosques verse 114 refers to, whether its final clause is a prediction, a statement of unworthiness, or a legal ruling, whether *wajh* in verse 115 is an attribute or a figurative expression, and whether the variant reading of verse 119 is prohibitive or passive.

Five boundaries have been observed. On verse 111: it has been stated explicitly that the Qur'an does not object to exclusivism as such but to exclusivism without proof, and that Islam's own exclusive claim at 3:85 stands — so that the verse is not read as teaching that all paths are equivalent. On verse 114: the juristic difference over non-Muslim entry has been presented as a genuine difference with evidence on both sides rather than as settled, and 22:40 has been cited as the definitive text establishing that Islam protects monasteries, churches, synagogues and mosques alike — so that no reader may take this verse as a warrant for attacking anyone's house of worship. On verse 115: the common misreading that the verse abolishes the qibla has been named and corrected from 2:144 and 2:149–150. On verse 116: the difference with Christian doctrine on divine sonship has been stated as a genuine and irreconcilable difference rather than explained away, while the reverence Islam holds for Jesus, stated at verse 87, has been maintained. And on verse 120: it has been stated that the verse concerns the demand that the Prophet abandon his message and is not a statement about the impossibility of good relations, with 60:8 cited as the governing text for civil conduct. And throughout: no reader may use any verse in this passage to declare any individual among the inhabitants of the Fire.
