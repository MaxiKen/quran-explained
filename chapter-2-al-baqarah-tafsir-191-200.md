# Surah al-Baqarah (The Cow, 2) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 191–200

This batch must be read slowly, because it contains the most misused passage in the Qur'an and the verses that correct the misuse are in the same passage. The combat verses continue, and with them four consecutive restrictions: the response is to be reciprocal (191), it ceases when the other side ceases (192), it is for the removal of persecution and not for compulsion in religion (193), and it is to be exactly proportionate (194). Then the passage on spending, with the warning that the destruction lies in withholding rather than in giving (195). Then the pilgrimage: the rulings of the Hajj and 'Umrah, the ransom, and the fast of the one who cannot afford the animal (196); the conduct of the pilgrimage, with three prohibitions of which the third is the one everybody breaks (197); commerce permitted during the rites, and remembrance as taught rather than invented (198); the pouring forth from where all the people pour forth, which abolishes a privilege claimed by lineage (199); and the redirection of ancestral boasting into the remembrance of God (200).

## Verse 191

**"Kill them wherever you come upon them and drive them out of the places from which they have driven you out. For persecution is far worse than killing. And do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque unless they attack you there. If they do so, then fight them—that is the reward of the disbelievers."**

This verse cannot be read alone. It is the continuation of the sentence begun at 2:190, whose governing clause is *alladhina yuqatilunakum* — **those who fight you**. Everything in 191 is governed by that restriction, and detaching it is the single most consequential misreading of the Qur'an in circulation, committed in opposite directions by critics of the religion and by those who would use it as a licence.

*Waqtuluhum haythu thaqiftumuhum* — "and kill them wherever you come upon them."

*Thaqiftumuhum* is to come upon, to encounter, to overtake. The commentators were clear that the referent is the combatants of 190 — the people who were at war with the community and fighting it — and the transmitted reports place it specifically in the context of the Quraysh, who had driven the Muslims from their homes and were making war on them.

*Wa akhrijuhum min haythu akhrajukum* — "and drive them out from where they drove you out."

**And note the exact reciprocity, which is the verse's governing principle.** The verb is the same in both clauses: *akhrajukum* — they drove you out; *akhrijuhum* — drive them out. The response is measured by the act and does not exceed it. Not annihilation for expulsion. Expulsion for expulsion.

*Wa al-fitnatu ashaddu mina al-qatli* — "and *fitnah* is worse than killing."

***FITNAH*** — from a root meaning to test and to try, and specifically to test gold in fire. In the Qur'an the word carries several senses, and here the majority of the commentators take it as **persecution**: the persecution of people for their religion — torture, expulsion, coercion to abandon the faith. So the argument runs: their forcing you out of your religion and out of your homes is worse than your killing them in the fighting that ends it.

(Some commentators read the *fitnah* here as the associating of partners with God, which is also a transmitted reading. On either reading the structure of the argument is the same: a greater harm justifies the lesser.)

The verb's use elsewhere settles what persecution means in practice. 85:10: "Indeed, those who have **burned** [*fatanu*] the believing men and believing women and then have not repented will have the punishment of Hell." And 2:217: "And they will not cease fighting you until they turn you back from your religion, if they are able."

*Wa la tuqatiluhum 'inda al-masjidi al-harami hatta yuqatilukum fihi* — "and do not fight them at the Sacred Mosque **until they fight you therein**."

**And this is the restriction that proves the verse is not what it is taken for.** A verse that is quoted as a general licence to kill contains, inside itself, an exemption for the Sacred Precinct that holds unless the other side attacks there first. The transmitted reports connect this to the year of al-Hudaybiyyah, when the Muslims were prevented from reaching the House and the Prophet ﷺ concluded the treaty rather than fight in the precinct.

*Fa-in qatalukum fa-qtuluhum kadhalika jaza'u al-kafirin* — "but if they fight you, then kill them. Such is the reward of the disbelievers."

**And note that even the final clause is conditional: *if* they fight you.**

For practice, and this is the section that matters most in this batch, five things must be said together.

**First: this verse continues 190's restriction to combatants and cannot be detached from it.** It is not a general command about disbelievers. It is a ruling about a people at war with the community who had expelled it from its homes.

**Second: the response is measured by the act.** Expulsion for expulsion. The verse does not authorise more than was done, and the proportionality is stated in the next batch of verses even more explicitly at 194.

**Third: the sanctuary is exempt.** Even in a verse of combat, the Sacred Precinct is protected unless attacked. A reading that produces indiscriminate violence has already discarded a clause that is in the same sentence.

**Fourth: the jurisprudence of war is a specialised discipline built on the whole corpus, and it is not derivable from a commentary.** There are genuine and well-known differences among the classical schools and among contemporary scholars about the scope and conditions of these rulings. What is not in dispute is what the text states: combatants, reciprocity, conditionality, and sanctuary.

**And fifth, the boundary that has been maintained throughout this work and must be repeated here in its strongest form: no individual may declare another person's blood lawful, and no private person may act on his own reading of these texts against another human being.** Whatever the law permits in this area, it permits to a constituted authority under the conditions the scholars have laid down. A person who quotes this verse to justify an act of his own has taken a ruling of war and applied it without the state, the authority, the conditions, or the knowledge that were the whole of its restraint.

## Verse 192

**"But if they cease, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: but if they desist, then indeed Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.

*Fa-ini intahaw fa-inna Allaha ghafurun rahim* — "so if they desist, then indeed Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."

*Intahaw* is form VIII of the root meaning to end and to forbid: to desist, to cease, to refrain.

**And this short verse is the proof that the preceding one is not a licence.**

A command to fight that is followed immediately by "if they desist" is a command about a **state of active hostilities that terminates when the hostilities do.** The permission is not standing. It is conditional on the fighting continuing, and the condition is stated in the very next breath.

That matters because the most common misreading — in both directions — treats 191 as a permanent ordinance. It is not. The surah sets the condition, and then repeats it at 193, and then grounds it in a general principle at 194.

And note what the consequence of their desisting is stated to be. The verse does not say "if they desist, then leave them alone," which would be the minimum. It says: **if they desist, then Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.** The forgiveness is named as His disposition toward them. The cessation of hostilities does not merely suspend the permission; it moves the matter into the register of mercy.

For practice, two things.

The first is the principle, which reaches far beyond war: **hostility in this religion is tied to an act and ends with the act.** It is never a standing posture toward a category of people. The surah has now said this twice in two verses, and it will say it a third time at 193. The repetition is deliberate: this is the passage most likely to be read as authorising perpetual enmity, and the text closes that reading three times over.

And the second is the application to ordinary life. Most human conflict persists long after the thing that started it has stopped. The verse's rule — the response ends when the aggression ends — is the rule that would end most feuds, in families and communities and workplaces, if anyone applied it. And the verse's framing adds something harder: the person who stops is to be met with the recognition that God is forgiving toward him, which is a different thing from merely ceasing to fight him.

## Verse 193

**"Fight against them ˹if they persecute you˺ until there is no more persecution, and ˹your˺ devotion will be to Allah ˹alone˺. If they stop ˹persecuting you˺, let there be no hostility except against the aggressors."**

In simple terms: and fight them until there is no persecution and the religion is for Allah. But if they desist, then no hostility except against the wrongdoers.

*Wa qatiluhum hatta la takuna fitnatun wa yakuna al-dinu lillahi* — "and fight them until there is no *fitnah* and the religion is for Allah."

**And this clause — *hatta la takuna fitnah* — is the most misquoted line in the Qur'an, and its correct meaning has to be stated with full force.**

The reading that produces so much harm takes *fitnah* here as **disbelief**, so that the verse is made to mean: fight until there is no disbelief left, until everyone is Muslim, until Islam dominates. That reading is wrong, and it is refuted from within the Qur'an.

**What *fitnah* means here is persecution** — the same sense it carried at 2:191, where it was the forcing of people out of their religion and out of their homes. So the verse means: fight until the persecution stops, until people are no longer tortured, expelled and coerced for their faith, and until worship and obedience are directed to God without suppression.

Three texts settle it beyond argument:

**2:256, in this very surah:** "There is no compulsion in religion [*la ikraha fi al-din*]." A reading of 193 that makes it a command to compel belief is contradicted by a verse in the same surah.

**10:99:** "And had your Lord willed, those on earth would have believed — all of them entirely. Then, [O Muhammad], would you compel the people in order that they become believers?"

**60:8:** "Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, Allah loves those who act justly."

A religion that forbids compulsion in belief, that asks how anyone could compel faith, and that commands justice and kindness toward those who are not fighting — does not contain a verse commanding war until everyone believes. The *fitnah* to be ended is the persecution, not the disbelief.

*Fa-ini intahaw fa-la 'udwana illa 'ala al-zalimin* — "but if they desist, then no hostility except against the wrongdoers."

**And note the qualifier, which is one of the most carefully chosen words in the passage.** The exception is not "except against the disbelievers." It is not "except against them." It is *illa 'ala al-zalimin* — **except against the wrongdoers.**

That is a category defined by **conduct**, not by identity. At the exact point where the verse could have named a category of people, it named a category of acts. A person who does not wrong is not in this exception regardless of what he believes; and a person who does wrong falls under it.

For practice, three things.

The first is the meaning of *hatta la takuna fitnah*, and it is the answer to give whenever this verse is quoted. The objective is the end of persecution and the freedom to worship God — not forced conversion and not domination. Anyone quoting 193 to justify compelling belief is quoting it against 2:256, which is in the same surah, and against 10:99 and 60:8.

The second is the third repetition of the conditionality. 190 restricts to combatants. 192 makes it cease on their ceasing. 193 makes it cease on their ceasing again. **The permission to fight in this passage is conditional at every single point where it is stated**, and there is no statement of it anywhere in the passage that is not conditional.

And the third is the *zalimin* qualifier, applied generally. The verse models something that is very difficult and very necessary: opposing an act without condemning a category of people. Hostility is permitted toward wrongdoing. It is not permitted toward a group. Most human conflict fails at exactly this point, and the verse succeeds at it in four words.

## Verse 194

**"˹There will be retaliation in˺ a sacred month for ˹an offence in˺ a sacred month, and all violations will bring about retaliation. So, if anyone attacks you, retaliate in the same manner. ˹But˺ be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is with those mindful ˹of Him˺."**

In simple terms: the sacred month for the sacred month, and all inviolable things are [subject to] retaliation. So whoever transgresses against you, transgress against him in the like of what he transgressed against you. And be conscious of Allah, and know that Allah is with the God-conscious.

*Al-shahru al-haramu bi-al-shahri al-harami* — "the sacred month is for the sacred month."

The transmitted context places this in the year of al-Hudaybiyyah, when the Muslims were prevented from reaching the House during a sacred month and were reluctant to fight within it. The ruling: the sanctity is reciprocal — a violation of it by the other side opens a proportionate response within it.

*Wa al-hurumatu qisasun* — "and the inviolable things are [subject to] retaliation."

*Hurumat* is the plural of *hurmah*: the sacred, protected, inviolable things — life, property, honour, the sacred months, the precinct. And the principle stated is that **violations of sanctities are subject to *qisas*** — the same principle of exact equivalence established at 2:178.

*Fa-mani i'tada 'alaykum fa-'tadu 'alayhi bi-mithli ma i'tada 'alaykum* — "so whoever transgresses against you, transgress against him in the **like** of what he transgressed against you."

***BI-MITHLI MA*** — "in the like of what" — and this is the whole verse.

The principle is **exact proportionality**. Not more. Not "as much as you can." In the measure of what was done, and no further.

And note the striking feature of the wording: the verb used for the response is the **same verb** used for the original aggression — *i'tada*, to transgress. The classical commentators explained this as correspondence of language rather than moral equation: the response is called by the same name because it matches the act measure for measure, while the moral characters remain different — the first is aggression and the second is justice.

But there is a warning inside that verbal choice that is worth hearing. The response is close enough to the original act to be named with the same word. Which means the person responding is standing nearer to the thing he is responding to than he would like to think.

*Wa-ttaqu Allaha wa-'lamu anna Allaha ma'a al-muttaqin* — "and be conscious of Allah, and know that Allah is with the God-conscious."

**And here is the safeguard, and it is the most important practical thing in the verse.**

The verse has just permitted retaliation. And the very next clause places the brake on it: *ittaqu Allah*. Not an external check, not a court, not a supervisor — consciousness of God.

That is why the closing promise matters so much. **Allah is with the God-conscious.** The one who restrains his retaliation out of consciousness of God is promised with-ness, which is greater than what the retaliation would have obtained. The comparison is 2:153: *inna Allaha ma'a al-sabirin* — with the patient. The surah has now promised with-ness to both the patient and the God-conscious, which are the two states most necessary at the moment a person has been wronged.

For practice, three things.

The first is *bi-mithli*, and it is the rule for every response to a wrong. The measure is the measure of the act. Anything beyond it is not justice but a second aggression, and it transfers the wrongdoer's position to you.

The second is the verbal correspondence, as a warning. Because retaliation is named with the same word as aggression, the person retaliating should assume that he is at genuine risk of becoming what he is punishing. That is not a reason to forgo justice. It is a reason to forgo the extra.

And the third is the brake. *Ittaqu Allah* is the only thing standing between a permitted retaliation and an excess, and it is internal. Which means the practical discipline for a wronged person is not to calculate the retaliation more carefully but to increase the consciousness of God that governs the calculation — and to remember that the promised with-ness is attached to the restraint.

## Verse 195

**"Spend in the cause of Allah and do not let your own hands throw you into destruction ˹by withholding˺. And do good, for Allah certainly loves the good-doers."**

In simple terms: and spend in the way of Allah, and do not throw [yourselves] with your own hands into destruction. And do good; indeed, Allah loves the doers of good.

*Wa anfiqu fi sabili Allahi* — "and spend in the way of Allah."

The immediate context is the struggle of the preceding verses, and the commentators read the spending here as including support of the expedition. But the phrase *fi sabil Allah* is general in the Qur'an and the tradition has always applied it to every form of spending in God's cause, with charity at its head.

*Wa la tulqu bi-aydikum ila al-tahlukati* — "and do not throw with your hands into destruction."

***Tahlukah*** is from the root meaning to perish: destruction, ruin.

**And here is the crux, and the verse's whole point, because the answer is the opposite of what everyone expects.**

The transmitted occasion, narrated by Abu Ayyub al-Ansari and recorded by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and others, is that when the religion spread and its supporters multiplied, some of the Ansar said to one another privately that their wealth had been depleted, and they held back from spending and from going out — and this verse was revealed. **The destruction it warns against is the withholding**, not the giving.

That inverts the ordinary human calculus completely. The instinct is that spending depletes and withholding preserves. The verse says the reverse: the ruin is in holding on, and the safety is in giving.

A second reading is also transmitted and is the basis for the general prohibition of self-destruction: the verse is read as forbidding a person from throwing himself into ruin, from which the scholars derived the prohibition of suicide. Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah: **"Whoever kills himself with something will be punished with it on the Day of Resurrection."** Both readings are held and they are compatible.

*Wa ahsinu inna Allaha yuhibbu al-muhsinin* — "and do good [*ahsinu*]; indeed Allah loves the doers of good [*al-muhsinin*]."

***IHSAN*** — and this is one of the great words of the religion, and it needs its definition.

The definition is in the hadith of Jibril, recorded by Muslim from 'Umar: when Jibril asked about *ihsan*, the Prophet ﷺ said: **"That you worship Allah as though you see Him; and if you do not see Him, [know that] He sees you."**

That is the summit of the religion, and it is a statement about awareness rather than about behaviour. The *muhsin* is the person whose awareness of being seen governs what he does when no one is looking.

The thread back through the surah is worth noting: 2:112 made the condition of the accepted religion *aslama wajhahu lillahi **wa huwa muhsin*** — surrender **and** *ihsan*. 2:83 commanded *ihsan* to parents. 2:178 required the debtor to pay *bi-ihsan*. And here the command is general and the reward is named: **Allah loves the muhsinin.**

For practice, two things.

The first is the inversion. **The destruction is in the withholding.** That is worth holding against the specific fear that produces it — the fear that giving will leave one short. The verse's answer is not a promise of material replacement. It is stronger: it is a statement that the withholding is itself the ruin. A person who has closed his hand out of fear has already sustained the loss he was trying to avoid.

And the second is *ihsan*, which the hadith of Jibril places above outward correctness. It is possible to do an act correctly and without *ihsan* — to give the right amount with the wrong presence, to pray the right units with an absent heart. The *muhsin* is the one who does it as though seeing. And the promise attached is the one the Qur'an attaches to very little: **Allah loves them.**

## Verse 196

**"Complete the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage for Allah. But if prevented ˹from proceeding˺, then ˹offer˺ whatever sacrificial animals you can afford. And do not shave your heads until the sacrificial animal reaches its destination. But if any of you is ill or has a scalp ailment ˹requiring shaving˺, then compensate either by fasting, charity, or a sacrificial offering. In times of peace, you may combine the pilgrimage and minor pilgrimage then make the sacrificial offering you can afford. Whoever cannot afford that ˹offering˺, let them fast three days during pilgrimage and seven after returning ˹home˺—completing ten. These offerings are for those who do not live near the Sacred House. And be mindful of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment."**

This is the principal verse of the pilgrimage law, and it legislates in five movements.

**1. *Wa atimmu al-hajja wa al-'umrata lillahi*** — "and complete the Hajj and the 'Umrah for Allah."

*Atimmu* is to bring to completion, to perfect. Once the rites are begun, they are to be completed. On whether 'Umrah is obligatory in itself: the schools differed and continue to — al-Shafi'i and Ahmad held it obligatory once in a lifetime for the one able, while Abu Hanifah and Malik held it a confirmed Sunnah. That is a genuine disagreement and it should not be flattened.

*Lillah* — **for Allah** — is the qualifier on the completion: finished for Him, which excludes doing it for reputation or as a commercial or social exercise.

**2. *Fa-in uhsirtum fa-ma istaysara mina al-hadyi*** — "but if you are prevented, then whatever is easy of the sacrificial animal."

***Uhsirtum*** is from a root meaning to be confined, prevented, besieged: the state of the *muhsar*, the pilgrim blocked from completing the rites. The archetypal case is al-Hudaybiyyah in the sixth year, when the Prophet ﷺ and the companions were prevented from reaching the House, and they slaughtered the sacrificial animals, shaved, and exited the state of consecration there. The majority of the jurists extended *ihsar* to prevention by illness as well as by an enemy; some restricted it to the enemy. That is a live disagreement.

**3. *Wa la tahliqu ru'usakum hatta yablugha al-hadyu mahillahu*** — "and do not shave your heads until the offering reaches its place."

The consecrated pilgrim does not shave until the sacrifice has been made at its appointed place.

*Fa-man kana minkum maridan aw bihi adhan min ra'sihi fa-fidyatun min siyamin aw sadaqatin aw nusuk* — "but whoever among you is ill or has an ailment of his head, then a ransom of fasting or charity or sacrifice."

**And this is specified by one of the most useful hadiths in the religion.** Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Ka'b ibn 'Ujrah: the Prophet ﷺ came to him while he was lighting a fire under a pot, and lice were falling on his face — he was in consecration and afflicted. The Prophet ﷺ said: "Does the lice of your head trouble you?" He said yes. And he said: **"Then shave your head, and fast three days, or feed six poor people, or sacrifice a sheep."**

So the three options are a **choice**, not a sequence, and their measures are given by the Sunnah: three days of fasting, feeding six poor people, or a sheep. The jurists differ on some details of the feeding measure.

**4. *Fa-idha amintum fa-man tamatta'a bi-al-'umrati ila al-hajji fa-ma istaysara mina al-hadyi*** — "and when you are secure, then whoever performs *tamattu'* with the 'Umrah up to the Hajj, then whatever is easy of the offering."

***Tamattu'*** is the technical term for entering consecration for 'Umrah, completing it and leaving that state, then entering consecration for Hajj in the same journey. The three modes are *tamattu'*, *qiran* (both in one consecration) and *ifrad* (Hajj alone), and the schools hold that *tamattu'* and *qiran* carry the offering.

**5. *Fa-man lam yajid fa-siyamu thalathati ayyamin fi al-hajji wa sab'atin idha raja'tum tilka 'asharatun kamilatun*** — "but whoever does not find [one], then fasting three days in the Hajj and seven when you have returned — that is ten complete."

Whoever cannot afford the animal fasts instead: three days during the pilgrimage and seven after returning home. The jurists differ on the precise window for the three and on whether they may be fasted at Mina.

***TILKA 'ASHARATUN KAMILATUN*** — "**that is ten complete**" — and the commentators asked why the obvious arithmetic is stated. The answer they gave is that the explicit total removes ambiguity: it forecloses any reading in which the three and the seven might be alternatives, or in which the total might be thought uncertain. It is an emphatic clarification of a ruling, in a verse full of numbers where a mistake is easy.

*Dhalika li-man lam yakun ahluhu hadiri al-masjidi al-harami* — "that is for the one whose family is not present at the Sacred Mosque." The concession of *tamattu'* and its offering is for those who do not reside in the precinct. The residents of the Haram are not liable for it, on the majority view, with some disagreement recorded.

*Wa-ttaqu Allaha wa-'lamu anna Allaha shadidu al-'iqab* — "and be conscious of Allah, and know that Allah is severe in punishment."

And note the closing, which is **severe** — unlike the merciful closing of 194 two verses earlier. The commentators connected this to the subject matter: the rites are precisely the area in which people take liberties, treating a missed obligation as nothing, and the severity of the closing matches the laxity of the practice.

For practice, three things.

The first is the *ihsar* ruling, which has a wider spiritual use than its legal one. A person can be prevented from completing something he set out to do for God — by illness, by circumstance, by an obstacle he did not choose — and the ruling is not failure and not guilt. He does what is within reach and exits the state honourably. The religion legislates for the blocked path.

The second is the Ka'b ibn 'Ujrah hadith, and what it shows about the law's method: an obligation is laid down, a genuine hardship arises, and the relief is given in **three alternative forms** so that it is available to the poor, the wealthy and the able-bodied alike. That is the shape of the religion's concessions throughout — never a single relief that only some can use.

And the third is the "ten complete." It is a small thing, and it is a window onto how seriously the religion takes clarity in rulings. A verse that has just laid out three numbers states their total explicitly so that no one is left guessing. The teacher and the parent can learn from that: when you give a ruling, a boundary or an instruction, remove the ambiguity rather than leaving it to be discovered.

## Verse 197

**"˹Commitment to˺ pilgrimage is made in appointed months. Whoever commits to ˹performing˺ pilgrimage, let them stay away from intimate relations, foul language, and arguments during pilgrimage. Whatever good you do, Allah ˹fully˺ knows of it. Take ˹necessary˺ provisions ˹for the journey˺—surely the best provision is righteousness. And be mindful of Me, O people of reason!"**

In simple terms: the Hajj is [in] known months. So whoever has obligated himself therein to the Hajj — no *rafath*, no *fusuq*, no *jidal* during the Hajj. And whatever good you do, Allah knows it. And take provision, for indeed the best provision is *taqwa*. And be conscious of Me, O people of understanding.

*Al-hajju ashhurun ma'lumatun* — "the Hajj is [in] known months."

The months of the Hajj are Shawwal, Dhu al-Qa'dah, and Dhu al-Hijjah — the jurists differing on whether Dhu al-Hijjah is included in full or only up to its tenth. On entering consecration before these months, the schools differ; the majority hold it valid though disliked.

*Fa-man farada fihinna al-hajja* — "so whoever has obligated himself therein for the Hajj." *Farada* — made binding upon himself: the commentators read it as entering the state of consecration, by which the pilgrimage becomes obligatory upon him.

*Fa-la rafatha wa la fusuqa wa la jidala fi al-hajj* — "then no *rafath*, no *fusuq*, no *jidal* during the Hajj."

**And the three prohibitions are the heart of the verse, and the third is the one that matters most in practice.**

**1. *Rafath*** — sexual intimacy and the speech leading to it. And note the thread: **this is the same word as 2:187**, where it was *permitted* on the nights of the fast. The same word, permitted there and prohibited here — which is itself the point: the ruling attaches to the state, not to the act. What is lawful in one state is forbidden in another, and the religion is not inconsistent for saying so.

**2. *Fusuq*** — from a root meaning to depart from obedience: sin, disobedience, corruption. The commentators read it variously as sin generally, or as specifically the slaughtering to idols that the pre-Islamic Arabs practised at the rites, or as abusive speech. All are transmitted.

**3. *Jidal*** — argument, contention, disputation, quarrelling. The commentators read it as quarrelling with one's companions and those serving one, or as disputing about the rites themselves.

**And here is the observation that makes this verse practically useful to everyone, pilgrim or not.** The three are graded from the physical through the moral to the verbal, and in practice people guard the first two scrupulously and abandon the third entirely. A pilgrim who would not dream of breaking the first prohibition will spend the whole pilgrimage irritable, disputing, correcting, complaining and contending. The verse names that as one of the three things to be abandoned.

The reward is stated in exactly these terms. Recorded by al-Bukhari and Muslim from Abu Hurayrah: **"Whoever performs the Hajj and does not commit *rafath* nor *fusuq* returns [free of sin] as on the day his mother bore him."** The Prophet ﷺ named the first two. The third is the one that keeps people from the full return.

*Wa ma taf'alu min khayrin ya'lamhu Allahu* — "and whatever good you do, Allah knows it." After three prohibitions, an opening, and the encouragement is that nothing done is lost.

*Wa tazawwadu fa-inna khayra al-zadi al-taqwa* — "and take provision, for indeed the best provision is *taqwa*."

**And the occasion of this clause is one of the most instructive in the Qur'an.** Recorded by al-Bukhari from Ibn 'Abbas: the people of Yemen used to perform the Hajj without taking provisions, saying "we are the ones who trust [in Allah]" — *al-mutawakkilun* — and when they arrived at Makkah they would beg from the people. And Allah revealed: **"And take provision, for indeed the best provision is taqwa."**

**This is the definitive correction of a false reliance on God, and it needs to be stated carefully because it cuts against a very common piety.**

The verse does not abolish *tawakkul*, reliance upon God. It abolishes a counterfeit of it that consists of neglecting the means and then living off other people. True reliance takes the provision and trusts the Provider. Neglecting the provision and calling it trust is not reliance; it is a burden transferred to someone else.

And the hadith that is always quoted in this connection contains the correction inside itself. Recorded by al-Tirmidhi, Ibn Majah and Ahmad from 'Umar: "If you were to rely upon Allah with the reliance He is due, He would provide for you as He provides for the birds: they go out in the morning empty and return full."

**Note the detail that is almost always skipped: the birds go out.** They do not sit in the nest waiting to be fed. The hadith used to support reliance on God is itself a command to go out and seek — the reliance is in the going, not in the staying.

*Khayra al-zadi al-taqwa* — the best provision is *taqwa*. And note the structure: the material provision is commanded, and then the superior provision is named, in the same sentence. That is the surah's characteristic method, seen at 2:184 (the concession given, then "and that you fast is better for you") and at 2:158.

*Wa-ttaquni ya uli al-albab* — "and be conscious of Me, O people of understanding." And the thread is now established: 2:179 addressed *uli al-albab*; 2:164 and 2:170 and 2:171 turned on the reasoning faculty; and here again the reasoners are addressed directly.

For practice, three things.

The first is the third prohibition. **Guard your tongue and your temper during worship more carefully than you guard the rituals.** The tradition's own summary of the Hajj's reward names two of the three; the third is where the failure happens. And this extends to every collective act of worship — the mosque, the fasting household, the religious gathering. The form is usually preserved. The quarrelling is what ruins it.

The second is the provision and the true meaning of reliance. Take the means. Trust the One who gives them increase. Do not neglect the means and call it trust, and do not trust the means and forget the One. The birds go out — and return full.

And the third is *khayr al-zad al-taqwa*, which is a sentence worth memorising. The best thing you can carry into anything — a journey, a year, a project, a marriage, the grave itself — is consciousness of God.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 198

**"There is no blame on you for seeking the bounty of your Lord ˹during this journey˺. When you return from ’Arafât, praise Allah near the sacred place and praise Him for having guided you, for surely before this ˹guidance˺ you were astray."**

In simple terms: there is no blame on you in seeking bounty from your Lord. And when you pour forth from 'Arafah, remember Allah at the Sacred Place of Rites. And remember Him as He has guided you, for indeed before it you were among those astray.

*Laysa 'alaykum junahun an tabtaghu fadlan min rabbikum* — "there is no blame on you in seeking bounty from your Lord."

The transmitted occasion, recorded by al-Bukhari from Ibn 'Abbas: the Arabs used to trade at the markets of 'Ukaz, Majannah and Dhu al-Majaz during the pilgrimage season, and when Islam came they were reluctant to trade during the rites, fearing it would count against them — and this verse was revealed.

**And note the description of the commerce: *fadlan min rabbikum* — bounty from your Lord.** The trading is not described as a worldly distraction tolerated during a spiritual occasion. It is described as bounty **from God**. Which settles a question that recurs in every religious setting: the religion does not require the suspension of ordinary life, and earning a living is itself provision from Him.

*Fa-idha afadtum min 'Arafatin* — "and when you pour forth from 'Arafah."

***Afadtum*** is from a root meaning to pour forth, to stream out — and the word for the crowd streaming down from the plain is itself the image.

And on 'Arafah: the hadith recorded by Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i, Ibn Majah and Ahmad, from 'Abd al-Rahman ibn Ya'mur, states: **"The Hajj is 'Arafah."** The standing at 'Arafah on the ninth is the pillar without which the pilgrimage is not valid.

*Fa-dhkuru Allaha 'inda al-mash'ari al-harami* — "then remember Allah at the Sacred Place of Rites." Al-Mash'ar al-Haram is identified as Muzdalifah, where the pilgrims spend the night in remembrance after the standing.

*Wa-dhkuruhu kama hadakum* — "and remember Him **as He has guided you**."

**And this is the crux, and it carries a principle of the first importance.**

The remembrance is to be **in accordance with the guidance** — as He taught, not as one invents. The commentators took it in two senses, both transmitted: remember Him with the guidance He gave you (so the form of the remembrance is taken from the revelation, not devised), or remember Him abundantly as His guidance to you has been abundant.

The first reading is the one that governs practice, and it states a rule the tradition holds throughout: **acts of worship are *tawqifi* — they are taken from the text and not invented.** A person may not devise a form of remembrance and attribute it to the religion. That is the principle that separates the Sunnah from innovation, and it is stated here in four words.

*Wa in kuntum min qablihi la-mina al-dallin* — "and indeed, before it, you were among those astray."

The verse reminds them of their prior state at the moment of commanding gratitude. The remembrance is grounded in the memory of what one was before the guidance — which is why the remembrance is not a formality.

For practice, three things.

The first is the commerce clause, and its application is broader than the pilgrimage. The religion does not ask a person to suspend his livelihood in order to worship. The market at the pilgrimage is described as bounty from God. A person who thinks that earning is a distraction from the religious life has misread this verse.

The second is *kama hadakum*, and it is the rule for worship. Worship as taught, not as devised. It is worth applying to the specifically contemporary forms — the remembrances, celebrations and practices that have no basis in the text but are performed as religion. The verse's standard is: as He guided you.

And the third is the closing reminder. Gratitude is produced by remembering where one was. A person who has forgotten that he was astray will not remember God with much force, which is why the tradition recommends the regular recollection of one's own earlier state.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 199

**"Then go forth with the rest of the pilgrims. And seek Allah’s forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful."**

In simple terms: then pour forth from where the people pour forth, and seek Allah's forgiveness. Indeed, Allah is Forgiving, Merciful.

*Thumma afidu min haythu afada al-nasu* — "then pour forth from where **the people** pour forth."

**And the crux is *al-nas* — "the people" — and the occasion behind it, which is one of the most significant in the whole pilgrimage.**

The transmitted account, recorded by al-Bukhari from 'A'ishah, is this: the Quraysh — and those who allied themselves with them, known as the *Hums* — used to stand at al-Muzdalifah and did not go out to 'Arafah with the rest of the Arabs. They claimed an exemption on the grounds that they were the people of the Sacred Precinct and should not leave it. The rest of the Arabs went out to 'Arafah.

**And this verse abolished that exemption: pour forth from where the people pour forth.**

The significance of this is very large, and it should be stated as plainly as possible: **the verse removes a privilege claimed by lineage and custodianship from the rites of the religion.** The Quraysh were not to stand apart because of who they were. They were to do what everyone else did, from the place everyone else did it.

**And note that this is one of the most consistent threads in the whole surah:**

- 2:124 — the covenant of leadership is not reached by wrongdoers; it is not inherited.
- 2:134 — no inherited guilt and no inherited merit.
- 2:158 — a rite touched by idolatry is reclaimed rather than abolished, and no one owns it.
- 2:170 — "we found our fathers upon it" is not a warrant.
- 2:189 — righteousness is not an inherited practice entered by the back door.
- **2:199 — no exemption in the rites by virtue of who your people are.**

The Prophet ﷺ, himself of Quraysh, went out to 'Arafah with the people. Which is the verse enacted.

*Wa-staghfiru Allaha* — "and seek Allah's forgiveness."

**And note what the command is at this point in the rites.** At the moment of the pouring forth, after the standing that is the pilgrimage itself, the instruction is **istighfar** — to ask forgiveness.

That is worth holding, because most people imagine the climax of the pilgrimage as a state of elevation or a feeling. The Qur'an names the climax as a request: ask to be forgiven.

*Inna Allaha ghafurun rahim* — "indeed Allah is Forgiving, Merciful."

For practice, two things.

The first is the abolition of privilege. There is no exemption in this religion from a common obligation on the grounds of status, family, scholarship, office, or community standing. The Quraysh tried it with the best claim anyone has ever had — they were literally the custodians of the House — and the verse refused it. The person who thinks his position excuses him from something everyone else must do is in the position this verse addressed.

And the second is the *istighfar*. The summit of the greatest collective act of worship in the religion is a request for forgiveness, not a declaration of attainment. That is the correct shape of the religious life generally: the highest point is where one asks, not where one announces.

## Verse 200

**"When you have fulfilled your sacred rites, praise Allah as you used to praise your forefathers ˹before Islam˺, or even more passionately. There are some who say, “Our Lord! Grant us ˹Your bounties˺ in this world,” but they will have no share in the Hereafter."**

In simple terms: and when you have completed your rites, remember Allah as you remember your fathers, or with stronger remembrance. And among the people is he who says: "Our Lord, give us in this world" — and he has no share in the Hereafter.

*Fa-idha qadaytum manasikakum fa-dhkuru Allaha ka-dhikrikum aba'akum aw ashadda dhikran* — "and when you have completed your rites, remember Allah as you remember your fathers, or with stronger remembrance."

***KA-DHIKRIKUM ABA'AKUM*** — "as your remembrance of your fathers" — and the background is essential.

The transmitted account: when the Arabs completed the pilgrimage they would gather at Mina and **boast** — reciting the glories of their forefathers, their tribes, their battles, their generosity, their poetry. It was a formal session of *mufakharah*, competitive boasting about lineage, and it was conducted with real energy.

**And the verse does not ask them to stop. It asks them to redirect.**

Remember God **as** you remember your fathers — with that same intensity, that same eloquence, that same gathering of the heart — **or more intensely** (*aw ashadda dhikran*).

That is a remarkable move, and it is worth noticing for what it says about what the religion wants from a person. It does not ask for less passion. It asks for the same passion, aimed somewhere else. The religion is not asking you to become a less intense person. It is asking you to be as intense about God as you already are about your family's name, your nation, your side, your grievance.

And the standard set is high: **at least as much as you gave the ancestors, and preferably more.** Most people would fail that test badly if they measured honestly.

*Fa-mina al-nasi man yaqulu rabbana atina fi al-dunya wa ma lahu fi al-akhirati min khalaqin* — "and among the people is he who says: 'Our Lord, give us in this world' — and he has no share in the Hereafter."

***Khalaq*** is from a root meaning to measure and to apportion: a share, an allotment, a portion. He has no portion in the Hereafter.

**And note what is being described: a person at prayer.** He says *rabbana* — "our Lord." The address is right. The form is right. The language of supplication is correct. And the content is entirely about this world.

**Which is the verse's real point: it is possible to pray correctly and ask wrongly.**

And it must be stated carefully, because the opposite error is also a mistake. Asking for good in this world is not forbidden — the next verse, 2:201, gives the prescribed prayer and it includes *rabbana atina fi al-dunya hasanah*, "our Lord, give us good in this world." So the fault is not asking for this world. **The fault is asking for this world only.**

The correction is given in the very next verse: "Our Lord, give us in this world [that which is] good and in the Hereafter [that which is] good, and protect us from the punishment of the Fire." 200 and 201 are a matched pair — the wrong prayer and the right one, set side by side.

The supporting texts are consistent: 17:18–19, on the one who desires the immediate life; 42:20; and 87:16–17: "But you prefer the life of this world, while the Hereafter is better and more enduring."

For practice, three things.

The first is the redirection. The verse's method is worth imitating on oneself: **identify what you speak about with the most energy, and note that the religion wants that same energy for God.** Most people can name it immediately — a family's reputation, a nation, a political cause, a grievance, an achievement. The verse does not say "stop caring about that." It says: remember God at least that much.

The second is the wrong prayer, and it is a genuinely searching test. A person can have a perfectly correct religious vocabulary, address God properly, pray regularly — and ask, in substance, only for the things of this life. The diagnostic is simple and uncomfortable: **look at what you have actually asked for over the last month.** If the list is entirely worldly, the form of the prayer was right and the content was this verse's warning.

And the third is the correction, which is in the next verse and which is worth using daily. The religion does not ask a person to stop wanting things in this world. It asks him to want them **and** the Hereafter — and it supplies the exact words: *rabbana atina fi al-dunya hasanatan wa fi al-akhirati hasanatan wa qina 'adhaba al-nar*.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

Two halves, and the seam runs between 195 and 196.

**The bounded permission (191–195).** The combat rulings continue, and with them four restrictions in a row: the response is to be reciprocal — expulsion for expulsion (191); it ceases the moment the other side ceases, and the desisting are met with the naming of God's forgiveness (192); it is for the ending of persecution and not for compulsion in religion, and even the permissible hostility is directed at **wrongdoers** — a category of acts, not of people (193); and it is to be exactly proportionate, with consciousness of God named as the only brake and the with-ness of God promised to the one who restrains (194). Then spending in His way, with the inversion at the centre of it: the destruction lies in the withholding, not in the giving (195).

**The pilgrimage (196–200).** The Hajj and 'Umrah are to be completed, with the ruling of the one prevented, the ransom specified in three alternative forms, and the fast of ten days for the one who cannot afford the offering (196). The pilgrimage has known months, and during it three things are abandoned — intimacy, sin, and argument — with provision commanded and the best provision named as *taqwa*, in a verse that corrects a false reliance on God (197). Commerce during the rites is permitted as bounty from the Lord, and the remembrance is to be as taught and not as devised (198). The pouring forth is from where all the people pour forth, which abolishes a privilege claimed by lineage, and the summit of the rites is a request for forgiveness (199). And the boasting of the forefathers is redirected rather than extinguished — remember God at least that much — together with the warning of the prayer that is correctly addressed and wrongly aimed (200).

Three threads hold the batch. First, **every permission in this passage carries its limit inside it**: combatants (190), sanctuary (191), cessation (192, 193), proportionality (194), and *taqwa* as the internal brake (194). Second, **no privilege by status or lineage survives anywhere in this religion** — the covenant not inherited (124), no inherited merit (134), no owned rite (158), ancestry not a warrant (170), and now no exemption in the rites for the custodians of the House (199). And third, **the religion redirects energy rather than extinguishing it** — take the provision, and the best provision is *taqwa* (197); seek the bounty of your Lord (198); remember God as you remembered your fathers, or more (200).

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given. This is a synthesised commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and supplying precise references from memory would mean inventing them. Where the commentators and jurists differ, the disagreement has been presented rather than resolved: the two readings of *fitnah* in verse 191; whether *ihsar* covers illness as well as an enemy; whether the 'Umrah is obligatory or a confirmed Sunnah; the readings of *kama hadakum* in verse 198; and the windows for the three fast-days in verse 196.

Transmitted reports have been flagged as reports throughout: the Hudaybiyyah contexts, the markets of 'Ukaz and Majannah, and the pre-Islamic gathering of boasting at Mina. The gradings of the narrations on reliance and on "The Hajj is 'Arafah" have been noted as discussed rather than asserted.

Four boundaries have been observed, and the first is the most important thing in this batch. On verses 191–193: the governing restriction of 190 (*alladhina yuqatilunakum*) has been restated as controlling every verse that follows it; the correct meaning of *hatta la takuna fitnah* has been given as the ending of **persecution** and not of disbelief, with 2:256, 10:99 and 60:8 cited as settling it; the *zalimin* qualifier at 193 has been identified as a category of conduct rather than of identity; and the standing rule has been repeated in its strongest form — that no individual may declare another's blood lawful and no private person may act on his own reading of these texts. On verse 194: the brake on permitted retaliation has been located in *taqwa* rather than in any external check, and the with-ness of God has been attached to the restraint. On verse 197: the correction of false *tawakkul* has been made without abolishing true reliance, and the detail that the birds in the hadith **go out** has been given as the hadith's own answer. And on verse 200: asking for good in this world has been explicitly permitted, with the fault located in asking for it **only**, and the correction identified as the very next verse.
