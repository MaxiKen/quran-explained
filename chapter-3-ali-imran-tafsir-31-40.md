# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 31–40

We come now to the passage that gives the surah its name. Verses 31–40 are the door into the story of the family of 'Imrān — Mary, her mother's vow, her upbringing in the sanctuary, and the announcement of John that follows from watching God feed her.

The placement is not incidental. The surah has just spent twenty verses in hard argument with the People of the Book — about their refusal to be judged (23), their invented immunity (24), their misdirected loyalty (28). And now, instead of pressing the argument, it tells a story about a family they also revere. That is the strategy, and it is worth naming: the surah is going to establish its claims about Jesus by narrating his family, from inside the shared inheritance, rather than by contesting Christian doctrine head-on. The confrontation at Najrān will not arrive until verse 61. Everything before it is preparation.

And the preparation begins with a test.

---

## Verse 31

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “If you ˹sincerely˺ love Allah, then follow me; Allah will love you and forgive your sins. For Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”"**

In simple terms: Say: if you love God, then follow me, and God will love you and forgive you your sins. God is Forgiving, Merciful.

This is one of the most quoted verses in the Qur'an, and one of the least comfortable. It is commonly called *āyat al-muḥibbah*, the verse of love, and what it does is put a test to a claim.

**The reported occasion.** Two accounts are transmitted.

The first, from al-Ḥasan: some people in the time of the Messenger of God ﷺ had declared that they loved God, and this verse came down about them. The second, transmitted by al-Kalbī from Abū Ṣāliḥ from Ibn 'Abbās: when the Jews said "**we are the children of God and His beloved**" — the claim the Qur'an records at 5:18 — this verse was revealed in response.

Both are **reported** accounts. What matters more than which one is the occasion is how the early authorities framed the verse's function. Al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī and a number of the Salaf said it plainly: "Some people claimed that they loved God, so **God tested them with this verse**." The word is *imtaḥana* — put to the test, examined. That is what this verse is: an examination of a claim.

**The logic, and the direction it runs.** Look at the structure carefully, because the direction is everything:

*In kuntum tuḥibbūna Allāha* — **if** you love God. The love is the premise. The verse does not command you to love God, and it does not promise that following will produce love in you. It takes the love as already claimed.

*Fa-ittabi'ūnī* — **then follow me.** The proof. The claim is tested by a single, concrete, checkable thing.

*Yuḥbibkumu Allāhu* — **God will love you.** The result. And note the change of subject: your love for Him is the premise; **His love for you** is the prize. The verse's whole weight sits on that asymmetry. Your love is what you assert. His love is what you need.

So the verse is not saying "follow me so that you may come to love God." It is saying: **you already say you love Him; here is the only evidence that would make it true; and here is what that evidence earns you.**

**Why "follow me" and not something more obviously religious?** It would have been possible for the verse to say "then pray," or "then give charity," or "then obey the law." It says *ittabi'ūnī* — follow **me**. Following a person is a whole-life category: where he goes, how he eats, what he refuses, what he endures, the shape of his days. The test is not a ritual. It is a person, and whether your life is being conformed to his.

And this is where the verse becomes uncomfortable for everyone, including believers. Ibn Kathīr states the consequence without softening it: this verse "judges against those who claim to love God yet do not follow the way of Muhammad." Such a person's claim is not true "until he follows the *sharī'ah* of Muhammad and his religion in all his statements, actions and conditions." And he adduces the ṣaḥīḥ hadith: "Whoever performs an act that does not conform to our religion, it is rejected" (reported by al-Bukhārī and Muslim).

That is a demanding standard, and it is meant to be. But note where it presses: not on the person who fails and repents, but on the person who **claims** while declining the test. The verse is aimed at the gap between profession and practice, and it closes the gap by making profession worthless without practice.

**A note on the order of the two gifts.** *Yuḥbibkumu Allāhu wa-yaghfir lakum dhunūbakum* — God will love you **and** forgive you your sins.

The love comes first. You are not loved because you were forgiven; you are **forgiven because you are loved**. That sequence is not incidental and it is not a trivial point about word order. It is the difference between a religion of transaction and a religion of grace. On the transactional reading, the sequence would be: obey, be forgiven, then be loved. The verse has it the other way round: be loved, and the forgiveness follows from the love.

**A brief theological note on the love of God.** The Sunni position is that God's love is a real attribute, affirmed as He has affirmed it, without asking how (*bi-lā kayf*), without likening it to the love of creatures, and without reducing it to something else. Some later theologians — and the Mu'tazila before them — interpreted God's love as His will to bestow good (*irādat al-iḥsān*), or identified it with the good He bestows, in order to avoid ascribing to Him the neediness that creaturely love implies. The debate is real and it turns on how far the language of attribute may be pressed. I flag it rather than resolve it; the practical content of the verse does not depend on which side one takes.

**The contemporary shape of the claim.** The specific claim being tested in the seventh century was "we are God's children and His beloved." The claim has not disappeared; it has only changed costume. It now sounds like: "I love God, I just don't need the religion." "I'm spiritual, not religious." "God knows my heart." "I don't need anyone to tell me how to worship." To each of those, this verse says exactly one thing: then follow him. Not then feel more. Not then believe harder. Follow him.

And the mercy is in the closing: *wa-Allāhu ghafūrun raḥīm*. The verse that sets the highest possible bar ends by naming the two names that describe what God does with people who fall short of it.

## Verse 32

**"Say, ˹O Prophet,˺ “Obey Allah and His Messenger.” If they still turn away, then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers."**

In simple terms: Say: obey God and the Messenger. But if they turn away, God does not love the disbelievers.

**The shift in register.** Verse 31 was conditional: *if* you love. Verse 32 is imperative: **obey**. The movement from the conditional to the flat command is deliberate. Having tested the claim of love, the verse states the bare obligation. Love may be claimed or not; obedience is not optional either way.

**"Obey Allah and His Messenger."** *Aṭī'ū Allāha wa-al-rasūl.* This is one of the most repeated formulas in the Qur'an — it appears well over a dozen times (3:32, 3:132, 4:13, 4:59, 4:69, 5:92, 8:1, 8:20, 8:46, 24:54, 47:33, 58:13, 64:12). It is the Qur'an's standard statement of where authority lies, and the pairing is never collapsed into one: God **and** the Messenger.

That pairing is the scriptural basis for the authority of the Sunnah, and it deserves a note on what it does and does not mean. The established position is that obedience to the Messenger is not a second, independent authority running alongside obedience to God; it is obedience to God, because the Messenger speaks only what he has been given. The Qur'an says so directly at 4:80: "Whoever obeys the Messenger has obeyed God." And the Messenger is protected (*ma'ṣūm*) from error in what he conveys of the religion. So the formula establishes one authority with two names, not two authorities.

What it does **not** mean is unlimited personal authority — obedience in private whims or in matters left to human judgment. The Prophet ﷺ himself distinguished between the religion he was sent with and his own human opinion in worldly matters (the well-known narration about the pollination of the palm trees, reported by Muslim, in which he said: "You know best about your worldly affairs"). The obedience claimed by this verse is obedience within the religion, and the religion is what he conveyed.

**"If they still turn away."** *Fa-in tawallaw.* The same verb that appeared at verse 20 — "and if they turn away, your duty is only to deliver." The surah is consistent: turning away is a real possibility, and the response to it is not compulsion.

**"Then truly Allah does not like the disbelievers."** *Fa-inna Allāha lā yuḥibbu al-kāfirīn.* And note how this completes verse 31. Verse 31 promised: follow, and God will **love** you. Verse 32 closes: turn away, and God does not **love**. The whole passage is running on a single currency — love — and the two verses are the two possible outcomes.

Note also the phrasing. The verse does not say "God hates them." It says *lā yuḥibb* — He does not love. In the Qur'an's vocabulary, that negation is severe, but it is a negation of a specific thing. And note that what is not loved is *al-kāfirīn* — those who disbelieve, described by their act of covering over the truth they were given. It is not a statement about a fixed class of people.

I want to be explicit about the boundary here, because this verse is used carelessly. It describes those who have received the message and turned away from it. It does not license any reader to identify a particular person as one of them, and it certainly does not license enmity toward people on the basis of their not being Muslim — the same surah will command justice and kindness toward those who have not fought you (60:8–9, already cited at verse 28), and the Prophet ﷺ dealt with, traded with, and married among the People of the Book throughout his life. The verse is a statement about a posture before God, and the knowledge of who finally holds it belongs to God.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 33

**"Indeed, Allah chose Adam, Noah, the family of Abraham, and the family of ’Imrân above all people ˹of their time˺."**

In simple terms: God chose Adam, and Noah, and the family of Abraham, and the family of 'Imrān, above all peoples.

**"Chose."** *Iṣṭafā* — from the root *ṣ-f-w*, purity. The form carries both selection and purification: He selected them and made them pure. It is not a random preference and it is not a reward computed after the fact; it is a deliberate divine act, and the Qur'an attributes it entirely to God.

**The four names, and the shape of the list.** Two individuals followed by two houses.

*Adam* — the father of mankind, chosen and taught and made God's vicegerent.

*Nūḥ* — the second father of mankind, the one through whom humanity was restarted after the flood.

*Āla Ibrāhīm* — the house of Abraham, from whom come the Israelites through Isaac and the Arabs through Ishmael, and most of the prophets the Qur'an names. Calling Abraham's descendants a *family* rather than naming individuals is itself a claim: the election runs through a line.

*Āla 'Imrān* — the house of 'Imrān, from whom come Mary and Jesus.

So the list moves from the origin of the human race, to its restart, to the great prophetic line, to the family whose story is about to be told. And that is the point of the verse in its place: **before the surah narrates Mary and Jesus, it establishes that this family stands in the same company as Adam, Noah and Abraham.** The Najrān Christians are about to hear an account of Jesus that honours him more than they can object to and less than they claim.

**"Above all people."** *'Alā al-'ālamīn.* The supplied translation adds "˹of their time˺", and that qualification is the majority's. The reasoning is straightforward: Adam was not chosen over people who did not yet exist, and each of these was chosen over the people of his own era. Most commentators therefore read "the worlds" here as "the peoples of their times." The addition is an interpretation, but it is a necessary one.

**Now the 'Imrān question, and it is a real one.** There are two figures called 'Imrān in the Biblical and Qur'anic material, and readers have conflated them for a very long time.

The first is **'Imrān father of Moses and Aaron** — the Biblical Amram of Exodus 6:20. His daughter was Miriam, sister of Moses and Aaron.

The second is **'Imrān father of Mary**, the mother of Jesus.

The confusion is easy to see: both men had a daughter named Maryam/Miriam. And the Qur'an compounds it for a modern reader by calling Mary "**sister of Aaron**" at 19:28, which looks like a straightforward genealogical error — Aaron's sister Miriam lived, on any chronology, well over a millennium before Mary the mother of Jesus.

**The mainstream Muslim answer** has two parts, and both are well attested.

First, the 'Imrān of this surah is the father of Mary. The surah is named for **his** family, and the family it proceeds to narrate is Mary's. Muslim tradition holds that there were many centuries — the sources speak of roughly eighteen — between the Biblical Amram and the father of Mary.

Second, the phrase "sister of Aaron" is explained by the Arab usage of extended kinship. In the narration reported by Muslim, Mughīrah ibn Shu'bah said: when he came to Najrān, the Christians there asked him, "You recite 'O sister of Hārūn' in the Qur'an, whereas Moses was born long before Jesus by such-and-such a period." He did not know what to say and asked the Prophet ﷺ, who explained that they used to name people after their prophets and righteous forebears. Ibn Kathīr glosses it with the Arab idiom: "This is similar to saying 'O brother of Tamīm' to a man of the Tamīm tribe, and 'O brother of Muḍar' to a man of the Muḍar tribe." An alternative transmitted explanation is that Mary had a brother literally named Aaron, or that a pious man of her family was called Aaron.

Scholars such as Barbara Stowasser have stated the conclusion firmly: to identify Mary the mother of Jesus with Miriam the sister of Moses "is completely wrong and in contradiction to the sound hadith and the Qur'anic text."

**A modern scholarly framing** is worth adding, because it approaches the same difficulty from a different angle and it is now common in Islamic studies. Angelika Neuwirth, Nicolai Sinai and Michael Marx represent a consensus in recent scholarship that the Qur'an is not making a genealogical mistake here at all but is using **typology** — deliberately placing Mary within the Israelite sacred lineage so that she reads as the culmination of that history. On that reading, "sister of Aaron" is a theological statement, not a family-tree claim.

I give both the traditional answer and the modern one, because they are answers to slightly different questions. The traditional answer establishes that the text is not in error. The modern one proposes a reason the names were chosen. Neither requires the reader to accept that the Qur'an confused two women.

## Verse 34

**"They are descendants of one another. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."**

In simple terms: offspring, one from another. And God is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.

**"Descendants of one another."** *Dhurriyyatan ba'duhā min ba'd.* One seed, generation from generation.

And this short verse is doing a great deal of work. It prevents the list in verse 33 from being read as four unrelated divine preferences. Adam, Noah, Abraham's house and 'Imrān's house are **one chain** — a single chosen lineage running through history, each generation emerging from the one before it. Election is being presented as continuous, not episodic.

**But — and this is the balance the Qur'an holds throughout — the lineage does not save the individual.** This is essential, and it is where both the tribal error and the atomistic error go wrong.

The Qur'an is unambiguous that belonging to a chosen line confers no automatic immunity. Noah's son refused the ark and drowned, and Noah's plea for him was refused (11:42–47). Abraham's father was an idolater, and Abraham was told to dissociate from him after praying for him (9:114). Lot's wife perished with her people despite being in the prophet's household (66:10). And the wife of Noah and the wife of Lot are named in the Qur'an as the example of two righteous men married to two women who were not saved (66:10).

So the correct reading of verses 33–34 together is: **God elects a line, and every individual within that line is accountable for himself.** Election is real; inheritance is not automatic. That is the double claim, and it is the exact mean between the error of relying on ancestry — which is what the Jews of Madinah were accused of in verse 24 — and the error of imagining that the individual stands before God with no history and no community.

**"And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing."** And here is the detail I would not want you to miss, because it is one of the surah's quiet masterpieces.

Why these two names, here? Because **the next two verses are about a vow and a hidden child.** Verse 35 will have the wife of 'Imrān say: "You are the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing" — quoting back the very two names with which this verse closed. And verse 36 will say that "God knew best what she delivered."

So the closing formula is not decorative. It is the announcement of the next scene. God hears the vow that has not yet been spoken, and knows the child who has not yet been born. The Qur'an's closing names are almost always load-bearing, and this is a perfect small instance: **the narrator's statement about God becomes the supplicant's address to Him in the very next verse.**

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 35

**"˹Remember˺ when the wife of ’Imrân said, “My Lord! I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service, so accept it from me. You ˹alone˺ are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”"**

In simple terms: when the wife of 'Imrān said: My Lord, I have vowed to You what is in my womb, freed for Your service. So accept it from me. You are the All-Hearing, the All-Knowing.

**The woman is not named.** The Qur'an calls her *imra'at 'Imrān* — the wife of 'Imrān. Tradition names her Ḥannah (Hannah, Anna), and that name is known from the extra-Qur'anic tradition, not from the text. I note this once so that the distinction is clear; the Qur'an's practice of not naming her is itself worth noticing, since it is about to name her daughter more than any other woman in the Book.

**"I dedicate what is in my womb entirely to Your service."** *Innī nadhartu laka mā fī baṭnī muḥarraran.*

*Nadhara* — I vowed, I pledged. And the key word is *muḥarraran*, from the root *ḥ-r-r*, which means **freedom**. The same root gives *taḥrīr* (liberation) and *ḥurr* (a free man). A *muḥarrar* is one who has been set free — freed from every other claim, from ordinary life, from the world's service — and devoted entirely to God.

So the vow is not "I will raise this child religiously." It is: **this child is released from everything else and belongs only to You.** In the Israelite setting the Qur'an is describing, this meant dedication to service in the sanctuary.

**The expectation behind the vow.** The transmitted accounts say she expected a male, because in the custom of that sanctuary, service was performed by men. Whether or not one accepts that specific detail as established — and it is **reported** rather than Qur'anic — the next verse makes the expectation visible on its own: her first words on delivering are about the child's sex. Something in her had assumed a boy.

**"So accept it from me."** *Fa-taqabbal minnī.* And here is the piety of the sentence. She has vowed; she has given; and then she asks whether it will be **accepted**. The one who gives does not assume the gift was wanted. That is the whole difference between a vow and a bargain: a bargain assumes the consideration, a vow waits to be received.

It is the same verb that will be used of God's response in verse 37 — *fa-taqabbalahā rabbuhā*, "her Lord accepted her." The question of verse 35 and the answer of verse 37 are the same word. She asks; the surah reports that He did.

**"You alone are truly the All-Hearing, All-Knowing."** And this is the echo I flagged at verse 34. She closes her prayer with the exact pair of names the narrator used one verse earlier. The effect is intimate: the God the text describes is the God she addresses, and she addresses Him with His own description of Himself.

**And note what is being vowed.** She is dedicating **what is in her womb** — an unknown. Not a child she has met, not a son whose character she can vouch for, not a gift she has inspected. Whatever is there, whoever it turns out to be, is God's. That is the highest form of the vow and the hardest: most of us are willing to give God the good parts of a life we have already examined. She gives Him the unexamined thing.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 36

**"When she delivered, she said, “My Lord! I have given birth to a girl,”—and Allah fully knew what she had delivered—“and the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed.”"**

In simple terms: when she delivered, she said: My Lord, I have delivered a female — and God knew best what she delivered — and the male is not like the female. I have named her Mary, and I seek refuge for her and her offspring with You from Satan, the outcast.

**"And Allah fully knew what she had delivered."** This interruption is the frame around everything else in the verse, and it is placed exactly where it needs to be: between her announcement and her assessment. Whatever she is about to say about the child's sex, the text tells you first that God's knowledge is the measure and hers is not.

**Now the crux, and I am going to be careful and direct about it, because this is one of the most abused verses in the Qur'an.**

*Wa-laysa al-dhakaru ka-al-unthā* — "and the male is not like the female." Who says this, and what does it mean? The commentators give two answers, and both are transmitted.

**Reading A — the mother says it.** On this reading she is disappointed. She had vowed a child to the sanctuary's service, and in the custom she knew, that service was performed by males. So she says, in effect: I have given birth to a girl, and a girl cannot do what a boy could do. Read this way, the sentence records a **limited human expectation** — and the interruption "God knew best what she delivered" is precisely God's correction of it. She thinks her vow has been frustrated. The next verse shows that it has been answered beyond anything she asked.

**Reading B — God says it.** On this reading the sentence is not her complaint but God's reply: the male she hoped for could never have reached the station of this female. What she was given is not a diminished version of what she wanted; it is greater than it. Read this way, the sentence is a statement about **this particular child's rank**.

The difference shows up in the translations. Some render it with her as the speaker (Pickthall: "the male is not as the female"); some invert the comparison to capture Reading B (Maududi: "a female is not the same as a male"; Qarai: "and the female is not like the male"). Both renderings are trying to express one of the two transmitted readings.

**What must be said plainly, in both directions, is what this verse is not.** On neither reading is it a statement about the relative worth of men and women in general.

On Reading A, it is the record of a disappointed expectation which the text immediately corrects. On Reading B, it is a claim about one woman, and the claim is that she is *superior*. There is no reading on which this verse demotes women, and there is no reading on which it can be generalised into a rule about gender.

And the surah removes all doubt by what it does next. Mary is the **only woman named in the Qur'an**. A surah is named for her family and another for her. Within ten verses she will be told: "O Mary, God has chosen you and purified you and chosen you above the women of all peoples" (3:42). And the child she bears without a father is made a sign to all the worlds (3:49, 21:91).

I want to name the misuse outright, because it is constant. This verse is quoted in books, sermons and online to argue that women are lesser. That reading cannot survive contact with the passage it sits in. The mother's disappointment is recorded **in order to be answered** — by the interruption, by the acceptance in verse 37, and by the whole career of the daughter. A reader who takes this verse as a licence to diminish women is reading a correction as if it were a ruling.

**"I have named her Mary."** *Wa-innī sammaytuhā Maryama.* The classical sources discuss the meaning of the name, most commonly connecting it to a sense of devoted service or worship; the Hebrew/Aramaic *Miriam* is usually explained along those lines. The etymologies offered are **reported** rather than certain, and I give them as such. What is certain is that she names the child — an act of claiming and of recognition at the moment when, on one reading, she is most disappointed.

**"And I seek Your protection for her and her offspring from Satan, the accursed."** *Wa-innī u'īdhuhā bika wa-dhurriyyatahā mina al-shayṭāni al-rajīm.*

*U'īdhuhā* — I place her in Your refuge. *Al-rajīm* — the one driven away with stones, the outcast.

**And this petition has a famous answer in the Sunnah.** Abū Hurayrah reported that the Messenger of God ﷺ said: "No child is born but that Satan touches him when he is born, whereupon he cries out loudly from the touch of Satan — except Mary and her son." Abū Hurayrah then recited this very verse as the explanation (reported by al-Bukhārī and Muslim). A related narration adds that when Satan tried to touch Jesus, he struck the veil instead (al-Bukhārī).

Two things about that hadith are worth knowing. First, the commentators have disagreed on whether the "touch" is a physical touch — which some take literally as the cause of the newborn's cry — or a metaphorical intimation, a first whisper. Views cited for the metaphorical reading include al-Baḥr al-Muḥīṭ, al-Rāzī's tafsir, and Fayḍ al-Qadīr; the position generally held by the Ahl al-Sunnah is to affirm it as stated. Second, al-Qurṭubī cautioned that the hadith must not be read to mean the touched child is thereby misguided. It is a hadith about a specific protection granted to Mary and her son, and nothing more should be built on it.

**And one grammatical detail that should not be skipped.** She asks protection for her **and her offspring** — *wa-dhurriyyatihā*, **her** offspring. In ordinary Arabic usage *dhurriyyah* descends through the male line. Here the lineage is traced through the woman. In a surah named after a family, the family is traced through Mary.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 37

**"So her Lord accepted her graciously and blessed her with a pleasant upbringing—entrusting her to the care of Zachariah. Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions. He exclaimed, “O Mary! Where did this come from?” She replied, “It is from Allah. Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit.”"**

In simple terms: so her Lord accepted her with a goodly acceptance and made her grow a goodly growth, and entrusted her to Zakariyyā. Whenever Zakariyyā entered upon her in the sanctuary, he found provision with her. He said: O Mary, whence has this come to you? She said: it is from God. God provides for whom He wills without reckoning.

**"So her Lord accepted her."** *Fa-taqabbalahā rabbuhā.* The question of verse 35 — "*so accept it from me*" — is answered with the same verb. And note the pronoun: **her**. Not "it." The child her mother feared would not qualify is accepted, personally, by name.

**"Graciously."** *Bi-qabūlin ḥasanin* — with a beautiful acceptance. The indefinite noun intensified by the adjective: an acceptance beyond the ordinary. And there is a quiet grammar point here. The vow had been for a *muḥarrar* — a child freed for service. The service she was freed for turned out to be something no one had planned: to be the mother of a prophet, and a sign.

**"And blessed her with a pleasant upbringing."** *Wa-anbatahā nabātan ḥasanan* — and made her grow, a goodly growth. The verb is agricultural: *anbata*, to cause to sprout. She is described the way a plant is described that has been well-watered and well-sited. There is nothing mechanical about it; it is the language of tending. God did not simply accept her and leave. He raised her.

**"Entrusting her to the care of Zachariah."** And here is a reading note. The printed text reads *kaffalahā* — "**He** entrusted her [to Zakariyyā]," with God as the subject. It is also transmitted in the canonical readings as *kafalahā* — "**Zakariyyā** took charge of her." Both are established, and the practical sense converges: Zakariyyā became her guardian. The difference is only in whether the verse foregrounds God's arrangement or Zakariyyā's act.

Which Zakariyyā? He is generally identified with the prophet Zakariyyā, the father of John — and the surah immediately moves to his prayer for a child, which is the natural sequel and supports the identification. Reports describing the relationship more precisely (that he was her maternal uncle, or married to her mother's sister) are **transmitted** in the commentaries but are not from the Qur'an.

**"Whenever Zachariah visited her in the sanctuary, he found her supplied with provisions."** *Kullamā dakhala 'alayhā Zakariyyā al-miḥrāba wajada 'indahā rizqan.*

*Kullamā* — **every time.** Not once. This was a repeated pattern.

*Al-miḥrāb* — the sanctuary, the upper chamber, the place of prayer. The word's range covers a private devotional space as well as the prayer-niche of a mosque; in this context it is the place set apart where she was being raised.

*Wajada 'indahā rizqan* — he **found** provision with her. The verb is the verb of discovery. He did not bring it, arrange it, or understand it. He came in and it was there.

The classical sources variously report that this provision was out-of-season fruit — summer fruit in winter and winter fruit in summer — and the reports of this kind are **transmitted** in the tafsir. They are illustrative rather than established; the verse itself says only *rizq*, provision.

**"He exclaimed, 'O Mary! Where did this come from?'"** *Qāla yā Maryamu annā laki hādhā.*

Note the tenderness. He does not demand an account; he asks. And he addresses her by name — *yā Maryam* — which for a guardian asking a puzzling question of his ward is the gentlest possible opening.

**"She replied, 'It is from Allah.'"** *Qālat huwa min 'indi Allāh.*

And here is the precision of her answer. She does not say "God gives it to me" or "God sends it." She says: **it is from God** — *huwa min 'indi Allāh*. The provision is located in its source rather than in its means. Not a statement about the channel but about the origin. That is the habit of mind this whole passage is forming: to see the thing and name its source in one movement.

**"Surely Allah provides for whoever He wills without limit."** *Inna Allāha yarzuqu man yashā'u bi-ghayri ḥisāb.*

And this is the **third** time we have met this clause in this project, and the recurrence is worth pausing on:

- **2:212** — closing the verse about the life of this world being made appealing to those who disbelieve, and the mindful ranking above them on the Day of Judgement.
- **3:27** — closing the great prayer about sovereignty: You give dominion and take it, You bring the living from the dead, **and You provide for whom You will without reckoning**.
- **3:37** — here, spoken by Mary herself, in a chamber, about a plate of food.

The arc is the point. The God who disposes of Persia and Byzantium disposes of a girl's supper, and the same sentence covers both. And there is something further: the person who says it here is not a king or a prophet but a young woman in a room, and she says it as the obvious explanation for why there is food.

**And now the narrative logic, which is easy to miss.** Why does verse 38 open with Zakariyyā praying? Because of what he has just seen. Watching God provide for Mary — supernaturally, repeatedly, without any visible means — is what made him hope for the thing that was naturally impossible in his own life. **Witnessed grace provokes petition.** He saw provision come to a child who had no provider, and it opened his mouth for a child who had no cause to exist.

That is the mechanism of hope in the Qur'an's telling of it. People do not usually pray because they have worked out that God is able. They pray because they have watched Him do it for someone else.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 38

**"Then and there Zachariah prayed to his Lord, saying, “My Lord! Grant me—by your grace—righteous offspring. You are certainly the Hearer of ˹all˺ prayers.”"**

In simple terms: there, at that moment, Zakariyyā called upon his Lord, saying: My Lord, grant me from Yourself a goodly offspring. You are the Hearer of supplication.

**"Then and there."** *Hunālika* — at that place, at that moment. The adverb is doing real work. He did not go home and think it over. The sight of provision in the sanctuary produced the prayer in the sanctuary. There is no gap between the witness and the asking — and that immediacy is one of the things the Qur'an admires in the people it praises.

**"Grant me—by your grace."** *Hab lī min ladunka.*

*Hab* is the verb of pure gift — the same verb the firmly-rooted used at verse 8: *hab lanā min ladunka raḥmah*, "grant us from Yourself mercy." And **min ladunka** — "from Your very presence" — is the identical phrase.

So the prayer of the people of knowledge in verse 8 and the prayer of this prophet in verse 38 are built from the same two words. The surah is threading its supplications together: mercy from Your presence (8), offspring from Your presence (38). The point of *min ladunka* in both is that the gift is asked directly, unmediated, uncaused by anything in the recipient.

**"Righteous offspring."** *Dhurriyyatan ṭayyibatan.* *Ṭayyib* is good, wholesome, pure, pleasant — the word used for good land, good food, good words. He asks not merely for a child but for a child who is good.

Note the word *dhurriyyah* again — **descendants**, not just a child. He is asking for a line. And that detail matters for the next verse, as we shall see.

**"You are certainly the Hearer of all prayers."** *Innaka samī'u al-du'ā'.* And note the root. This is the third time in five verses that God has been named as **hearing**: verse 34 closed "All-Hearing, All-Knowing"; verse 35's supplicant echoed it; and now verse 38 names Him "the Hearer of supplication." The surah is building the attribute that the answer will demonstrate — and the answer arrives, in the next verse, while he is **still standing in prayer**.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 39

**"So the angels called out to him while he stood praying in the sanctuary, “Allah gives you good news of ˹the birth of˺ John who will confirm the Word of Allah and will be a great leader, chaste, and a prophet among the righteous.”"**

In simple terms: and the angels called to him as he stood praying in the sanctuary: God gives you good news of John, confirming a Word from God, and a master, and chaste, and a prophet from among the righteous.

**"While he stood praying."** *Wa-huwa qā'imun yuṣallī.* The announcement comes **inside** the prayer, not at its end and not outside it. The posture in which the news arrives is the posture of worship — and note *qā'imun*, standing: alert, upright, present.

And this is the demonstration of what verse 38 just claimed. He called God "the Hearer of supplication," and the reply comes before he has finished speaking. The surah does not say "and then, after some time, the angels came." It says the angels called him **while he was praying**.

**"Allah gives you good news."** *Anna Allāha yubashshiruka.* And here is a detail worth noticing from where we have just come. Twenty verses earlier, at verse 21, the same verb *bashshara* was used witheringly: "give **them** good news of a painful punishment." The surah mocked a false assurance with the vocabulary of good news. Here the vocabulary is restored to its proper use. This is real good news, and the surah is careful to let the reader feel the difference.

**"Who will confirm the Word of Allah."** *Muṣaddiqan bi-kalimatin mina Allāh.* And this is the theologically loaded phrase of the verse.

The overwhelming consensus of the commentators is that "**a Word from God**" here means **Jesus**. He is called God's word because he was brought into being by the creative command *kun* — "Be" — as the surah will state at 3:47 and 3:59, and as 4:171 will put it directly: "His word which He conveyed to Mary, and a spirit from Him."

So John's role is defined in relation to Jesus: he confirms him. And note the delicacy of what the Qur'an is doing. It gives Jesus the highest possible title — *kalimah min Allāh*, a word from God — and it gives him that title in a verse about **John**. The title is real, and the surah will spend the next twenty verses ensuring it is not over-read.

This is exactly the ground on which the argument with the Najrān Christians is being prepared. They call Jesus the Word. The Qur'an calls him a Word from God. The difference between those two formulations — the definite article, the preposition, and what each implies about his nature — is the entire dispute, and it will be joined explicitly at verse 59 and verse 61.

**"And will be a great leader."** *Wa-sayyidan.* The commentators offer a range, and the range is instructive:
- Mujāhid: "honoured by God."
- Ibn Zayd: "the noble man."
- 'Ikrimah: "one who is not overcome by anger."

That third gloss is the one to sit with. 'Ikrimah defines mastery as **self-mastery**. A *sayyid* is not the one who commands the most people but the one whom anger cannot command. The received Arabic usage of *sayyid* covers chief, master, lord; the commentators unanimously pull it inward, toward character rather than rank.

**"Chaste."** *Wa-ḥaṣūran.* And here is the verse's main crux, and it is a genuinely disputed one.

Three readings are transmitted:

**(a) Celibate** — one who abstains from women. *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* glosses it "has no desire for women," and al-Hilālī renders "keeping away from sexual relations with women."

**(b) Chaste but not celibate** — immune from *unlawful* sexual relations. This is Ibn Kathīr's position, stated explicitly: "it does not mean he refrains from sexual relations with women, but that he is immune from illegal sexual relations." Ma'arif al-Qur'an argues the same and adduces a textual proof: Zakariyyā **prayed for offspring** (*dhurriyyah*, verse 38) — a son who would himself have descendants. A childless John would not have answered that prayer.

**(c) Master of his desires generally** — not incapable, but in complete command of his appetites. This broader reading is attributed to al-Qurṭubī and is the one developed in the Sufi commentaries: "liberated from passions and spared from carnal human properties, despite being human."

**The balance that has to be drawn.** Whatever one concludes about John, **celibacy is not prescribed in Islam**, and this must be stated without hedging because the alternative reading has caused real harm.

The definitive text is the ṣaḥīḥ narration: three men came to the houses of the Prophet's ﷺ wives asking about his worship. When they were told, they considered it little and said, "Where are we compared to the Prophet? His past and future sins have been forgiven." One said, "As for me, I will pray all night forever." Another said, "I will fast continuously and never break my fast." Another said, "I will keep away from women and never marry." The Prophet ﷺ came to them and said: "By God, I am the most mindful of God among you and the most God-fearing of Him — yet I fast and I break my fast, and I pray and I sleep, and I marry women. **Whoever turns away from my Sunnah is not of me**" (reported by al-Bukhārī and Muslim).

And the Qur'an is explicit at 57:27 that monasticism was something Christians "invented," not something God prescribed.

So: if *ḥaṣūr* is read as celibacy, it is a description of one prophet's particular calling — and it sits awkwardly, as Ma'arif notes, with the prayer that produced him. It is not a rule for anyone else. The weight of the classical tafsir I have consulted falls on reading (b), and I would note that reading (b) also has the advantage of leaving the word's moral force intact: John is being praised for something he **achieved** rather than a condition he was born with.

**"And a prophet among the righteous."** *Wa-nabiyyan mina al-ṣāliḥīn.* Note the preposition: **from among** the righteous. He is not set above a company; he belongs to one. Even at the moment of being given good news about him, he is placed among others.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 40

**"Zachariah exclaimed, “My Lord! How can I have a son when I am very old and my wife is barren?” He replied, “So will it be. Allah does what He wills.”"**

In simple terms: he said: My Lord, how shall I have a boy, when old age has overtaken me and my wife is barren? He said: thus it is; God does what He wills.

**"How can I have a son?"** *Annā yakūnu lī ghulāmun.* And the question is *annā* — **how, whence** — not *hal* (whether). He is not asking whether it can happen. He is asking by what means.

That distinction matters, because the Qur'an records several such questions and it is important not to confuse them with doubt. The commentators generally read Zakariyyā's question as a request to be shown the mechanism, not as scepticism about the power — and the plain evidence for that reading is internal: **he had just asked for it.** A man who spends verse 38 praying for offspring is not, in verse 40, expressing disbelief that God could manage it.

Note also that he names **two** impossibilities: "old age has overtaken me" and "my wife is barren." Neither is remediable, and together they close every natural route. That is the setting the Qur'an likes for this kind of announcement — the door shut from both sides.

**And note the parallel questions in the surah.** Mary will ask the same thing at verse 47: "My Lord, how can I have a son when no man has touched me?" And Abraham, back in al-Baqarah, asked "My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead" (2:260) — which was **not** a question from doubt but a request for the heart to be reassured, as he said himself.

The pattern is worth holding: in each case the question is *how*, and in each case the answer does not supply a mechanism.

**"He replied: So will it be. Allah does what He wills."** *Qāla kadhālika Allāhu yaf'alu mā yashā'.*

And this is the answer, and it is worth being clear about what kind of answer it is. It is **not** an explanation. It does not say how. It says: it will be so, because God does what He wills.

The Qur'an's consistent response to "how" questions about creation is to relocate the question from mechanism to agent. Not *here is the process*, but *here is the One*. That is not an evasion; it is the claim. And it is the same answer that will be given to Mary at verse 47: "Thus it is; God creates what He wills. When He decrees a thing, He only says to it 'Be,' and it is."

(Sign: the surah will continue, at verse 41, by giving Zakariyyā a sign — that he will not speak to people for three days except by gesture. So the "how" is not answered, but a token is given. That belongs to the next batch.)

**One closing observation on the whole scene.** Zakariyyā's question and its answer take four lines. His wife's barrenness, his own age, the impossibility, the reply — and then, in the next verse, the sign. There is no rebuke in the answer. God does not say "have you no faith?" He says, in effect: this is what I do.

That is the tone the Qur'an takes with its own prophets when they ask the understandable question. They are not scolded for wanting to know how. They are told who is doing it.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses open the surah's central narrative, and they do it by first closing a question that has been running since verse 19.

**Verses 31–32 settle the test.** The claim to love God is tested by a single thing — following the Messenger — and the reward named is not safety or status but **being loved by God**, with forgiveness following from the love rather than preceding it. Turn away, and what is withheld is the same thing: love. The whole passage runs on one currency.

**Verses 33–34 establish the lineage.** God chose Adam, Noah, the house of Abraham and the house of 'Imrān, and they are one chain — election running through history rather than scattered across it. But the chain does not confer immunity: within every chosen line, individuals have refused and perished. Election is real; inheritance is not automatic. And the verse closes on "All-Hearing, All-Knowing," which the very next supplicant will quote back to God.

**Verses 35–37 tell the vow and its answer.** A woman vows the unexamined contents of her womb to God's service; delivers a daughter; says the thing that has been said about daughters ever since — and is answered, first by the narrator's interruption, then by God's acceptance, then by a childhood in which provision simply appears. Mary's own explanation of that provision is the third occurrence of *bi-ghayri ḥisāb* in this reading: the God of Persia and Byzantium is the God of a girl's supper.

**Verses 38–40 give the consequence.** Watching grace done for someone else is what opens Zakariyyā's mouth. He prays, in the same words the firmly-rooted used for mercy; the angels answer him inside the prayer itself; and his question about means is answered not with a mechanism but with an agent.

**Four threads are worth carrying forward.**

The first is **"from Your presence."** *Min ladunka* appears at verse 8 and again at verse 38 — mercy from Your presence, offspring from Your presence. The surah's supplications are woven from the same thread.

The second is **"without reckoning."** *Bi-ghayri ḥisāb* at 2:212, 3:27, and 3:37. The clause is the same whether the subject is kingdoms or supper, and the person who says it here is a young woman explaining where fruit came from.

The third is **the "how" questions.** Abraham at 2:260, Zakariyyā here, Mary at 3:47 — three *annā* questions, and all three answered by relocating the question from mechanism to agent.

The fourth is **the naming of women in this passage.** The mother is not named; the daughter is, and is the only woman named in the whole Qur'an. Her mother's disappointment is recorded in order to be answered. And the descendants are traced through *her* — *wa-dhurriyyatihā*. In a surah named for a family, the family runs through the woman, and the next verses will show why.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes, or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where a report is transmitted with a variable chain — the occasions for 3:31, the name Ḥannah, the out-of-season fruit, the relationship between Zakariyyā and Mary, the etymology of "Maryam" — I have flagged it as reported rather than established. Where the disagreement is real — who speaks the words "the male is not like the female," what *ḥaṣūr* means, whether the touch at birth is literal or metaphorical, how God's love is to be understood — I have given the arguments on each side. On 3:36 I have stated explicitly that no available reading supports the verse's common use against women; on *ḥaṣūr* I have indicated that the weight of the consulted tafsir falls on the non-celibate reading while noting the contrary; and on 3:33 I have given both the traditional Muslim answer on the two 'Imrāns and the modern scholarly framing in terms of typology, without collapsing either into the other.
