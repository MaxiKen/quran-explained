# Surah Ali 'Imran (The Family of 'Imran, 3) — Verse-by-Verse Tafsir, Verses 41–50

These ten verses carry the whole weight of the surah's argument with Christianity, and they do it without a single polemical sentence.

Notice what happens. The angel announces a son to Mary (45). She asks how (47). The answer is the creative command *kun fa-yakūn* — "Be!" and it is. Jesus is then described as honoured in both worlds, as developing from infancy to maturity, as taught by God, as working six miracles by permission, and as confirming the Torah while lifting some of its prohibitions (45–50).

Every one of those claims is something the Christians of Najrān could have affirmed. That is precisely the strategy. The surah is not going to deny Jesus anything; it is going to grant him everything the Qur'an grants him and then let the accumulated weight of those grants carry a conclusion the Najrān delegation would refuse. The confrontation does not arrive until verse 59 and verse 61. Everything here is groundwork, laid in the shared vocabulary of people who already revered this man and this woman.

Two threads from the previous batch continue right through. The first is the "how" question: we saw it at 2:260 with Abraham and at 3:40 with Zakariyyā, and Mary asks it here at 3:47 — and the answer she gets is stronger than either. The second is the naming: he is *'Īsā ibn Maryam*, Jesus **son of Mary**, every single time the Qur'an names him. That is not a stylistic habit. It is the doctrine, carried in a grammatical form.

## Verse 41

**"Zachariah said, “My Lord! Grant me a sign.” He said, “Your sign is that you will not ˹be able to˺ speak to people for three days except through gestures. Remember your Lord often and glorify ˹Him˺ morning and evening.”"**

In simple terms: he said: My Lord, appoint for me a sign. He said: your sign is that you shall not speak to people for three days except by gesture. And remember your Lord much, and glorify Him in the evening and the morning.

**He asks for a sign after being told.** That is worth noticing before anything else. Zakariyyā has just been given the announcement. He believes it. What he asks for is not proof that God *can* do it but a marker — something to fix the moment, to tell him when the thing promised has begun. The commentators generally read it this way, and the internal evidence supports them: a man who has spent verse 38 praying for offspring does not, in verse 41, doubt the power.

**"Your sign is that you will not speak to people for three days."** *Āyatuka allā tukallima al-nāsa thalāthata ayyāmin illā ramzan.*

And here is the first thing to see. **The sign is an inability.** Normally a sign is a display — a staff becoming a serpent, a sea parting, fire falling. Here the confirmation is that he will be struck mute. It is the most counter-intuitive sign in the Qur'an, and it is worth asking why.

The answer is in the second half of the verse, and it is the heart of this verse for anyone reading devotionally.

**"Remember your Lord often and glorify Him morning and evening."** *Wa-udhkur rabbika kathīran wa-sabbiḥ bi-al-'ashiyyi wa-al-ibkār.*

Look at what has happened. God has not silenced Zakariyyā. God has **redirected him**. The man who has been asking, petitioning and speaking is now told: you will not speak to people — for three days — and in that silence you will remember Me much. The affliction is a retreat. The inability to speak to creatures becomes the capacity to speak to the Creator.

That is why the sign takes the form it does. A man about to receive a miraculous child is given three days in which he cannot talk about anything else, and must talk to God instead. The sign is not a punishment for asking; it is the preparation for receiving.

**"Except through gestures."** *Illā ramzan.* *Ramz* is a sign, a gesture, a nod, a hint — communication that is not articulate speech. So he is not cut off from people entirely; he can indicate. What is removed is speech, and speech is exactly the faculty he had been using to ask.

**The parallel account, and the reconciliation.** Sūrat Maryam gives the same event with slightly different wording: "your sign is that you will not speak to people for **three nights**, [being] sound" (19:10).

Two differences: "three days" here, "three nights" there; and the addition of *sawiyyā* — sound, healthy, unimpaired.

The reconciliation the commentators give for the first is straightforward: three days together with their nights is the same span, described once by its days and once by its nights. This is a standard feature of Qur'anic style — the same event narrated in two surahs with complementary details, neither of which contradicts the other.

The second difference is the more interesting one. *Sawiyyā* means he was **not ill**. He was not struck dumb by disease or injury; his tongue was bound while the rest of him was entirely sound. That matters, because it distinguishes this from an affliction. His silence is a held state, willed and timed, and it lifts on schedule. A person struck dumb by illness in the ancient world was pitiable; Zakariyyā, walking about in full health but unable to form a word, was a walking sign.

**"Morning and evening."** *Bi-al-'ashiyyi wa-al-ibkār.* *'Ashiyy* is the late afternoon into evening; *ibkār* is the early morning, the first part of the day. The two ends of the day. So the remembrance is not a single occasion but a bookending — the day opens and closes with *tasbīḥ*.

There is a quiet practical instruction here for anyone who has ever found their prayer life squeezed out by a busy season. Zakariyyā is told to remember God **much** (*kathīran*) and to glorify Him at **two fixed points** of the day. The much-ness is the spirit; the two ends are the scaffolding. Start there when you cannot manage more.

## Verse 42

**"And ˹remember˺ when the angels said, “O Mary! Surely Allah has selected you, purified you, and chosen you over all women of the world."**

In simple terms: and when the angels said: O Mary, God has chosen you and purified you and chosen you above the women of all peoples.

**Three verbs.** *Iṣṭafāki* — He selected you. *Wa-ṭahharaki* — and purified you. *Wa-iṣṭafāki 'alā nisā'i al-'ālamīn* — and selected you above the women of the worlds.

Note the repetition. The first *iṣṭafā* is absolute: chosen. The second is comparative: chosen **over**. The structure takes her from election to supremacy in two steps.

**"Purified you."** *Ṭahharaki*, from *ṭ-h-r*. Purified **from what**? The commentators give two answers, and both are live in this passage. The first is that she was purified from sin — moral and spiritual purity. The second, which the narrative strongly supports, is that she was purified **from the slander of her people** — that is, God vindicated her. Given that the next thing that happens to her is a pregnancy without a husband and the accusation that follows it, and given that her son's first act will be to speak from the cradle in her defence (19:30–33), the vindication reading has the narrative on its side. Both are held; I would not choose between them.

**Now the crux, and it is a substantial one: was Mary a prophet?**

This is a genuine and well-documented dispute in Islamic theology, and I am going to lay out both sides properly, because a great many people hold a position on it without knowing that the other exists.

**The majority position: she was not a prophet; she was a *ṣiddīqah*.** The overwhelming majority of Sunni scholars hold that no woman was ever a prophet, and that Mary's rank is expressed by a different word — *ṣiddīqah*, the supremely truthful one. The proof-text is 16:43: "We have not sent before you any other than **men**, to whom We revealed." Al-Nawawī states that Imām al-Ḥaramayn recorded the scholars' agreement in *al-Irshād* that Mary is not a prophet, and Ibn Ḥajar states in *Fatḥ al-Bārī* that this is the position of the majority.

**The minority position: she attained prophethood without messengership.** This is held by Ibn Ḥazm al-Ẓāhirī, by al-Qurṭubī, and — on one report — by Abū al-Ḥasan al-Ash'arī.

Ibn Ḥazm's argument is definitional. He defines prophethood as "the acquisition of certain, undoubted knowledge that cannot be acquired through personal endeavour or human experience" — that is, knowledge by revelation. On that definition, anyone God addresses with revelation is a prophet, and he names four women who received it: Mary, Sarah, the mother of Moses, and Āsiyah the wife of Pharaoh. His evidence for Mary is 19:17: "Then We sent to her Our angel."

Al-Ash'arī's criterion is stated even more plainly: "anyone who received a commandment, a prohibition or any information from God via an angel is a prophet."

**And the counter to 16:43?** Ibn Ḥazm's answer is a distinction the scholars draw throughout: the verse speaks of **messengers** — those *sent* with a message to a people — not of prophets as such. Every messenger is a prophet; not every prophet is a messenger. Ibn Ḥazm agrees without qualification that **no woman was ever a messenger**. He is claiming only *nubuwwah*, not *risālah*.

The opposing response to the minority is simply that not everyone whom the angels addressed was a prophet.

There is also a narration that al-Qurṭubī cites — "among women are four prophets: Eve, Āsiyah, the mother of Moses and Mary" — but its authenticity is disputed and it is not established, and I flag it as such rather than resting anything on it.

**What everyone agrees on**, and this is the part worth carrying: no woman was ever sent as a messenger with a law to a people; and Mary's rank is supreme either way. The disagreement is about the correct **technical term** for her station, and it turns almost entirely on how one defines *nubuwwah* — as the reception of revelation, or as the reception of a revealed law to be delivered. It is not a matter of creed, and a Muslim may hold either position.

**"Over all women of the world."** *'Alā nisā'i al-'ālamīn.* And there is a secondary question here: does this mean all women of **all time**, or the women of **her own era**?

Ibn Kathīr's heading over this verse reads "The Virtue of Maryam over the Women of **Her Time**," which takes the second reading. Others take it absolutely. The discussion matters because of the hadith material.

**The hadiths, and how to hold them.** In the narration recorded by al-Tirmidhī — and by the Six with the exception of Abū Dāwūd — 'Alī ibn Abī Ṭālib said that he heard the Messenger of God ﷺ say: "The best woman [in her time] was Maryam, the daughter of 'Imrān, and the best woman [of the Prophet's time] is Khadījah, the daughter of Khuwaylid." And in the same collection: "Many men achieved perfection, but among women none achieved it except Āsiyah the wife of Pharaoh and Maryam the daughter of 'Imrān."

There is also the well-known narration that the superiority of 'Ā'ishah to other women is like the superiority of *tharīd* to other foods.

These narrations use **different comparisons** — "best in her time," "achieved perfection," "superiority over other women" — and the scholars have discussed at length how to reconcile them. I am not going to adjudicate that, and I want to name why: this particular set of hadiths is routinely turned into a contest, with Muslims ranking Mary against Khadījah against 'Ā'ishah and using the result as a weapon. That is a misuse of all three. The texts belong to three different registers of comparison, and the tradition's own instinct was to hold them together rather than to pick a winner.

## Verse 43

**"O Mary! Be devout to your Lord, prostrate yourself ˹in prayer˺ and bow along with those who bow down.”"**

In simple terms: O Mary, be devoutly obedient to your Lord, and prostrate, and bow with those who bow.

**"Be devout."** *Uqnutī* — from *q-n-t*, the root we met at 3:17, where the muttaqūn were described as *al-qānitīn*. The word covers sustained, humble, willing obedience. Mary is being told to do the very thing that was listed as a mark of the people of Paradise.

**"And bow along with those who bow down."** *Wa-irka'ī ma'a al-rāki'īn.* And this is the clause I want to underline, because it settles a question that the popular imagination gets wrong.

Mary is commanded to pray **with the congregation**. Not in a cell. Not apart. *Ma'a al-rāki'īn* — together with the people who bow.

This matters because Mary is very easily turned into a figure of solitary, monastic devotion — the woman in the niche, the recluse, the virgin in retreat. And we have just come, at 3:39, from a passage establishing that monasticism is not prescribed in this religion. So this verse arrives at exactly the right moment to say: her devotion is not withdrawal. She is told to stand in the row with everyone else.

The Qur'an's saints are, almost without exception, people who pray with other people. Even Mary.

**A small grammatical observation.** The verse lists prostration (*sujūd*) **before** bowing (*rukū'*), which is the reverse of the order in the prayer, where bowing precedes prostration. Grammarians note that the conjunction does not necessarily imply sequence in a list of this kind, and the commentators generally treat the two as enumerated rather than ordered. I mention it because a careful reader will notice it, and because it is a small demonstration that the Qur'an's lists are not transcripts of ritual sequence.

**And note the addressee.** A command to worship is issued, by name, to a woman: *yā Maryamu uqnutī*. In a scripture where named women are few, one of them is directly commanded by God, in the imperative, to worship. There is no intermediary in this sentence.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 44

**"This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you ˹O Prophet˺. You were not with them when they cast lots to decide who would be Mary’s guardian, nor were you there when they argued ˹about it˺."**

In simple terms: that is from the reports of the unseen which We reveal to you. You were not with them when they cast their pens [to decide] which of them should take charge of Mary, nor were you with them when they were disputing.

**"This is news of the unseen that We reveal to you."** *Tilka min anbā'i al-ghaybi nūḥīhā ilayka.* And this is one of the Qur'an's most direct statements of its own epistemology. The Prophet ﷺ did not learn this from a person, a book, or a journey. It came by *waḥy*.

**"When they cast lots."** *Idh yulqūna aqlāmahum.* *Aqlām* is the plural of *qalam*, a pen — and specifically the reed pen or reed shaft. The practice being described is lot-casting: the candidates threw their pens, and the one whose pen was drawn (or, in some accounts, the one whose pen floated or stood out) was given charge of Mary. The transmitted accounts say Zakariyyā's lot was drawn.

**A necessary distinction here, because this looks close to something forbidden.** The pre-Islamic Arabs used *azlām* — divining arrows — to seek knowledge of the unseen, and the Qur'an forbids that explicitly at 5:90 ("intoxicants, gambling, stone altars and **divining arrows** are an abomination of Satan's handiwork").

What is happening in this verse is a different thing: **qur'ah**, the casting of lots to allocate something indivisible or to break a tie, where no knowledge of the unseen is being sought. That is lawful, and the Prophet ﷺ is reported to have used it himself — including drawing lots between his wives when travelling with them. The difference is the object: the *azlām* sought to discover a hidden outcome; the *qur'ah* merely distributes a known good among claimants without favouritism. One asks the unseen to speak; the other lets God decide a matter of allocation.

I flag the distinction because these verses are sometimes cited as though the Qur'an endorsed divination. It did not, and this is not an instance of it.

**The apologetic point, and it is the verse's purpose.** *Wa-mā kunta ladayhim* — "**you were not with them**." Stated twice: once for the casting of the lots, once for the disputing that surrounded it.

The argument is the one the Qur'an makes repeatedly and openly: this man was not present, he was unlettered, he had no access to the archives of a community whose disputes had taken place centuries earlier — and yet he narrates a contested scene from inside it, including the fact that they *disputed*. That last detail is the sharpest. A legendary account would smooth the conflict out. This one reports that they argued.

The same argument is made elsewhere: "That is from the news of the unseen which We reveal to you. You did not know it, neither you nor your people, before this" (11:49); "you were not with them when they plotted" (12:102); and at length in 28:44–46, "you were not on the western side when We decreed the matter to Moses… nor were you a dweller among the people of Madyan."

**And note where this verse sits.** It interrupts the narrative of Mary at the exact moment the story reaches the question of who raised her, in order to turn to the Prophet ﷺ and say: **you were not there.** The surah is arguing for the revelation while it is narrating the revelation's content. That double function — story and proof at once — is characteristic of this passage, and this verse is the clearest instance of it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 45

**"˹Remember˺ when the angels proclaimed, “O Mary! Allah gives you good news of a Word from Him, his name will be the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary; honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and he will be one of those nearest ˹to Allah˺."**

In simple terms: when the angels said: O Mary, God gives you good news of a Word from Him, whose name is the Messiah, Jesus, son of Mary — honoured in this world and the Hereafter, and among those brought near.

**"A Word from Him."** *Bi-kalimatin minhu.* And here the thread from 3:39 is tied. There, John was described as "confirming a Word from God." Now Mary is told that the Word is hers. The two announcements are two halves of one sentence, separated by six verses.

**The name, and the title.** *Ismuhu al-Masīḥu 'Īsā ibnu Maryama.*

And this is a good place to be honest about a word that gets a great deal of confident handling.

**What does *al-Masīḥ* mean?** The overwhelming majority of scholars across Islamic history — including al-Bayḍāwī and al-Zamakhsharī, who say so openly — hold that it is an **arabised borrowing**: the Hebrew *Māshīaḥ* ("anointed one"), most likely arriving through the Syriac *m'shiha*. On this reading, *al-Masīḥ* is simply "the Messiah," the same title Christians used, naturalised into Arabic.

Later exegetes attempted native Arabic derivations, and the results are instructive:

- From the root *m-s-ḥ*, "to wipe, stroke, anoint": that he was anointed with blessing and prophethood; that he healed the sick by wiping them with his hand; that he was wiped clean of sin; or that he was touched by Gabriel's wing at birth to protect him from Satan.
- From the root *s-y-ḥ*, "to travel": that he was called this because of his itinerant life.

The lexicographer al-Fīrūzābādī collected **forty-nine** different meanings that had been proposed by exegetes for this title.

Forty-nine. That number is itself the finding. When a single word generates forty-nine competing native derivations, the sensible conclusion is not that forty-eight scholars were wrong and one was right — it is that the word was borrowed, and that the instinct to derive it from Arabic roots was a mistake from the start. I give the derivations because you will meet them, and I give the number because it should temper anyone's confidence in any of them.

It is also worth noting that in the Qur'an itself the "anointed" sense plays very little role. The title occurs eleven times and functions almost as part of his name. Some modern scholars have argued on that basis that it is better rendered in English as "Christ" — a title that has become a semi-surname — than as "the Messiah," which carries the whole Davidic freight that the Qur'anic usage does not invoke. That is a defensible translational judgment, and I note it as one.

**"Jesus, son of Mary."** *'Īsā ibnu Maryam.* And this is the detail to hold.

He is named **through his mother**. Not "son of Joseph," not "son of God," not with a lineage at all — his *nasab*, his patronymic, is Mary. And the Qur'an does this without variation. Every time it names him, it names him this way.

That is the doctrine carried in a grammatical form. In a culture where a person's identity *was* his father's name, giving a man his mother's name is not a neutral stylistic choice. It is the claim the whole passage is built to establish, and it is made in the sentence that introduces him.

**"Honoured in this world and the Hereafter."** *Wajīhan fī al-dunyā wa-al-ākhirati.* *Wajīh* is from the root for **face** — it describes someone who has a "face" in the sense of standing, countenance, weight. He is given dignity in both lives. Note that the Qur'an gives him this without qualification: honour in this world is not disdained. Elsewhere the Qur'an is severe about the pursuit of worldly standing; here it is granted as a gift.

**"And he will be one of those nearest."** *Wa-mina al-muqarrabīn.* *Al-muqarrabīn* — those brought near — is a rank above the general company of the righteous, and it is the same word used of the highest angels (4:172: "the angels brought near"). The Qur'an places him very high indeed. Whatever argument the surah is about to make against his divinity, it is not an argument against his station.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 46

**"And he will speak to people in ˹his˺ infancy and adulthood and will be one of the righteous.”"**

In simple terms: and he will speak to people in the cradle and in maturity, and he will be of the righteous.

**The lexical point first, because a mistake here has caused real confusion.** The supplied translation's "adulthood" is correct. The Arabic word is *kahlan*, and *kahl* denotes the age of full maturity and strength — the lexicons put it roughly between thirty and fifty. **It does not mean old age.** Some English translations render it "old age", and that rendering is wrong on the lexicon, and has been used to manufacture a difficulty that does not exist.

**So why mention it at all?** This is the interesting question, and it is the one the commentators actually argue about. Speaking in the cradle is a miracle. Speaking as a grown man is the most ordinary thing in the world. Why list the ordinary alongside the miraculous?

The classical answers, and they are not mutually exclusive:

**First, and decisively in this context: to establish that he changed.** He was an infant and then he was a man. He grew. He developed. And a god does not grow. This verse is addressed, in the first instance, to the delegation from Najrān, who claimed divinity for him: the miracle of infant speech paired with the plain fact of his maturation is an argument that he is a creature, having passed through the stages of a creature's life. The miracle establishes the prophet; the maturation establishes the humanity. The verse needs both halves to do its work.

**Second:** two different speeches for two different purposes. He spoke in the cradle to vindicate his mother against the slander that was about to break over her (as we see at 19:30–33), and he spoke in maturity with revelation and prophethood. The two moments correspond to the two tasks of his life.

**Third — the reading attributed to Abū Muslim, and developed at length by Ashraf 'Alī Thānawī:** the point is that **his infant speech was like the speech of a man in his prime**. On this reading the second word qualifies the first rather than adding to it. A baby who babbles is not a sign; a baby who speaks with the rationality, the vocabulary and the eloquence of a grown man is. The reference to maturity is there to tell you what kind of infant speech this was.

**Fourth — the reading attributed to al-Aṣam:** that he would live to reach the age of maturity.

**Fifth — the reading attributed to al-Ḥusayn ibn al-Faḍl:** that *wa-kahlan* refers to his being a mature man **after his descent at the end of time**, when he will speak to the people and deal with the Dajjāl. On this reading the verse is one of the proofs adduced for his return. It is transmitted, and I record it as a transmitted reading; it is not the majority's primary sense, and nothing in the passage requires it.

**A note on how this verse gets used.** Because "kahlan" implies he reached maturity, some have pressed it into arguments about his death — reasoning that he cannot have died young if he was to speak "in maturity." This is contested ground, and the verses that address the matter directly are 4:157–158, which belong to al-Nisā' and will be handled in their place. I note here only that this verse's plain function in context is to establish his human development, and that conclusions drawn from it beyond that should be held loosely.

The same pairing — *al-mahdi wa-kahlan* — recurs at 5:110, in God's address to Jesus on the Day of Judgement.

**"And he will be one of the righteous."** *Wa-mina al-ṣāliḥīn.* The identical closing that was given to John at 3:39. Both are placed **among** the righteous — members of a company, not solitary figures.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 47

**"Mary wondered, “My Lord! How can I have a child when no man has ever touched me?” An angel replied, “So will it be. Allah creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it, ‘Be!’ And it is!"**

In simple terms: she said: My Lord, how shall I have a child when no human being has touched me? He said: thus it is; God creates what He wills. When He decrees a matter, He only says to it "Be!" and it is.

**The third "how."** We have now met all three. Abraham at 2:260: "My Lord, show me how You give life to the dead." Zakariyyā at 3:40: "How shall I have a son when old age has overtaken me and my wife is barren?" And Mary here. In every case the question is *annā* — **how**, by what route — and in every case the answer relocates the question from mechanism to agent.

**"When no man has ever touched me."** The Arabic is *lam yamsasnī basharun* — no **human being** has touched me. *Bashar* is a mortal, a human, a member of the species. The phrasing is more comprehensive than "no man has married me," and that comprehensiveness is the point: she is not describing her marital status. She is saying that no member of the human species has had any contact with her of the relevant kind.

**And note the answer's escalation.** Compare it with what Zakariyyā was told at 3:40: *Allāhu yaf'alu mā yashā'* — "God **does** what He wills." Here Mary is told: *Allāhu yakhluqu mā yashā'* — "God **creates** what He wills."

The verb is raised to meet the case. Zakariyyā's difficulty was a natural impossibility — age and barrenness — and the answer named an *act*. Mary's difficulty is the absence of any natural cause at all, and the answer names a **creation**. That is a stronger claim, and the text makes it deliberately.

**"When He decrees a matter, He simply tells it: 'Be!' And it is."** *Idhā qaḍā amran fa-innamā yaqūlu lahu kun fa-yakūn.*

This is the Qur'an's standing formula for creation without means. It appears at 2:117, 6:73, 16:40, 19:35, 36:82 and 40:68, and it is used for Jesus specifically here and at 3:59 and 19:35.

And this is the reason he is called **"a Word from God."** Not metaphorically. He came into being by a *word* — by the command *kun*. The title at 3:39 and 3:45 is not honorific decoration; it is a description of the mechanism. Every prophet was sent with words. This one was brought into existence by one.

**One tension worth naming, because it is real.** The verse says that when God decrees a thing, He says "Be" and it **is** — instantaneous. But Mary's pregnancy will run its nine months, and the narrative in Sūrat Maryam describes labour pains and a delivery (19:23–26).

How are these held together? The answer the tradition gives is the distinction between the decree and its execution in time: the creative command is not a process and cannot be, but what is decreed may unfold gradually according to the wisdom of the One who decreed it. The Qur'an states both — the instantaneous *kun* and the gradual gestation — and a reader who holds only one has only half the account. God's act is not slowed by the time the thing takes to appear.

**And the missional point.** This verse is the direct answer to the claim that will be confronted at Najrān. The Christians argued from Jesus' fatherless conception to his divinity. The Qur'an grants the fatherless conception entirely — it insists on it more firmly than they do — and derives from it a different conclusion: not that he is God, but that the One who created him without a father is the One who created Adam without a father or a mother, and Eve without a mother, and every human being from a drop. That argument is stated explicitly at 3:59, twelve verses from here.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 48

**"“And Allah will teach him writing and wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel,"**

In simple terms: and He will teach him the Book and wisdom, and the Torah and the Gospel.

**A reading note.** The verb is transmitted in two ways: *nu'allimuhu* — "**We** will teach him" — and *yu'allimuhu* — "**He** will teach him." Both are canonical readings, and the sense is identical: the teaching is from God. Al-Jalālayn records both.

**"Writing."** The word here is *al-kitāb*, and the commentators disagree on what it covers. Ibn Kathīr says it appears to mean **writing** — literacy, the art of the pen — rather than "scripture." *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* gives both: "the scriptures of the prophets who came before; as it is said: writing."

The disagreement is not trivial. If it means writing, the verse is telling us that God taught him literacy — a striking claim in a culture where literacy was a specialised craft, and one that sits interestingly against the Prophet Muhammad's ﷺ own *ummī* status. If it means scripture, it is the first item in a curriculum of revelation. Both readings are transmitted, and they may both be intended, since *al-kitāb* carries both senses in the Qur'an.

**"Wisdom."** *Al-ḥikmah*. Also variously glossed: *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* says "the lawful and the prohibited"; others say the Sunnah, or the wisdom of the prophets who preceded him. In Qur'anic usage *ḥikmah* is a wide word covering sound judgment, right understanding, and the ability to put knowledge into its proper place.

**Now the crux: the Gospel.** How could Jesus be taught **the Gospel**, when the Gospel is what was revealed *to* him?

This is a genuine difficulty and the commentators address it directly. The answer recorded in *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* is a chronological one: God taught him **the Torah in his mother's womb, and the Gospel after he came out of it**. On that reading, the Torah was placed in him before birth, and the Gospel was given to him after — which is to say, the Gospel here is the revelation he would subsequently receive and be taught in advance.

A related approach is that he was taught the *content* of the Gospel prior to its formal revelation — that God prepared him for the scripture he was to be given by instructing him in it beforehand.

Both are attempts to hold the obvious sense of the verse, and I present them as transmitted resolutions rather than as demonstrations. The honest statement is: the verse says he will be taught the Torah and the Gospel, and the tradition has offered these accounts of how that is to be understood.

**What the verse as a whole is doing.** It gives a four-item curriculum — the Book, wisdom, the Torah, the Gospel — and it attributes all four to God as teacher. Jesus is presented here as **taught**, not as self-originating. The one whom the Christians of Najrān revered as divine is described, in the sentence immediately following the announcement of his miraculous conception, as someone who had to be instructed.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 49

**"and ˹make him˺ a messenger to the Children of Israel ˹to proclaim,˺ ‘I have come to you with a sign from your Lord: I will make for you a bird from clay, breathe into it, and it will become a ˹real˺ bird—by Allah’s Will. I will heal the blind and the leper and raise the dead to life— by Allah’s Will. And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses. Surely in this is a sign for you if you ˹truly˺ believe."**

In simple terms: and [He will make him] a messenger to the Children of Israel, [saying:] I have come to you with a sign from your Lord — I fashion for you out of clay something in the form of a bird, and I breathe into it, and it becomes a bird by God's leave. And I heal the one born blind and the leper, and I give life to the dead, by God's leave. And I tell you what you eat and what you store in your houses. Surely in that is a sign for you, if you are believers.

**"A messenger to the Children of Israel."** Note the scope, stated plainly and limited: his mission is to Israel. This is part of the same argument. A universal deity does not have a regional mission.

**The signs.** And note the precision of the first one, because the wording is doing theological work.

*"I will make for you a bird from clay."* The Arabic is *ka-hay'ati al-ṭayri* — **like the form of a bird**. *Hay'ah* is shape, configuration, outward form. He makes a shape. He does not make a bird.

*"Breathe into it, and it will become a real bird — by Allah's Will."* The sequence is exact: he fashions, he breathes, and the **becoming** is by God's leave. The human act is the shaping and the breathing; the life is God's. The verse distributes the agency in a single clause, and it does so in the same terms that 3:6 used for every human being — God is the one who *forms*; the creature only shapes.

**"I will heal the blind and the leper."** The words are *al-akmah* and *al-abraṣ*. *Al-akmah* is specifically one who is **blind from birth** — a harder and more visible case than *al-a'mā*, one who has lost sight. And *al-abraṣ* is the one with the white skin condition — leprosy or vitiligo. The choice of the harder word in each pair is characteristic: the Qur'an names the case that cannot be explained away.

**"And raise the dead to life — by Allah's Will."** *Wa-uḥyī al-mawtā bi-idhni Allāh.*

**"And I will prophesize what you eat and store in your houses."** *Wa-unabbi'ukum bi-mā ta'kulūna wa-mā tad'dakhirūna fī buyūtikum.*

And this one deserves more attention than it usually gets, because it is the least spectacular item on the list and in some ways the most unnerving. Creating a bird, healing the sick, raising the dead — those are public. This one is domestic. He knows what you had for dinner last night, and what you have put away in the cupboard against tomorrow. That is knowledge of the inside of a person's life, and it means nothing done at home is hidden from him.

It is also, not incidentally, the same category of knowledge that 3:5 and 3:29 attribute to God: knowledge of the hidden. Which is one more way the verse keeps pointing away from the messenger and toward the One who sent him.

**Now the thing that governs the whole verse: *bi-idhni Allāh* appears twice.**

Once after the bird, once after the healing and the raising. The repetition is not incidental and it is not a stylistic tic. It is the point of the verse.

Every miracle in this list is granted in full and re-attributed in the same breath. The Qur'an denies nothing. It says: yes, he did these things — **by permission**. And permission is the relationship of a servant to a Lord. No one needs permission to do what is theirs by nature.

This is the surah's entire answer to the argument from miracles, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the hinge of the whole dispute with Christianity. The Christians of Najrān would have reasoned: no one does these things unless God is with him, and if God is with him in this way, he is God. The Qur'an's reply is not to diminish the miracles. It is to point out that the category is wrong: signs authenticate a **prophet**; they do not constitute a **deity**. Other prophets did comparable things by the same permission, and no one claimed divinity for them.

**A small fiqh note.** Ma'arif al-Qur'an observes on this verse that the making of the bird's shape was the making of a *form*, which was permitted in that *sharī'ah* and was abrogated in ours. That connects directly back to 3:6, where forming was identified as God's own act — which is why Islam's prohibition on image-making is at bottom a claim about who forms.

**"Surely in this is a sign for you if you truly believe."** *Inna fī dhālika la-āyatan lakum in kuntum mu'minīn.*

And note the condition. The sign is a sign **if you are believers**. The Qur'an does not treat a miracle as self-interpreting. The same event will be read one way by one person and another way by another — which is exactly the argument of 3:7, exactly the argument of 3:13, and exactly the thing the surah has been saying since its seventh verse. The variable is not the evidence. It is the person looking at it.

===VERSE-END===

## Verse 50

**"And I will confirm the Torah revealed before me and legalize some of what had been forbidden to you. I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of Allah and obey me."**

In simple terms: and [I have come] confirming what was before me of the Torah, and to make lawful for you some of what was forbidden to you. And I have come to you with a sign from your Lord, so be mindful of God and obey me.

**"Confirming the Torah revealed before me."** *Wa-muṣaddiqan li-mā bayna yadayya mina al-Tawrāti.*

And here is a parallel worth pausing on, because it is exact. The Qur'an describes **itself** at 3:3 as "confirming what came before it" — *muṣaddiqan li-mā bayna yadayhi*. And it describes **Jesus** here in the identical words.

That is not careless repetition. It is the claim that his mission and the final mission are the same kind of thing: a confirmation arriving in a new form. The surah has been building toward the Najrān argument for thirty verses, and this is one of its load-bearing beams — the Qur'an does not present itself as a novelty, and it presents Jesus as having done the same thing it does.

**"And legalize some of what had been forbidden to you."** *Wa-li-uḥilla lakum ba'ḍa alladhī ḥurrima 'alaykum.*

And this is one of the most consequential half-lines in the Qur'an for the science of law.

**The abrogation crux.** Ibn Kathīr states it directly: "This part of the Ayah indicates that 'Īsā abrogated some of the Laws of the Tawrah, and informed the Jews of the truth regarding some issues that they used to dispute about." Ma'arif al-Qur'an says the same: "the unlawfulness of some things in the earlier code would stand abrogated by the new one, that of 'Īsā, whose station of prophethood was the conclusive argument for that claim of abrogation."

The significance for *uṣūl al-fiqh* is large. **Abrogation (*naskh*) operates between the revealed laws of different prophets, not only within a single revelation.** If Jesus could lift part of the Torah's prohibitions, then the pattern is established: a later messenger may supersede the law of an earlier one. That pattern is what makes the claim that the *sharī'ah* of Muhammad ﷺ abrogates aspects of previous laws a continuation of precedent rather than an innovation.

**But note the word "some."** *Ba'ḍa* — **some**, a part.

This is the balance the verse holds and it is easy to lose. He **confirms** the Torah and he lifts **some** of its prohibitions. Both verbs are in one sentence. He is not abolishing the Torah; he is not preserving it unchanged either. Continuity and change are stated in a single line, and the proportion is specified.

What was actually lifted is discussed in the light of 6:146 and 4:160, where the Qur'an indicates that certain foods were made unlawful **for the Israelites** as a consequence of their wrongdoing rather than as a universal prohibition. That framing matters: some of what was forbidden to them was a burden specific to them, and part of what Jesus did was relieve a burden that had been imposed rather than inherent.

**"I have come to you with a sign from your Lord."** *Wa-ji'tukum bi-āyatin min rabbikum.* And note the pronoun: **your** Lord. Not "my Lord" — he does not hold God over against them. The God he calls them to is the God they already acknowledge, and the sign is from Him to them.

**"So be mindful of Allah and obey me."** *Fa-ittaqū Allāha wa-aṭī'ūni.*

And there is the mission in two imperatives. And note that this is the same pairing the surah used at 3:32 — "obey God and the Messenger." Jesus' own words, as the Qur'an reports them, establish the same structure. Obedience to the messenger is not a novelty introduced by Muhammad ﷺ; it is how the relationship between God and a community has always been ordered.

===VERSE-END===

## Synthesis of the Ten Verses

These ten verses are the surah's longest sustained act of preparation before the confrontation at verses 59 and 61, and every one of them is a claim that a Christian could affirm and a Muslim must affirm.

**Verse 41 opens with a sign that is an inability** — three days of silence, given to a man who has just been given the thing he asked for, and turned into three days of remembrance. God does not mute Zakariyyā; He redirects him.

**Verses 42–43 concern Mary.** She is chosen, purified, and chosen above the women of the worlds — and the question of whether that amounts to prophethood is a real and documented dispute, turning on whether *nubuwwah* means the reception of revelation or the delivery of a law. All sides agree she was never a **messenger**, and all sides agree on her rank. Then she is commanded to worship **with those who bow**, which settles the shape of her devotion: not a recluse, but in the row.

**Verse 44 turns aside to the Prophet ﷺ** and says: you were not there. It is one of the Qur'an's clearest self-arguments, and it is placed precisely where the narrative reaches a detail only a witness could know — and, characteristically, it reports that they *disputed*.

**Verses 45–47 announce the son.** He is "a Word from Him," titled *al-Masīḥ* — a borrowed title whose forty-nine proposed Arabic etymologies are themselves the evidence of borrowing — and named, always, through his mother. He will be honoured in both worlds and among those brought near. He will speak in the cradle and in maturity, which is the surah's quiet proof that he grew, and a god does not grow. And when Mary asks the third of the "how" questions, the answer escalates from "God does what He wills" to "**God creates** what He wills" — *kun fa-yakūn*, which is simultaneously the mechanism of his origin and the reason for his title.

**Verses 48–50 describe the mission.** He is taught — by God — the Book, wisdom, the Torah and the Gospel. He works six signs, and twice in one verse the text says **by God's leave**, granting the miracle and re-attributing it in the same breath. And he comes confirming the Torah while lifting **some** of its prohibitions, which establishes that abrogation between the laws of different prophets is a precedent, not an innovation.

**Five threads run through the batch.**

The first is **"son of Mary."** *Ibn Maryam*, every time. The doctrine is carried in the grammar.

The second is **the three "hows"** — Abraham, Zakariyyā, Mary — each answered by relocating the question from mechanism to agent, with the answer growing stronger at each asking.

The third is **"by His leave."** Twice in verse 49. The surah's whole reply to the argument from miracles is in those two words.

The fourth is **taught, not self-originating.** He is taught by God (48), he acts by permission (49), he obeys (50). The one being prepared for the reader is a servant.

And the fifth is the one that governs all of it: **the surah denies him nothing.** It insists on the virgin birth more firmly than his followers did. It calls him a Word from God, honoured in both worlds, among those brought near, worker of six miracles, confirmer of the Torah. Every one of those is a claim the Najrān delegation could have signed. And the surah will now spend the next ten verses showing what those claims, taken together, actually entail.

**A note on honesty in citation.** No page numbers, volumes or publication details are given, because this is a synthesized commentary rather than a citation apparatus, and inventing precise references would be worse than omitting them. Where a report is transmitted with a variable chain — the lot-casting for Mary's care, the reconciliation of the three days and three nights, the timing of the Torah and Gospel instruction, the narration of the four women prophets — I have flagged it as reported rather than established. Where the disagreement is real — Mary's prophethood, the meaning of *kahlan* and its eschatological reading, the sense of *al-kitāb* in 3:48, the scope of *al-Masīḥ* — I have given the arguments on each side and said where I think the weight falls without presenting any of it as settled. On *al-Masīḥ* I have recorded the forty-nine derivations as evidence of the word's borrowed character rather than as a list of live options; on Mary's prophethood I have stated explicitly that the dispute turns on a definition and that a Muslim may hold either position; and on 3:46 I have noted that uses of the verse in arguments about Jesus' death go beyond its plain function in context, deferring the matter to 4:157–158.
