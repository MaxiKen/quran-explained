# Sūrah 4 — an-Nisāʾ (The Women)

*176 verses; Madinan — al-ʿAwfī from Ibn ʿAbbās, and likewise Ibn Mardawayh from ʿAbdullāh b. al-Zubayr and
Zayd b. Thābit: it was revealed in Madīnah [Ibn Kathīr]. Al-Qurṭubī allows one exception: the verse
"God commands you to render back the trusts to their owners" (4:58) was sent down in Mecca in the year of
the conquest concerning ʿUthmān b. Ṭalḥah al-Ḥajabī. Al-Naḥḥās held the whole sūrah Meccan; but ʿĀʾishah
said, "No sūrah of an-Nisāʾ was revealed but that I was with the Messenger of God ﷺ" (Bukhārī) — and he
consummated his marriage with her only in Madīnah, so the sūrah is Madinan without doubt [Qurṭubī]. Ibn
ʿAbbās said, "Ask me about Sūrat an-Nisāʾ, for I learned the Qurʾān while I was still young" (al-Ḥākim
graded it sound by the criteria of the two Ṣaḥīḥs) [Ibn Kathīr]. Ibn Masʿūd said: "There are five verses
in Sūrat an-Nisāʾ that I would prefer to this world and all it contains" — 4:40, 4:31, 4:48, 4:64 and
4:110 [Ibn Kathīr].*

**Sources cited inline:** **[Ṭabarī]** *Jāmiʿ al-Bayān* · **[Qurṭubī]** *al-Jāmiʿ li-Aḥkām al-Qurʾān* ·
**[Ibn Kathīr]** *Tafsīr al-Qurʾān al-ʿAẓīm* · **[Jalālayn]** *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* · **[Saʿdī]** *Taysīr
al-Karīm al-Raḥmān* · **[Maʿārif]** *Maʿārif al-Qurʾān* · **[Study Quran]** *The Study Quran* (2015).

**The sūrah.** It is named *an-Nisāʾ*, "the Women," and after al-Baqarah it is the longest sūrah of the
Qurʾān and the longest Madinan one. The last verse of Āl ʿImrān closed with an exhortation to *taqwā*;
this sūrah opens with *taqwā* in the form of a command, and the opening section is taken up with the
rights of God's servants (*ḥuqūq al-ʿibād*) — orphans, relatives and wives [Maʿārif]. **[Maʿārif]** notes
that the sūrah treats these rights as living relationships that cannot be weighed out on a scale: the
rights of those in one's charge — children, parents, spouses and orphans — depend on upright conduct,
mercy and a heartfelt desire for their good, which is why the sūrah opens by grounding them in reverence
for the One who created all people from a single soul. **[Saʿdī]** observes that the sūrah is built on
*taqwā*, kindness to kin and the marital bond, laid down in vv. 1–4 and then unfolded in the greatest
detail to its close: as though the sūrah were founded on these matters, detailing what was left general
and clarifying what was left obscure.

---

## 4:1

*"O mankind, fear your Lord, who created you from a single soul, and created from it its mate, and from
the two of them spread abroad many men and women; and fear God, through whom you ask one another, and the
wombs. Surely God is ever Watchful over you."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** *Taqwā* here means: be on your guard, O people, against opposing your Lord in
what He commands you and forbids you, lest there befall you a punishment you cannot withstand. He then
describes Himself as the One alone in creating all beings from a single person, making known that their
beginning was one soul — and so He reminds them that they are all the children of one man and one woman,
that each is a part of the other, and that the right of one over the other is as obligatory as the right
of a brother: they are bound in kinship to one father and one mother. The obligation of care between them
is the same even when the kinship is distant; and He inclined them toward one another so that they would
deal justly and not oppress each other, the strong yielding to the weak what God has made due.
"From a single soul" means from Adam. **[Jalālayn]** Identifies the single soul as Adam, and its mate as
Eve, created from one of his left ribs; from the pair God scattered — separated and spread — many men and
women. On *tassāʾalūn*: the original *tatasāʾalūn* has its *tāʾ* assimilated with the *sīn*, and a
variant reading gives *tasāʾalūn*, so that one of you says to another, "I ask you by God" or "for God's
sake"; and *al-arḥām*, kinship ties, is mentioned lest they be severed — read by some as *wa-l-arḥāmi*,
a supplement to the pronoun in *bihi*, meaning God. They used to implore one another by ties of kinship.
**[Qurṭubī]** Notes that the command joins the rights of God to the rights of kinship in a single verse,
and that God's closing description — *raqīb*, "watchful" — is the guarantee that none of these rights is
unseen.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** God commands His creatures to fear Him by worshipping Him alone,
and reminds them of His power in creating them all from a single person, Adam — and from Adam's left rib,
while he slept, He created his wife Eve; when Adam woke and saw her, he loved her and was drawn to her,
and she to him. The authentic *ḥadīth* states: "Woman was created from a rib, and the crookedest part of
the rib is its upper part; if you try to straighten it, you break it; and if you enjoy her, you enjoy her
with crookedness in her."

**Belief.** **[Saʿdī]** The verse is the sūrah's foundation: God opens it with the command of *taqwā* and
with exhortation to worship and to kinship. The reasons that make *taqwā* binding are that He is your
Lord — who created you, provided for you and nurtured you with His great favors, among them that He
created you from one soul and made its mate from it, so that each might find repose in the other, the
favor completed and joy attained. And He is "Watchful": He sees His servants in their motion and rest, in
secret and in public, in every state — so He should be guarded against, and one should be shy before Him
by keeping His *taqwā*. And He informs them that He created them from a single soul and scattered them
across the earth, while all return to one origin, so that they incline toward one another and are tender
toward one another.

**Language.** **[Qurṭubī]** *Tassāʾalūn* is *tatasāʾalūn* with the *tāʾ* merged into the *sīn*; the
particle *bi* in *tassāʾalūn bihi* carries the sense "by whom you ask" — an oath. *Al-arḥām* is
accusative, joined to the object of fear: fear God, and fear breaking the wombs.

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** draws out the design of the opening: God paired the command to fear Him with
the command to be good to kin and the prohibition of severing them, so that it would be established that,
just as His own right must be fulfilled, so must the rights of His creatures — especially the nearest of
kin, whose rights are part of God's own right. "And from it He created its mate" is itself an instruction
to honor the rights of spouses, for wives are created from husbands: between them and them is the closest
lineage, the strongest connection and the firmest bond. **[Maʿārif]** observes that the Qurʾān here
addresses all mankind, not believers alone, because the rights it is about to legislate belong to humanity
as such; and that society is built on the recognition that those who share one origin are bound to one
another. **[Study Quran]** Reads the same opening as grounding the sūrah's family legislation — covering
woman (v. 2), then orphans and inheritance, then marriage (v. 3) — in the unity of the human family,
so that the rights that follow are not tribal privilege but the debt owed by one soul to another.

**Cross-references.** "It is He who created you from a single soul, and from it made its mate, that he
might find rest in her" (7:189); "He created you from a single soul, then made from it its mate" (39:6);
"O mankind, We created you from a male and a female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one
another" (49:13).


## 4:2

*"And give the orphans their property, and exchange not the bad for the good, and consume not their
property along with your own — surely that is a great sin."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The address is to the guardians of orphans: give the orphans their property
when they reach puberty and sound judgement is perceived in them. "Do not exchange the bad for the good"
— do not take the lawful and give them the unlawful in exchange: do not take what is choice from the
orphan's wealth and substitute your own inferior goods, saying "an animal for an animal, a dirham for a
dirham." Mujāhid said: the lawful in place of the unlawful. **[Jalālayn]** The verse was revealed
concerning an orphan who claimed his property from his guardian and was refused it. Do not exchange the
unlawful for the lawful by taking the orphan's good property and leaving him your faulty property; and
absorb not his property into your own — consuming both together; surely that is *ḥūban kabīran*, a
serious sin.

**Context.** **[Qurṭubī]** Muqātil and al-Kalbī report that the verse came down concerning a man of the
tribe of Ghaṭafān who held a large estate belonging to his orphaned nephew. When the boy came of age and
asked for the property, his uncle withheld it, and the verse was revealed. Then the uncle said, "We take
refuge in God from *al-ḥūb al-kabīr*," and returned the property — and the Prophet ﷺ said, "Whoever is
preserved from the greed of his own soul and returns [what he holds] this way, will alight in his abode,"
meaning his garden. **[Study Quran]** gives the same occasion in its general form: an orphan in the care
of his uncle who refused him his property when he came of age; and it notes that *yatīm* in Qurʾān and
law is a child who has lost his father or both parents.

**Rulings.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Do not exchange your bad things for their good ones": Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib
and al-Zuhrī said it means do not substitute a weak animal of yours for a fat one of theirs; Ibrāhīm
al-Nakhaʿī and al-Ḍaḥḥāk said, do not give something bad in place of something good. Al-Suddī described
the practice: a guardian would take a fat sheep from the orphan's flock and put in its place a weak sheep
of his own, saying, "a sheep for a sheep," and take a good dirham and leave a counterfeit one, saying,
"a dirham for a dirham." "And do not devour their wealth along with your own" means: do not mix the two
together so that you consume both — as Mujāhid, Saʿīd b. Jubayr, Muqātil, al-Suddī and Sufyān b. Ḥassān
said. And *ḥūb* means a grave and enormous sin, according to Ibn ʿAbbās and a long list of the earliest
commentators. **[Saʿdī]** Consuming the orphan's property unjustly is the first of the rights of creation
God takes up in this sūrah: the orphans have lost the fathers who would have kept them, they are small
and weak and cannot manage their own affairs, so the Compassionate Lord commanded His servants to treat
them well, not to approach their wealth except in the best manner, and to hand over their property
complete and full when they reach maturity and sound judgement. **[Maʿārif]** On the word *yatīm*:
literally it means the one who is alone or unique — a pearl alone in its shell is called *al-durr
al-yatīm*, "the orphan pearl." In the terminology of the law, an orphan is a child whose father has died
(among animals, the young one whose mother has died); and the *ḥadīth* states, "No orphanhood after
puberty" (Mishkat) — orphanhood ends at maturity. **[Maʿārif]** adds that the guardian's task is not
merely to preserve the orphan's property but to keep it separate, maintain it, invest it and not expose
it to loss.

**Reflection.** The command is put in the plural and addressed to guardians, but its weight falls on the
one who imagines the orphan cannot see. **[Qurṭubī]** notes that the verse's phrase "a great sin"
prompted the uncle to return what he held — so the Qurʾān's naming of the crime before the punishment is
itself part of the cure. And **[Saʿdī]** draws out of the wording that the property is called the
orphan's property even while the guardian holds it: whatever is in a guardian's hand for an orphan is
never his to spend as if it were his own.

**Cross-references.** "And do not approach the property of the orphan except in the best way" (6:152,
17:34); "Come not near the property of the orphan except to improve it" (17:34); the treatment of the
guardian's permissible maintenance at 4:6 and 2:220: "If you intermingle with them, they are your
brothers."


## 4:3

*"And if you fear that you will not deal justly with the orphans, then marry such women as seem good to
you — two, three, or four; but if you fear that you will not be just, then one, or what your right hands
possess. That is nearer to preventing you from injustice."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The commentators differed. One view: "If you fear, O guardians of the orphans,
that you will not deal fairly in their dowries — giving them the dowry of their peers — then do not marry
them; instead marry other women whom God has made lawful for you, from one up to four; and if you fear
that you will swerve from justice when you marry more than one, then marry one, or what your right hands
possess." The clearest report supporting this is that of al-Zuhrī from ʿUrwah from ʿĀʾishah — and
Ṭabarī's editor notes that he transmits this *ḥadīth* by many chains and that it is established and
sound, in the two *Ṣaḥīḥs* among others. ʿĀʾishah said: "O son of my sister, it is the orphan girl who
is in the care of her guardian; he desires her wealth and her beauty, and wants to marry her at less than
the customary dowry. So they were forbidden to marry them unless they dealt justly with them and gave
them their full dowry, and were commanded to marry other women besides them."

**Meaning.** **[Jalālayn]** If you fear that you will not be equitable toward the orphans and are
distressed in that matter, then also fear lest you be unjust toward women when you marry them: marry
*ma ṭāba lakum*, "whatever women seem good to you" — two, three or four — but do not exceed that. If you
fear you cannot be equitable in their maintenance and their share, then only one, or restrict yourself to
what your right hand owns, for a slave-girl does not have the same rights as a free wife. That is nearer
to keeping you from injustice.

**Rulings & wisdom.** **[Saʿdī]** If you fear that you will not deal justly with the orphan women under
your care because you do not love them, then turn to other women and marry what your hearts choose of
women of religion, wealth, beauty, lineage and noble descent — but the best quality to choose is
religion, as the Prophet ﷺ said: "A woman is married for four things: for her wealth, her beauty, her
lineage and her religion; so take the one of religion, and your hands will be filled with good." The
verse teaches that a man should choose before marriage, and the Law even permitted him to look at the
woman he intends to marry so that he acts with insight. Then God set out the permitted number: two,
three or four — the verse came to state a favor, and by consensus it is not lawful to exceed what God has
named. A man whose desire is not satisfied by one is permitted one after another up to four, since four
usually suffice; but this is permitted only for the one who is safe from injustice and confident of
fulfilling their rights. If he fears otherwise, let him restrict himself to one or to what his right hand
possesses — for there is no obligation of equal division among slave-girls. *Dhālika adnā allā taʿūlū*:
that is nearer to your not deviating. In it is a principle: whoever is about to expose himself to a
matter in which he fears injustice and failure to fulfill the obligation should not expose himself to it,
even if the matter is permitted; safety and well-being are the best gifts given to a servant.

**Context.** **[Maʿārif]** During the *Jāhiliyyah*, guardians holding orphan girls under their charge
would pick those who were good-looking or had valuable property, marry them or marry them off to their
sons, fix whatever dowry they chose — usually the lowest — and maintain them in whatever manner they
wished, for there was no father to guard their rights. The verse cuts off every device to take the
orphan's property through marriage, and it is one more instance of the sūrah's theme: it is the duty of
guardians to protect the rights of orphans honestly. **[Study Quran]** notes that all commentators are
explicit that the verse did not establish a new license for polygamy or encourage it, but limited the
more excessive practices of pre-Islamic Arabia by setting four as the maximum, and that a man who takes
more wives than he can provide for may be tempted to pilfer the property of orphans in his care — so the
just treatment of orphans and of wives belong together. **[Qurṭubī]** adds a further ruling drawn from
the same nexus: because the verse ties the two, a guardian may buy from the orphan's property for himself
and sell to himself, but only without favoritism, and the principal retains the right to inspect the
transaction.

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** reads the verse's condition as the whole point: the permission is fenced
twice — by the ability to be just, and by the fear of injustice — so the Law permits plurality as a
concession to necessity and closes the door wherever justice cannot be guaranteed. And **[Study
Quran]** records the reading attributed to ʿĀʾishah that v. 127, which criticizes men who wish to marry
orphan girls without giving their bridal gifts, is the divine clarification of this verse.

**Cross-references.** "You will never be able to deal justly between wives, however much you may desire
it" (4:129); "Marry the unmarried among you and the righteous among your slaves" (24:32); the wives of
the Prophet ﷺ and the limit of four at 33:50–52.


## 4:4

*"And give the women their dowries as a free gift; but if they are pleased to remit to you any of it,
then consume it with wholesome enjoyment."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "Give the women their dowries as a gift" means: give them their *mahr* as an
obligatory gift and a binding duty. The root is *naḥala* — *naḥala fulānun fulānan kadhā*, he gave him
such and such as a *niḥlah* — so *niḥlah* is a gift that binds the giver. Qatādah said: it is an
obligation. Ibn ʿAbbās said: *niḥlah* here means the dowry. Ibn Jurayj said: a specified obligation.
Ibn Zayd said: *al-niḥlah* in the speech of the Arabs is what is binding; she may not be married except
with something binding for her, a *ṣidāq* that he names as due to her — and after the Prophet ﷺ no one
should marry a woman without a binding dowry, nor should the naming of the dowry be a lie without truth.

**Language & rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** *Ṣaduqāt* is the plural of *ṣaduqah*; the address is to the
husbands, according to Ibn ʿAbbās, Qatādah, Ibn Zayd and Ibn Jurayj — God commanded them to give the
dowries to their wives freely, as a gift from them. It is also said the address is to the guardians
(Abū Ṣāliḥ): a guardian used to take the woman's dowry and give her nothing of it, and they were
forbidden that and commanded to hand it to them. Al-Kalbī's report: in the *Jāhiliyyah*, if the woman
was of the same clan as the guardian he gave her little or nothing of her dowry; if she was a stranger he
carried her on a camel to her husband and gave her nothing else. **[Jalālayn]** *Niḥlatan* is a verbal
noun: a present given out of the kindness of one's heart; "if they are pleased to offer you any of it of
their own accord" — *nafsan* specifies "they," meaning the women — then consume it with wholesome
appetite, a praiseworthy consequence with no harm for you in the Hereafter.

**Context.** **[Maʿārif]** In pre-Islamic Arabia the injustice about dowries took several forms. First,
the dowry that was the girl's right was not given to her at all: the guardians took it from the husband
directly — rank injustice — and the command "give the women their dowers" removed it, addressed to the
husbands so that they give the dowries to their wives and not to others, and to the guardians so that if
they receive a dowry on the girl's behalf they hand it straight to her. Second was the attitude of the
giver: a man who had to pay would pay sourly and unwillingly, as though paying a penalty — and this
injustice was removed by the word *niḥlah*, which in Arabic usage means giving something cheerfully.
**[Study Quran]** Likewise takes the verse as establishing that the bridewealth is a free gift, given to
the woman herself and not to her family, and as prohibiting such devices as arranging the marriage of
widows in exchange for their bridewealth, or keeping the bridewealth of one's daughters or of orphan
girls in one's care (citing Ibn Kathīr, Qurṭubī, Ṭabarī and al-Zamakhsharī). What a wife willingly
remits to her husband may be enjoyed by him, provided it is given freely and without compulsion.
**[Qurṭubī]** records the same point from the other side: the guardian or the husband who takes any of
her dowry without her free consent stands under this verse's prohibition.

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** The verse begins "and give" — with the woman named as the recipient — and
closes by making even her free remission a thing to be consumed "with wholesome enjoyment," that is,
with a good, praiseworthy outcome and no harm in the Hereafter. In between stands the word *niḥlah*: a
gift, not a price. The dowry is thus neither a payment to the family nor a debt paid with resentment but
the woman's own property, given cheerfully and protected even from her guardian's hand.

**Cross-references.** "Give them their dowries as an obligation" (4:24); "And marry them with the
permission of their people, and give them their dowries in kindness" (4:25); the dowry of the Prophet's
wives and the ruling that a wife may remit it (4:128); "And do not forcibly inherit women" (4:19).


## 4:5

*"And give not the feeble-minded your property, which God has made a means of support for you, but
provide for them and clothe them from it, and speak to them words of kindness."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The commentators differed about who the *sufahāʾ* are. One group said: women
and children — Saʿīd b. Jubayr said "the orphans and the women"; al-Ḥasan said "do not give [property]
to the small ones and the women," and elsewhere "the woman and the boy," and he said, "women and the
young — and women are the most foolish of the foolish." Ibn ʿAbbās, via al-Ḍaḥḥāk, said: your foolish
son and your foolish wife. The root of *safah* in the language is a lightness in the body and a
confusion in the mind. "Which God has made a means of support (*qiyāman*) for you": the property is what
sustains the servants' religion and their worldly affairs — make them a standing support, and do not
hand it over to one who will destroy it. **[Jalālayn]** "Provide for them and clothe them from it": feed
them and clothe them from the estate; and speak to them decent words — prepare for them a kind reception
by giving them their property when they reach maturity.

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** Because God had commanded that the orphans' property be handed over, and that
dowries reach the wives, He then made plain that the fool and the minor are not to be given their
property — and the verse therefore establishes the institution of the guardian (*waṣī*), the trustee
(*walī*) and the surety (*kafīl*) for orphans. The scholars agree that a bequest of guardianship to a
free, trustworthy, just Muslim is valid; they differed about a bequest to a free woman — the generality of
scholars held it valid, and Aḥmad argued from the fact that ʿUmar made his bequest to Ḥafṣah, whereas
ʿAṭāʾ b. Abī Rabāḥ said a woman may not be a guardian and, if it were done, the charge should be
transferred to a man of her people. They also differed about a bequest to a slave: al-Shāfiʿī, Abū
Thawr, Muḥammad and Yaʿqūb refused it; Mālik, al-Awzāʿī and Ibn ʿAbd al-Ḥakam allowed it.

**Rulings.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** The prohibition covers not only youth but insanity, erratic behaviour, a
weak intellect or a weakness in religion — and likewise bankruptcy, when creditors ask that a
bankrupt person's property be placed in escrow because his debts cannot be paid from it. Ibn ʿAbbās said
"the unwise" means children and women; Ibn Masʿūd, al-Ḥakam b. ʿUyaynah, al-Ḥasan and al-Ḍaḥḥāk said:
"women and boys"; Saʿīd b. Jubayr said: the orphans; Mujāhid, ʿIkrimah and Qatādah said: they are the
women. On "speak to them words of kindness," Ibn ʿAbbās (via ʿAlī b. Abī Ṭalḥah) said: do not give your
wealth, which God has made you responsible for and a means of sustenance, to your wife or your children —
hold on to your money and be the one who spends on them for clothing, food and provision; Mujāhid said
it means kindness and keeping good relations.

**Language & reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** A *safīh* is one who cannot manage property well — either
because his mind is lacking, as with the insane and the feebleminded, or because his judgement is not
yet formed, as with the child and the spendthrift. God forbade the guardians to hand over property to
such people for fear of its corruption and loss, because God made wealth a means of standing for His
servants in the affairs of their religion and their world, and these people cannot maintain it. So the
guardian must provide for them from it, clothe them, and spend from it on their necessities, and speak
to them a kind word: promising them that they will receive their property when they reach sound
judgement, and dealing gently with them to console their hearts. That God attributes the property to the
guardians ("your property") indicates that they must guard it as they guard their own. The verse is
proof that the maintenance of the insane, the child and the spendthrift is paid out of their own
property when they have any; and it is proof that the guardian's word is accepted concerning reasonable
maintenance and clothing, because God made him a trustee over their wealth and the word of a trustee
must be accepted. **[Study Quran]** documents and rejects the view that *sufahāʾ* means women: the word
is a masculine plural and so covers individuals of either sex who are mentally incompetent — an
interpretation that would contradict vv. 2 and 4 and is not upheld by Islamic law, which gives women full
control of their property. It also records Qurṭubī's opinion that the verse may prohibit entrusting
one's property to non-Muslims who would not recognize Islamic financial rules such as the prohibition of
usury.

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** The order of the sūrah is deliberate: the command to give orphans their
property and women their dowry might suggest that property should be surrendered in every case, even to
those who cannot protect it — so these verses remove the misunderstanding and lay down that the estate
must be watched and tested until sound judgement appears, at which point it is handed over. And
**[Maʿārif]** notes the balance: God has put in man's heart a natural desire to protect property, and
the verse governs both — protect what is yours, and administer what is theirs as carefully as your own.

**Cross-references.** "And do not approach the property of the orphan except in the best way, until he
reaches his full strength" (17:34); "If you intermingle with them, they are your brothers" (2:220); "And
test the orphans until they reach the age of marriage" (4:6); "And do not give the foolish their
property" is echoed in the law of the *ḥajr* (legal interdiction).


## 4:6

*"And test the orphans until they reach the age of marriage; then, if you perceive in them sound
judgement, deliver to them their property; and consume it not wastefully and in haste, before they grow
up. And whoever is rich, let him be abstinent; and whoever is poor, let him consume in an honourable way.
And when you deliver to them their property, take witnesses over them; and God suffices as a Reckoner."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "Test the orphans" means: try the minds of your orphans — their understanding,
their religion and their management of property. Qatādah and al-Ḥasan said: test the orphans. Al-Suddī
said: try their minds. Mujāhid said: their intellects. Ibn ʿAbbās said: test them. Ibn Zayd said:
examine him in his judgement and his mind, how he is — and when it is perceived that sound judgement is
present in him, his property is handed over to him, and that is after the wet dream.

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** The verse is addressed to all, explaining how the orphans' property is to be
delivered, and it is reported to have been revealed about Thābit b. Rifāʿah and his uncle: Rifāʿah died
leaving a small son, and the uncle came to the Prophet ﷺ and asked, "My brother's son is an orphan in my
care — what is lawful for me from his property, and when do I hand it over to him?" So God sent down this
verse. About the meaning of the test, it was said: the guardian should study his ward's character and
listen to his purposes until he comes to know his nobility, his diligence in his own interests and his
control of his property, or his neglect of it — and when he senses good in him, the scholars said there
is no harm in giving him something of his property to dispose of; if he develops it and manages it well,
the test is complete and the guardian must deliver the whole estate. On the age: al-Shāfiʿī placed
majority at the completion of fifteen years, and other jurists at other ages.

**Rulings.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** "Until they reach the age of marriage" — the age of puberty; according to
the majority of scholars it comes when the boy has a wet dream (and the same for the girl's
menstruation). He cites the Prophet's ﷺ saying, "The pen is lifted from three: from the sleeper until he
wakes, from the child until he grows up, and from the insane until he comes to his senses," transmitted
by Abū Dāwūd and others, as the principle that the orphan's property is held until the capacity that
makes him legally responsible arrives. **[Study Quran]** sets out the range of juristic opinion: sound
judgement is possession of a working intellect, an upright character, an understanding of property
management and a sound religion such that custodial care is no longer needed; the age of majority is put
at eighteen by many, fifteen or seventeen by others, and where sound judgement does not appear, some
jurists allow the guardian to withhold the property for up to seven further years; some hold that it is
the age of moral responsibility rather than a biological threshold. Testing may mean giving the ward
some money and observing how he manages it.

**Rulings.** **[Study Quran]** On the guardian's consumption: the rich man should abstain entirely from
the orphan's property, and the poor man may partake "in an honourable way," meaning only what he needs
for necessities — particularly food — and that this is treated as a loan that must be repaid, even after
his death. The guardian may benefit from the milk of the orphan's livestock, the fruit of the orphan's
land or the service of his riding animals, as long as he uses the property in a way that maintains it and
does not profit monetarily by it. Some held this limited permission abrogated by v. 10; the command to
take witnesses when handing the property over is generally considered mandatory, and some say witnesses
should be taken whenever a loan is taken from the orphan's property.

**Meaning & rulings.** **[Saʿdī]** The test is that a portion of his property is given to the ward who is
near sound judgement, so that he may dispose of it in a manner fitting his state, and his soundness or
foolishness becomes clear. If he remains unable to manage it, his property is not delivered to him — he
stays under interdiction even if he lives to a great age; but if his sound judgement and his management
of property are shown and he reaches the age of marriage, then deliver their property to them, complete
and full. "Do not consume it wastefully" — that is, going beyond the lawful limit God permitted you in
their property to the unlawful He forbade. "And in haste, before they grow up" — do not consume it while
they are small and cannot take it from you or prevent you, racing to get it before they grow up and take
it back; this is what many guardians do who have no fear of God and no mercy or love for their wards:
they see this as a chance and rush at what God has forbidden them — and it is this particular state that
God forbade. **[Jalālayn]** adds that the witness command is so that if a dispute occurs the guardian
has a clear proof, and that God suffices as a Reckoner — the *bāʾ* in *bi-llāh* being extra.

**Reflection.** **[Maʿārif]** The ward's life falls into three stages: minority before puberty; after
puberty; and after sound judgement in managing affairs. In the first, guardians are told to educate and
train the child by giving him hands-on experience; in the second his physical maturity is watched; in the
third the estate is handed over. The whole passage is a charter of trust: the property is tested,
watched, invested, witnessed over — and then released, with God as the Reckoner on the day when the
witnesses' ledger is closed.

**Cross-references.** "Do not approach the property of the orphan except in the best way, until he
reaches his full strength" (17:34); "And let those fear [God] who, were they to leave behind them weak
children, would be afraid for them" (4:9); "Whatever good you do, God knows it" (2:197).


## 4:7

*"Unto the men a share of what parents and near kin leave, and unto the women a share of what parents
and near kin leave, be it little or much — a share ordained."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** For the male children of the deceased a portion of his inheritance, and for
the female children a portion, from the little or much he left behind, a portion ordained and obligatory,
known and delimited. This verse, reports say, was sent down because the people of the *Jāhiliyyah* used
to give inheritance to the males to the exclusion of the females; Qatādah: "They used not to make women
inherit, so 'and to the women a share of what parents and near kin leave' was revealed."
**[Jalālayn]** The verse repudiated the pre-Islamic practice in which women and children received no
inheritance: to the men, young and old, a share of what parents and kinsmen leave, and to the women a
share of what parents and kinsmen leave, whether the estate be little or much — God made it an obligatory
share apportioned to them.

**Context.** **[Qurṭubī]** The verse was revealed concerning Aws b. Thābit al-Anṣārī, who died leaving a
wife named Umm Kuḥḥah and three daughters by her. Two men, the sons of the dead man's paternal uncle and
his executors — named Suwayd and ʿArfajah — took his estate and gave his wife and daughters nothing;
for in the *Jāhiliyyah* they did not let women or small children inherit, saying, "None is given
inheritance but he who fights on the back of a horse, thrusts with the spear, strikes with the sword and
gathers the spoils." Umm Kuḥḥah brought the matter to the Messenger of God ﷺ, and he summoned the two
men; they said, "O Messenger of God, her children ride no horse, bear no burden and do not wound an
enemy." He said, "Go away until I see what God brings down concerning them." Then God revealed this
verse, refuting them and annulling their claim — for the young heirs ought to have been more entitled to
the property than the grown, since they cannot work for themselves; they had inverted the rule.
**[Study Quran]** gives the occasion in the same shape — a widow with three daughters left destitute by
her husband's male heirs — and notes that in pre-Islamic Arabia only adult men were entitled to inherit;
the verse establishes that both men and women hold a fixed and mandatory share, with the detailed
apportionment following in vv. 11–13.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ibn Kathīr]** Saʿīd b. Jubayr and Qatādah said: the idolaters used to give adult
men a share of the inheritance and deprive women and children of it. Ibn Mardawayh reported that Jābir
said: Umm Kujjah came to the Messenger of God ﷺ and said, "O Messenger of God, I have two daughters whose
father has died, and they have nothing." So God revealed, "Unto the men a share of what parents and near
kin leave." Ibn Kathīr notes that everyone is equal in God's decision that they inherit, though their
shares differ according to the degree of kinship, whether one is a relative, a spouse or the like; and
he records Ibn ʿAbbās's statement (via al-Bukhārī in connection with the next verse) that its rulings are
still in force.

**Belief & reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** The Arabs in the *Jāhiliyyah*, in their arrogance and harshness,
would not let the weak inherit — neither women nor children — assigning inheritance to the strong males,
claiming they were the people of war, fighting and plunder. So the Merciful, Wise Lord willed to
legislate for His servants a law in which their men and women, their strong and their weak, stand equal;
and He placed a general statement before the detailed shares so that souls would settle upon it, the
detail coming after the summary when souls were already longing for it and the estrangement born of
vile custom had gone. "A share ordained" — apportioned by the All-Knowing, All-Wise; and because someone
might think the women and children have a share only out of great wealth, God added "be it little or
much." Blessed be God, the best of judges. **[Maʿārif]** draws the sūrah's thread: this is the first
verse that refutes the custom of the *Jāhiliyyah* under which women simply could not inherit; it declares
their entitlement to the share fixed by the Law and strictly forbids any attempt to reduce it.

**Cross-references.** "God instructs you concerning your children: for the male the equivalent of the
portion of two females" (4:11); "And to each We have appointed heirs of what parents and near kin leave"
(4:33); "Men shall have the benefit of what they earn, and women of what they earn" (4:32).


## 4:8

*"And when the division is attended by kinsfolk, orphans and the poor, provide for them from it, and
speak to them words of kindness."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The commentators differed about this verse: is its ruling established
(*muḥkam*) or abrogated (*mansūkh*)? One group said it is established. Ibn ʿAbbās said, "It is
established and is not abrogated" — meaning "and when the division is attended by kinsfolk." Ibrāhīm and
al-Shaʿbī said the same: it is established. Mujāhid said: it is obligatory upon the heirs, according to
what their own hearts are pleased to give. Others held it abrogated, and others referred it to the
bequest made at death.

**Rulings.** **[Qurṭubī]** God made plain that those who have no claim on the estate by inheritance and
yet attend the division — relatives, orphans and the poor — should be honored and not deprived: if the
estate is large, they should be given, and if it is land or little, apologies should be offered to them,
for a small gift that comes from little wealth carries a great reward — a single dirham may outrun a
hundred thousand. On this view the verse is established, as Ibn ʿAbbās said, and a group of the Successors
acted on it — ʿUrwah b. al-Zubayr among them — and Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī commanded it. But it is also
reported from Ibn ʿAbbās that the verse is abrogated by "God instructs you concerning your children: for
the male the equivalent of the portion of two females" (4:11), and Saʿīd b. al-Musayyib, Abū Mālik,
ʿIkrimah and al-Ḍaḥḥāk held that view. Qurṭubī's own weighing: the first is sounder, since the verse
makes plain the heirs' entitlement and recommends sharing with those who attend. **[Ibn Kathīr]** Al-
Bukhārī recorded from Ibn ʿAbbās that this verse was not abrogated; Ibn Jarīr recorded from him that it
still applies and should be implemented. Mujāhid said: it is required from those who have anything to
inherit, paid from whatever portions their hearts are satisfied to give away; and similar was reported
from Ibn Masʿūd, Abū Mūsā, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān b. Abī Bakr, Abū l-ʿĀliyah, al-Shaʿbī and al-Ḥasan. Ibn Sīrīn,
Saʿīd b. Jubayr, Makḥūl, Ibrāhīm al-Nakhaʿī, ʿAṭāʾ b. Abī Rabāḥ, al-Zuhrī and Yaḥyā b. Yaʿmar said the
payment is obligatory; others said it refers to the bequest at death; and others that it was abrogated.

**Reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** This is one of God's beautiful, mighty rulings, mending hearts. If the
relatives who cannot inherit, the orphans and the poor stand by while the estate is being divided, their
souls stretched toward it and their hearts fixed on it — soothe them with what does not harm you and
benefits them. From the sense of it, everyone who looks longingly at what is in a man's hands should be
given something from it where possible, as the Prophet ﷺ said, "When one of you is brought his food by
his servant, let him seat him with him; and if he will not seat him, let him hand him a morsel or two."
The Companions, when the first fruits of their trees ripened, would bring them to the Messenger of God ﷺ,
who would bless them and then look for the youngest child present and give it to him, knowing how eager
the child would be. All this is where giving is possible; where it is not — because the property belongs
to those under interdiction, or because something more important intervenes — the words of kindness are
the answer: send them away with a beautiful refusal, in speech neither coarse nor ugly.
**[Maʿārif]** Notes that this command is a moral obligation, and the beauty of the Qurʾānic arrangement
is that on one side stands the just rule — the nearer in kinship excludes the farther — and on the other
this concern for the disappointment of the excluded relative, so that a whole verse is given to how he is
to be treated. **[Study Quran]** records the debate and notes that many consider the verse still binding
though largely ignored.

**Cross-references.** "Eat of its fruit when it bears fruit and give its due on the day of harvest"
(6:141); "And in their wealth was a right for the beggar and the deprived" (51:19); "And speak to people
kindly" (2:83).


## 4:9

*"And let those fear [God] who, if they left behind them weak offspring, would be afraid for them; so
let them fear God and speak a fitting word."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** The commentators differed. One group said: "Let those fear" — let those who are
present with a man making a bequest beware of ordering him to scatter his wealth in bequests to people
who do not inherit; rather, let them tell him to keep his wealth for his children. If he were the one
making the bequest, he would be pleased that those present urged him to preserve his wealth for his
children and not to leave them dependent, weak and unable to manage. Ibn ʿAbbās said: this concerns a
man at the point of death whom one hears making a bequest that harms his heirs — God commanded the one
who hears it to fear God, to guide him aright, and to look after his heirs as he would have loved it to
be done for his own heirs if he feared they would be lost.

**Meaning.** **[Qurṭubī]** On the grammar: the *alif* of *li-yakhsha* is elided because the verb is
jussive in the imperative; Sībawayh holds that the *lām* of the command may not be dropped by analogy
with the prepositions except in poetic necessity, while the Kūfans allowed its elision with the jussive.
The object of *li-yakhsha* is omitted because the sense makes it clear, and *khāfū* is the answer to
*law* — as if to say, "had they left, they would have feared." As to the meaning, one group said: it is
an exhortation to guardians — do with the orphans what you would wish done with your children after you,
which is why God said immediately, "Those who consume the property of the orphans unjustly." Another
group said: it is addressed to all people, commanding them to fear God concerning orphans and concerning
people's children, even if they are not in their care, and to take the matter seriously.

**Rulings.** **[Saʿdī]** It was said this is an address to those present at the deathbed when the dying
man veers in his will: they are commanded to enjoin justice and fairness on him — the evidence being
"and let them speak a fitting word," a speech conforming to equity and kindness, telling the one who
wishes to make a bequest to think of his children as they would wish their own children to be treated
after them. It was also said to mean the guardians of the weak — the insane, the young and the feeble:
they should serve their religious and worldly interests as they would wish others to serve their own weak
offspring. "So let them fear God" in their guardianship over others, treating them in a way that contains
the fear of God — not humiliating them, standing over them, but binding them to the fear of God.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Maʿārif]** The verse addresses Muslims generally, exhorting them to make certain
that the inheritance reaches the deceased's children fully and fairly and to abstain from anything that
would reduce the children's share. Its general sense covers the case where a Muslim is seen disposing of
his property in a will in a way that would harm his children and other heirs: he must be stopped — just
as the Prophet ﷺ stopped Saʿd b. Abī Waqqāṣ from giving the whole, or half, of his property in charity
and allowed him only one third (Mishkat, chapter on bequests), because had a half or the whole gone to
charity the share of the heirs would have been consumed or curtailed. It also covers the guardians of
orphans: they are to protect the orphans' property and deliver it in full at maturity, never allowing
negligence to touch their trust; and if they wish others to deal well with their children after them,
they should deal that way with the children of others.

**Reflection.** **[Study Quran]** Those who divide an inheritance, or make their final bequests, should
keep their own minor children before their eyes and leave them provision sufficient for them; those
present at a deathbed should not encourage bequests that would significantly reduce what goes to the
children. Alternately, the verse tells those making bequests to think also of the orphan, since they
would fear for their own children were they to become orphans dependent on the kindness of others — and
it was revealed before the verses detailing fixed shares for children. **[Saʿdī]** ends the verse with
the tongue: the "fitting word" is the means by which the weak are protected, whether it is the counsel
that stops a father from disinheriting his children or the honest account given to a ward of what is
being preserved for him.

**Cross-references.** "And let not those who are miserly with what God has given them of His bounty
think it good for them" (3:180); "And when the relatives and the orphans and the poor are present at the
division" (4:8); the bequest rule of 2:180: "prescribed for you, when death approaches one of you, if he
leaves wealth, that he make a bequest to parents and near kin."


## 4:10

*"Truly, those who consume the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming fire into their bellies,
and they shall burn in a blazing flame."*

**Meaning.** **[Ṭabarī]** "Those who eat the property of the orphans unjustly" — that is, without right
— "only eat fire into their bellies" on the Day of Resurrection: by their eating the orphans' property
wrongly in this world, it becomes the fire of Hell for them; and they shall be made to enter the blaze by
that eating. Al-Suddī said: when a man rises having eaten the orphan's property unjustly, flame comes
out of his mouth, his ears, his nose and his eyes, so that whoever sees him knows him by it.
**[Jalālayn]** The eating of it is fire, because it leads there: they shall be exposed to — or, in
another reading, shall be made to enter — a blaze, an intense fire in which they will burn.

**Ḥadīth & āthār.** **[Ṭabarī]** and **[Qurṭubī]** transmit the report of Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī: the
Prophet ﷺ told us about the night of his journey, "I saw a people whose lips were like the lips of
camels, and those set over them were seizing their lips and thrusting a stone of fire into their mouths
that came out from below them...." *(weak — the report comes through Abū Hārūn al-ʿAbdī, whom the
tradition critics graded weak)*. **[Study Quran]** connects the verse to the same vision in which those
who wrongly consumed the property of orphans were seen with flames emerging from their bodies.

**Rulings & language.** **[Qurṭubī]** The verse was revealed, says Muqātil b. Ḥayyān, concerning a man of
Ghaṭafān called Marthad b. Zayd, who was guardian of his young nephew's property and consumed it. The
majority hold that its address is the guardians who eat what is not lawful for them from the orphan's
property; Ibn Zayd said it was revealed concerning the disbelievers who did not let women and children
inherit. Taking property by every means is called "eating" because eating is the purpose and through it
most things are destroyed; the bellies are mentioned specifically to show their degradation and to
censure them for the opposite of noble character. The consumed thing is called "fire" because of where it
leads — as in "I see myself pressing wine," meaning grapes — and it was also said that "fire" here means
the unlawful, because the unlawful brings the Fire, so God named it by its name.

**Belief & reflection.** **[Saʿdī]** This is the greatest threat that occurs concerning a sin,
indicating the heinousness and ugliness of consuming the property of orphans and that it necessitates
entry to the Fire — which shows that it is among the gravest of the major sins. God's mercy and pity for
the orphan is such that He equates the devouring of his property with the devouring of fire, and the
verse is the sūrah's seal on the whole passage on orphans' rights: the property guarded for the weak is
not food but a trust, and to swallow it is to swallow its punishment in the same act.
**[Maʿārif]** Many commentators read the metaphor — eating the orphan's property unjustly is like
stuffing one's belly with fire because that is what will happen to him in the hereafter. But those of
deeper insight, with proofs, do not support the presence of a figure of speech: whatever is eaten from
the orphan's property unlawfully is, in reality, nothing but fire, though it does not look like it at the
time — as one says that the matchbox in his hand is fire, or the arsenic is a killer; handling the box
does not burn the hand and handling the arsenic does not kill, yet a little friction and a little
swallowing show that the description was exactly true. The Qurʾān's general teaching supports this: deeds,
good and evil, are the trees whose fruits the doer shall taste.

**Cross-references.** "And do not approach the property of the orphan except in the best way" (6:152);
"Those who devour usury shall not rise except as one rises whom Satan has driven to madness by his
touch" (2:275); "On the Day when their tongues and their hands and their feet will bear witness against
them" (24:24); the hedge against mixing at 2:220.
