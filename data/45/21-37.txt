=21=

This verse demolishes one of the most dangerous assumptions that can take root in the human heart: the idea that good and evil are ultimately equivalent and that everyone meets the same meaningless end. The interrogative form, “Or do those who commit evil deeds simply think,” challenges the moral blindness of the Meccan pagans who saw no connection between conduct and consequence beyond this life.

The phrase “commit evil deeds” translates a word that implies actively earning and accumulating sin, a lifestyle of wrongdoing rather than an occasional lapse. These are people who not only deny the truth but also act corruptly, oppress others, and flout the moral law revealed by God. The heart of their error lies in the assumption that God will “make them equal—in their life and after their death—to those who believe and do good.” The pagans looked at the believers who were often poor, socially marginalised, and persecuted, and compared them with their own prosperous, influential selves. They concluded that either there is no God, or that God must favour them, or that all distinctions are erased in death and no final reckoning exists. This attitude led them to live without restraint. The verse refutes this assumption with a sharp exclamation: “How wrong is their judgment!” Their entire moral calculus is based on a false premise. They have judged the situation using only worldly eyes, measuring success in terms of wealth and power, while completely ignoring the reality of the Hereafter.

The mention of “in their life and after their death” explicitly encompasses the two phases of existence: the present world and the eternal life to come. The equality they assume does not even hold fully in this life, because the believer already possesses the inner peace, dignity, and divine support that the disbeliever lacks. But in the afterlife, the contrast will be absolute and irreversible.

This verse draws a clear line that runs through the entire Quran: human beings are not judged by their worldly status but by their faith and righteous deeds. The logic is irrefutable. If a just and wise Creator made the universe, He would not treat the obedient and the rebellious, the righteous and the corrupt, as if their choices made no difference. Such equality would be a form of injustice, and Allah is far above injustice. The verse is a powerful wake-up call to anyone who comforts themselves with the thought that “everyone is basically good” and that divine mercy will simply ignore wilful rejection and persistent evil without repentance. It insists on moral seriousness, reminding us that our choices carry eternal weight and that God’s justice will draw a clear distinction between two very different ways of life.

=22=

Building on the previous verse’s rejection of moral equality, this verse provides the ultimate theological foundation for the principle of just recompense. It begins with the affirmation that “Allah created the heavens and the earth for a purpose,” or in other renderings, “in truth” and with a profound wisdom. The creation of this vast, intricate, and orderly universe was not an aimless act of play or a product of random chance. To suggest that the cosmos emerged from nothing, developed its breathtaking complexity through blind processes, and will dissolve into nothing without any ultimate meaning, is to strip existence of all significance. The Quran insists that behind the universe stands a Creator who acts with deliberate intent. That intent, as this verse reveals, is directly linked to moral accountability. The purpose is articulated as: “so that every soul may be paid back for what it has committed.

” This is a clear statement that the world exists as a testing ground where human beings exercise free will, and that the final outcome will be a perfect and complete recompense for every deed. The word “soul” (nafs) emphasises individual accountability. No one will bear the burden of another, and no one will be rewarded for deeds they did not perform. The “paying back” is exact and proportionate. The last phrase, “And none will be wronged,” provides the ultimate assurance. Divine justice is absolute and free from any trace of oppression. No one will be punished for a sin they did not commit, nor will any good deed be diminished or forgotten. If a believer suffers in this world, that suffering is not overlooked; it will either expiate sins or be compensated with reward. If a disbeliever enjoys worldly prosperity, that fleeting enjoyment is the full extent of their reward for any good they may have done, and it will be of no avail on the Day of Judgment. This verse links the grand cosmic scale of creation to the intimate personal reality of every human being’s fate. It answers the deep human longing for justice. In a world where the wicked often prosper and the righteous often suffer, the assurance that ultimate justice will be delivered in the Hereafter is the anchor that prevents faith from capsizing. It also elevates human actions to cosmic significance. Every choice matters because the entire universe was created with a purpose that culminates in the judgement of those choices.

=23=

This verse contains one of the most psychologically penetrating diagnoses of disbelief in the entire Quran. The question “Have you seen those who have taken their own desires as their god?” is addressed to the Prophet, drawing his and our attention to a devastating spiritual pathology. The person described has not necessarily erected a physical idol. Instead, he has elevated his own hawa, his whims, passions, and personal inclinations, to the status of a deity. Whatever his desire dictates becomes his law. If he feels like doing something, he does it, regardless of whether it pleases or displeases God. If a divine command clashes with his personal preference, his desire wins.

This is a form of idolatry more subtle and widespread than the worship of stone statues, and it can afflict people in every age, including those who might outwardly profess faith. The consequence of this self-worship is then described: “And so Allah left them to stray knowingly.

” This is a terrifying sentence. When a person persistently and defiantly chooses his own desires over divine guidance, Allah withdraws His helping grace and allows that person to wander further into misguidance.

The phrase “knowingly” indicates that Allah does this with full knowledge of the person’s condition and as a just response to their choices. The sealing of “their hearing and hearts” and the covering of “their sight” are the spiritual consequences of their persistent rejection. They had ears but refused to listen to the truth, so their hearing became blocked to spiritual realities. They had hearts that could have understood and loved God, but they chose to fill those hearts with base desires, so a seal was placed upon them. They had eyes that could have seen the signs, but they averted them, so a cover veiled their sight.

The verse then asks, “Who then can guide them after Allah?” If the source of all guidance has abandoned them to their chosen path, who else could possibly rescue them? No prophet, no preacher, no book, no miracle can guide a person whom Allah has left to stray.

The final rhetorical question, “Will you all not then be mindful?” is a direct appeal to the listeners to reflect on this condition and to guard themselves against the subtle encroachment of desire-worship in their own lives. The verse is not just about the pagan Arabs; it is a mirror held up to every human being, warning against the moment when personal preference becomes the ultimate arbiter of right and wrong.

=24=

The surah now places before us the actual words and worldview of those who deny the resurrection. “And they argue” indicates that they did not keep their disbelief to themselves but actively promoted it and debated in support of it. Their claim is stark: “There is nothing beyond our worldly life.

” This is the creed of materialism, the belief that this physical existence is all there is. They saw no higher purpose, no afterlife, no divine judgement. Their observation of life cycles seemed to them to confirm this view: “We die; others are born.” They witnessed one generation passing away and another taking its place, and they inferred that this endless cycle of birth and death was the entire story. There was no return, no resurrection, no standing before God. Their conclusion, that “nothing destroys us but the passage of time,” reflects a deification of time itself. They attributed the decay, ageing, and eventual death of human beings to the impersonal force of time, rather than to the will of a Creator who gives life and decrees death. In their view, a long life simply wore a person out, and there was no intelligent agent behind it.

The verse then demolishes this entire worldview with a single devastating observation: “Yet they have no knowledge in support of this claim. They only speculate.

” This is a rational critique. Their materialist creed is not based on evidence or proof; it is built on guesswork and assumption. They cannot prove that there is nothing beyond death, because that would require infinite knowledge which no human possesses. Their denial of the afterlife is as much a leap of faith as any religious belief, but it is a leap into darkness rather than light. The verse exposes the intellectual arrogance of those who present their speculation as if it were established fact. This critique remains profoundly relevant in any age where scientific materialism claims that only the physical world is real and that consciousness, morality, and purpose are mere illusions produced by matter. The Quran insists that such claims are not the conclusion of rigorous inquiry but the unproven assumptions of a worldview that has already ruled out the divine. The believer is taught to recognise that the deniers of the Hereafter have no secure ground to stand on; they are clutching at guesses while rejecting the certainty that revelation provides.

=25=

This verse exposes the intellectual bankruptcy and moral stubbornness of the deniers when confronted with divine truth. The phrase “whenever Our clear revelations are recited to them” indicates a repeated pattern. The Prophet would recite the Quran, with its powerful arguments, its vivid descriptions of the resurrection, its appeals to reason and conscience, and its undeniable linguistic beauty. The pagan leaders had no rational or scriptural counter-argument to offer. Instead of engaging with the message, they resorted to a demand that they knew could not be met under normal circumstances. Their “only argument” was to say: “Bring our forefathers back, if what you say is true!” They asked for the impossible as a condition of belief. They wanted the Prophet to physically resurrect their deceased ancestors on the spot to testify to the truth of his message. This was not a sincere request for evidence; it was a derisive challenge designed to mock the Prophet and to rally their followers behind them. After all, they reasoned, if he could not bring back their dead fathers, then his claim of a future resurrection must be false. The request also carried an emotional charge. They were appealing to the memory and honour of their forefathers, whom the Prophet’s message implicitly condemned as polytheists who had gone astray. By asking for their return, they were saying: if your religion is true, let us hear it from our own respected ancestors, not from you. The Quran records this argument not to dignify it but to show the depths to which a person can sink when they are determined not to believe. The demand itself reveals a misunderstanding of the nature of divine testing. This life is the realm of faith, where belief in the unseen is required. The demand for direct physical proof that removes all need for faith is a refusal to play by the rules that God has established.

Moreover, even if their forefathers were brought back, the Quran states elsewhere that those whose hearts are sealed would still find excuses not to believe. The verse thus functions as a warning that those who insist on conditions for belief that God has not chosen to fulfil are simply exposing their own unwillingness to submit, not a deficiency in the evidence provided.

=26=

In response to the pagans’ mocking demand to bring back their forefathers, the Prophet is instructed to deliver a clear and systematic answer that reframes the entire discussion. The answer begins not with the resurrection of the dead in this world, but with the fundamental reality of life and death itself. “It is Allah Who gives you life” establishes that the very existence they take for granted is a gift from the Creator. They did not bring themselves into being, and the life they enjoy is entirely dependent on God’s will. This first fact should, if they were rational, lead them to acknowledge God’s power and their own dependence.

The second stage, “then causes you to die,” reminds them that death is also under divine control. They attributed death merely to the passage of time, but the Quran corrects this: death is not an impersonal force; it is a deliberate act of the Creator who appointed the term of every soul. The One who had the power to initiate life and the authority to decree death certainly has the power to do what comes next.

The third stage is the climax: “then will gather you all on the Day of Judgment, about which there is no doubt.” The gathering of all humanity for judgement is not a far-fetched fantasy; it is the logical completion of the sequence. The One who began the process will bring it to its intended conclusion.

The phrase “about which there is no doubt” asserts the absolute certainty of this event, regardless of human scepticism. The verse ends with the sombre note: “But most people do not know.” Their ignorance is not an excuse; it is a result of their failure to reflect, their arrogance, and their love of worldly life. They lack the deep, certain knowledge that comes from faith and spiritual insight. The Prophet’s response, therefore, does not indulge the demand for a miraculous display. Instead, it points to the observable facts of life and death as sufficient signs for those who are willing to see.

For the believer, this verse is a concise creed of resurrection. It traces the human journey from birth to death to resurrection in three simple phrases, anchoring each in the power and will of Allah. It provides a model for how to respond to sceptics: not by trying to produce the supernatural on command, but by directing attention to the signs of God’s power that are already present in the natural order.

=27=

After the rational argument for the resurrection, the surah now lifts the curtain on the ultimate reality that underpins everything. “To Allah alone belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth” is a declaration of absolute sovereignty.

The word “kingdom” (mulk) encompasses ownership, dominion, and authority. Everything in the universe, from the mightiest galaxy to the smallest atom, is under the unchallenged rule of its Creator. This truth is stated in the present tense, indicating that it is a current reality, not something that will only become true in the future. The implication is profound: even now, as the deniers strut arrogantly on the earth and imagine themselves to be powerful and independent, they are living within God’s kingdom, sustained by His provision, and subject to His laws. Their rebellion changes nothing about His ownership.

The verse then shifts to the future: “On the Day the Hour will be established, the people of falsehood will then be in total loss. ” The establishment of the Hour, the moment when the present order of the world is shattered and the resurrection begins, is the event that will make the hidden reality fully manifest. Until that moment, the people of falsehood may appear to be winning. Their wealth, power, and numbers may give an impression of success. But when the Hour is established, all their apparent gains will be exposed as a mirage.

The phrase “total loss” translates a word that means clear, manifest, undeniable loss. There will be no ambiguity, no possibility of appeal, and no way to recover what has been squandered. The loss is described as “total” because it encompasses everything: they lose the pleasures of this world, they lose their souls, they lose any hope of mercy, and they lose the eternal bliss that was offered to them but which they rejected. The verse serves as a stark reminder that the measure of success and failure cannot be taken from the temporary conditions of this life. True profit or loss is determined only when the Hour is established and every soul stands before its Lord. For the believers, this is a source of comfort and motivation. Their struggles, sacrifices, and the mockery they endure are not signs of failure; they are the temporary trials of people who hold fast to the truth in the kingdom of God.

=28=

The surah now paints a scene from the Day of Judgment that gives the chapter its name, Al-Jathiyah, meaning the Kneeling. The Prophet is addressed with the words “And you will see,” a form that makes the scene vividly present to his mind and to every subsequent reader. “Every faith-community” refers to all the nations, religious groups, and ideological factions that have existed throughout history. Each group that followed a particular creed or path, whether true or false, will be gathered together and will be found “on its knees. ” The image of kneeling conveys multiple meanings. It signifies submission, for on that Day every false claim of independence will collapse and all will be brought low before the majesty of God. It also indicates a state of fear, anticipation, and utter helplessness. No one will be standing proudly or moving about freely; all will be fixed in a posture that combines humility and dread.

The second part of the verse explains what will happen as they kneel. “Every community will be summoned to its record of deeds.” The record is the book in which every deed, word, and even intention has been meticulously recorded by the angels. This summoning is an act of perfect justice. Each community will face its own ledger, containing the specific history of what they believed and how they lived.

The third part of the verse is the divine pronouncement: “This Day you will be rewarded for what you used to do.” The reward here is comprehensive, encompassing both good and evil. The statement is addressed to all communities, believers and disbelievers alike. It confirms the principle of recompense that has been repeatedly emphasised in the surah.

The phrase “what you used to do” underscores that the judgement is based on their own actions, not on arbitrary decree. They are being given exactly what their hands sent ahead. The verse thus combines a terrifying image of universal abasement with the profound reassurance of absolute justice. No community is overlooked, no individual is forgotten, and the record is complete and accurate. For the believers, the kneeling is a prelude to being raised to honour, while for the deniers it is the posture of those who will be driven to their doom.

=29=

After stating that every community will be summoned to its record, this verse provides more detail about the nature of that record and the divine process behind it. The record is described as “This record of Ours,” a possessive form that connects it directly to God. It is not a human document that could contain errors, biases, or omissions. The record is divinely authored in the sense that it is produced under God’s command and by His appointed angels, and it perfectly reflects what He knows.

The phrase “speaks the truth about you” is striking. The record is personified as a witness that speaks with unerring truthfulness. On a day when human beings might try to deny their crimes or make excuses, the record will testify against them with absolute accuracy. The idea of a book speaking is a powerful metaphor that captures the complete exposure of every hidden deed. Nothing will remain concealed, and no defence lawyer will be able to challenge the testimony of this silent but eloquent witness.

The verse then reveals the background to this record: “Indeed, We always had your deeds recorded by the angels.” This statement explains how such a comprehensive and accurate record came to be. Throughout a person’s life, from the age of accountability until death, honourable angels are assigned to record every action. The Quran mentions in other places that two angels, one on the right and one on the left, note down deeds, and that not a word is uttered without a ready observer. The continuous recording means that no deed, however small or seemingly insignificant, is ever lost. A moment of sincere charity, a whispered prayer in the depth of night, a tear shed in repentance, as well as a secret act of dishonesty or a moment of arrogance, all are preserved.

The word “always” underscores the uninterrupted nature of this recording. There were no vacations, no lapses in attention. This verse serves a dual purpose.

For the believer, it is a source of comfort and motivation, knowing that good deeds are never forgotten and will be presented as evidence for a merciful reward. For the wrongdoer, it is a terrifying warning that the illusion of getting away with sin is just that, an illusion. The record is already being compiled, and on the Day of Kneeling it will speak nothing but the truth.

=30=

Having described the general scene of judgement, the surah now begins to separate the two groups whose ultimate fates were foreshadowed earlier. This verse focuses on the blessed outcome of the believers. The description “those who believed and did good” pairs faith with righteous action. Belief in the heart must manifest in the limbs; true faith is never an abstract mental assent divorced from conduct. The “good” they did encompasses all acts of worship, charity, kindness, honesty, and moral excellence performed in obedience to God and with sincerity. Their reward is described in the most beautiful terms: “their Lord will admit them into His mercy.

” The use of “their Lord” (rabbuhum) is especially tender. It reminds us that the One judging them is not a cold, distant magistrate but the Lord who created them, sustained them, guided them, and cares for them. The act of admission into “His mercy” is rich with meaning. Paradise itself is an embodiment of God’s mercy, and entering it is to be enveloped in divine compassion, pleasure, and nearness. The verse does not say they will merely enter a garden of physical delights; they will enter into mercy itself, which is the source of all the joys of the Hereafter. This mercy encompasses forgiveness of sins, elevation of ranks, and the greatest gift of all, the vision of Allah and His eternal good pleasure.

The verse then declares: “That is truly the absolute triumph.” The word “triumph” (fawz) indicates a massive success, a victory of the highest order. It is described as “absolute” or “great” to emphasise that no triumph in this world can compare to it. The wealthy person who gains the whole world but loses the Hereafter has achieved nothing. The believer who may have been poor, unknown, and persecuted in this life has, by attaining this mercy, won the only victory that matters in the end. This verse serves as the ultimate consolation and goal for the believers. It reorients the definition of success away from worldly standards and toward the eternal criteria of faith and righteous deeds. Every difficulty endured for the sake of God is reframed as a step on the path to the absolute triumph.

=31=

The surah now turns to the other group, the disbelievers, and presents the beginning of their confrontation with divine justice. The verse opens with the phrase “And as for those who disbelieved,” establishing a direct contrast with the believers of the previous verse. The disbelievers will not be silently cast into punishment. Instead, they will be addressed, and the address takes the form of a rebuking question that forces them to acknowledge their guilt.

The question is posed by Allah or by the angels under His command: “Were My revelations not recited to you?” The possessive “My revelations” reminds them that what they rejected was not the word of a mere human but the very speech of their Lord. The revelations included the Quranic verses and the signs in creation that pointed to the truth.

The question implies that they were given clear, sufficient, and repeated opportunities to believe. There was no injustice in their being held accountable, because the proof had been established against them.

The second part of the question exposes the two core sins that defined their response: “yet you acted arrogantly and were a wicked people?” Arrogance (istikbār) was their internal disease, the swollen pride that made them feel too important to submit to God and follow a messenger from among their own kind. Wickedness (fujūr) was their external behaviour, the immoral and corrupt lifestyle they led in defiance of divine commands. The combination of inner arrogance and outer wickedness is presented as the comprehensive reason for their condemnation.

The question is rhetorical, and the disbelievers will have no answer. They will not be able to claim ignorance, because the revelations reached them. They will not be able to claim that they were righteous people who simply made an honest mistake, because their arrogance and wickedness are manifest. The verse, though recounting a future event, serves as a powerful present warning. It invites the listener to imagine standing in that position and to examine whether any trace of the same arrogance or wickedness resides in their own heart. The call to repentance is implicit: turn back now before the question is asked and no answer can avail you.

=32=

Continuing the divine interrogation of the disbelievers on the Day of Judgment, this verse quotes their own words back to them, exposing the frivolous and dismissive attitude they adopted toward the most serious truth. The verse recalls how, in their worldly lives, they were repeatedly warned by sincere believers who told them, “Surely Allah’s promise of judgment is true and there is no doubt about the Hour.” This was the clear, unambiguous call of the prophets and their followers. They did not speak with hesitation or uncertainty. They affirmed with confidence that God’s promise of resurrection and judgement is absolutely true and that the Hour is an event about which there can be no legitimate doubt. The warning was delivered with love and urgency, intended to save them from eternal ruin. But the disbelievers responded with a mockery that revealed their deep-seated cynicism. Their reply, quoted verbatim in this verse, consisted of three dismissive statements. First, “We do not know what the Hour is!” This was not an honest admission of limited knowledge but a sarcastic dismissal, implying that the Hour was a meaningless concept, an undefined fantasy not worth taking seriously. Second, “We think it is no more than speculation.” They labelled the divinely revealed truth as mere conjecture, lumping the prophets’ message together with soothsayers’ guesses and philosophers’ theories. They refused to acknowledge the categorical difference between divine revelation and human speculation. Third, “and we are not convinced that it will ever come.” This was their final word: settled, obstinate disbelief. They were not open to evidence or argument; they had made up their minds that the whole idea was a myth. The verse, by placing these words in the context of the Day of Judgment, creates a devastating irony. Those who once mockingly claimed not to know what the Hour was now find themselves standing right in the middle of it, facing the very judgement they said they were not convinced would ever come. Their own words become the evidence against them.

The verse teaches believers the importance of taking divine warnings with absolute seriousness. It also provides a glimpse into the psychology of disbelief: it often clothes itself not in well-reasoned arguments but in a dismissive, mocking tone that seeks to make the truth seem ridiculous without ever genuinely engaging with it.

=33=

This verse describes the moment when the full reality of the disbelievers’ situation crashes upon them. The first part, “And the evil consequences of their deeds will unfold before them,” uses a verb that means to appear, become manifest, or be laid bare. Throughout their worldly lives, the true nature and full consequences of their sins were hidden from them. They saw their deeds in a flattering light, rationalised their wrongdoings, or simply never considered the ultimate outcome. But on that Day, the veil is torn away. The evil of what they committed will stand before them as a visible, undeniable reality. This unfolding is not merely an intellectual realisation; it is an overwhelming, experiential confrontation. They will see the dark forms of their sins, the punishments they have earned, and the terrible destination that awaits them.

The second part adds another layer: “and they will be overwhelmed by what they used to ridicule.” The very things they mocked, the resurrection, the judgement, the hellfire, the scales of justice, will descend upon them and completely encircle them.

The word translated “overwhelmed” conveys being surrounded, hemmed in, and engulfed from all sides with no possibility of escape. Their mockery is now repaid with terrifying reality.

This principle of divine justice, that the thing mocked becomes the source of punishment, is a recurring theme in the Quran. The people of Noah mocked the ark and were drowned. The people of Hud mocked the wind and were destroyed by it. The Meccans mocked the idea of hellfire and will be its fuel. The verse serves as a profound warning against the habit of ridiculing sacred things. In an age of widespread scepticism, it is common to hear jokes about religion, the afterlife, or divine commandments.

This verse reminds that such mockery is not harmless humour; it is an offence against truth that will one day turn back upon the mocker in the most horrible way. For believers who have been on the receiving end of such ridicule, this verse offers deep comfort. It assures them that the laughter of the mockers will be silenced and that justice will be served in a manner more complete than they can imagine.

=34=

With the record exposed and their mockery turned against them, the disbelievers now hear the sentence pronounced. The voice, whether of Allah or of the angels speaking by His command, delivers a judgement that is perfectly proportionate to their crime.

The opening phrase, “This Day We will neglect you as you neglected the meeting of this Day of yours,” is a masterpiece of divine justice. The word “neglect” (nansa) can be understood as leaving them in the torment, deliberately forgetting them in the sense of showing them no mercy, no attention, no relief. They will be abandoned to their suffering. The reason given is that they “neglected the meeting of this Day.” They lived as if this Day would never come. They ignored the warnings, pushed the thought of judgement out of their minds, and made no preparation for it. They treated the most certain event in their future as if it were a fantasy. Now, their neglect is returned to them in the form of divine neglect. The punishment is thus not arbitrary; it is the mirror image of their own attitude and actions. The sentence continues: “Your home will be the Fire. ” The word “home” (ma’wa) implies a permanent dwelling, a place of refuge and return. For them, the Fire will be the only shelter they have, an abode of unimaginable horror from which there is no moving out.

Finally, the verdict concludes with the shattering statement, “and you will have no helpers.” In the world, they relied on their wealth, their allies, their false gods, and their own cleverness. On that Day, every support will vanish. No intercessor will speak for them except by God’s leave, and they will have earned no such intercession.

The word “helpers” encompasses any kind of aid, defence, or rescue. They will stand utterly alone, utterly powerless, and utterly without hope. This verse encapsulates the Quranic teaching that the afterlife is a realm of absolute personal responsibility. The networks of support that people construct in this world, whether social, financial, or political, will dissolve entirely. The only thing that remains is one’s relationship with Allah.

For the believer, this is a call to prioritise that relationship above all else, so that on that Day, instead of neglect, they will be received with mercy and honour.

=35=

The verse continues the pronouncement of the sentence, now explicitly stating the causes of this dreadful fate. The phrase “This is because” introduces the reasons for the punishment, leaving no room for the condemned to claim that they are being treated unjustly. Two specific causes are named. First, “you made a mockery of Allah’s revelations. ” This echoes the earlier description of the disbelievers who, whenever they learned anything of God’s revelations, turned them into an object of jest. They did not merely disbelieve privately; they actively ridiculed the signs, the messengers, and the message. This mockery was a sin of the tongue and heart that compounded their disbelief and made their opposition aggressive rather than passive. Second, “and were deluded by your worldly life. ” The life of this world, with its pleasures, amusements, and apparent permanence, deceived them. They became so engrossed in the pursuit of wealth, status, and physical gratification that they forgot their ultimate purpose and their inevitable return to God. The delusion was so complete that they mistook the temporary for the eternal and the trivial for the vital. These two causes, mockery of truth and deception by the world, are deeply intertwined. A person who is deluded by the world will find the stern moral demands of revelation irksome and will mock them to justify ignoring them. A person who mocks revelation has already chosen the world as his ultimate value. After stating the causes, the verse delivers the final, irreversible judgement: “So from that Day on they will not be taken out of the Fire, nor will they be allowed to appease their Lord.” The punishment is eternal and unrelenting. There will be no parole, no eventual release.

The phrase “nor will they be allowed to appease their Lord” indicates that they will not be given the opportunity to make amends, to offer excuses, or to seek God’s pleasure through any further deeds. The time for action has ended; only recompense remains. This verse is one of the most severe in the Quran regarding the finality of the disbelievers’ fate, and it serves as the ultimate warning against treating the faith lightly or allowing worldly life to become one’s god. It calls every listener to a radical re-evaluation of priorities before the door of opportunity closes forever.

=36=

The surah, having traversed the entire arc of creation, revelation, warning, judgement, and the contrasting fates of the righteous and the wicked, now concludes with a majestic doxology. The opening phrase, “So all praise is for Allah,” uses the comprehensive word al-ḥamd, which encompasses praise, gratitude, and the acknowledgment of absolute perfection.

The placement of this phrase after the detailed descriptions of divine justice is deeply meaningful. The believer, having witnessed in the preceding verses the perfect wisdom of God in guiding some and leaving others astray, the absolute justice of His judgement, and the mercy bestowed on the faithful, can only respond with total praise. Even the punishment of the wicked is part of the perfection of divine governance, for it upholds justice and vindicates the truth. Allah is then described with a threefold lordship: “Lord of the heavens and Lord of the earth, Lord of all worlds.” This triadic description emphasises the comprehensive scope of His dominion. “Lord of the heavens” affirms that He is the Creator, Sustainer, and Ruler of everything in the celestial realm, from the mightiest angel to the most distant star. “Lord of the earth” affirms His sovereignty over the human realm, the animal kingdom, the mountains, oceans, and all that lies within the planet. “Lord of all worlds” expands the scope even further to include every realm of existence, seen and unseen, known and unknown.

The word “worlds” (ʿālamīn) is a plural that encompasses all categories of creation: the world of angels, the world of jinn, the world of humans, the world of animals, and whatever other creations exist beyond our knowledge. This verse serves both as a doctrinal affirmation and as a formula of worship. It teaches that the proper human response to the entire narrative of existence, with its tests, its prophets, its scriptures, and its final judgement, is to praise the Lord of it all. The believer is thus invited to make this verse his own utterance, joining the angels and all of creation in declaring the praise of the One Who holds the heavens, the earth, and all worlds in His complete and merciful authority. It is a fitting culmination, lifting the heart from the fear of judgement to the adoration of the Judge.

=37=

The surah closes with a verse of immense weight and beauty that summarises the attributes of God that have been most relevant to the surah’s themes. “To Him belongs all Majesty in the heavens and the earth” uses the Arabic word al-kibriyā’, which is often translated as majesty, grandeur, pride, or supreme greatness. It is an attribute that belongs exclusively to God. In a sacred hadith, Allah says, “Majesty is My cloak and grandeur is My garment; whoever competes with Me in either of these, I will cast him into the Fire. ” This indicates that any human claim to ultimate greatness, any arrogance, any swollen sense of self-importance, is a direct encroachment upon a divine attribute. The surah has repeatedly condemned arrogance, the istikbār of the deniers who refused to submit to the truth. This closing verse reminds us why human arrogance is such a grave sin: it is an attempt to seize what belongs to God alone. True majesty, true greatness, is entirely His, in the heavens and in the earth. No human being, no matter how powerful, wealthy, or admired, possesses an atom of real majesty in comparison to the Creator.

The verse then concludes with two names that opened the surah in verse two: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” This deliberate echo creates a frame around the entire chapter. The Almighty (al-ʿAzīz) is the One Whose power is absolute and unassailable. The surah has demonstrated this power through the signs in creation, the subjection of the heavens and the earth, the giving of life and death, and the establishment of the Hour and the judgement. The All-Wise (al-Ḥakīm) is the One Whose every action, command, and decree is founded on perfect wisdom. The surah has shown this wisdom in the sending of scriptures, the appointment of prophets, the testing of nations, the rewarding of the faithful, and the punishing of the wicked. By ending on these two names, the surah brings the listener full circle, back to the foundational truth announced at the beginning: the revelation of this Book is from the Almighty, All-Wise. Everything that has been said in between, every warning and promise, every scene of judgement and mercy, every call to reflection and gratitude, flows from and points back to the reality that God possesses absolute power and perfect wisdom. The listener is left with a profound sense of awe, an urge to praise, and a renewed determination to live as one who believes, does good, and hopes for entry into the mercy of the Lord of all worlds.
