=21=

After warning the rebellious of the eternal Fire, Allah now reveals a profound aspect of His mercy even within the framework of punishment. "We will certainly make them taste some of the minor torment" refers to afflictions and hardships that strike in this worldly life before the great, overwhelming torment of the Hereafter. This "minor torment" takes many forms: it could be a humiliating defeat in battle as happened at Badr, a severe famine that gripped Mecca for years, illnesses, loss of wealth, or the torment of the grave which is a foretaste of the afterlife. The purpose is not mere vengeance but is made beautifully clear in the next phrase: "so perhaps they will return.

" The Arabic word la'allahum carries the meaning of hope and expectation. The worldly punishment serves as a divine wake-up call, a merciful jolt designed to shake the heedless soul out of its deep slumber. When a person suffers, the false supports of this world crumble, and they are more likely to turn back to their Lord in humility and repentance. The verse thus frames even calamities as opportunities for correction.

The phrase "before the major torment" underscores the urgency: the current life is a limited window of probation. If the minor warning is not heeded, what follows will be infinitely more severe and inescapable. This balance between warning and hope demonstrates that Allah desires reformation, not destruction, and it encourages believers to view their own trials as potential means of purification and return to the right path.

=22=

This verse poses a rhetorical question that identifies the height of human injustice. "And who does more wrong" invites reflection: there is no person more unjust and self-destructive than the one "who is reminded of Allah’s revelations then turns away from them." To be reminded of the verses of the Quran, the signs in creation, or the admonitions of the prophets is to be given the most valuable gift—a clear map to truth and salvation.

Yet this person deliberately ignores it, as if the reminder had never reached his ears. He does not even engage, refute, or consider; he simply turns away with disdain, busying himself with worldly distractions. This action is a direct violation of the very purpose of human faculties, especially the intellect which was given to discern truth.

The verse then declares the inevitable consequence: "We will surely inflict punishment upon the wicked." Here, "the wicked" translates al-mujrimun, those who are steeped in crime against their own souls through persistent rejection and sin. The punishment is certain and decreed by divine justice, matching the severity of their crime. This verse serves as a powerful reproach to anyone who habitually hears the Quran and the call to faith without allowing it to penetrate their heart. It underscores that the greatest wrong is not against other people but against one's own soul by cutting it off from the source of eternal life, and it leaves no room for excuses on the Day when accounts are settled.

=23=

In the face of constant rejection and mockery, the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, is comforted with a reference to a previous messenger. "Indeed, We gave the Scripture to Moses" reminds him that divine revelation is an established pattern. The Torah was a comprehensive book sent down to a great prophet, just as the Quran is now being sent down. This historical parallel serves a dual purpose: it strengthens the Prophet’s heart and challenges the disbelievers’ claim that it is strange for God to reveal a book to a man. The parenthetical statement "so let there be no doubt that you too are receiving revelations" is directed at the Prophet and, by extension, at all who hear the Quran.

The Arabic construction can also be understood as "so be not in doubt about the meeting with Moses" or "about the reception of the Quran," but the clearest meaning is that just as Moses was truly a recipient of revelation, so is Muhammad. The verse then says, "and We made it a guide for the Children of Israel." The Torah was a source of light and direction for the Israelites, containing laws, wisdom, and prophecies about the final messenger. By mentioning this, Allah hints that the Quran holds an even more universal and complete guidance. The verse weaves together the comfort of prophetic brotherhood with the certainty of the Quran's divine origin, encouraging the believers to stand firm in their conviction despite the noise of the deniers.

=24=

Continuing the reference to the Children of Israel, this verse explains how they were led after Moses. "We raised from among them leaders" translates the word a'immah, which means spiritual guides and exemplars who lead others to truth by divine appointment. These leaders could refer to the prophets sent among the Israelites after Moses, or to the righteous scholars and devout men who upheld the Torah. Their defining quality was "guiding by Our command," meaning their guidance was not based on personal opinion or worldly interest but was rooted in the divine instructions they received.

The verse then reveals the two essential qualities that qualified them for this exalted position: "when they patiently endured and firmly believed in Our signs. " Patience, or sabr, was required to face the trials of leadership, the stubbornness of their people, and the hardships of upholding truth in a hostile environment. Their firm belief in the signs of Allah, meaning both the revealed verses and the signs in the cosmos, gave them unshakeable conviction. This verse provides a timeless principle for leadership in faith: it is not inherited or achieved by ambition, but is a divine trust given to those whose hearts are filled with patience and certainty. It was a subtle reminder to the Quraysh that true leaders are not the wealthy chiefs but those who embody these spiritual traits, and it serves as a model for all who seek to guide others toward righteousness.

=25=

This verse offers a decisive conclusion to the discussion about the People of the Book and the disagreements that arose among them after receiving guidance. "Indeed, your Lord will decide between them" points to a future, definitive judgment. The "differences" include the theological disputes that fractured the Israelites into sects, their arguments over the truth of the Torah, and their conflicting positions regarding the Prophet Muhammad and the Quran. In this world, these disagreements can cause confusion, strife, and even doubt in the hearts of believers who witness the bitter divisions among those who claim to follow earlier scriptures. But the verse assures the Prophet and his followers that these disputes are not permanent and will not remain unresolved. On the Day of Judgment, Allah, the All-Knowing, will render His perfect verdict, making clear who was right and who was wrong, rewarding the faithful and punishing the deviant.

This promise of final arbitration brings immense peace to the believer’s soul. It teaches that one should not become overly distressed by the endless arguments of this life, for the ultimate justice lies with the Lord. The verse thus redirects the focus from earthly polemics to the certainty of the Hereafter, and it serves as a warning to those who knowingly distort divine truth, for their judgment is inescapable and will be based on perfect knowledge.

=26=

The surah now appeals directly to the disbelievers’ own experience and observations. "Is it not yet clear to them" is a question filled with wonder at their persistent heedlessness. The evidence is right before their eyes: "how many peoples We destroyed before them." The Arabian Peninsula was littered with the ruins of vanished civilizations—the 'Ad, the Thamud, the people of Lot—whose once-proud dwellings lay desolate along the trade routes the Quraysh traveled regularly to Syria and Yemen. "Whose ruins they still pass by" emphasizes that these are not distant, unfamiliar histories but tangible, visible remnants they encounter in their daily and seasonal journeys. They walk through the empty homes and abandoned cities of those who denied their messengers, seeing the physical proof of divine punishment with their own eyes. "Surely in this are signs," clear indications pointing to the consequences of rejecting truth.

The verse concludes, "Will they not then listen?" The call to listen indicates that the stories of the past carry spoken warnings as well; travelers relate these histories, and the ruins themselves seem to speak of the fate of arrogance.

The question implies that their failure is not a lack of intellect but a refusal to pay attention and draw the obvious conclusion that the same Lord who destroyed those peoples can just as easily hold them to account.

=27=

Moving from the historical sign of destruction to the natural sign of revival, the verse directs their attention to the earth beneath their feet. "Do they not see how We drive rain to parched land" paints a picture of a barren, lifeless terrain that appears utterly dead, cracked, and devoid of vegetation. The driving of rain is attributed directly to Allah, showing His complete control over the life-giving water. When the rain descends, the "parched land" is transformed, "producing various crops." The diversity of crops—grains, fruits, vegetables—emerges from what was just dry dust, a manifest sign of resurrection.

The phrase "from which they and their cattle eat" reminds them that their very sustenance and the survival of their livestock depend on this process, so they cannot pretend to be indifferent to it. The verse then asks, "Will they not then see?" Here the call is to insight and deeper understanding, not just physical sight. The parallel is striking: just as dead earth is revived by water, dead humans will be revived by their Lord for judgment.

This is an argument they witness annually, yet they fail to make the connection. The verse exposes the inconsistency of those who believe in the natural cycle of life from dead soil but deny the divine power to resurrect human beings from their graves, making their denial all the more irrational.

=28=

The surah returns to the direct speech of the deniers, capturing their skeptical and impatient tone. "They ask mockingly" reveals the spirit behind the question; it is not a sincere search for knowledge but a challenge dripping with sarcasm. The phrase "Day of Decision" refers to the Day of Judgment, which is described as the Day of Opening or Victory in other parts of the Quran—a day when all matters will be finally decided between truth and falsehood, and between the believers and the disbelievers. Their demand, "When is this Day of Decision, if what you say is true?" is designed to pressure the Prophet into naming a specific date. They believe that the failure to provide a date proves the whole warning is a bluff. This was a common tactic of the Quraysh, who thought that since the punishment had not arrived immediately, it never would. They confused divine forbearance with weakness or falsehood.

The question reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of the purpose of the warning: the exact timing is deliberately hidden to keep people in a state of readiness and to test their faith. By mocking the unseen, they only compound their guilt, and this verse sets the stage for the sharp reply that follows, exposing their short-sightedness and the futility of their demand.

=29=

In answer to their mocking inquiry, the Prophet is instructed to deliver a response that cuts to the heart of the matter. "Say, O Prophet" signals a direct divine command on how to address the challenge. The response begins by focusing not on the "when" but on the "what" of that Day: "On the Day of Decision it will not benefit the disbelievers to believe then." This reveals the tragic irony of their demand. They ask for the Day as if they would then accept the truth, but when that Day actually arrives, the door of faith will be slammed shut. Belief that comes only after seeing the reality of punishment with one's own eyes is not true faith; it is forced acknowledgment that carries no merit and brings no salvation.

The verse adds, "nor will they be delayed from punishment." Their earlier request for the Day to come quickly will be met with a terrifying reality: once it begins, there is no postponement, no second chance, and no respite. The punishment will overtake them immediately. This reply is a powerful psychological reversal. Their mockery about the delay is answered with the warning that the delay is their only window of mercy; once it closes, their regret will be eternal and their wished-for "Decision" will be their ruin. The verse underscores the urgency of seizing the present moment for genuine repentance before it is too late.

=30=

The surah ends with a concise and powerful instruction to the Prophet that captures the entire attitude he must adopt. "So turn away from them" does not mean abandoning his mission or ceasing to convey the message. Rather, it means to withdraw his heart from their insults, their endless arguments, and their mockery. He is to stop allowing their rejection to cause him personal distress, for he has fulfilled his duty of delivering the clear warning. The command is a form of emotional and spiritual detachment from the hostility of the stubborn disbelievers, leaving their affair to Allah.

The second part, "and wait!" is a call to patient anticipation of Allah's decree. The Prophet is to wait for the promised victory for the truth or for the divine judgment upon those who reject it.

The verse concludes, "They too are waiting." This sets up a powerful contrast. The disbelievers are waiting, hoping for the Prophet's downfall, for the movement to fizzle out, or for some misfortune to befall him. But what they wait for is fundamentally different from what the Prophet waits for. He waits for the fulfillment of Allah's promise of help and justice. This closing verse, like many at the end of Meccan surahs, offers profound comfort. It assures the believers that the final outcome is in Allah's hands, and that the apparent delay is not neglect. The silent wait itself becomes a form of strength, leaving the deniers to their own doom while the faithful rest in the certainty of their Lord’s plan.
