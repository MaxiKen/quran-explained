=61=

After the warning to those who fabricate lies against Allah, this verse turns to the all-encompassing nature of divine observation. The address begins with the Prophet and then expands to include all people. “There is no activity you may be engaged in O Prophet or portion of the Quran you may be reciting, nor any deed you all may be doing except that We are a Witness over you while doing it.” The three categories cover the entire spectrum of human action: the Prophet’s personal affairs, his recitation and delivery of the Qur’an, and every deed performed by all of humanity. In each case, Allah declares Himself as a direct Witness at the very moment the action is being done.

This is not a future observation or a recorded memory; it is a present, ongoing witnessing. The phrase “We are a Witness” uses the plural of majesty, emphasising the grandeur and authority of the Observer. This immediate divine presence should transform how a believer views every moment of life. There is no hidden corner where a person can act unseen by the Lord.

The verse then intensifies the description: “Not even an atom’s weight is hidden from your Lord on earth or in heaven.” An atom, or a tiny particle, represents the smallest conceivable thing. Nothing of that minuscule size escapes His knowledge, whether it is deep within the earth or in the farthest reaches of the sky. The verse goes further: “nor anything smaller or larger than that.” If something smaller than an atom exists, it is known; and obviously everything larger is known. All of it “is written in a perfect Record.” This refers to the Preserved Tablet, the al-Lawḥ al-Maḥfūẓ, in which every detail of creation, every event, and every action is inscribed with absolute accuracy and permanence.

For the believer, this verse brings a dual awareness: immense comfort and profound accountability. Comfort arises because no good deed, no silent tear, no secret struggle is ever lost; it is witnessed and recorded by the Most Merciful. Accountability arises because no sin, no hidden intention, no whispered plot goes unnoticed.

The verse also underscores the divine origin of the Qur’an, for only the One whose knowledge encompasses all atoms could reveal a scripture of such depth. It invites every person to live with the consciousness of being constantly under the loving but just gaze of their Creator, a consciousness that purifies actions and aligns intentions.

=62=

Having established the all-encompassing knowledge and witness of Allah, the verse now highlights a special category of people who fully internalise this reality and live accordingly. “There will certainly be no fear for the close servants of Allah, nor will they grieve.

” The phrase “close servants” translates awliyā’ Allāh, often rendered as the friends or allies of Allah. These are individuals who have drawn near to Him through sincere faith, devotion, and righteous deeds. The promise given to them is comprehensive and absolute. “No fear” means they will be safe from whatever terrifies others on the Day of Judgment and from any ultimate harm. The fears that plague humanity—fear of the future, of death, of punishment, of loss—will be lifted from them because they have placed their trust entirely in the One who controls all outcomes. “Nor will they grieve” means they will not suffer the sorrow and regret that comes from missed opportunities, lost worldly possessions, or past mistakes, because Allah’s mercy has enveloped them and their reward surpasses anything they could have lost. The verse uses a strong negation “certainly no fear” (allā khawfun) to emphasise the unshakeable nature of this security.

This promise was a source of profound hope for the early Muslims who faced persecution, poverty, and social ostracism. It assured them that their proximity to Allah would translate into eternal peace. Importantly, being a close servant is not limited to prophets or a select few; the door is open to anyone who fulfills the characteristics mentioned in the next verse. In a world filled with anxiety, depression, and existential dread, this verse offers the ultimate solution. The path to fearlessness and freedom from grief lies in becoming a close servant of Allah. That closeness is cultivated through consistent worship, love of Allah, and alignment of one’s will with His commands. The verse calls every believer to aspire to this station, to aim not merely for salvation but for a special nearness that guarantees complete inner peace, both in this life and the next.

=63=

This verse defines the close servants of Allah mentioned previously by outlining their two core qualities. “They are those who are faithful” translates alladhīna āmanū, those who have true and deep faith. This faith is not a mere verbal claim or a cultural identity; it is a sincere conviction in the heart that Allah is the only Lord, that Muhammad is His messenger, and that all that revelation contains is true. This faith is dynamic and constantly affirmed through acts of worship, reflection, and obedience.

The second quality is “and are mindful of Him,” which translates wa kānū yattaqūn, and they have taqwa. Taqwa is a comprehensive concept that involves being constantly aware of Allah, fearing His displeasure, and taking practical steps to avoid what He has forbidden. It is a state of heightened spiritual consciousness where a person guards their thoughts, speech, and actions because they know Allah is watching. The combination of faith and taqwa is the essence of the Islamic spiritual path. Faith provides the foundation of belief, while taqwa builds a protective barrier around that faith, preventing sins from corroding the heart. These two qualities are not separate; genuine faith inevitably produces mindfulness, and mindfulness strengthens and deepens faith. The close servants of Allah are not distinguished by wealth, lineage, or outward status, but by this internal reality. This democratises the concept of sainthood in Islam; any man or woman, rich or poor, known or unknown, can be among the awliyā’ if they embody true faith and taqwa.

The verse also provides a practical checklist for self-assessment. A person can ask themselves daily: Is my faith alive and deepening? Am I truly mindful of Allah in my private moments as well as public? The progression in these verses is beautiful: first the promise of no fear and no grief, then the description of the people who receive it. It shows that such security is not arbitrary but the logical fruit of a life rooted in faith and mindfulness. This clarity invites every believer to walk the path, knowing exactly what is required to earn the title of Allah’s close servant.

=64=

The description of the close servants’ reward continues with the announcement of glad tidings. “For them is good news in this worldly life and the Hereafter.” The good news in this worldly life, as explained by the Prophet (peace be upon him) and the companions, includes the sincere dreams that bring comfort and guidance, the love and respect that Allah places for them in the hearts of the believers, and the inner peace and contentment that comes from a life of faith. It also includes the glad tidings of a good end and the assurance felt in the heart about Allah’s mercy. The good news in the Hereafter is the grand reception at death, the angels’ greeting of peace, the light on the Day of Judgment, and the ultimate entry into Paradise. This dual blessing means that a close servant is not waiting for the afterlife to experience the fruits of their faith; glimpses of divine favour begin in this very world.

The verse then adds a powerful assurance: “There is no change in the promise of Allah.” Allah’s words are immutable. What He has promised will absolutely come to pass, without alteration or delay. No external force can prevent it, and Allah does not go back on His word. This provides an anchor for the soul, especially during times when outward circumstances appear contrary to the promise.

The verse concludes, “That is truly the ultimate triumph. ” The triumph is not a temporary worldly victory but an everlasting success that encompasses safety, joy, and divine pleasure. This statement reframes the entire concept of winning and losing. The ultimate triumph is not about accumulating wealth, fame, or power, but about being among those who receive Allah’s good news and achieve His eternal approval.

For the believer, this verse turns the heart towards optimism and hope, while reinforcing the importance of steadfastness. Every effort made in the path of faith and taqwa is a step towards this comprehensive good news, and no sacrifice for the sake of Allah is ever wasted. The verse encourages a life of joyful anticipation, constantly looking forward to the fulfilment of the divine promise.

=65=

After outlining the beautiful destiny of the close servants, the verse addresses the Prophet directly with a comforting instruction. The Prophet faced a relentless barrage of verbal abuse, mockery, slander, and rejection. The disbelievers called him a magician, a poet, and a madman. They cast doubt on his prophethood and insulted the message he carried. Such words naturally cause grief to a sincere heart, and the Prophet deeply felt the pain of their rejection and the weight of their accusations. The command “Do not let their words grieve you” acknowledges his humanity while directing him towards a higher perspective. The reason he should not grieve is immediately provided: “Surely all honour and power belongs to Allah.

” The word ‘izzah means honour, power, and dignity. All of it belongs to Allah entirely. The disbelievers’ words cannot diminish the Prophet’s true honour, which is derived from his connection to Allah, nor can their arrogance overpower the divine will. Their mockery is a sign of their own spiritual bankruptcy, not of any defect in the truth.

The verse then adds: “He is the All-Hearing, All-Knowing. ” Allah hears every word they utter and knows every intention behind them. Nothing escapes His attention. This means that the Prophet is not alone in facing the insults; Allah is fully aware, and He will deal with them justly. The Prophet need not respond to every taunt or let it consume his heart, because the ultimate Judge is listening and recording. This verse provides a profound lesson in emotional resilience for every believer. In a world where people of faith are often ridiculed, misrepresented, or pressured to conform, it is natural to feel grief and frustration. But the remedy is to remember that honour belongs to Allah, and He grants it to whom He wills. The words of opponents can only harm us if we let them shake our connection to the source of all honour. By turning to the All-Hearing and All-Knowing, we place our grievances in His hands, freeing our hearts to continue our mission with peace and dignity.

=66=

This verse reinforces the theme of Allah’s absolute sovereignty as a further consolation and a rebuttal. “Certainly to Allah alone belong all those in the heavens and all those on the earth.” Every being, whether angels in the sky or humans and jinn on earth, is under Allah’s ownership, control, and authority. The polytheists worship created beings, but those very beings belong to Allah. The angels they venerate, the prophets they deify, the idols they carve—all are His property and serve Him. This makes their worship of anything else utterly irrational.

The verse then poses a piercing question: “And what do those who associate others with Allah really follow?” It invites an honest examination of the foundation of polytheism.

The answer follows immediately: “They follow nothing but assumptions and do nothing but lie.” Their belief system is not built on revelation, reason, or sensory evidence; it is based entirely on conjecture, inherited customs, and groundless speculations.

The word “assumptions” indicates that they have no firm knowledge, only guesses and wishful thinking. The phrase “do nothing but lie” points to their active fabrication of falsehoods about Allah, claiming that He has partners or that their idols possess divine powers. This double indictment strips polytheism of any intellectual legitimacy. For the Prophet, this was a reminder that the opposition he faced was built on emptiness; he was not up against a formidable alternative truth, but against illusions and falsehoods.

For the believer today, this verse provides clarity when confronting various forms of shirk, whether it be the worship of idols, saints, ideologies, or the self. The foundations of all such deviations are assumptions and lies.

In contrast, true faith rests on certainty from revelation and sound reason. The verse also warns against following cultural norms without verification, as many people float through life on a sea of assumptions, never anchoring themselves to the truth. It calls every person to examine the basis of their beliefs: are they grounded in Allah’s revelation or in human conjecture?

=67=

After the abstract discussion of sovereignty and falsehood, the verse returns to tangible signs in creation that everyone can observe. “He is the One Who has made the night for you to rest in and the day bright.” The night is described with a purpose: rest. The darkness naturally slows down activity, encourages sleep, and provides a daily break from the exertions of life. It is a mercy from Allah that aligns with human biology. The day is “bright,” meaning illuminated, allowing people to see, work, earn a living, and engage in their various pursuits. The alternation is so perfectly balanced that life on earth flourishes. If the night were permanent, life would freeze; if the day were constant, exhaustion would destroy all creatures. The One who established this cycle is the same One who owns all in the heavens and the earth, and who will hold all to account.

The verse ends: “Surely in this are signs for people who listen.” Listening here implies more than hearing sounds; it means attentive, thoughtful listening that leads to understanding and reflection. Those who truly listen to the message of the Qur’an and to the silent testimony of creation will find clear signs pointing to the Creator’s wisdom and the truth of the Hereafter. The alternation of night and day is a sign that the universe is not a random accident but a designed system. It also hints at the possibility of resurrection: the One who brings the day to life after the death-like state of night can bring the dead back to life. This verse calls the believer to pause and reflect on the most ordinary phenomena, which are, in reality, extraordinary blessings. Every sunset and sunrise becomes a reminder of Allah’s power and mercy. In a busy world, taking a moment to listen—to truly listen—to these signs can renew faith and gratitude, grounding the heart in the reality of the Creator’s constant care.

=68=

The surah now addresses a specific form of the falsehood mentioned earlier: the claim that Allah has children. This claim was made by the polytheists who said the angels were Allah’s daughters, and by the Jews and Christians who said Ezra and Jesus are the son of God respectively. The verse quotes their assertion: “Allah has offspring.” The immediate response is a declaration of divine transcendence: “Glory be to Him! ” Subḥānahu is an exclamation that declares Allah free from all imperfections and from any need for offspring.

The concept of offspring implies a need for continuity, a sharing of nature, and a bodily origin, all of which are impossible for the Eternal Creator. The verse then provides the reason: “He is the Self-Sufficient.” Al-Ṣamad, here rendered as Self-Sufficient, is the One who needs nothing and no one, while all creation is in need of Him. Offspring are desired to assist, carry on a legacy, or provide comfort; Allah is far above such needs. “To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth.” This ownership further underscores why He has no need for a child. A child would be an heir or a partner; but Allah already owns everything, so what would a child add?

The verse then issues a sharp challenge: “You have no proof of this!” Their claim is baseless; no revelation ever sanctioned it, and no rational proof supports it. It is a pure fabrication.

The verse concludes with a rhetorical question that is also a warning: “Do you say about Allah what you do not know? ” Speaking about Allah without knowledge is one of the gravest sins. It combines ignorance with audacity. This verse is fundamental in purifying the concept of God from pagan corruptions. It establishes that Allah’s perfection necessitates His being free from any need for offspring.

For believers, it is a reminder to speak about Allah only with what He has revealed, and to refrain from speculating on matters of the unseen. The verse also serves as an invitation to the People of the Book to correct their doctrines, aligning with the pure monotheism proclaimed by all prophets.

=69=

After exposing the baselessness of claiming offspring for Allah, the Prophet is instructed to deliver a definitive verdict. The command “Say” personalises the announcement. “Indeed, those who fabricate lies against Allah will never succeed.

” The phrase “fabricate lies against Allah” covers all false claims about Allah, including the attribution of partners, children, or any statement that contradicts His revelation. The declaration that they will “never succeed” is absolute and unqualified. This success is not limited to the worldly sphere, where they might appear to thrive temporarily, but refers to the ultimate and eternal success. In the eternal scale, those who invent falsehoods about the Almighty will be the complete losers, even if they accumulate power, wealth, and followers in this brief life.

The use of “never” closes the door on any possibility of their eventual triumph. This verse is a powerful comfort for the believers who may feel outnumbered or marginalized by those who spread false ideologies and religious distortions. It reassures that the arc of justice bends inevitably towards the divine truth, and the fabricators will not succeed when it matters most—on the Day of Judgment. It also serves as a dire warning to anyone who, whether intentionally or through negligence, spreads untruths about Allah and His religion. This includes inventing doctrines, justifying practices without evidence, or misinterpreting scripture to suit personal desires. The verse calls believers to a deep commitment to truthfulness in matters of faith, ensuring that everything they attribute to Allah is firmly based on authentic revelation. When we encounter falsehoods being propagated, we can maintain inner peace, knowing that their apparent spread is not real success, and their ultimate failure is decreed by the Lord of truth.

=70=

This verse explains the limits of whatever worldly gains the fabricators of lies may acquire. “It is only a brief enjoyment in this world.” Their life of comfort, power, or pleasure is fleeting, measured in a handful of years or decades. Compared to eternity, it is vanishingly small.

The word “brief” (matā‘un qalīl) underscores the insignificance of whatever they enjoy. It is like a short trip or a small provision that quickly runs out.

The verse then draws the line connecting temporal enjoyment to the ultimate outcome: “then to Us is their return.” The return to Allah is inevitable. Every soul will stand before Him, stripped of all the props of worldly life. After the return comes the consequence: “then We will make them taste the severe punishment for their disbelief.” The punishment is described as “severe” (shadīd) to emphasize its intensity, which is a direct recompense for their act of disbelieving and fabricating lies.

The word “taste” indicates a direct, experiential contact with the punishment. This verse puts worldly prosperity in its proper perspective. It is not a sign of divine favour nor an indication of ultimate success. The fabricators may seem to be winning, but they are merely enjoying a short interval before an eternal fall.

For the believer, this teaching cultivates patience and a long-term view. When envious of the seeming ease of the unrighteous, we remind ourselves that it is a brief enjoyment that will be accounted for. It also instills fear of a bad ending. Every moment of worldly enjoyment that is built on falsehood is accumulating a debt that will be paid in the Hereafter. The verse thus motivates a believer to prefer eternal reward over temporary pleasure and to avoid selling the permanent for the perishable.

The sequence—brief enjoyment, return to Allah, severe punishment—is a sobering journey that the Qur’an lays out with clarity so that no one is deceived by the glitter of the dunya.

=71=

The surah now turns to historical examples that mirror the Prophet’s own situation, beginning with the story of Noah. The command “Relate to them” indicates that this story is not mere entertainment but a means to strengthen the Prophet and warn his opponents. Noah, after centuries of patient preaching, faces a people who find his presence and his reminders unbearable.

The phrase “my presence and my reminders to you of Allah’s signs” shows that the very act of calling to truth had become a burden for them; they could not tolerate his voice or his message. Noah’s response is a masterclass in prophetic courage and reliance on Allah. He declares: “I have put my trust in Allah.

” This is the turning point where he detaches from any hope in his people and anchors himself entirely in the Divine. Based on this trust, he issues a bold challenge: “So devise a plot along with your associate-gods—and you do not have to be secretive about your plot—then carry it out against me without delay! ” This is not arrogance but the confidence of a prophet who knows that Allah is his protector. He tells them they do not need to conspire in secret; they can openly plan whatever harm they wish, using all their idols, and implement it immediately. He is challenging the entire apparatus of falsehood, fully convinced that it cannot harm him without Allah’s permission. This episode was deeply resonant for the Prophet Muhammad, who faced similar hostility and threats of assassination. It taught him and his followers that reliance on Allah can bring a person to a state of fearlessness where they can look at the combined forces of falsehood and see them as powerless.

For the believer today, this verse is a lesson in developing true tawakkul. When one’s trust in Allah is complete, the fear of people, organizations, or governments diminishes. It also teaches that delivering the message may bring hostility, but the prophet’s response is not to compromise or flee, but to stand firm, proclaim his trust in Allah, and leave the outcome to Him.

=72=

Noah continues his address by presenting another proof of his sincerity. “If you turn away,” acknowledging the likely outcome of their rejection, he does not plead or negotiate. Instead, he reminds them of an undeniable fact: “I have never demanded a reward from you for delivering the message.” Throughout his long centuries of calling, he never asked for money, status, or any worldly compensation. His message was entirely free of personal gain. This eliminates one of the main accusations often levelled against prophets—that they are seeking wealth or power. Noah’s selflessness was a clear sign that he was not a charlatan. The statement “My reward is only from Allah” reinforces this point. He looks to Allah alone for recompense, which frees him from any obligation to please his audience or soften the message to suit their whims. He can speak the pure truth without fear of losing his salary or support. He then adds: “And I have been commanded to be one of those who submit to Allah.” Submission, or Islam, is his identity and his mission. His entire being is aligned with the will of Allah, and this submission defines his purpose. The verse highlights the qualities of a true caller to faith: sincerity, independence from people, reliance on Allah, and unwavering submission. These are the foundations upon which effective da’wah rests. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was another parallel; he too could point to his entire life before prophethood as proof that he had never sought worldly gain from his people.

For every believer engaged in conveying truth, this verse teaches that our reward is with Allah, not with the response of the audience. This frees us from the pressure to compromise or to measure success by worldly metrics. It also serves as a check on our own intentions: are we calling to Allah purely for His sake, or is there a hidden desire for recognition, approval, or material benefit? Noah’s declaration is a timeless standard of prophetic integrity.

=73=

The story reaches its decisive conclusion. Despite Noah’s powerful challenge and clear proofs of his sincerity, “they still rejected him.

” The word “still” underscores their persistent obstinacy in the face of a message delivered over many centuries. The rejection was comprehensive and final. The divine response unfolds in two starkly contrasting acts. “So We saved him and those with him in the Ark.” Salvation is specifically mentioned first, highlighting Allah’s care for the believers even in the midst of a cataclysmic punishment. The Ark becomes the vessel of mercy, carrying Noah and the small band of faithful followers to safety. Then “We made them successors” means that after the flood, the believers were established on the earth, inheriting the land and becoming the new civilization. This demonstrates that true succession and long-term survival belong to the people of faith, even if they are initially a persecuted minority. The other act is the punishment: “and drowned those who rejected Our signs.” The flood covered the land, and the mockers who had once threatened Noah met their end in the very waters they thought impossible.

The verse concludes with the recurring Qur’anic refrain: “See then what was the end of those who had been warned!” It is an invitation to look at the ruins and the stories, to reflect and take heed. The historical fate of Noah’s people is a real, not mythical, event whose lesson remains alive. For the Quraysh, this was a direct parallel: they were rejecting a messenger, mocking his warnings, and relying on their power, just as Noah’s people had done. The verse urges them to consider what happened to their predecessors before the same fate befalls them.

For believers, the story reaffirms the pattern: persecution, patience, deliverance, and then succession. It instills hope in times of hardship, reminding us that the ark of faith will always carry its passengers to safety, while the flood of divine punishment will eventually engulf the obstinate rejectors. The warning is also a call to repentance while the door of mercy is still open.

=74=

The historical narrative continues to show the ongoing pattern of prophethood and rejection. After Noah, a succession of messengers was sent. Each was sent specifically to “their own people,” speaking their language and understanding their culture, maximizing the clarity of the message. They came “with clear proofs,” which include the evident truth of their teachings, the miracles they were granted, and the soundness of their arguments. Despite this, the people’s response was consistent: “But they would not believe in what they had rejected before.

” The phrase indicates that the rejection was not a fresh, reasoned decision but a continuation of a prior stance of denial. The arrival of new messengers and clearer proofs did not change the hearts that were already sealed by their own obstinacy. They had already taken a position of disbelief, and they stubbornly maintained it.

The verse then explains the mechanism behind this: “This is how We seal the hearts of the transgressors.” The sealing of the heart is a divine action that follows persistent human transgression. When a person repeatedly chooses falsehood over truth, suppressing the evidence and indulging in sin, their spiritual perception becomes dulled, and eventually the heart is locked in that state. This sealing is a consequence of their own choices, a just recompense for their stubbornness. It serves as a warning that continuous rejection can lead to a point of no return. For the Prophet, this verse placed his struggle in a wider historical frame. He was not the first to face a people who rejected clear proofs; this was an ancient pattern. It reassured him that the fault was not in the message but in the hardened hearts of the recipients.

For the believer, the verse is a cautionary tale. We must guard against the incremental hardening of the heart. Every sin, every ignored reminder, every turned-away opportunity for repentance is a step towards a potential seal. Conversely, it motivates constant repentance and renewal, ensuring the heart remains soft and receptive to guidance.

The verse also explains why some people, despite overwhelming evidence, never embrace faith; their transgressions have sealed their inner sight.

=75=

The chain of messengers continues, now focusing on the well-known story of Moses and Aaron. They were sent not just to any people, but to “Pharaoh and his chiefs,” the ruling elite of Egypt.

This is significant because the resistance to truth often originates from the powerful, who see the message as a threat to their authority and privilege. Moses and Aaron came “with Our signs,” a comprehensive term covering the miracles such as the staff, the shining hand, and the other plagues, as well as the clear arguments for monotheism. The response of the Egyptian establishment was not honest inquiry but arrogance: “But they behaved arrogantly and were a wicked people.” Arrogance (istakbarū) means they considered themselves above the truth, deeming the messengers and their message beneath their dignity. This pride was compounded by their pre-existing wickedness; they were already engaged in oppression, tyranny, and the enslavement of the Children of Israel. The message of Moses threatened their entire system, so they rejected it out of hand.

This verse draws a direct line between Pharaoh’s behaviour and that of the Quraysh chiefs. The Meccan leaders also behaved arrogantly, viewing the Prophet’s call as an affront to their status and the traditions of their forefathers. By recounting this story, the Qur’an is telling the Quraysh that their prototype is Pharaoh, and that their fate could be similar.

For the believer, the verse offers a lens through which to view the opposition to truth throughout history. Often, the greatest resistance comes from those who stand to lose worldly power. This should not discourage the callers to truth; it is a sign that the message is effective, threatening the structures of falsehood. It also warns against the arrogance that can creep into any heart, including that of a believer, when faced with a truth that demands a change in lifestyle or a humbling of the ego. The wickedness of Pharaoh and his chiefs is a reminder that arrogance and sin are mutually reinforcing, leading to a complete spiritual downfall.

=76=

This verse describes the moment of confrontation. “When the truth came to them from Us” refers to the arrival of Moses with the clear signs, particularly the transformation of the staff into a serpent and the hand becoming radiant. The truth is identified as coming “from Us,” emphasising its divine origin and unassailable reality. It was not a philosophical speculation or a debatable claim; it was a manifest truth with supernatural backing.

However, the response of Pharaoh and his chiefs was to dismiss it with a label: “This is certainly pure magic!” The word “certainly” shows their desperate attempt to sound confident, while “pure magic” is an intensified accusation, meaning obvious and undeniable sorcery. This reaction is a classic psychological defence mechanism: when faced with a truth that one is unwilling to accept, the easiest way is to discredit it. By calling it magic, they acknowledged that something extraordinary had occurred—they saw the staff swallow the magicians’ snakes—but they reframed it as a trick rather than a sign from God. This allowed them to maintain their position without having to submit. The verse mirrors exactly what the Quraysh said about the Prophet Muhammad and the Qur’an, calling him a magician. By equating the two responses, the Qur’an is telling the Meccans that they are walking in the footsteps of Pharaoh, and history has already recorded his end.

For the believer, this verse reveals the irrationality of rejection. Truth can be staring someone in the face, and yet they can label it magic, madness, or outdated. The cause is not intellectual but spiritual; the heart is unwilling to accept the consequences of the truth. This teaches us that arguments alone are often insufficient to change people; what is needed is a softening of the heart. It also calls believers to be courageous in accepting truth, even when it is difficult or goes against the current, rather than resorting to dismissing it with convenient labels. The Pharaoh’s accusation is a timeless example of self-deception in the face of undeniable reality.

=77=

Moses’ reply is recorded, showing his astonishment and his logical rebuttal. He begins with a question: “Is this what you say about the truth when it has come to you?

” The question expresses amazement that they could call something “pure magic” when it is clearly the truth. The phrase “when it has come to you” emphasises that they have witnessed it directly; this is not hearsay. Their accusation is an insult to the truth itself. Moses then asks another pointed question: “Is this magic?” The rhetorical nature of the question challenges them to think about what they witnessed. Magic typically consists of illusions, deceptions, and temporary effects. But what Moses brought was a living serpent that devoured the magicians’ illusions, and a hand that shone with a light unlike any trickery. To call it magic was intellectually dishonest. Moses then delivers a statement of principle: “Magicians will never succeed.

” This is both a statement about the nature of magic and a prophecy. Magic, which relies on falsehood and deception, ultimately fails because it cannot stand against the truth. Whatever temporary success a magician might have, it will eventually crumble. More specifically, Moses is predicting that the magicians of Pharaoh, who will soon be summoned, will be defeated.

This statement also carries a spiritual truth: those who rely on falsehood and deception will not achieve true, lasting success. Their apparent victories are hollow and temporary. Moses’ response is a model for believers when faced with dismissive accusations. He does not engage in name-calling or lose his temper; he calmly and logically questions the absurdity of their claim and states the divine law that falsehood cannot ultimately prevail over truth. This verse empowers the caller to truth to remain confident, knowing that the truth has an inherent strength that falsehood lacks. It also warns those who employ deception and mislabel the truth that their apparent triumphs are fleeting, and real success belongs only to the people of truth.

=78=

The response of Pharaoh and his chiefs is a classic argument of those who have no logical refutation. They shift the focus from the signs themselves to the perceived social and political consequences of accepting the message. “Have you come to turn us away from the faith of our forefathers?” They frame the call to monotheism as an attack on tradition and heritage. Ancestral faith, even if false, is given a sanctity that makes questioning it taboo. This appeal to tradition has been a constant weapon against prophets throughout history. People often prefer the comfort of the familiar over the challenge of the truth.

The second part of their accusation reveals their real fear: “so that the two of you may become supreme in the land? ” They project their own lust for power onto Moses and Aaron, accusing them of wanting to overthrow the existing order and establish their own rule. This is a classic tactic to discredit reformers—accuse them of having hidden political ambitions. The irony is that Pharaoh himself claimed supreme authority and divinity, yet he accuses the messengers of seeking power. The concluding statement, “We will never believe in you!” is a declaration of absolute refusal. It is not a conditional rejection based on further evidence but a closed door. This illustrates the state of a heart that has fully aligned itself with falsehood, making belief impossible as long as it remains in that state.

For the believer, this verse offers a sharp analysis of the arguments against faith. Often, objections to Islam are not rooted in genuine intellectual difficulty but in fear of breaking with family tradition, or in a mistaken belief that religion is a power grab. The response to such accusations is to clarify, as Moses did, that the call is purely to worship Allah and that the messengers seek no worldly kingdom.

The verse also serves as a warning against the blind following of ancestral ways without verification, reminding us that each person will stand alone before Allah, and the faith of forefathers will not avail if it was false.

Pharaoh’s response to Moses’ challenge is not to engage with his arguments but to escalate the confrontation. “Pharaoh demanded, ‘Bring me every skilled magician.’” This demand reveals his strategy. He has decided that Moses’ signs are a form of superior magic, and the only way to defeat him is to gather the most expert magicians from across his kingdom.

The phrase “every skilled magician” indicates that Pharaoh intended to make this a decisive public spectacle. He would not just bring a few magicians but amass the very best, ensuring that the defeat of Moses would be total and humiliating in front of the masses.

The word “skilled” highlights that magic was a respected and advanced art in ancient Egypt, and its top practitioners were likely close to the establishment. Pharaoh’s mindset reflects the arrogance that dismisses truth as a problem to be outmaneuvered rather than a message to be considered. He never paused to ask, “What if this is actually from God? ” His heart was sealed, and he saw the situation purely in terms of power and control.

The verse also underscores how those in power will use their resources—money, media, experts—to suppress the truth and maintain their dominance. However, the very gathering of magicians that Pharaoh planned would become the stage for one of the greatest public vindications of truth in history.

For the believer, this verse shows that the opponents of truth often rely on massive resources and propaganda to counter the message. They may appear to be preparing a devastating blow.

Yet, unbeknownst to them, their preparations are setting the stage for Allah’s victory. The believer should not be intimidated by the impressive array of falsehood’s forces, for the staff of Moses, representing divine power, will eventually swallow all their deceptions. It also teaches that human efforts, no matter how grand, are futile when deployed against the will of Allah. Pharaoh’s command initiated a chain of events that led directly to his own humiliation and the mass acceptance of faith by the magicians themselves.

The stage is set, and the magicians have arrived. The verse captures the moment of confrontation. “When the magicians came” suggests a grand assembly, likely in front of Pharaoh, his court, and a large crowd. Tension must have filled the air. Moses, far from showing any sign of fear or hesitation, immediately addresses them: “Cast whatever you wish to cast!

” This is a remarkable display of confidence. He does not negotiate, does not ask for rules, and does not try to go first. He invites them to throw down everything they have, without restriction. This confidence is rooted not in personal ability but in complete trust in Allah’s promise. Moses knows that the truth he carries is from the Lord of the worlds, and no amount of magic can overcome it. By telling them to cast first, he also removes any suspicion that his own act would be a trick that pre-empts theirs. He wants the crowd to see the full extent of their magic and then witness what Allah will do. This open challenge is similar to the Prophet Muhammad’s challenge to the Quraysh to produce a surah like the Qur’an. Truth does not need to be coy or restrictive; it can afford to let falsehood display its full power before crushing it.

The verse also highlights the psychology of the caller to truth. When the odds seem overwhelming and the forces of falsehood are fully mobilised, the believer should not panic. Instead, with reliance on Allah, they can face the challenge head-on, knowing that Allah’s help is near. Moses’ instruction to the magicians can be seen as a metaphor: let the falsehood of this world show whatever it has to offer—its entertainment, its ideologies, its threats—and then watch how the truth makes it all futile. For the magicians themselves, this moment became the turning point of their lives. What they were about to witness would transform them from Pharaoh’s servants into believers who would willingly face torture and death for the sake of the truth they saw with their own eyes.
