=81=

The magicians cast their ropes and staffs, and through their sorcery they appeared to the onlookers as slithering serpents, filling the scene with an overwhelming illusion. At that very moment Moses spoke with the calm certainty of one who knows he carries the truth. He did not panic or hesitate; he declared, “What you have produced is mere magic.

” The word “mere” reduces their impressive display to its true nature—a hollow trick, an illusion with no substance. He then announced its fate: “Allah will surely make it useless.” This was a prophecy, not a wish. Whatever power or effect the magic seemed to possess, Allah would nullify it completely. The statement is absolute and rooted in a fundamental principle of divine action: “for Allah certainly does not set right the work of the corruptors. ” The corruptors are those who spread mischief, deceive people, and use falsehood to oppose the truth. Allah’s way is not to grant success and stability to their works. Even if they appear to flourish temporarily, their efforts are intrinsically flawed and destined for collapse.

In this context, the magicians represented a system of deception employed by Pharaoh to maintain his tyranny. Their magic was a tool of corruption, reinforcing the false claim that Pharaoh was a god and keeping the people subjugated. Allah’s principle ensures that such works, no matter how skilfully crafted, will ultimately be brought to ruin. For Moses, this declaration was an act of faith before the actual destruction of the magic took place. He was publicly proclaiming the outcome, putting his trust entirely in Allah’s power.

For the believer facing any formidable display of falsehood—whether it be a dominant ideology, a deceptive media campaign, or the allure of worldly power—this verse teaches us to see through the illusion. What appears powerful is, in reality, mere magic that Allah can render useless in an instant. We are called to announce this truth with conviction, not being intimidated by the apparent strength of corruption.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who use deception for evil ends; their success is an illusion, and Allah will eventually expose and destroy their works, because He does not set right what is inherently corrupt.

=82=

After Moses’ declaration that Allah would nullify the magic, this verse states the inevitable outcome. The verse does not narrate the moment of the staff swallowing the illusions; instead, it focuses on the principle behind that event. “And Allah establishes the truth by His Words.” The “Words” here refer to His commands, decrees, and revelations. Allah’s words are the ultimate reality. When He wills a thing, He says “Be,” and it is.

In the context of Moses and Aaron, the truth was the message of monotheism and the prophethood of Moses. This truth was established not merely by philosophical argument but by the divine command that gave the staff its power, making the falsehood disappear. The truth becomes firmly rooted, undeniable, and triumphant, no matter how many forces array against it.

The phrase “even to the dismay of the wicked” highlights that this establishment of truth is galling and painful for those who oppose it. The wicked, who had hoped to see the truth humiliated, are left frustrated and angered by its victory. The magicians themselves, witnessing their craft devoured, immediately recognised the divine origin of Moses’ sign and fell into prostration, acknowledging the Lord of the worlds. For Pharaoh and his chiefs, this was a devastating blow; their strongest weapons had been rendered useless, and the very experts they relied on had defected to the truth.

The verse teaches a timeless lesson: truth is not established by human effort alone but by the divine Word that backs it. The believers are agents of this truth, but the ultimate victory belongs to Allah. This should fill the hearts of the faithful with confidence. When we see truth being maligned and falsehood celebrated, we remember that Allah’s Word will prevail, and the dismay of the wicked is part of the process.

The verse also provides a criterion: what aligns with Allah’s Words will endure; what opposes them will vanish. In personal life, we should anchor ourselves in the truth of the Qur’an, knowing that it is the established reality, while the falsehoods of the world are temporary illusions that will inevitably cause dismay to those who cling to them.

=83=

After the spectacular defeat of the magicians, one might expect a mass conversion. However, this verse reveals a sobering reality. Despite the clear sign, “no one believed in Moses except a few youths of his people.

” The phrase “his people” refers to the Children of Israel, the very community Moses was sent to deliver. Only a small group from among them—described as “youths,” implying they were young, less entrenched in the fear and indoctrination of Pharaoh’s regime—had the courage to embrace faith openly. The rest remained paralyzed by fear. The verse explains the reason: “while fearing that Pharaoh and their own chiefs might persecute them.” The Israelites were an oppressed minority living under a brutal dictatorship. Pharaoh’s power was absolute, and even their own elders and leaders were often co-opted or intimidated by the regime. The psychological grip of tyranny was so strong that witnessing a divine miracle was not enough to break the chains of fear for most. This shows that intellectual conviction alone is sometimes insufficient when overwhelming terror and social pressure are present.

The verse then underscores the nature of the regime: “And certainly Pharaoh was a tyrant in the land, and he was truly a transgressor. ” His tyranny was characterized by extreme oppression, and his transgression meant he had crossed all bounds of morality and humanity. The fear of such a tyrant was a legitimate and powerful force. This verse provides deep insight into the challenges faced by nascent faith communities under oppressive systems. It explains why, even after the truth is clearly manifested, mass conversion may not immediately occur. The few who do believe are often the brave youth who have less to lose and more willingness to risk. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a parallel; initially, his followers were mostly the young and the socially vulnerable, while the establishment figures held back out of fear of the Quraysh leadership. The verse honours those “few youths” who chose faith despite the danger. It also serves as a timeless encouragement: the small numbers and the powerful opposition do not indicate the falsehood of the message. The truth may initially be embraced by only a handful, but with patience and reliance on Allah, that handful can eventually overcome the tyranny. Pharaoh’s tyranny was real, but it was no match for the divine plan unfolding.

=84=

Facing a fearful and hesitant community, Moses provides them with the essential spiritual prescription. He addresses them as “O my people!” with love and concern. He does not rebuke them for their fear; instead, he points them to the solution. His statement is conditional: “If you do believe in Allah and submit to His Will. ” Moses acknowledges their belief, but he urges them to take the next step. True faith and submission (Islam) naturally lead to a specific action: putting one’s trust in Allah.

The word for trust is tawakkul, which means a complete reliance and delegation of one’s affairs to Allah, accompanied by the conviction that He alone can bring benefit or harm. Moses is teaching them that if they have truly submitted to Allah, they must transfer their fear from Pharaoh to Allah. Tawakkul does not mean abandoning practical measures; it means that after taking all possible human precautions, the heart is at peace, knowing that the ultimate outcome is in Allah’s hands. The Israelites were afraid of persecution, but Moses directs them to counter that fear with trust. If Allah is their Lord, then Pharaoh’s power is subordinate to His. A believer who places trust in Allah may still face hardships, but the terror of those hardships loses its grip on the heart, because the soul knows that nothing happens except by Allah’s permission and that the final victory is for the righteous. This verse is a timeless formula for overcoming fear. Every believer faces “Pharaohs” in their lives: oppressive authorities, difficult life circumstances, or overwhelming anxieties. The response is not to rely on one’s own strength or to despair, but to reaffirm belief and submission, and then to actively place trust in Allah. Tawakkul is the bridge between faith and peace. Moses’ instruction to his people is also an instruction to us. When we say we believe, the test of that belief comes when we are threatened. Do we then panic, or do we consciously deposit our trust in Allah? Moses knew that the only way a small, fearful group could stand against a tyrant was by anchoring their hearts in the Most Powerful. This tawakkul would later carry them to the edge of the sea, where their trust would be rewarded with a miraculous deliverance.

=85=

The response of the believers among the Children of Israel is both a declaration and a supplication. They immediately put into practice what Moses taught them. Their statement, “In Allah we trust,” is a powerful affirmation of tawakkul. It is not a passive resignation but an active decision to lean entirely on Allah.

The Arabic phrasing conveys complete reliance: upon Allah alone we have placed our trust. This is the voice of a community that has decided to overcome its fear by turning directly to the Almighty. Immediately after the declaration of trust, they follow it with a supplication: “Our Lord! Do not subject us to the persecution of the oppressive people. ” The word “persecution” translates fitnah, which can mean trial, torture, or a test that shakes one’s faith. They are pleading with Allah to spare them from falling victim to the brutal oppression of Pharaoh and his regime, an oppression that could potentially break their spirits or drive them away from their faith. This supplication reveals a beautiful balance. They are trusting in Allah, but they also recognise their own weakness and the severity of the trial. They ask Allah to protect them, not merely from physical harm, but from the fitnah—the spiritual and psychological assault that could accompany such persecution. They know that enduring torture is difficult, and they ask Allah, in His mercy, to shield them from what they may not be able to bear. This teaches the believer that tawakkul and du’a are inseparable. Trusting in Allah does not mean we stop asking Him for relief.

In fact, the most complete trust is expressed when we beg Allah to avert calamities, even as we accept that if they come, He will give us the strength. The verse also validates the fear of persecution as a real and legitimate concern. The believers did not pretend to be superhuman; they acknowledged the danger and sought divine protection.

This is a model for all believers facing oppression: declare your trust in Allah openly, and then pour out your heart in supplication, asking for safety from trials that could overwhelm your faith. The sequence is a source of immense strength and comfort.

=86=

The supplication continues and intensifies. After asking not to be subjected to persecution, they make a direct plea for deliverance: “and deliver us by Your mercy from the disbelieving people.

” The word “deliver us” implies a complete rescue, an escape from the grip of Pharaoh and his followers. They are asking not just for endurance in the face of persecution but for a way out, a salvation that comes from Allah alone. The crucial phrase here is “by Your mercy.” They recognise that any deliverance is not earned by their own merit or strength but is a gift of Allah’s mercy. They are a small, oppressed group with no military power, no political influence. Their only hope is the vast, encompassing mercy of the Almighty. This shifts the entire focus from the external enemy to the inner relationship with Allah. The disbelieving people are identified as the source of the threat. The believers were living in the midst of a society steeped in rejection of Allah, led by a man who claimed divinity. The call to be delivered from them is a call to be separated from the environment of disbelief and oppression. This supplication foreshadows the Exodus, where Allah would indeed deliver them by His mercy through the miraculous parting of the sea.

For the believer in any age, this supplication is a model. There are times when the believer needs not just patience but a way out. The prayer combines trust, acknowledgment of human vulnerability, and an appeal to the attribute of Allah most needed in the moment—His mercy. It also teaches that seeking deliverance from an environment of active disbelief and hostility is a legitimate and prophetic request. One should not feel guilty for wanting to be in a place where faith can be practised freely. The Israelites’ prayer was answered in the most dramatic fashion, proving that no matter how impossible the situation appears—with a tyrant’s army behind and a sea ahead—Allah’s mercy can carve a path where none existed. Every believer who feels trapped by circumstances can recite these words with the same hope, knowing that the Lord who parted the sea for Moses is still the Lord of mercy and deliverance.

=87=

In response to their supplication and trust, Allah provides a strategic and spiritual directive. The revelation comes to both Moses and Aaron. The command is specific: “Appoint houses for your people in Egypt.” The Israelites were to designate certain dwellings as gathering places, distinct and set apart. In an environment of hostility, having physical spaces where the community could assemble, support one another, and practise their faith in relative safety was crucial.

The next command elevates these houses: “Turn these houses into places of worship.” The homes were not just shelters but were to become masaajid, places of prostration, where Allah would be remembered and worshipped. This transformed the private space into a sanctuary of faith, a fortress for the soul.

The third command is to “establish prayer.” Prayer is the central pillar that connects the believer to Allah. In the midst of oppression, maintaining the discipline of prayer is the lifeline that sustains spiritual strength and collective identity.

The final command is to “give good news to the believers. ” Despite the grim circumstances of slavery and Pharaoh’s tyranny, Moses and Aaron were to deliver glad tidings. This good news likely included the promise of deliverance, victory, and eventual inheritance of the land. This combination of practical organisation, spiritual fortification, and hopeful messaging is a comprehensive strategy for a community under pressure. It teaches that faith is not just an internal feeling but requires practical structures and communal support. The houses of the believers became incubators of resilience. For the Prophet Muhammad, a parallel is seen in the house of Al-Arqam in Mecca, which served as a secret meeting place for early Muslims to learn and pray.

For believers today, this verse emphasises the importance of making our homes places of worship, where prayer is established and the remembrance of Allah is constant. Even when the outside environment is hostile or spiritually toxic, the home can be a sanctuary of faith. The command to give good news reminds leaders and fellow believers to inject hope into the community, countering despair with the certainty of Allah’s promises. This divine strategy prepared the Israelites for the impending exodus by building a solid, prayerful, and hopeful foundation.

=88=

After receiving the divine strategy, Moses turns to Allah with a powerful supplication. He begins by stating a fact: “Our Lord! You have granted Pharaoh and his chiefs luxuries and riches in this worldly life.” The wealth and splendour of Egypt were evident. Moses acknowledges that these are gifts from Allah, but he immediately highlights their misuse: “which they abused to lead people astray from Your Way.” Instead of using their blessings for gratitude and righteousness, they employed their resources to oppress, deceive, and divert people from the path of monotheism. The very gifts meant to be a means of guidance became tools of misguidance. Moses then makes two requests that can seem harsh but must be understood in context. First, “destroy their riches.” He asks that their wealth, which has become a source of arrogance and power used against the truth, be obliterated. The purpose is not revenge but neutralising the instruments of misguidance. Second, “harden their hearts so that they will not believe until they see the painful punishment. ” This is a prophetic invocation of divine justice. Moses is not asking for an arbitrary hardening but is essentially praying that their hearts, which have already persistently rejected the truth after clear signs, be sealed as a just recompense, so that they can no longer cause further harm by pretending to believe or by continuing to mislead others. The hardening is a consequence of their own choices; Moses’ supplication aligns with the divine decree that persistent, arrogant rejectors will have their hearts sealed, as seen in other verses.

The phrase “until they see the painful punishment” indicates that they will finally witness the truth only when it is too late for repentance. This supplication was answered, as Pharaoh’s wealth was drowned with him, and he declared belief only when the waves engulfed him, a belief that was not accepted. This prayer teaches that there is a time to pray against oppressors, especially those who use their resources to systematically destroy faith. It is not a prayer of personal vendetta but a call for the removal of the corruptive influence. It also warns against the love of wealth that leads to spiritual ruin. The wealth that Pharaoh enjoyed became a curse because it fueled his rebellion. Believers must constantly check their relationship with worldly blessings, ensuring they are used to draw closer to Allah, not to drift away.

=89=

The supplication of Moses and Aaron is met with a direct and clear answer from Allah. The words “Your prayer is answered” are a definitive declaration that the dua has been accepted. This immediate divine response is a source of immense comfort and confidence. The specific prayers regarding the destruction of Pharaoh’s riches and the hardening of his heart were already set in motion by divine decree. Allah assures His two messengers that the matter is settled in the heavenly realm.

However, the answer comes with two crucial instructions. The first is “So be steadfast.” The acceptance of the prayer does not mean instant gratification. There would be a period of waiting, during which the oppression might even intensify. Steadfastness is required to remain firm on the path, to continue the mission, and to trust in Allah’s timing. The promise of victory is sure, but the path to it requires patience and perseverance.

The second instruction is “and do not follow the way of those who do not know. ” Those who do not know are the ones who lack true knowledge of Allah, His promises, and His ways. Their way is characterized by impatience, doubt, and deviation. Moses and Aaron, as leaders, are warned not to be influenced by the ignorance of the masses or the pressure of their own followers who might grow restless. They must maintain the high ground of prophetic wisdom and trust. This response is a model for every believer who makes supplication. Sometimes we pray earnestly for relief or for justice, and we may receive an inner conviction that our prayer is accepted.

Yet the external situation remains unchanged. This verse teaches that between the acceptance of the prayer and its visible manifestation, there is a required period of steadfastness. During that time, we must guard against adopting the mindset of those who do not know—those who measure divine response only by immediate, tangible results and who might suggest that the prayer was unheard. The believer’s role is to remain firm, confident that Allah’s word is true, and that His timing is perfect. The dual address to Moses and Aaron shows the importance of leadership solidarity in facing trials. Together, they reinforced each other’s resolve, anchored in the certainty that Allah had heard their plea.

=90=

The narrative now moves to the dramatic climax of the Exodus. “We brought the Children of Israel across the sea.

” The use of “We” highlights divine agency; it was Allah who parted the sea and provided the dry path. The Israelites, who had been praying for deliverance, were now experiencing it first-hand.

However, salvation was not the end of the threat. “Then Pharaoh and his soldiers pursued them unjustly and oppressively.” Pharaoh’s pursuit was driven by tyranny and aggression. The words “unjustly and oppressively” emphasize that their chase was not a legitimate act of state but a gross transgression against an innocent people whom they had already enslaved. Even as they entered the sea bed, their hearts were full of arrogance. The scene then shifts to the moment of reckoning for Pharaoh. As the walls of water collapsed and drowning became certain, “he cried out, ‘I believe that there is no god except that in whom the Children of Israel believe, and I am now one of those who submit.’” This is a stunning confession. The same Pharaoh who had claimed divinity, who had persecuted Moses, who had witnessed plague after plague and remained obstinate, now at the point of death proclaims the purest form of tawhid. He even specifies the God of the Children of Israel, leaving no ambiguity. His statement mirrors exactly what Moses had called him to for years.

However, the timing is everything. This is iman at the moment of death, when the truth is seen with the eyes but the door of willing faith has closed. The verse sets up the profound lesson that belief under the grip of certain death is not the same as belief based on faith in the unseen. Pharaoh’s case becomes the textbook example of a repentance that comes too late.

For the believer, this scene evokes a mixture of awe and fear. It is a reminder to never delay repentance, for the moment of death allows no postponement. It also shows that the worst of tyrants can intellectually recognise the truth when faced with it, but the test is to accept it when one still has the choice to deny. Pharaoh’s declaration will be addressed in the next verse with divine justice.

=91=

The response to Pharaoh’s deathbed declaration is a sharp rebuke, either from the angels or directly from Allah. The single word “Now?” is dripping with condemnation. After a lifetime of arrogance, oppression, and rejection, after every sign was shown and every warning was given, he finally utters the words of faith when the waves are about to close over his head.

The question exposes the worthlessness of such a conversion. Faith is a voluntary submission of the heart and will before the unseen becomes seen. When the punishment is already descending, faith ceases to be a test and becomes a forced acknowledgement, which holds no moral weight.

The verse continues: “But you always disobeyed and were one of the corruptors.” His entire life was a record of disobedience (’aṣayta) and corruption (mufsidīn). He not only refused to follow the commands of Allah but actively worked to corrupt the land, oppressing the Israelites, killing their sons, and spreading the falsehood of his own divinity. His corrupting influence was systemic and prolonged. The contrast between his past life and his last-minute words is stark. A lifetime of transgression cannot be wiped away by a declaration coerced by drowning. This establishes a crucial principle in Islamic theology: repentance is accepted until the moment the soul reaches the throat, but when the final pangs of death arrive or the divine punishment is actually witnessed, the door of repentance closes. The verse serves as the ultimate warning to every human being. Do not gamble with your soul by thinking you can live a life of sin and then repent at the last second. First, no one knows when the last second will arrive. Second, a deathbed repentance born of panic, not genuine transformation, may not be sincere. Pharaoh’s words were technically correct, but they were rejected because they lacked the substance of a lifetime of faith. This teaches believers to hasten to repentance now, to avoid disobedience now, and to live as those who submit, not as those who plan to submit only when forced by circumstances. The verse is a chilling epitaph for the archetype of tyranny.

=92=

The divine decree continues with an extraordinary posthumous treatment of Pharaoh. “Today We will preserve your corpse” is a specific miracle and a sign. The body of Pharaoh, instead of being lost to the sea or decomposing beyond recognition, would be kept intact. The purpose is stated clearly: “so that you may become an example for those who come after you. ” The Arabic word “āyah” means a sign, a lesson, an example. Pharaoh’s preserved body would serve as a tangible reminder for future generations of the fate of a tyrant who claimed divinity and rejected the messenger of Allah. The preservation of his mummy, which was discovered in modern times and is now displayed in a museum, stands as a remarkable fulfilment of this verse. Those who see it can reflect on the story, and for the believers, it strengthens faith; for the heedless, it is just another artifact.

The verse then adds a lament: “And surely most people are heedless of Our examples!” Despite the countless signs in history, in nature, and in the preserved remains of past civilizations, the majority of humanity does not take heed. They pass by the lessons without reflecting, or they interpret them purely in material terms without grasping the spiritual warning. Heedlessness is the disease that prevents people from benefiting from the signs of Allah. Pharaoh’s story, preserved in the Qur’an and physically manifested in his mummy, is one of the most powerful examples, yet many remain unmoved.

For the believer, this verse is a call to be among the few who do take heed. When we hear the stories of past nations, when we see historical relics, we should not merely consume them as information or entertainment. We should actively connect them to the reality of divine justice and apply the warning to our own lives. The preservation of Pharaoh’s corpse is also a proof of the Qur’an’s divine origin, as the Prophet Muhammad had no access to the knowledge of Egyptian mummification.

This fact, now accessible to all, is an ongoing sign that challenges the heedlessness of the modern world. It invites every rational person to reflect: what is the lesson behind the survival of this particular tyrant’s body, and what does it mean for my own life and accountability?

=93=

With Pharaoh destroyed, the verse turns to the aftermath for the Israelites. “Indeed, We settled the Children of Israel in a blessed land.” The blessed land refers to the region that includes Palestine and the surrounding areas, a land sanctified and endowed with spiritual and material blessings. After centuries of slavery, they were given a homeland. Allah also “granted them good, lawful provisions,” meaning wholesome food, resources, and livelihoods that were tayyib and halal. This was the physical manifestation of Allah’s mercy and the fulfilment of His promise.

However, the verse then notes a tragic turn: “They did not differ until knowledge came to them.” The knowledge refers to the Torah and the clear instructions given through Moses. With the arrival of this divine knowledge, one would expect unity, but instead, differences and divisions erupted.

This is a profound observation about human nature. Knowledge is a test; when people receive it, some accept it fully, others distort it, and divisions arise based on how people respond to the truth. The Israelites, despite the miracles they had witnessed, eventually split into sects and disputed over the scripture. This was not due to ignorance but due to the misuse of knowledge they had received.

The verse then reassures: “Surely your Lord will judge between them on the Day of Judgment regarding their differences.” The final resolution of all religious disputes is deferred to the Day of Judgment, where Allah, with His perfect knowledge, will make clear the truth and hold everyone accountable for their stance. For the Prophet and the believers, this was a lesson that disagreement among religious communities is part of the divine test, and the ultimate judgment belongs to Allah alone. It also warns this ummah not to repeat the mistakes of those who were given scripture and then fell into division. Knowledge should lead to unity on the truth, not to sectarianism.

The verse also highlights the pattern of blessing followed by ingratitude, a recurring theme that we must guard against. Recognizing that final judgment rests with Allah frees us from the need to forcefully resolve every disagreement in this world, while still striving for truth and justice.

=94=

After recounting the detailed story of Moses and Pharaoh, the verse addresses the Prophet Muhammad directly with a powerful rhetorical technique. “If you are in doubt about these stories that We have revealed to you, then ask those who read the Scripture before you.” The surface instruction is that if any uncertainty were to arise, the Prophet could consult the Jews and Christians who had knowledge of previous scriptures, and they would confirm the essential truth of these events.

However, the deeper purpose of this statement is to emphasize the absolute certainty of the revelation. The Prophet, may peace be upon him, was never in doubt, but the formulation “If you are in doubt…” is a way of saying, “Even if a doubt were conceivable, the evidence is overwhelming and verifiable.” It challenged the disbelievers to check with the People of the Book, who would recognise the truth of these stories.

In fact, the Qur’an’s accurate retelling of events not previously known in Arabia was itself a proof. The verse continues, “The truth has certainly come to you from your Lord, so do not be one of those who doubt. ” This is a direct command to remain anchored in certainty. The truth has “come,” it is present, established, and undeniable. The warning against being among the doubters is a protection for the Prophet and for all believers. Doubt, when indulged, can erode faith.

The verse teaches that when revelation is confirmed by multiple lines of evidence—internal coherence, historical verification, and the testimony of earlier scriptures—there is no rational room for doubt. For the believer, this verse is a call to seek certainty in faith, to study the proofs, and to avoid the company and mindset of those who constantly waver. It also highlights the continuity of the divine message; the Qur’an came to confirm what was before it, and the learned among the People of the Book were witnesses to its truth. In a broader sense, the verse encourages believers to be confident in their scripture; the stories it tells are not myths but historical realities, and any honest investigation will only strengthen faith. The Prophet’s confidence, rooted in this divine assurance, is a model for all who face skepticism.

=95=

This verse continues the instruction from the previous one, adding a further warning. After being told not to be among the doubters, the command follows: “and do not be one of those who deny Allah’s signs.” Denial is a step beyond doubt. Doubt is internal hesitation; denial is the outward rejection and opposition to the truth after it has been made clear. The signs of Allah include the verses of the Qur’an, the miracles of the prophets, and the signs in creation. To deny them is to close oneself off from guidance. The consequence is stated bluntly: “or you will be one of the losers.” This loss is absolute and eternal. The losers are those who forfeit the pleasure of Allah and the eternal bliss of Paradise in exchange for the fleeting gains of this world or the satisfaction of their arrogance. The loss is not merely a deprivation but an active incurring of punishment. The verse, while addressed to the Prophet as a form of emphasis and protection, is a universal warning. If the Prophet himself is warned, how much more must the ordinary believer take heed? The path to loss is marked by denial of clear signs. The signs of Allah in the Qur’an are not ambiguous; they are clear, detailed, and supported by evidence. Those who reject them do so not because of insufficient proof but because of a spiritual sickness.

For the believer, this verse instills a reverent fear of ever falling into denial, even in subtle ways, such as ignoring a command, explaining away a prohibition, or treating the Qur’an as a cultural artifact rather than the living word of God. It also provides a clear metric of success and loss. Success is not measured by wealth, status, or popularity, but by one’s relationship with Allah’s signs. The one who accepts them, lives by them, and remains steadfast is on the path of ultimate success. The one who denies them, regardless of worldly achievements, is a loser in the truest sense. This sharp, binary reality cuts through the confusions of a world that often blurs the lines between truth and falsehood, reminding us that the outcome is tied to a single, decisive factor: our response to the signs of Allah.

=96=

After the stories of past rejectors and the clear call to accept the signs, the surah addresses a painful reality. The Prophet has been delivering the message with utmost sincerity, yet some people remain obstinate. This verse explains the spiritual state of such individuals. “Indeed, those against whom Allah’s decree of torment is justified will not believe. ” A divine decree has been issued against them based on Allah’s eternal knowledge that they would persist in disbelief and corruption. The decree of torment is justified because of their own choices and the state of their hearts. They have reached a point of no return, where their repeated rejection has sealed their faculties, and the word of punishment has become binding. It is crucial to understand that this decree is not an arbitrary predestination that overrides their free will. Rather, it is the divine judgment that confirms what they have already chosen for themselves. Their disbelief is not because the decree prevents them; the decree reflects the fact that they have already exhausted their capacity for faith and will not turn back. The verse uses the word “justified” to emphasize the perfect justice of this decree. Allah does not wrong anyone; He knows that these individuals, if given a thousand more chances, would still choose disbelief. This knowledge forms the basis of the decree. For the Prophet, this verse provided a measure of closure and understanding. He was not to blame himself for their lack of faith; their state was already adjudicated in the divine court. It freed him from the psychological burden of thinking he could save everyone.

For the believer, this verse is a sobering reminder of the seriousness of persistent sin and rejection. No one should feel secure that they can delay repentance indefinitely. There is a point where the heart becomes so hardened that it cannot respond, and the decree of torment becomes justified. The fear of reaching such a state should drive the believer to constantly seek renewal and repentance.

The verse also clarifies why, despite the clearest proofs, some people never embrace faith. It is not a deficiency in the message but a terminal condition of the heart, a condition that one must vigilantly guard against through humility and constant connection with Allah.

=97=

This verse completes the thought of the previous one, emphasizing the totality of the barrier that now exists between such people and faith. Even if “every sign were to come to them,” they would still not believe.

The phrase “every sign” encompasses all possible miracles, proofs, and evidences that a human being could witness. If the dead were brought back to speak to them, if the heavens were split open, if all the warnings of the prophets were made visibly present, it would not make a difference. Their rejection is no longer based on a lack of evidence; it is a fixed state of the heart.

The verse then explains the only thing that will break this state: “until they see the painful punishment.” When the punishment finally descends upon them, either at the moment of death or on the Day of Judgment, they will finally “believe” in the sense of acknowledging the truth. But, as established with Pharaoh, such an acknowledgment is worthless. It is belief under duress, which does not constitute the faith that is accepted by Allah. The verse thus draws a line in the sand: voluntary faith is only possible before the punishment is witnessed. Once the unseen becomes seen, the test is over.

This teaching carries an urgent call to every person. We often think that if only we could see a miracle, we would become better believers. But the Qur’an teaches that the problem is not the absence of signs; it is the state of the heart. The signs are already abundant—in creation, in revelation, in the story of Pharaoh preserved before our eyes—yet many hearts remain unmoved. The demand for ever more spectacular proofs is often a smokescreen for an unwillingness to submit. True belief is to trust the unseen based on the signs that Allah, in His wisdom, has already provided.

The verse also serves as a warning to those who say, “I will believe when I see the punishment.” That will be too late. The time to believe is now, while the door is open, and the belief is still valuable as a free choice. The painful punishment, once seen, will only bring regret, not salvation.

For the believer, this reinforces the value of iman bil-ghayb, faith in the unseen, as the distinguishing mark of the sincere.

=98=

After the gloomy picture of those who only believe when it is too late, the verse presents a shining exception. “If only there had been a society which believed before seeing the torment and, therefore, benefited from its belief, like the people of Jonah.” This expression conveys regret that more communities did not follow this example. Most nations rejected their messengers until the punishment arrived, and then their faith was worthless. But the people of Jonah, the inhabitants of Nineveh, were different. When they saw the signs of impending punishment, they did not wait for it to hit them. They immediately repented, turned to Allah in faith, and their belief was accepted because it occurred while there was still a window of opportunity. The verse highlights the principle: belief that comes before the actual descent of the punishment is the belief that benefits.

The phrase “benefited from its belief” means their faith actually averted the worldly punishment and, more importantly, secured divine pleasure. The verse then describes what happened: “When they believed, We lifted from them the torment of disgrace in this world and allowed them enjoyment for a while.” The punishment had been hanging over them, and they had seen its warning signs—the prophet Jonah had left them in anger, and the sky reportedly darkened. But their collective, sincere repentance led to the removal of that disgraceful punishment. They were then permitted to continue their lives in enjoyment for their appointed term. This story is a beacon of hope. It teaches that no matter how close punishment may seem, sincere repentance can still turn it away. It also proves that collective societal repentance is possible and effective. The community of Jonah is the only one in the Qur’an described as averting a decreed punishment through faith. This makes their story an enduring model for all nations. For individuals, it shows that the door of mercy is open until the very last moment before the punishment is actualized, but one must not delay to the point of no return. The Prophet’s own name is shared with this prophet, highlighting the theme of mercy and second chances that pervades the surah. The verse is a call to act immediately upon receiving the warning, rather than gambling with divine respite.

=99=

This verse addresses the deep concern the Prophet felt for the guidance of his people. He would have done anything to bring them to faith, and he grieved over their rejection. Here, Allah provides a theological and psychological balm. “Had your Lord so willed, all people on earth would have certainly believed, every single one of them! ” The emphasis is absolute: if Allah had willed to compel everyone into belief, He could have done so instantly, without any effort from the Prophet. He could have created humanity with no free will, or He could have overwhelmed their minds with undeniable manifestations that left no room for choice. But He did not. His wisdom dictated that this world be a realm of testing, where people choose to believe or disbelieve based on their own will.

The question that follows is a gentle rebuke: “Would you then force people to become believers?” The Prophet’s intense desire for their guidance was natural and noble, but he needed to align his heart with the divine will. He was not sent to compel faith but to deliver the message. Forcing belief is not only impossible for a human being, but it would also defeat the purpose of the test. Faith obtained by compulsion is not genuine faith.

This verse establishes a fundamental Islamic principle: there is no compulsion in religion. The responsibility of the messenger is to convey with clear proofs; the matter of guidance rests with Allah.

This teaching freed the Prophet from the burden of feeling personally responsible for those who rejected him. It also has profound implications for how Muslims interact with people of other faiths. We are not commanded to force anyone to accept Islam. Our role is to present the truth wisely and beautifully, and to leave the outcome to Allah.

The verse also resolves the age-old philosophical question of why a Merciful God does not make everyone believers. The answer lies in the nature of the test and the value of freely chosen faith. The world is designed to allow genuine, voluntary surrender to Allah, which is infinitely more valuable than compulsory obedience. This wisdom should be a source of peace for the believer who is pained by the disbelief of others.

=100=

The surah reinforces the principle established in the previous verse by stating a universal law. “It is not for any soul to believe except by Allah’s leave.” Faith is not merely a product of human reasoning or effort, although those are essential components.

Ultimately, the acceptance of faith in the heart is a light that Allah grants. His “leave” or permission includes His tawfīq, the divine enabling that opens the heart to the truth. This does not mean Allah arbitrarily withholds faith from sincere seekers. Rather, those who demonstrate genuine willingness and strive for guidance receive the divine leave to believe. Those who turn away, out of arrogance or heedlessness, are denied that leave as a just consequence.

The verse then delivers a warning: “and He will bring His wrath upon those who are unmindful.” The unmindful are those who remain in a state of ghaflah, heedless of Allah’s signs, the purpose of life, and the reality of the Hereafter. Their minds are absorbed in the worldly life, and they do not reflect. The “wrath” or rijs here is interpreted by many commentators as a spiritual punishment, a darkness and turmoil that settles upon the heart, or a state of uncleanness that further distances them from faith. It is both a cause and a consequence of their heedlessness. This verse perfectly balances the roles of human effort and divine enablement. It prevents the believer from becoming arrogant about their faith, as if it were their own achievement. Every believer must humbly acknowledge that their belief is a gift from Allah, a leave granted out of His mercy. This should foster constant gratitude and a prayerful dependence on Allah to keep one’s heart firm. Simultaneously, the verse warns against heedlessness, as it leads to divine punishment and the loss of the ability to believe. The connection between mindfulness and faith is direct: the more mindful one is of Allah, the more one receives the divine leave to deepen in belief. The verse serves as both a source of comfort—that our faith is sustained by Allah—and a call to vigilance against the heedlessness that invites His wrath. The Prophet is thus reminded that his mission is not to create faith but to deliver the message, while the state of hearts is in the hands of the Lord of hearts.
