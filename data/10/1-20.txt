=1=

The surah opens with the disconnected letters Alif-Lãm-Ra, which appear at the beginning of several Qur’anic chapters. These letters immediately capture attention because they are made up of the very alphabet used by the Arabs, yet no human being can produce anything comparable to the divine speech that follows. They serve as a challenge and a reminder that this Book, though composed of ordinary letters, is far beyond human capability. Immediately after these letters comes the description: “These are the verses of the Book, rich in wisdom. ” The word “Book” here refers to the Qur’an itself, a scripture that is not merely a collection of rulings or stories but a perfectly measured whole.

The phrase “rich in wisdom” translates the Arabic ḥakīm, which carries the sense of something made precise, firm, and full of profound purpose. Every verse is placed exactly where it belongs, and within its commands, prohibitions, parables, and promises lies a deep wisdom that may not always be immediately obvious but becomes clearer with reflection. The Meccan polytheists heard these letters and these words, yet they turned away, failing to see that a speech so full of wisdom could only come from the All-Wise. For us today, this opening teaches that when we approach the Qur’an, we should not rush through it. We must seek the wisdom embedded in its verses, knowing that it addresses both the mind and the heart. The juxtaposition of simple letters and the majestic description of the Book reminds every listener that divine guidance is accessible, yet its depths are inexhaustible. A believer’s entire life can be spent uncovering the layers of wisdom contained in these very verses, finding that they speak to every situation, every era, and every soul that turns to them sincerely.

=2=

The verse addresses a deep-seated objection of the Meccan disbelievers: why would Allah send a mere human being, someone they knew as Muhammad the trustworthy, to deliver a divine message? They found it astonishing that revelation would come to a man who ate, drank, and walked in the marketplace just like them.

The question form “Is it astonishing to people...?” exposes the irrationality of their stance, because throughout history, all messengers sent to guide their communities were human beings, not angels. The instruction given to the Prophet is twofold: “Warn humanity and give good news to the believers. ” Warning is directed at those who persist in wrongdoing and disbelief, alerting them to the consequences that await. Glad tidings, on the other hand, are for those who accept faith, assuring them of “an honourable status with their Lord,” a position of truthfulness and nearness in the hereafter. This combination of warning and good news reflects the balance of the entire Qur’anic message, which never speaks of punishment alone but always pairs it with the promise of immense mercy for those who respond. The disbelievers, however, refused to see any of this. They labelled the Prophet a “clear magician,” an accusation that reveals their confusion. Magic was something that deceived the eyes and divided people, yet the Prophet’s character was known for its honesty, and the Qur’an’s message called to unity and justice. When people are unwilling to accept the truth, they often resort to irrational labels, trying to dismiss what they cannot genuinely refute. This verse consoles the Prophet and every caller to truth thereafter, showing that rejection is rarely about the message itself but often about arrogance and the unwillingness to submit to a fellow human being chosen by God. The believer finds comfort in knowing that the honour promised is certain, regardless of how opponents may slander the messenger.

=3=

The verse turns the attention directly to the identity of the Lord who sent the revelation. He is the One who created the vast heavens and the earth in six Days, a period known only to Him, demonstrating that creation was deliberate and not instantaneous, containing wisdom in its gradual unfolding. After creation, He “established Himself on the Throne,” a matter whose exact modality we do not question, yet we understand that it signifies His supreme authority, majesty, and complete control over the entire cosmos. From that position of absolute sovereignty, He is “conducting every affair,” meaning that nothing happens in the universe except by His decree and management. Every leaf that falls, every event that occurs, and every destiny that unfolds does so under His command.

The verse then states a crucial principle: “None can intercede except by His permission.” The Meccan idolaters imagined that their deities could intercede for them independently, but here Allah clarifies that intercession belongs entirely to Him; no one may speak on the Day of Judgment unless He grants them leave. This demolishes the concept of unqualified intermediaries. Following this comes the natural conclusion: “That is Allah—your Lord, so worship Him alone.” Worship must be directed only to the One who creates, sustains, and governs all affairs.

The question “Will you not then be mindful?” is an invitation to reflect, to use the intellect to recognise that the Creator of such a magnificent universe deserves exclusive devotion. For a listener today, this verse reorients the heart towards tawhid, reminding us that the control of our lives is not in the hands of employers, governments, or any created being, but solely with the One who established Himself on the Throne. It calls us to free ourselves from the fear of anything besides Him and to place our trust where true power lies.

=4=

Having established that Allah alone is the Lord and Creator, this verse directs our gaze towards the inevitable end: “To Him is your return all together.” No one is exempted, and every human being will stand before Him. This collective return underscores that all divisions of wealth, status, and power in this world will vanish, and everyone will face their Lord as an individual bearing their deeds. The statement “Allah’s promise is always true” provides absolute assurance; there is no doubt or uncertainty in what He has decreed. He will indeed gather all creation.

The verse then explains a key purpose of creation and resurrection: “He originates the creation then resurrects it so that He may justly reward those who believe and do good.” This clarifies that the world is not a meaningless cycle, but a testing ground. The combination of belief and good deeds is repeatedly emphasised, for faith without action is incomplete, and deeds without faith lack a foundation. Justice demands that those who sincerely believe and act righteously receive their reward.

In contrast, “those who disbelieve will have a boiling drink and a painful punishment for their disbelief.” The image of a boiling drink is vivid, highlighting the intensity of the suffering that awaits those who reject the truth. Notice that the reward is described as a just recompense, while the punishment is a consequence of their own choice to disbelieve.

This teaching is essential because it counters the modern tendency to assume that God’s mercy automatically erases the need for accountability. Mercy and justice are both perfectly realised with Allah; He does not wrong anyone, and the outcome is based on what souls have earned.

This verse reminds every believer that the hardships of this life are temporary and that a just and certain return awaits, encouraging steadfastness.

=5=

The signs in creation continue to be unfolded. Allah describes the sun as a “radiant source,” a blazing lamp that generates its own light and heat, and the moon as a “reflected light,” a body that borrows its illumination.

This distinction, made over fourteen centuries ago, aligns perfectly with what modern astronomy has confirmed, yet the verse’s primary purpose is not scientific but spiritual. The moon’s phases are “precisely ordained,” changing shape night after night in a flawless pattern that never fails. From this precise celestial order, humanity derives “the number of years and calculation of time.” Calendars, agricultural planning, and religious observances all depend on the movements of these heavenly bodies.

This is a practical blessing that people often take for granted. However, the verse immediately adds, “Allah did not create all this except for a purpose. ” The sun and moon are not random cosmic accidents; they serve a divine intention and point to the wisdom of their Maker. Those who look at the sky merely for navigation or weather prediction miss the deeper reality that these signs are made clear “for people of knowledge.” Knowledge here is not dry data but a profound understanding that leads to recognition of the Creator. A true person of knowledge looks at the sun’s warmth, the moon’s gentle phases, the regularity of time, and sees the attributes of Allah: His power, His precision, His mercy, and His care for creation. In a world obsessed with technology, this verse calls us back to the natural signs around us, signs that never change and always guide to the One who set them in motion. The rhythm of day and night, the months and years, all become reminders to the heart that this orderly universe has a Lord who never sleeps.

=6=

Continuing directly from the previous sign, this verse expands the scope to include “the alternation of the day and the night” and everything else Allah has created across the heavens and the earth. The alternation of day and night is something so routine that many never pause to consider it.

Yet within this cycle lie profound signs: the gradual fading of light into darkness, the way the night provides rest and the day enables activity, and the perfect balance that makes life possible. If the sun remained overhead constantly, the earth would burn; if night continued unbroken, life would freeze and perish. The precise alternation points to a designer who perfectly measured the needs of His creation.

The phrase “all that Allah has created in the heavens and the earth” encompasses the stars, the mountains, the oceans, the animals, and the intricate systems within the human body. These are not merely physical phenomena but “signs for those mindful of Him.” Mindfulness, or taqwa, is the filter through which a person sees the world correctly. A mindful person looks at a tree, a bird, or a drop of rain and immediately senses the presence of the Giver. The disbeliever sees only the material surface and thus misses the meaning. This verse encourages the development of a conscious, reflective heart that constantly links the observed world back to its source. In practical terms, a believer walking outside can turn every sight into a moment of remembrance: the clouds moving by Allah’s command, the sun setting according to His law, the crops growing from His mercy. This transforms daily life into an act of worship.

The verse also serves as a powerful response to atheism, which sees the universe as a product of blind chance. The Quran insists that the order, beauty, and purpose in creation are clear signs for those who have the humility and awareness to recognise their Lord.

=7=

After presenting the signs in creation that should lead to mindfulness, the surah now describes a group who completely ignore these signs. The verse begins with “Indeed, those who do not expect to meet Us,” identifying people who have no anticipation of standing before their Lord for judgment. Their lack of expectation is not innocent ignorance but a wilful turning away; they live as if this life is all there is.

The description continues: “being pleased and content with this worldly life. ” Their entire satisfaction, goals, and ambitions are confined to what they can see, touch, and accumulate here. They have settled for the temporary and made it their ultimate concern.

The word used for contentment suggests a deep-seated satisfaction that leaves no room for longing for the hereafter. Because they are fully occupied with worldly pleasures, they become “heedless of Our signs. ” Heedlessness, or ghaflah, is a dangerous state where the signs of Allah in scripture and in creation fail to penetrate the heart. The person sees the sun rise and set, hears the verses recited, witnesses life and death, yet none of it awakens a sense of accountability. This portrait is not limited to the Meccan idolaters of the seventh century; it describes a timeless human condition. Many people today chase careers, entertainment, and material comforts while deliberately pushing away thoughts of death and resurrection. They live entirely within the horizon of the dunya. The verse, by pausing here without yet mentioning the consequence, allows the listener to reflect: Am I among those who truly expect to meet Allah, or has my heart grown content with this fleeting world? The structure of the passage creates a moment of self-examination before the next verse unveils the frightening outcome, making the warning deeply personal and immediate.

=8=

This verse completes the condition of those described previously. The ones who did not expect the meeting with Allah, who were satisfied with the worldly life and heedless of divine signs, are given a stark recompense: “they will have the Fire as a home.

” The word “home” implies a permanent dwelling, a place of residence from which there is no departure. It is not a temporary punishment but an eternal abode. The reason is given as “because of what they have committed.” The punishment is not arbitrary; it is the direct consequence of their own actions, choices, and the deeds they sent ahead. Their crimes were not just passive ignorance but active commission of wrongdoing while deliberately ignoring the truth. Throughout the Qur’an, punishment is linked to what hands have earned, establishing perfect divine justice. For the companions of the Prophet, hearing this verse would have reinforced the urgency of preparing for the afterlife. They understood that every action, no matter how small, contributes to the record that determines one’s eternal lodging.

This teaching powerfully confronts the modern tendency to assume that a merciful God would never punish. While Allah’s mercy is vast, He is also just, and those who persistently reject His signs and commit evil without repentance have chosen their own destination.

The verse also highlights the danger of heedlessness; it is not enough to simply believe intellectually while living as if the hereafter does not matter. The conduct of a person’s life reveals what they truly expect. For a believer, this serves as a powerful motivation to examine one’s state daily, to ensure that the love of the world has not made the heart forget the inevitable meeting with its Creator. It reminds us that the temporary pleasures of this life, if they distract from Allah, are a deceptive trade for an eternal home of bliss that could have been attained.

=9=

In beautiful contrast to the previous two verses, here the fate of the believers is unveiled. “Surely those who believe and do good” combines the two essential elements of salvation: sincere faith in the heart and righteous deeds performed by the limbs. Faith that does not translate into action is weak, and deeds without faith are hollow. For such people, “their Lord will guide them to Paradise through their faith. ” The guidance here is not only the initial guidance to Islam but a special, continuous guidance that leads them across the bridge over Hell and into the gardens of eternal bliss. Their faith becomes a light that illuminates their path on the Day when all other lights will be extinguished. The verse beautifully portrays the reward: “rivers will flow under their feet in the Gardens of Bliss.

” The image of flowing rivers beneath their dwellings conveys a sense of effortless abundance, comfort, and serenity. Unlike the toil required to irrigate land in this world, in Paradise the blessings come without struggle, directly under the inhabitants’ authority. The “Gardens of Bliss” suggests a place where every form of joy, delight, and satisfaction is perfected. There is no trace of sorrow, fatigue, or fear. For the early Muslims facing persecution in Mecca, this verse offered immense hope. Their present suffering was not the end; a home of eternal ease awaited them.

This promise is equally relevant for believers today who may feel overwhelmed by hardships, moral confusion, or the glitter of a godless world. The verse assures that the path of faith and good deeds, though it may appear difficult at times, leads to an outcome that far surpasses any temporary sacrifice. It reframes the believer’s perspective: every prayer, every act of charity, every struggle to remain honest is not a burden but an investment in a future where rivers flow beneath one’s feet. The certainty of this reward transforms the way a believer faces trials, filling the heart with patience and hope.

=10=

This verse takes us inside the Gardens of Bliss to witness the very words that will fill the air. The inhabitants’ spontaneous expression begins with “Glory be to You, O Allah!” acknowledging the absolute perfection, purity, and transcendence of their Lord. In Paradise, with all veils removed, the believers will directly perceive the majesty of Allah, and the natural response is to glorify Him. There is no need for ritual obligation; glorification becomes the very breath of their existence. The greeting they exchange is “Peace!

” This is a word loaded with meaning: safety from all harm, serenity of the soul, and the complete absence of conflict, envy, or ill-feeling. It reflects the environment of Paradise, where no harsh word will ever be heard. Their closing prayer is “All praise is for Allah—Lord of all worlds! ” This phrase, which they uttered in this world during prayer and in moments of joy, becomes the eternal seal of their speech. Praise envelops the beginning and end of their experience, because they will see that every delight they enjoy is a direct gift from the Lord of all creation.

This verse teaches that the remembrance of Allah is not a burden but the highest pleasure of the believers, both in this world and the next. In this life, we taste a small sample of that sweetness when we sincerely say “SubhanAllah” and “Alhamdulillah. ” The verse also connects beautifully with the Fatihah and the daily prayers of a Muslim, showing that what we practice now is a preparation for the language of Paradise. It reminds us that our words matter; training the tongue in praise and glorification today is like rehearsing for an eternal reality.

For those who feel distant from Allah, this image of Paradise offers a powerful incentive to draw closer, for the inhabitants there are not just receiving passive pleasures; they are actively delighting in the glorification and praise of their Beloved.

=11=

This verse addresses a frequent human attitude: impatience. In moments of anger or foolishness, people sometimes call down curses or wish for immediate punishment upon themselves and others, just as they eagerly ask for blessings. The verse states, “If Allah were to hasten evil for people as they wish to hasten good, they would have certainly been doomed.” Allah, in His mercy, does not answer the impulsive calls for evil that people make. If He responded to every curse uttered in frustration or every rash challenge demanding punishment, humanity would have been destroyed long ago.

This is a profound demonstration of divine forbearance. Instead of immediate retribution, Allah grants time. Then the verse adds, “But We leave those who do not expect to meet Us to wander blindly in their defiance. ” Those who reject the afterlife are left to continue in their rebellion, not out of neglect, but as a form of respite that will ultimately justify the judgment against them.

The phrase “wander blindly” captures the spiritual disorientation of a person without the guidance of faith; they stumble from one worldly desire to another, without a clear destination or purpose. This verse serves as a warning to those who mock the concept of punishment and dare Allah to send it immediately. History is filled with individuals and nations who felt secure and demanded to see the threatened doom, only to be overtaken when they least expected it.

For the believer, this verse nurtures patience and trust in Allah’s timing. When we see evil people prospering or when we are tempted to wish immediate destruction upon oppressors, we are reminded that Allah’s wisdom governs all affairs. He grants respite for a reason, and His delay is not neglect.

The verse also calls us to guard our tongues, ensuring we do not invoke harm upon ourselves or others in moments of anger, for our Lord is kind and merciful.

=12=

This verse paints a remarkably accurate picture of a common human pattern. When distress strikes, a person becomes intensely earnest in supplication. The three postures mentioned—“lying on their side, sitting, or standing”—cover all possible states, indicating that hardship makes a person turn to Allah in every position, at all times, unable to rest or find peace. In those moments of vulnerability, the heart recognises its complete dependence on the Creator. The ego is humbled, and sincere prayers pour forth.

Yet the tragic twist comes immediately: “But when We relieve their hardship, they return to their old ways as if they had never cried to Us to remove any hardship.” As soon as the crisis passes, the memory of that desperate dependence fades. The individual goes back to the very disobedience and neglect that they had momentarily abandoned. The ingratitude is so stark that it is as if the whole episode of distress and relief never occurred.

This is not an isolated incident; it describes a widespread behaviour of those whose faith is superficial and situational. The verse concludes, “This is how the misdeeds of the transgressors have been made appealing to them.” When a person repeatedly turns away after being rescued, their spiritual perception becomes corrupted, and their evil deeds start to appear attractive and justifiable.

This is a severe warning against treating Allah as a last resort, a problem-solver to be called upon only in emergencies and forgotten once the difficulty is removed. The lesson for the believer is to maintain consistency in devotion during times of ease as well as hardship. The Prophet (peace be upon him) taught that recognising Allah in times of ease ensures His recognition of us in times of difficulty. The verse calls us to examine our own behaviour: do we run to prayer and repentance only when calamity strikes, and then drift away once comfort returns? True faith is steadfast, expressed in standing, sitting, and reclining, not just when one is knocked down by tribulation.

=13=

The surah now turns to historical precedent to reinforce the warning. The Meccan idolaters are told that they are not the first to reject a messenger. “We surely destroyed other peoples before you when they did wrong.” The destruction is not random; it is the direct consequence of their wrongdoing. The pattern is consistent: wrongdoing reaches a level where divine punishment becomes inevitable. Crucially, the verse adds, “and their messengers came to them with clear proofs but they would not believe.” This underlines that these nations were not destroyed without warning. They received messengers armed with evident signs, miracles, and convincing arguments. The rejection was stubborn and deliberate, even after the truth was made crystal clear. The “clear proofs” include intellectual arguments, moral teachings, and sometimes tangible miracles that left no room for honest doubt.

Yet they chose to disbelieve, often out of arrogance, attachment to ancestral traditions, or fear of losing power. The concluding statement, “This is how We reward the wicked people,” establishes a universal law. The reward for persistent, defiant wickedness is destruction, either in this world or the next, or both. For the Prophet’s opponents, this was a direct threat that their own behaviour mirrored those destroyed civilizations. For later generations, this verse serves as a sobering reminder that Allah’s laws regarding nations are consistent. The ruins of past civilizations, some of which remain visible, are not just archaeological curiosities; they are silent testimonies to the consequences of rejecting divine guidance. A society that normalises oppression, mocks its reformers, and turns its back on clear moral truths is walking the same path. This verse urges us to read history with the eyes of faith, to see in the stories of the past a roadmap of cause and effect that is as relevant today as it ever was, and to take heed before it is too late.

=14=

After mentioning the destruction of previous nations, the verse directly addresses the contemporaries of the Prophet, and by extension all later generations: “Then We made you their successors in the land.” The term “successors” indicates that they inherited the earth after those earlier peoples were wiped out. They now occupy the lands, live in the homes, and utilise the resources that once belonged to others. This succession is not a reward in itself but a test. The purpose is stated clearly: “to see how you would act.” Allah already knows what they will do, but this life is designed to manifest those actions so that judgment can be delivered with perfect justice.

The question implicitly posed is whether the new generation will learn from the fate of their predecessors or repeat the same errors. Will they be grateful and obedient, or will they follow the path of arrogance and transgression? This verse instils a sense of historical and moral responsibility. Every person and every community stands in a long chain of civilizations that rose and fell. The comfort and opportunities we enjoy today are not guaranteed to last. They are a test, and the outcome depends on how we act. For the early Muslims, this was a call to avoid the mistakes of the disbelievers around them and to establish a just and God-conscious society. For us, the message is equally powerful. We live in a world where nations pride themselves on technological progress while often abandoning moral and spiritual values. The verse warns that material advancement without ethical and spiritual grounding is a recipe for eventual decline. Every individual should see their life as a brief period of succession: we are given a lifespan, resources, and opportunities, and we are constantly being watched to see what we will do with them. This awareness transforms daily choices into acts of immense significance.

=15=

This verse captures a specific challenge posed by the Meccan leaders. They did not merely reject the Qur’an; they demanded a customised version that would suit their tastes and remove its challenging commands. Their request, “Bring us a different Quran or make some changes in it,” exposes their arrogance. They wanted a scripture that would accommodate their idols, their unjust social practices, or perhaps one that did not call them to account. This was not an intellectual objection but an attempt to negotiate divine revelation. The Prophet is commanded to respond with absolute firmness: “It is not for me to change it on my own.” He makes it clear that he is not the author of the Book; he is a recipient and a conveyor. He cannot alter even a single word on his own authority.

The phrase “I only follow what is revealed to me” establishes the fundamental principle that prophethood is about faithful transmission, not personal opinion. Then the Prophet expresses his own deep sense of accountability: “I fear, if I were to disobey my Lord, the punishment of a tremendous Day.” Even the Messenger, the most honourable of creation, trembles at the thought of altering divine speech. This teaches that no human being, no matter how esteemed, has the right to modify Allah’s commands. The verse refutes the modern tendency to demand that religion be “updated” to fit contemporary whims. Revelation is not a product of its time to be edited; it is eternal guidance from the Knower of all times.

For believers, it is a call to submit completely, even when a verse goes against personal desires or cultural trends. The steadfastness of the Prophet in the face of such pressure is a model for all who are tempted to compromise their principles. Truth is not subject to human approval ratings.

=16=

The response to the disbelievers continues. The Prophet is instructed to say, “Had Allah willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor would He have made it known to you.” This profound statement reminds them that the Qur’an’s arrival in their midst was entirely by Allah’s will. It was not Muhammad’s own idea, and he could not have brought it had Allah not chosen to send it. The entire phenomenon of revelation was a divine decision, and they were the recipients of an immense privilege they were now rejecting. Then comes a powerful rational argument: “I had lived my whole life among you before this revelation.” The Prophet had spent forty years among the Quraysh before the first verse was revealed. During all those years, he was known as Al-Amin, the trustworthy, and Al-Sadiq, the truthful. He had never composed poetry, never claimed any special knowledge, and never displayed ambition for leadership. The sudden appearance of this majestic scripture, with its profound wisdom, accurate accounts of previous nations, and sublime laws, was entirely out of character for a man who had lived a simple, unlettered life among them.

The question “Do you not understand?” challenges them to use their reason. They had all the evidence they needed to recognise that this could not be the product of a human mind suddenly deciding to fabricate a religion. For us today, this verse remains a powerful testimony to the divine origin of the Qur’an. The Prophet’s life is an open book; the contrast between his pre- and post-revelation existence is stark and undeniable. The verse encourages every rational person to reflect on the life of the Messenger and to conclude that the Book he brought could not have originated from him but from the One who knows all things. It is an invitation to honest intellectual inquiry that leads to faith.

=17=

This verse poses a rhetorical question that condemns the most severe form of wrongdoing. “Who does more wrong than those who fabricate lies against Allah or deny His revelations?

” The question implies that no one can surpass the injustice of these two categories. The first involves inventing false statements and attributing them to Allah, such as claiming that He has partners, that He has forbidden what He has actually permitted, or that He has commanded what He never commanded.

This is a direct assault on divine truth. The second involves rejecting the genuine revelations that Allah has sent, calling them magic, poetry, or ancient fables. Both acts stem from the same root: a willingness to twist the truth for worldly benefit. The Meccan pagans were guilty of both, as they worshipped false gods and declared the Qur’an to be forged.

The verse then delivers its verdict: “Indeed, the wicked will never succeed.” Apparent worldly success—wealth, power, popularity—is not the measure of real success. True success is safety from Allah’s punishment and entry into His mercy. Those who persist in fabricating lies and denying signs are the ultimate losers, even if they seem to be winning in this life.

The verse draws a clear line between the righteous and the wicked, and it offers no ambiguity about their destinies. For a believer, this is a reminder of the sanctity of speaking about Allah. We must be extremely careful not to attribute to the religion what is not part of it, nor to explain verses based on pure speculation without proper knowledge. It also teaches us to love and defend the truth of revelation. When the Qur’an is dismissed or mocked, we should feel the weight of the injustice being done, and it should increase our own commitment to upholding and living by those revelations. The fate of the wicked is sealed, no matter the temporary illusion of their success.

=18=

This verse exposes the absurdity of idol worship. The Meccan polytheists worshipped deities made of stone and wood, yet these objects possessed no power whatsoever. The description “others who can neither harm nor benefit them” strips the idols of any functional reality. They could not bring good to their worshippers, nor could they ward off evil.

Yet the pagans had developed a theological justification: “These are our intercessors with Allah.” They did not claim the idols were creators; they claimed these beings were intermediaries who would bring them closer to Allah and plead on their behalf.

This is a form of shirk that persists today when people attribute intermediary powers to saints, ancestors, or spiritual beings, believing they can independently intercede. The Prophet is instructed to counter this claim with a penetrating question: “Are you informing Allah of something He does not know in the heavens or the earth? ” The implication is clear: if these supposed intercessors truly existed and had such a role, Allah would certainly know about them. The fact that Allah has revealed no such thing means they are fabricating a lie. They are claiming knowledge of the unseen that Allah Himself has not disclosed.

The verse concludes, “Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate with Him! ” Allah is far above having partners, equals, or necessary intermediaries. He is near to His servants, hears their prayers directly, and needs no assistance in governing His creation. For the contemporary reader, this verse teaches the purest form of tawhid. It warns against the subtle forms of shirk that creep into practice when people begin to rely on created beings for protection, provision, or intercession in a way that belongs to Allah alone. It calls us to direct our worship, our pleas, and our ultimate trust exclusively to the One who holds all harm and benefit in His hands.

=19=

This verse provides a sweeping historical perspective. Originally, all of humanity was “a single community,” united upon the natural way of pure monotheism, the religion of Adam and the early generations. There was no polytheism, no divergent sects.

However, as time passed, “they differed. ” This difference arose due to various factors: ignorance, arrogance, the whisperings of Satan, and the corruptions introduced by human beings into the original divine message. The verse then addresses a question that naturally arises: if these differences are so severe, why does Allah not immediately judge between them and put an end to the conflict?

The answer is, “Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord, their differences would have been settled at once.” Allah’s prior decree, His wisdom and plan, involves granting respite, allowing the test to run its course, and delaying the final judgment until the appointed Day. If He had willed, He could have forced everyone into one belief or punished the wrongdoers instantly, but His wisdom dictated otherwise. This delay serves as a test and an opportunity for repentance. For the believers living in a world of religious pluralism and confusion, this verse offers profound comfort and understanding. It explains why truth and falsehood exist side by side, why arguments rage, and why clear guidance is still rejected by many. It is all part of a divine test in which every soul must make its own choice, with the consequences to follow in the hereafter.

The verse also calls believers to focus on their own guidance rather than despairing over the state of humanity. The division will continue until Allah’s decree unfolds, but the individual can still hold fast to the truth. The original unity of humanity also serves as a reminder that the differences are not inherent to our nature; they are deviations from the original state, and the mission of the prophets is to call people back to that lost unity under the worship of one God.

=20=

The disbelievers demanded a tangible, extraordinary miracle of their own choosing. They asked, “Why has no other sign been sent down to him from his Lord?” They wanted something spectacular, perhaps similar to the staff of Moses or the she-camel of Salih. Their demand was not an honest search for truth but a way to stall and justify their disbelief. The Quran itself was the greatest sign, but they refused to accept it. The Prophet is commanded to respond by directing their attention to the nature of divine signs: “The knowledge of the unseen is with Allah alone.” Miracles are not summoned by human demand; they are sent according to Allah’s infinite wisdom. He knows what signs will be effective and when they should be given. The Prophet does not have access to the unseen treasury of Allah to pull out signs at will. His role is to convey the message, not to perform miracles on command. Then the verse ends with a firm and slightly ominous note: “So wait! I too am waiting with you. ” This is both a challenge and a warning. The Prophet is told to wait for Allah’s judgment, and the disbelievers are also waiting, perhaps for his downfall or for the sign they demand. Each party will see the outcome.

This statement carries a subtle threat: the disbelievers are waiting for something, but what will eventually come is either guidance if they repent or the painful consequences of their stubbornness. For the believer, this verse instils patience and trust in Allah’s timing. We are not to be shaken by demands from skeptics for constant proofs tailored to their whims. The truth stands on its own, and the final judgment belongs to Allah.

The verse also teaches a powerful etiquette: we should not speculate recklessly about matters of the unseen or presume that we know better than Allah when and how His signs should appear. We wait with firm faith, knowing that Allah’s plan is always perfect.
