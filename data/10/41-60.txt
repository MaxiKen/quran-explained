=41=

Having been told that some will eventually believe and others will persist in corruption, the Prophet is now given a clear stance to adopt towards those who reject his message. The denial mentioned here is not mere intellectual disagreement but a deliberate, obstinate rejection after the truth has been presented. The instruction “say” provides the exact words of disengagement. “My deeds are mine and your deeds are yours” establishes a principle of individual moral responsibility. Every person’s actions belong to them, and they will bear the consequences alone. The Prophet is not responsible for the disbelief of his people, just as they are not accountable for his faith.

This is not a declaration of indifference but a statement of fact regarding the Day of Judgment. The phrase “You are free of what I do and I am free of what you do” further emphasises the separation. The disbelievers are absolved of any responsibility for the Prophet’s worship and obedience to Allah, and he is absolved of their idolatry and sins. This mutual dissociation is a form of warning: the Prophet’s way is clear, and their way is clear; each will see the outcome.

This verse also carries a lesson in dealing with persistent rejection. After exhausting all efforts of persuasion, a believer may need to step back and leave the matter to Allah, without rancour or continued argument that yields no benefit. The verse does not command hatred or enmity but a dignified separation.

Historically, this was a stage before the later rulings on relations with non-Muslims. The Prophet maintained his character and justice, but he made it known that the religious paths had diverged. In today’s world, this verse teaches believers to uphold their principles firmly while respecting the freedom of conscience of others. We cannot compel belief, and we should not entangle our identity in the approval of those who reject the truth. Our deeds are ours, offered sincerely to Allah, and their rejection does not diminish their value or our commitment.

=42=

The surah now describes the disbelievers’ attitude towards the Qur’anic message. There were people who physically heard the Prophet reciting the verses, sitting in his gatherings and listening to the words.

Yet their hearing did not translate into understanding or acceptance. The verse compares them to the deaf. The rhetorical question “can you make the deaf hear even though they do not understand?” highlights the Prophet’s human limitations. He cannot force the words to penetrate hearts that have shut themselves off. The deafness here is not a physical condition but a spiritual one. Their ears function perfectly, but the inner faculty that processes and accepts truth is disabled by arrogance and heedlessness. The addition of “even though they do not understand” intensifies the point. They lack not only the ability to hear spiritually but also the willingness to use their intellect to comprehend what is being said. The verse consoles the Prophet that his inability to guide them is not a failure on his part; it is a consequence of their own chosen state. This also serves as a warning to all listeners of the Qur’an. Hearing the recitation with the ears is not enough. The heart and mind must be engaged. A person who listens to verses for years without reflection and change is like a deaf person before the divine speech.

For the believer, this verse is a call to active listening. When the Qur’an is recited, we should be fully present, seeking to understand and absorb, not merely letting the sounds wash over us.

The verse also teaches that guidance is ultimately in Allah’s hands. The Prophet, despite his deep care for his people, could not grant them spiritual hearing. This humbles every preacher and teacher; we deliver the message, but only Allah opens hearts. The imagery of deafness reminds us to pray constantly for ears that truly hear and minds that truly comprehend.

=43=

This verse parallels the previous one, moving from the metaphor of deafness to that of blindness. The disbelievers looked at the Prophet with their physical eyes. They saw his honesty, his noble character, his miracles, and the signs of truthfulness in his life.

Yet they did not perceive him as a messenger. The question “can you guide the blind even though they cannot see?” asserts that spiritual blindness cannot be cured by a human being acting alone. The blindness is a refusal to see the obvious evidence before them. They saw the Prophet every day, witnessed the transformation of those who believed, observed the Qur’an’s impact, but their inner vision was clouded.

The verse also distinguishes between looking and seeing. They “look at you” but they do not truly see.

This is a profound insight into human psychology. Two people can observe the same reality; one sees signs pointing to Allah, the other sees nothing remarkable. The difference lies in the state of the heart. The Prophet is comforted that he cannot force spiritual sight upon those who have chosen blindness.

The verse also implies that the disbelievers’ problem is not a lack of external evidence. The proof was standing right in front of them in the person of the Prophet, yet they remained in darkness.

For believers, this verse calls for self-examination. Do we merely look at the signs of Allah in creation and scripture, or do we truly see them? The Qur’an repeatedly invites humanity to “travel through the land” and “observe,” but observation without insight is useless. We must cultivate the inner eye of faith that discerns divine wisdom behind appearances.

This verse also teaches us that when we face those who mock or reject clear truth, we should not exhaust ourselves trying to force them to see. We continue to present the message with wisdom, but we recognise that only Allah can remove the blindness that covers hearts.

=44=

This verse establishes a foundational principle of divine justice. After describing those who are spiritually deaf and blind, the question might arise: is Allah being unfair to them by not guiding them?

The answer is an emphatic negation: “Indeed, Allah does not wrong people in the least.” The word “least” translates mithqāla dharratin, the weight of an atom, indicating that not even the smallest conceivable injustice occurs from Allah. His judgment is perfect, and every decree is based on His knowledge and wisdom. Any punishment or withholding of guidance is a consequence of prior actions and choices made by the individuals themselves.

The second part, “but it is people who wrong themselves,” clarifies the source of the problem. The wrongdoing is self-inflicted. By choosing disbelief, arrogance, and sin, people corrupt their own souls, block the path of guidance, and merit the consequences. No external force compels them; they exercise their free will to their own detriment. This verse is crucial for understanding the balance between divine decree and human responsibility. It refutes the idea that Allah arbitrarily condemns some to misguidance. Those who become spiritually deaf and blind do so because they first turned away, suppressed the truth, and persisted in wrongdoing. The harm they suffer is from their own hands.

This teaching is liberating for the believer because it affirms that Allah is never the cause of anyone’s ruin. It also instills a deep sense of personal accountability. We cannot blame fate, society, or even Satan for our spiritual failures; the ultimate responsibility lies with our own choices. The verse calls us to constant self-reckoning, to catch any tendency toward self-destructive behaviour before it hardens into a permanent state. It also fills the heart with trust in Allah’s justice; when we see people in different states of guidance, we know that Allah’s treatment of each soul is flawlessly fair.

=45=

The scene shifts to the Day of Gathering to illustrate how the long span of worldly life will appear in retrospect. When all of humanity is assembled for judgment, the duration of their earthly existence will seem utterly trivial. “It will be as if they had not stayed in the world except for an hour of a day.” A single hour is a tiny fraction of a day, and this comparison shrinks decades of life into a fleeting moment. The feelings of waiting, struggling, and planning that consumed them will evaporate, and they will realise how little time they truly had.

The phrase “as though they were only getting to know one another” suggests that the social relationships and networks they built in the world will feel as superficial and brief as a casual meeting between strangers. The deep bonds they thought were permanent will seem like passing acquaintances. This perception is both a mercy and a torment: for the believers, the brevity makes their sacrifices feel light; for the disbelievers, it magnifies the tragedy of what they lost for the sake of such a short worldly enjoyment.

The verse then pronounces the loss: “Lost indeed will be those who denied the meeting with Allah, and were not rightly guided.” Denying the meeting is the root cause of their ruin. Because they did not take the afterlife seriously, they squandered their brief life. The loss is total, irreversible, and eternal. This verse serves as a powerful wake-up call. It reorients our sense of time. A lifetime of eighty years, when viewed from the perspective of eternity, is indeed shorter than an hour. Every believer should frequently bring this image to mind, especially when tempted by sin or when feeling that acts of worship are burdensome. The temporary discomfort of discipline is nothing compared to the eternal outcome.

The verse also fosters detachment from excessive worldly concerns, reminding us that the relationships and possessions we cling to will one day feel as fleeting as a short encounter.

=46=

This verse addresses a question that might arise in the Prophet’s mind: will he live to see the punishment of the disbelievers or not? The answer provides comfort regardless of the outcome. “Whether We show you some of what We threaten them with” refers to the possibility that the Prophet would witness, during his lifetime, the worldly punishment of his opponents. This indeed happened at the Battle of Badr, where the leaders of Quraysh were slain. Alternatively, “or cause you to die before that” acknowledges that the Prophet might pass away before seeing their full punishment. Neither scenario diminishes the certainty of the threat. The return of all people is to Allah alone, and He is the Witness over their deeds. The punishment will unfold in its appointed time, whether in this world or the next, and the Prophet’s life or death does not affect its inevitability.

This verse teaches an important principle: a believer’s mission is to deliver the message and live righteously, not necessarily to see the final outcome of every conflict. Victory and punishment are in Allah’s hands, and His timing is perfect. The Prophet is relieved of any anxiety about witnessing the destruction of his enemies. His role is to warn, and Allah will hold everyone accountable. For modern believers, this verse offers solace when we are troubled by the apparent triumph of evil. We may not live to see the downfall of tyrants or the vindication of truth in our societies, but that does not mean justice has been denied. All will return to Allah, and He is the ultimate Witness. Nothing escapes His record. This perspective frees us from the obsession with immediate results and places our trust in the long-term justice of the Hereafter. It also reminds us that our own death is a return to Allah, where our deeds will be witnessed and judged with perfect accuracy.

=47=

This verse establishes a universal principle of divine justice in relation to human accountability. “For every community there is a messenger” means that no nation or people group has ever been left without guidance. Throughout history, Allah sent prophets to every community, conveying the essential message of tawhid and moral conduct in a language they understood. This ensures that no one can claim on the Day of Judgment that they were unaware of the truth. The sending of messengers is a manifestation of Allah’s mercy and justice.

The verse then explains the sequence: “After their messenger has come, judgment is passed on them in all fairness.” The arrival of a messenger marks the establishment of proof. Once the message is clearly delivered, those who accept it are saved, and those who reject it face the consequences. The judgment is “in all fairness” because it is based on their response to a clear and accessible warning. The concluding phrase “and they are not wronged” reinforces that the punishment that follows is never an act of oppression but a just recompense. This verse has profound implications. It confirms that the Quraysh could not claim ignorance; the Prophet had been sent to them with a clear message. For later times, it implies that the accountability of people who have not received the message of Islam in an undistorted form is a matter left to Allah’s perfect justice, which will consider all circumstances. It also places a responsibility on the Muslim ummah to continue conveying the message, so that the proof is established for people of our time. No community should be abandoned without access to the call of tawhid. This verse balances the exclusivity of salvation through Islam with the universal fairness of Allah, reminding us that He never punishes without first sending a warner.

=48=

The disbelievers’ mockery is once again brought to the forefront. They did not merely reject the idea of punishment; they sarcastically demanded its immediate occurrence. Their question, “When will this threat come to pass if what you say is true?” is not a sincere inquiry about timing but a taunt meant to undermine the believers’ confidence. By asking for a specific date, they believed they were exposing the emptiness of the warnings. This attitude is common among those who deny the afterlife; they treat the unseen as unreal and challenge believers to produce it on demand.

The question reveals their arrogance and their misunderstanding of divine wisdom. Allah’s threats are not subject to human timelines. The delay is not a sign of weakness or falsehood but a deliberate period of respite. The verse exposes their psychology: they want to believe that because the punishment is not immediate, it will never come.

This is a dangerous delusion. Throughout history, nations who mocked their warners were eventually overtaken when they least expected it. The verse does not provide an immediate answer to the “when” question; instead, it leaves the taunt hanging, allowing the following verse to address it properly.

For believers, this verse serves as a reminder that mockery of divine warnings is a recurring pattern and that we should not be shaken by it. The delay of punishment is a mercy, giving the mockers time to reconsider. It also tests the believers’ patience and trust. When we see people flouting Allah’s commands without immediate consequence, we should remember that the question “when?” has been asked by every generation of skeptics, and the answer has always been the same: at the appointed time, which no one knows except Allah. Our task is to remain steadfast and not be goaded into doubt by taunts.

=49=

In response to the taunting question, the Prophet is instructed to clarify his own human limitations. He is to say, “I have no power to benefit or protect myself, except by the Will of Allah.

” This is a profound declaration of dependence. Even the Messenger, the best of creation, cannot independently bring about benefit or ward off harm. If he cannot control his own fate, how can he be expected to dictate the timing of universal punishment? The timing of all events, including the arrival of divine punishment, rests solely with Allah.

This statement demolishes any notion of the Prophet possessing divine powers. He is a servant and a messenger, nothing more.

The verse then provides the actual answer to the “when” question: “For each community there is an appointed term.” Every nation, and indeed every individual, has a fixed lifespan and a decreed moment for their accountability to begin, whether in this life or the next. This term is known only to Allah and is governed by His infinite wisdom.

The verse adds, “When their time arrives, they cannot delay it for a moment, nor could they advance it. ” The precision of divine timing is absolute. There is no flexibility, no escape, and no early arrival. The disbelievers cannot force the punishment to come sooner through their taunts, nor can they postpone it when it finally descends.

This teaching brings immense peace to the believer. We live within a schedule set by the All-Wise. Our trials, our successes, and even the judgment of our oppressors are all bound by an appointed term. We are not at the mercy of random events or human whims. It also warns the heedless that the delay they enjoy should not be mistaken for permanent safety. The appointed term is approaching, moment by moment, and when it strikes, it will be neither early nor late, but exactly on time.

=50=

The response to the mockers continues with a vivid scenario designed to awaken their fear. The Prophet is told to ask them to “imagine if His torment were to overcome you by night or day.

” The mention of night and day is significant because the punishment of Allah can descend at any moment, without warning. At night, when people are asleep and feel secure, sudden destruction could strike. During the day, when they are engaged in their work and think they are safe, the torment could overtake them. The choice of these two times covers all possible states, emphasising that there is never a moment of guaranteed safety for those who defy Allah. The rhetorical question follows: “do the wicked realize what they are really asking Him to hasten?” They mockingly demand the punishment, but they have no idea of its true nature. What they are asking for is not a light matter that can be brushed off; it is a devastating calamity that will utterly destroy them. Their words are frivolous, but the reality behind the threat is terrifying. This verse is a powerful reminder to never be flippant about divine punishment. Sometimes people jokingly challenge fate or make light of hellfire. Such attitudes reflect a dangerous heedlessness. The Prophet is instructed to make them visualise the very thing they mock, stripping away their false sense of security.

For the believer, the verse instills a healthy fear of Allah’s punishment and a deep appreciation for the respite He grants. Every additional moment of life is an opportunity to repent and turn back. The fact that we are not struck down in the night or in the middle of the day is a mercy, not a sign that the threat is empty. This verse calls us to use the time we have wisely, while the torment is still held back.

=51=

This verse continues the rebuke by anticipating the possible reaction of the disbelievers when the punishment actually arrives. In the moment of overwhelming terror, some might desperately declare belief, hoping to avert the doom. The rhetorical question “Will you believe in it only after it has overtaken you?” exposes the futility of such last-minute faith. Belief at the point of death or when the punishment is already unfolding is not accepted, as established in other verses of the Qur’an. The door of repentance closes when the final signs appear or when the soul reaches the throat.

The word “Now?” is an expression of rebuke and astonishment. At the moment of crisis, they suddenly want to believe, but it is too late. The time for voluntary faith was the period of respite they had been given, which they squandered in mockery and demands for hastening.

The verse then reminds them: “But you always wanted to hasten it!” This is a sharp irony. They spent their days asking for the punishment to come quickly, treating it as a joke. Now that it has arrived, their panic-stricken belief is worthless. The verse illustrates the tragic consequence of taking Allah’s warnings lightly.

This teaching is extremely relevant. It warns against the common human habit of delaying repentance. Many people think they will turn to Allah “someday,” perhaps when they are older or when they feel ready. But no one knows when their appointed term will arrive. Death can come suddenly, and with it the end of the opportunity to believe and do good.

The verse also speaks to those who mock religious concepts of judgment; their mockery will not change the reality, and when the reality confronts them, their words will be of no use. For the believer, this is a call to immediate and continuous repentance, to live each day as if the punishment could come by night or day, and to be prepared before it is too late.

=52=

This verse delivers the final verdict spoken to the wrongdoers as they enter their punishment. The phrase “Taste the torment of eternity!” is a command that seals their fate.

The word “taste” suggests a direct, personal, and experiential encounter with the punishment; it is not a distant threat but a present, consuming reality. The qualifier “of eternity” emphasises that this is not a temporary cleansing but an everlasting state. The horror of eternal punishment is beyond human comprehension, and this verse forces the listener to confront it directly.

The second part of the address provides the justification: “Are you not rewarded except for what you used to commit?” This rhetorical question establishes the perfect justice of the recompense. It is not an arbitrary infliction but a precise reward—a wage, so to speak—for the deeds they freely chose to do.

The word “rewarded” here is used in an ironic sense; what they earned through their actions was this torment. They are getting exactly what their hands sent ahead. There is no exaggeration, no unfairness. The verse echoes the Qur’anic principle that on the Day of Judgment, no soul will be wronged, and every soul will be paid in full for what it did.

For the believer, this verse serves as both a warning and a confirmation of divine justice. The warning is clear: the sins we commit are not trivial; they are accumulating a “reward” that we will have to face if we do not repent. The confirmation is that Allah’s judgment is based on evidence and deeds, not on whim. This should motivate a constant examination of our actions. Every deed is a seed that will be harvested.

The verse also silences any argument that eternal punishment is unjust; it is the fair wage for a lifetime of wilful rejection and evil, and Allah knows the true weight of each sin. The believers, hearing this, cling more tightly to the hope of mercy while never underestimating the seriousness of their own shortcomings.

=53=

The disbelievers, still wavering between doubt and mockery, approach the Prophet with a question that seeks final confirmation. They ask, “Is this true?” referring to the resurrection, the judgment, the paradise, and the hellfire that have been described. The tone likely carried a mixture of skepticism and a demand for an absolute assurance. The Prophet is commanded to respond with the most emphatic language possible. “Yes, by my Lord!” The oath by Allah, using the intimate “my Lord,” underscores the personal conviction and the direct relationship the Prophet has with the source of truth. It leaves no room for ambiguity.

The repetition in “Most certainly it is true!” piles emphasis upon emphasis. Every word in the Arabic construction conveys certainty. The Prophet is not offering a philosophical argument or a possibility; he is declaring a fact as real as the ground beneath their feet.

The final statement, “And you will have no escape,” removes any remaining hope the disbelievers might harbour of avoiding the reality. Escape from divine judgment is impossible. There will be no ransom, no hiding, and no evasion. The verse paints a scene of the Prophet as a fearless warner, standing firm on the truth despite the pressure of a hostile society. His certainty is anchored in revelation, and he transmits it without dilution.

For believers today, this verse is a model of conviction. When the world asks us, “Is it true?” with a sarcastic or skeptical tone, our response should be rooted in the same unwavering certainty. Our faith is not a guess or a cultural tradition; it is the truth from the Lord of the worlds. The oath “by my Lord” teaches us that swearing by Allah to affirm the truth of His promises is a sign of faith, not doubt. And the closing warning about no escape should be carried in our hearts as we navigate life, ensuring we never build our plans on the assumption that we can somehow outrun the consequences of our actions.

=54=

The previous verse’s declaration of no escape is now illustrated with a vivid picture of the wrongdoers’ desperation. “If every wrongdoer were to possess everything in the world”—meaning all the wealth, resources, and treasures that exist on earth—and even double that amount, they would offer it all to ransom themselves from the punishment. The intensity of the suffering makes any material sacrifice seem trivial. A person who spent their life obsessed with accumulating wealth would instantly give up every penny to be saved, but the offer would not be accepted. This highlights that on that Day, worldly assets have zero value; only deeds matter.

The verse then describes their internal state: “They will hide their remorse when they see the torment.” The remorse they feel is overwhelming regret, a crushing grief over the life they wasted and the sins they committed. But they will attempt to hide it, perhaps out of shame, or because expressing it is futile. The torment leaves no room for public mourning or catharsis; the regret is a silent, burning pain within. Despite this severe outcome, the verse reaffirms divine justice: “And they will be judged in all fairness, and none will be wronged.” The punishment, however horrific, is the exact measure of what they earned. This balance is crucial to the Qur’anic worldview; hell is not a torture chamber of a capricious deity but a just recompense for specific, wilful evils.

For the believer, this verse serves as a sobering meditation. The image of a person willing to trade all of Earth’s riches for escape should make us reevaluate our own priorities. The wealth we chase will one day seem utterly worthless. The hidden remorse is a warning against ignoring the pangs of conscience today; it is better to feel the pain of repentance now, which leads to forgiveness, than to hide a useless remorse later. The verse instills a profound sense of accountability and a deep gratitude for the opportunity to repent before it is too late.

=55=

The discourse returns to foundational truths after the vivid scenes of judgment. “Surely to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth.

” This is the bedrock of tawhid, the declaration of absolute ownership. Everything that exists, from the highest star to the smallest grain of sand, belongs exclusively to Allah. He is the Sovereign, and His ownership is intrinsic, not acquired. This means He has the absolute right to command, forbid, reward, and punish. No one can question His authority or lay claim to anything as an independent owner. The natural conclusion of this ownership is the second part of the verse: “Surely Allah’s promise is always true. ” Because the entire universe is under His command, His promise of resurrection, judgment, and the hereafter is not a distant hope or a myth; it is an inevitable reality that the Owner of all things will bring to pass.

The word “always” emphasises that His promise was never broken in the past and will never be broken in the future. The verse then laments, “but most of them do not know.” This lack of knowledge is not simple ignorance but a failure to internalise the implications of divine ownership. If they truly knew and felt that everything belongs to Allah, they would also know that His promise is unbreakable. This verse serves as a cure for doubts about the afterlife. When a believer is tempted to think that the world is chaotic and justice will never prevail, they should recall that Allah owns everything. The One who holds the entire cosmos in His grip can certainly fulfil His word. The ignorance of “most of them” is a reminder that the majority opinion is not a measure of truth. The believer aligns themselves with the knowledge that comes from revelation, even if it places them among a minority. The verse calls us to deepen our yaqeen (certainty) by reflecting on Allah’s ownership of the universe, letting that certainty anchor our souls amidst the storms of doubt around us.

=56=

Continuing the theme of Allah’s absolute power and the certainty of the promise, this verse focuses on the most personal and undeniable reality: life and death. “He is the One Who gives life and causes death.” Every living being owes its existence to Allah’s creative act. The moment of birth, when a lifeless matter becomes a conscious soul, is a miracle that no human power can replicate. Similarly, the moment of death, when the soul is taken and the body becomes inert, is entirely under His control. No one can grant themselves life, and no one can permanently escape death. These two phenomena frame the entirety of human experience and serve as constant reminders of human dependence on the Creator.

The phrase is not merely a statement of fact but a declaration of exclusive divine prerogative. The false gods of the Meccans could neither give life nor cause death, highlighting their worthlessness.

The verse then draws the logical conclusion: “and to Him you will all be returned.” The same Lord who gave life the first time and decreed death will bring you back to life for the final accounting. The return is inevitable, just as the initial creation was. This verse provides a simple, powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who originated life from nothing can easily repeat the process. The cycle of life and death we witness every day is a foretaste of the greater resurrection.

For the believer, this verse should be a constant companion in thought. The awareness that Allah gave us life out of nothing should fill us with gratitude. The certainty of death should keep us prepared and humble. And the certainty of the return should shape every decision, reminding us that we are travelling towards an inescapable meeting with the One who gave us existence. It strips away the illusion of self-sufficiency and anchors the heart in the ultimate reality of our origin and destiny.

=57=

After the powerful arguments about life, death, and return, this verse turns with a direct and compassionate address to all of humanity. “O humanity!” is a call that transcends time and place, inviting every person to reflect on what is being offered. “Indeed, there has come to you a warning from your Lord.” The warning refers to the Qur’an itself, which alerts people to the consequences of their actions and the reality of the hereafter.

The use of “your Lord” carries a tone of care and authority; He is not a distant tyrant but the Sustainer who is warning out of mercy. The verse then enumerates four qualities of the Qur’an. First, it is “a warning,” alerting against harm. Second, it is “a cure for what is in the hearts. ” The hearts are afflicted with diseases such as doubt, hypocrisy, envy, greed, and despair. The Qur’an provides the remedy that cleanses these spiritual illnesses, replacing them with certainty, sincerity, contentment, and hope. Third, it is “a guide,” showing the straight path in matters of belief, worship, and conduct. Fourth, it is “a mercy for the believers. ” While the warning and guidance are universally presented, the full experience of the Qur’an as a mercy is reserved for those who accept and follow it. For them, every verse becomes a source of comfort, wisdom, and elevation. This verse is a comprehensive summary of the Qur’an’s role in human life. It is not merely a book of laws or history; it is a complete package for the well-being of the soul. In a world where mental and spiritual distress is widespread, this verse directs people to the divine cure. The ailments of the heart—anxiety, loss of purpose, moral confusion—find their resolution in the guidance of the Qur’an. The believer should approach the Qur’an with this awareness, seeking it as a patient seeks medicine, with urgency and trust, knowing that every recitation is a dose of divine mercy.

=58=

Following the description of the Qur’an as a mercy, the Prophet is commanded to direct the believers’ joy towards its true source. The command “Say” instructs the Prophet to teach this attitude. “In Allah’s grace and mercy let them rejoice.” The grace refers to the bounty of Allah, particularly the sending of the Prophet and the revelation of the Qur’an. The mercy encompasses all the benefits, guidance, and forgiveness that flow from this divine communication. The believers are being told that their real cause for celebration and happiness should be this spiritual treasure, not the material gains of the world.

The verse then draws a comparison: “That is far better than whatever wealth they amass.” People naturally rejoice when they accumulate wealth, property, or worldly success. But the Qur’an asserts that the grace and mercy of Allah are incomparably superior. Wealth is temporary, can be lost, and will be left behind at death. The divine gift of guidance, however, benefits a person in this life and for eternity. This verse reorients the human concept of prosperity. The true measure of richness is not a bank balance but the presence of faith and the Qur’an in one’s life. A believer who has nothing materially but possesses the Qur’an is wealthier than a disbeliever who owns the world but is devoid of guidance.

This teaching is profoundly countercultural today, as consumer society constantly pushes people to define happiness by acquisition. The verse does not forbid rejoicing in lawful worldly provisions, but it establishes a hierarchy of joy. The primary joy of the heart should be in Allah and His gifts of revelation and faith. When a believer wakes up each day, the greatest reason to be happy is that they are among those who have been given this grace and mercy. This perspective shields the heart from jealousy and discontent, because the most valuable asset is already secured.

=59=

The surah now confronts another form of the disbelievers’ corruption: their arbitrary tampering with divine law. The Prophet is instructed to ask them about “that which Allah has sent down for you as a provision.” This refers to the various types of food and livestock that Allah created for human benefit. The Meccan pagans had invented their own rules, declaring some animals and crops lawful and others unlawful based on their superstitions and traditions. They would, for example, dedicate certain livestock to their idols and forbid their consumption.

The question “Have you seen” is a challenge to produce evidence or divine sanction for these practices. The second part of the verse delivers the decisive rebuttal: “Has Allah given you authorization, or are you fabricating lies against Allah?” The authority to declare what is lawful and unlawful belongs exclusively to Allah. No human being, no matter their status, has the right to legislate religious prohibitions without divine permission. By doing so, the pagans were effectively claiming a role that belongs to God alone, which is a grave form of shirk. They were fabricating lies against Allah, attributing to Him laws He never revealed.

This verse establishes a fundamental principle of Islamic jurisprudence: the halal and haram are determined solely by Allah through His revelation. No priest, scholar, or government can make something religiously forbidden or obligatory beyond what Allah has decreed.

The verse also condemns the human tendency to impose arbitrary religious restrictions based on cultural taboos or misguided piety, while neglecting what Allah actually commands. For today’s believers, this verse is a call to return to the pure sources of law—the Qur’an and authentic Sunnah—and to avoid adding or subtracting from the religion. Innovations that declare the lawful as unlawful, or vice versa, are a continuation of the same transgression. It also encourages us to be grateful for the generous provisions Allah has sent down, rather than restricting them based on unfounded superstitions.

=60=

This verse follows the accusation of fabrication by asking a terrifying rhetorical question. “What do those who fabricate lies against Allah expect on Judgment Day?” They may enjoy their invented laws and false beliefs in this world, but what is their expectation for the Day when all lies will be exposed?

The question is meant to jolt the conscience. They cannot have any good expectation; the only thing awaiting those who deliberately lie about Allah is a severe punishment. The verse does not answer directly, leaving the imagination to fill in the grim reality. Then comes a beautiful balance: “Surely Allah is ever Bountiful to humanity.” Despite the grave sin of fabricating lies against Him, Allah continues to shower humanity with provisions, health, time, and opportunities to repent. His bounty is vast and continuous, extended to the believer and the disbeliever alike. This bounty includes the sending of messengers and the clear warning against fabrications.

The verse ends with the sad observation: “but most of them are ungrateful.” The appropriate response to such immense bounty is gratitude, which includes recognising the Giver, obeying His commands, and not inventing lies in His name.

However, most people respond with ingratitude, either by denying the bounty altogether or by using it to fuel their rebellion. This verse highlights the contrast between Allah’s generous character and humanity’s widespread thanklessness. It serves as a warning to those who alter religion for personal gain or cultural convenience: what is your expectation on the Day of Judgment? And it encourages believers to be among the grateful few who acknowledge Allah’s bounty by following His authentic guidance.

The verse also reframes our understanding of divine patience; the fact that fabricators are not struck down immediately is a sign of His bounty, not His approval. Every day of respite is an invitation to gratitude and truthfulness, and the wise are those who respond before the Day when expectations are realised.
