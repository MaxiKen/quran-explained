=21=

The refrain returns for the fourth time, now following the description of the two waters and the barrier between them. The merging of fresh and salt water, and the invisible barrier that keeps them distinct, is a marvel of divine engineering. It is a favour that sustains both terrestrial and marine life. The freshwater rivers quench the thirst of humans, animals, and crops. The salt seas regulate the climate, provide a home for countless species, and offer a means of travel and trade. The barrier between them ensures that neither ecosystem is destroyed. This delicate balance is not a random occurrence; it is a deliberate act of mercy from Ar-Rahman.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” challenges the listener to look at the coastline, the river mouth, the vast ocean, and to see a coordinated, intelligent design. To attribute this to blind natural forces is to deny the favour of the One who set the forces in motion. The refrain is a call to awaken from the slumber of familiarity. The meeting of the waters is something that humans have witnessed for millennia, but how many have truly seen it as a sign? The refrain forces the mind to stop, to reconsider, to move from passive observation to active recognition. The believer, upon hearing this, renews their commitment to see the world as a tapestry of divine favours. They hear the question and answer with their heart: “I deny none of them, my Lord. All of this is from Your mercy.

” The repetition of the refrain also creates a cumulative effect. With each occurrence, the weight of the unanswered question grows heavier on the denier. How many favours can one list before the absurdity of denial becomes overwhelming? The surah is building a case, and the refrain is the attorney’s relentless question, leaving the defendant, the human and jinn, no room to escape. The mercy in the refrain itself is that it is asked in this world, where it can still lead to repentance. It is a merciful interrogation, designed to save, not to condemn. The barrier between the waters, a silent, obedient servant, becomes a teacher, and the refrain is the lesson’s summary: do not deny the favours of your Lord.

=22=

The surah continues to list the treasures that emerge from the two seas. From both the fresh and salt water, or more specifically from the merged waters where rivers meet the sea, come forth pearls and coral.

The Arabic “yakhruju minhuma al-lu’lu’u wa al-marjan” states that both pearls and coral are produced from these waters. Pearls are formed by molluscs in both freshwater and saltwater environments. They are objects of exquisite beauty, formed from a grain of sand that the oyster or mussel coats with layers of lustrous nacre. Coral is the intricate, colourful skeleton of tiny marine polyps, building vast reefs that are among the most biodiverse ecosystems on the planet. Some scholars have interpreted “marjan” as referring to small, precious stones or red coral, which has been prized as jewellery for millennia. These are hidden treasures, lying beneath the surface of the water, brought forth by the mercy of Ar-Rahman for human beings to discover, admire, and utilise. The production of pearls and coral is a double sign. On the one hand, it is a sign of the wealth and beauty that Allah has placed in the natural world, a gift of adornment and economic value. The pearl, with its soft, iridescent glow, has been a symbol of purity and luxury across cultures. Coral, with its vibrant red and pink hues, has been carved into beads and amulets. These are gifts from the sea, provided without human effort, waiting for the pearl diver to risk the depths. On the other hand, the formation of a pearl inside a living creature is a sign of the transformation of irritation into beauty. The mollusc takes a wound, a foreign particle, and turns it into a gem.

This is a metaphor for the believer’s life: the trials and irritations of life, when processed with faith and patience, become pearls of wisdom and character. The believer, seeing a pearl or a piece of coral, sees not just a beautiful object but a sign of the Creator’s ability to bring forth beauty from the depths, light from darkness, value from what is hidden. It is a reminder that Ar-Rahman’s treasures are not only on the surface; they are in the deep places, both in the ocean and in the soul.

=23=

The refrain returns for the fifth time, prompted by the pearls and coral. These beautiful, precious objects are unmistakable favours from the Lord. They adorn the necks of queens and the fingers of brides. They provide livelihoods for fishermen and divers. They inspire awe and wonder.

The question is: can anyone deny that these are blessings from Ar-Rahman? The origin of pearls and coral is a mystery hidden beneath the waves. The human being did not invent the pearl; they discovered it. The process that creates a pearl inside a living creature is not a human invention. It is a natural wonder, a sign of a Creator who delights in beauty and who wishes to share that beauty with His creation. To deny this favour is to be blind to the generosity of the Giver. The refrain challenges the materialist worldview that reduces the pearl to a mere biochemical accident. The lustre, the shape, the very concept of a gem forming inside an oyster, all point to an Artist, not an accident. The believer, hearing this refrain, feels a personal connection to the pearl. It is a gift, wrapped in the shell of the ocean, placed there by Ar-Rahman for them to find.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” is a celebration of divine artistry. It invites the listener to take sides: either you acknowledge the Artist and live in a world full of meaning and gratitude, or you deny the Artist and live in a world of dead, meaningless matter.

The repetition of the refrain here, after the mention of pearls and coral, adds an aesthetic dimension to the surah’s argument. The favours of the Lord are not only practical, like grain and fruit; they are also beautiful, like pearls. This reveals that Ar-Rahman cares for the human need for beauty, for adornment, for the delight of the eyes. The believer’s heart swells with appreciation, and they answer the question with a silent vow to never deny any of these favours, but to enjoy them in gratitude and to use them as reminders of the One who gave them.

=24=

The surah now draws attention to a human-made object that is, paradoxically, a sign of divine favour: the ship. To Allah belong the ships that sail through the sea, with their raised sails, looking like mountains on the horizon.

The Arabic “wa lahu al-jawar al-munsha’atu fi al-bahri ka al-a’lam” describes the great sailing vessels, their sails billowing in the wind, moving gracefully across the vast ocean. These ships are created by human hands, yet they belong to Allah.

This is because the materials they are built from, the intelligence that designed them, the physical laws that allow them to float, the wind that propels them, and the sea that bears them, all belong to Allah. The human builder is merely an assembler of divine favours. The comparison to mountains is powerful. A mountain is the ultimate symbol of stability and permanence. A ship, by contrast, is fragile, temporary, and at the mercy of the waves.

Yet, from a distance, a tall ship can resemble a mountain moving across the water. This image evokes the majesty and grace of these vessels, but also their vulnerability. The ship that looks like a mountain can be reduced to splinters by a storm. Both the ship and the mountain are under the command of Ar-Rahman. The ship is a mobile mercy. It carries goods and people across the seas, connecting continents, facilitating trade, and enabling the pilgrimage. The Quraysh, who were a mercantile people, knew the importance of the ship intimately. Their winter and summer journeys, their trade caravans that brought wealth to Mecca, were mirrored by the ships of other nations that sailed the seas. The ship is a sign of human cooperation with divine law. By understanding the principles of buoyancy, wind, and navigation, humans are able to traverse the oceans. But that ability is itself a gift. The believer, seeing a ship or even a picture of one, reflects on the journey of life. The believer’s soul is like a ship sailing on the ocean of destiny, propelled by the winds of divine mercy, guided by the stars of revelation, heading toward the harbour of the Hereafter. The ship belongs to Allah, and so does the soul. The true owner is the One who created both.

=25=

The refrain returns for the sixth time, now after the mention of the ships. The ship is a concentration of countless favours: the wood from the trees, the iron for the nails, the flax or cotton for the sails, the human mind that conceived the design, the wind that fills the sails, the water that bears the hull, the stars that guide the navigator, the calm seas that allow safe passage. Every successful voyage is a litany of mercies.

The question asks: which of these will you deny? The Quraysh, who sent caravans by land, understood the risks of travel. They knew that a single storm could wipe out a season’s trade. Their own safety and prosperity depended on favours they could not control. The ship is a reminder of human dependence. Despite all technology, the most advanced vessel is still at the mercy of the elements, and the elements are at the command of Ar-Rahman. The refrain challenges the pride of human achievement. The shipbuilder may boast of his skill, but can he guarantee a safe voyage? The captain may boast of his knowledge, but can he calm the storm? The ship, for all its majesty, is a beggar, asking for safe passage from its Lord. The believer, hearing this refrain, thinks of the many times they have been carried, metaphorically, across the turbulent seas of life. Times when they were fragile, like a wooden vessel on a vast ocean, and a merciful wind carried them to safety. They recognise that the ship is a sign of the ark of salvation, the faith that carries the soul through the storms of this world. The refrain is a call to acknowledge the Captain of all ships, the Lord of the sea and the wind. To deny His favours is to claim self-sufficiency, to pretend that the ship sails by its own power. The truth is that every ship that ever sailed, from Noah’s ark to a modern cargo vessel, sailed by the mercy of Ar-Rahman.

The question of the refrain hangs over every mast and every sail, waiting for the heart to answer with gratitude.

=26=

The surah now shifts its focus to a fundamental truth that underlies all the favours mentioned so far: the temporality of all created things. Everything on the earth will perish.

The Arabic “kullu man ‘alayha fan” is a stark, absolute statement. “Every being on it” refers to every living creature, every human, every jinn, every animal, every plant, everything that walks or crawls or grows on the face of the earth.

The word “fan” means perishing, coming to an end, vanishing, dying. This is the inescapable destiny of all earthly life. The earth itself, as the following verse will clarify, is not eternal, but the focus here is on its inhabitants. This verse is a powerful reminder of mortality. Amidst the beautiful catalog of blessings, the Quran inserts this note of sobriety. The flowers that bloom will wither. The fruits that grow will be harvested and consumed. The people who enjoy them will grow old and die. The generations pass, one after another.

This is not a gloomy observation; it is a liberating truth. By acknowledging that everything on earth perishes, the believer is freed from the illusion that the material world is permanent. The favours of Ar-Rahman are wonderful, but they are not meant to be the final object of attachment. They are signs that point to the Giver, who is eternal.

The verse also prepares the heart for the next verse, which will contrast the perishing nature of creation with the eternal nature of the Creator. The Quraysh, who were deeply attached to their worldly possessions, their status, their lineage, their trade, are being reminded that all of it is fleeting. The rich man and his wealth, the poet and his fame, the warrior and his strength, all will perish. The only thing that remains is the face of the Lord. This verse is a medicine for the heart that clings too tightly to the world. It is a call to invest in what does not perish: the good deeds, the faith, the relationship with the Eternal. The believer hears this and feels a sense of urgency and detachment. The favours are to be enjoyed with gratitude, but the heart must be anchored in the One who never perishes. The verse is a doorway from the love of the creation to the love of the Creator.

=27=

This verse provides the glorious contrast to the previous one. While everything on earth perishes, the face of your Lord remains.

The Arabic “wa yabqa wajhu Rabbika” means that the essence, the being, the presence of your Lord endures forever. The word “wajh,” face, is understood by the scholars to refer to the Self, the very being of Allah. It is a way of expressing His eternal existence in a manner that befits His majesty. The face of the Lord is that which the believers hope to gaze upon in Paradise. It is the source of all light, all beauty, all mercy.

This is the One who does not perish, who does not change, who does not age, who does not die. Then the verse adds two powerful attributes: “full of Majesty and Honour.

” The Arabic “dhu al-jalali wa al-ikram” describes the Lord as possessing absolute majesty, grandeur, awe, and absolute honour, generosity, nobility. Majesty is what inspires fear and awe. Honour is what inspires love and hope. The Lord combines both in perfect balance. He is the Majestic before whom mountains crumble and the Honourable who bestows gifts upon His servants. The believers, as mentioned in the final verse of the surah, will see the face of their Lord and experience His honour. This verse is the pivot of the entire surah. It moves the gaze from the perishing signs to the Eternal Signified. The sun and moon, the trees and stars, the oceans and fruits, all are perishing. They are beautiful, but they are temporary. The Lord of Majesty and Honour is the only permanent reality. The believer, reflecting on this, feels a profound realignment of priorities. The world and its attractions lose their grip. The only thing truly worth seeking is the pleasure of the Eternal. The verse is a balm for the sorrow of loss. Every human being experiences the pain of losing loved ones, of watching beauty fade, of feeling time slip away. This verse says: do not despair. The perishing of things is a sign that they were never meant to last. The Eternal remains, and He is full of Majesty and Honour. The believer’s ultimate relationship is not with anything in the world but with the Ever-Living who never dies.

This is the summit of the surah’s theology, the peak from which all the favours are seen in their proper perspective.

=28=

The refrain returns for the seventh time, now following the profound declaration of mortality and eternity. The fact that everything on earth perishes while the Lord of Majesty and Honour remains is itself a tremendous favour. How can this be a favour? Because it liberates the soul from the tyranny of the temporary. If the things of this world were eternal, the human being would be trapped in an endless cycle of attachment to what is ultimately unsatisfying. The perishing of the world is a mercy because it forces the soul to look beyond, to seek the Eternal. The knowledge that the Lord remains forever is the greatest comfort in the face of death and loss.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” now takes on a deeper, more existential dimension. It is not just about the physical blessings of fruit and ships. It is about the very structure of reality: the passing of the world and the permanence of God. Can anyone deny that this is a wise and merciful design? The denier might cling to the illusion of worldly permanence, but death shatters that illusion. The believer accepts the favour of mortality, understanding that this world is a bridge, not a home. The Lord of Majesty and Honour is the true home. The refrain, placed here, is a profound invitation to reflect on the meaning of life. The favours mentioned before were all pointing to this ultimate favour: the existence of the Eternal Creator who will remain when all else has vanished. To deny this is to consign oneself to a universe of meaningless decay, a universe where everything perishes and nothing remains. The believer answers the question with a heart full of awe and gratitude: I deny none of Your favours. The favour of knowing that You remain, O Lord of Majesty and Honour, is the greatest favour of all. It gives meaning to the fleeting joys and strength to bear the fleeting pains. The refrain is the bridge between the transient and the eternal, and the believer crosses it with every recitation.

=29=

The surah expands on the relationship between the Creator and the creation. Everyone in the heavens and on earth asks of Him, begs from Him, is completely dependent on Him.

The Arabic “yas’aluhu man fi as-samawati wa al-ard” means that every single being, from the highest angel to the smallest ant, is in a state of constant need and supplication to Allah. The angels ask for forgiveness for the believers, for sustenance, for commands. The humans ask for guidance, provision, health, and mercy. The animals, in their own silent language, ask for their daily food. The entire cosmos is a chorus of need directed at the Self-Sufficient.

This is the opposite of the pagan conception where gods are distant and need to be appeased. The true God is constantly being asked, and He constantly gives.

The second part of the verse, “kulla yawmin huwa fi sha’n,” is a magnificent expression of Allah’s continuous activity. Every day, every moment, He is engaged in some matter, some affair. He creates, sustains, forgives, gives life, causes death, answers prayers, relieves distress, elevates some and humbles others, executes His decree. This does not imply any tiredness or limitation, for His activity is perfect and effortless. It means that the universe is not a wound-up clock left to tick on its own. It is a living, dynamic reality sustained by the continuous engagement of the Creator. The scholar al-Maqatil commented on this verse by listing some of the affairs: “He forgives sins, removes distress, elevates a people and humbles others. ” The world is not static; it is a theatre of ongoing divine action. The believer, understanding this, feels a profound sense of intimacy and reliance. The Lord is not absent. He is involved. He is aware. The needs of every moment are met by His ongoing activity.

This is a favour of unimaginable magnitude. The whole universe is dependent, and He is the only independent, yet He never tires of providing. The verse prepares the heart for the refrain, which will ask which of these favours, the favour of being heard, the favour of a responsive Lord, the favour of a world that is actively managed, can be denied.

=30=

The refrain returns for the eighth time, now after the declaration that all creatures beg from Him and that He is continuously engaged in managing the affairs of creation. The absolute dependence of all creation on Ar-Rahman is a favour of the highest order. It means that no one is self-sufficient. The king and the beggar, the angel and the ant, all stand in need before Him. This levels the playing field of existence. The human being who boasts of their wealth and power is, in reality, a beggar whose every breath depends on Allah. The knowledge that there is a Being who can be asked, who listens, who responds, is a mercy. Imagine a universe where no one was listening, where needs were swallowed by an indifferent void. That would be a terrifying universe. But the universe of Ar-Rahman is a universe where every silent plea is heard, every tear is seen, every need is known.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” challenges the listener to recognise the miracle of answered prayer. How many times has a person been in distress and called out, and found relief? How many times has a need been fulfilled in ways that were not expected? To deny these favours is to be ungrateful for the very air one breathes. The refrain also highlights the favour of Allah’s continuous engagement. The universe is not abandoned. It is not a ship without a captain. It is guided, moment by moment, by the Most Wise. The denier might claim that the world runs on its own, but this verse and its refrain refute that. The world runs on the mercy of the Ever-Active. The believer, upon hearing this refrain, feels a deep sense of peace. The troubles of the day, the anxieties of the future, are all in the hands of the One who is constantly managing affairs. The response to the refrain is a surrender of the heart: “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I am entirely dependent on You, and I trust in Your management of all affairs. ” The refrain is an anchor in the storm, a reminder that the one who begs is not ignored, and the Lord who gives never sleeps.

=31=

The surah now delivers a direct warning to the two species being addressed. After the long catalog of mercies and the repeated call for gratitude, the Day of Reckoning is announced. “We will soon attend to you” translates the Arabic “sanafrughu lakum,” which literally means “We will be free for you,” or “We will attend exclusively to you.” This does not imply that Allah is ever too busy, but it is a powerful rhetorical device used by the Arabs to indicate that a matter has become of paramount importance. It is as if to say: a day is coming when all other affairs of creation will be suspended, and your judgment will become the central focus. The “two multitudes” are the “thaqalan,” the two weighty species, humans and jinn. They are addressed directly: O you two heavy species!

The word “thaqalan” can also mean the two beings of importance, the two that are the centre of the moral drama of the universe. The warning is clear: do not be distracted by the favours of this world. A Day is coming when you will be held to account for how you responded to those favours. The favours were a test, and the judgment will be the result. This verse marks a shift in the surah. The catalog of cosmic signs is largely complete. Now the surah turns to the reality of the Hereafter, to the punishment of the deniers and the reward of the grateful. The mercy of the warning is evident. Ar-Rahman, out of His compassion, informs His creation of the coming judgment so that they may prepare. He does not wish to surprise them with punishment. He gives them notice. The believer hears this and feels a healthy fear, a fear that motivates increased devotion and repentance. The denier hears this and should feel a tremor of dread, though their arrogance may cause them to dismiss it.

The phrase “soon” indicates that the Day is imminent, as all prophets have warned. The attention of the Divine will soon be turned to the judgment of every soul. No favour will be left unaccounted for. The verse is a solemn call to mindfulness, a turning point that prepares the listener for the descriptions of Hell and Paradise that follow.

=32=

The refrain returns for the ninth time, now directly after the announcement of the coming judgment. This is a brilliant placement. One might ask: how is the warning of judgment a favour?

The answer is that the warning itself is a mercy. Ar-Rahman would not be fully merciful if He did not warn His creation of the consequences of their actions. A loving mother warns her child not to touch the fire. A merciful Creator warns His servants of the Fire of Hell. The warning is a chance to repent, to change course, to avoid destruction. The fact that the judgment is announced in advance is a favour. The fact that the Quran and the Prophet have been sent as warners is a favour.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” now includes the favour of the warning. Will they deny the message of the prophets? Will they deny the reality of the Day of Judgment? To deny the warning is to reject the medicine that can cure the disease. The deniers, by rejecting the warning, are effectively denying a favour. They are saying, in effect, that they do not need this mercy, that they can manage on their own. The believer, hearing this refrain, embraces the warning as a gift. They hear the severity of “We will soon attend to you,” and then they hear the gentle question of the refrain, and their heart melts with a mixture of fear and hope. They respond: “I deny not the favour of Your warning, my Lord. I take heed, I believe in the Day of Judgment, and I prepare for it. ” The refrain transforms the warning from a purely threatening statement into a loving invitation. It is as if Ar-Rahman is saying: I am warning you because I care for you. Will you deny My care?

The repetition of the refrain here ensures that the believer does not fall into despair from the warning, and the denier is given one more chance to reconsider. It is the persistent mercy of the Most Compassionate, asking again and again, waiting for the answer of faith.

=33=

The surah now addresses the two species directly with a powerful challenge. “O assembly of jinn and humans!

” This is a formal summons, a call to the entirety of the two accountable species. The challenge is issued: if you are able to pass beyond the regions of the heavens and the earth, to escape the judgment that has been announced, to flee from the grasp of Allah, then do so. Try to penetrate the boundaries of the cosmos. Try to find an exit from this universe.

This is a challenge to their power and their pride. The Quraysh, and the jinn who shared their arrogance, are being told that there is no escape. The heavens and the earth are the domains of Allah, and there is no door out of them.

The second part of the verse provides the crushing caveat: “But you cannot do that without authority.” The Arabic “sultan” means a manifest authority, a power, a proof, a permission. You cannot escape except with a divine authority that you do not possess. No one has that authority. No one can flee from Allah’s judgment.

This is the ultimate declaration of human and jinn helplessness. Every tyrant, every arrogant denier, every fugitive from justice, will be brought before the court of Ar-Rahman. There is no place to hide. The challenge is rhetorical, designed to illustrate the absolute sovereignty of Allah and the absolute captivity of creation within His domain. The believer, reading this, feels a profound sense of security. If the guilty cannot escape, then the justice for the oppressed is guaranteed. The oppressor who thought he was beyond reach will find that there is no crack in the cosmos through which he can slip. The verse is also an invitation to humility. The human being, with all their technology, cannot even leave the solar system, let alone the heavens and the earth. The space probes we send travel for years and barely leave the neighbourhood of our own planet. The heavens are vast beyond comprehension, and they are all the prison of the fugitive. The only escape is to run towards Allah, not away from Him. To flee to Allah in repentance is the safe flight. To try to flee from Him is an impossibility. The verse prepares the way for the description of the punishment that will be sent upon those who try to resist.

=34=

The refrain returns for the tenth time, following the challenge to escape the heavens and the earth. At first glance, this seems like a threatening verse; how is it a favour? The favour is in the very impossibility of escape. The fact that there is no escape from Allah’s judgment is a pillar of divine justice. If the wicked could flee, there would be no ultimate justice. The victims of oppression would never see their rights restored. The universe would be a place where might makes right, and the clever could evade all consequences. The inescapability of divine judgment is a mercy for the oppressed and a terror for the oppressor. It establishes that the moral order is not a suggestion; it is a law as firm as the law of gravity.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” challenges the denier to see the favour in the system of justice. The denier might wish for an escape, but that wish is itself an admission of guilt. The believer, hearing this, is grateful for a world where justice is built into the fabric of reality. The limits of the heavens and the earth are not a prison; they are a courtroom where every soul will receive its due. The refrain here also highlights the favour of knowledge. Allah, out of His mercy, tells the human and jinn that they cannot escape. This knowledge is meant to deter them from relying on the false hope of evasion. It is a warning that says: do not build your life on the assumption that you can get away with it. You cannot. This knowledge is a favour that can save a soul from destruction. The believer answers the refrain with a heart that rests in the justice of Allah. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I accept that I am under Your authority, and I seek not to escape but to be forgiven.” The refrain turns the challenge into a meditation on mercy. The same boundary that traps the sinner traps the saint, but for the saint it is the boundary of a loving home, not a prison. The believer is content to dwell forever within the realms of the Lord’s mercy, with no desire to escape.

=35=

The surah now provides a terrifying preview of the punishment that will be sent upon the deniers who attempt to resist or flee. A flame of fire and smoke, and molten brass or copper, will be sent against them.

The Arabic “shuwazun min narin wa nuhas” describes a pure, smokeless flame of fire, or a blast of fire mixed with smoke, and “nuhas” meaning molten copper or brass. This is a dual punishment, a combination of searing flame and molten metal. The imagery is of an unleashed, unstoppable torrent of heat and liquid metal pouring over the fleeing sinners. This punishment will be sent in the Hereafter, and the verse warns that when it comes, the deniers will not be able to help one another.

The Arabic “fa la tantasiran” means you two will not be victorious, you will not be able to defend or save each other. The unity of the disbelievers, their alliances, their armies, their collective strength, all will be useless. The flame and the molten copper will overwhelm every defence.

The mention of “nuhas” is particularly striking. Molten copper is a substance of intense, clinging heat. It is a reminder of the fires of this world, intensified to a degree beyond human endurance. The pagans who boasted of their metalwork and their weapons will be confronted with a metal that is no longer a tool but a torment. The verse is a stark warning that the arrogance of the deniers will be met with a force they cannot negotiate with or repel. The believer, hearing this, feels a tremor of fear. The punishment is real, and its description is meant to deter. But the believer also remembers that this punishment is for the deniers, those who denied the favours. The believer is among those who acknowledge the favours, and so they are hopeful in the mercy of Ar-Rahman.

The verse also serves as a comfort. The oppressors who seemed invincible in this world will be utterly helpless on that Day. No ally will save them. The flame and the molten copper will be the instruments of a justice that was delayed but never cancelled.

=36=

The refrain returns for the eleventh time, now after the description of the flames and molten copper. This placement is one of the most striking in the surah. How can the threat of such a horrific punishment be followed by a question about favours?

The answer reveals the depth of the Quran’s logic. The warning of punishment is itself a favour. It is a mercy that warns before it strikes. The flame and the molten copper are the consequences of denying the favours. By asking the question here, the surah is saying: even as I describe the punishment, I am still giving you a chance to avoid it. The description of Hell is a favour because it informs the listener of what awaits them if they continue on their path. A person who is warned of a minefield and given a map of where the mines are buried has received a favour. The Quran is that map.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” is a lifeline thrown into the fire. It is as if Ar-Rahman is saying, even now, even after hearing this, will you continue to deny? The denier, by persisting in disbelief, is denying the favour of the warning. They are throwing the map back in the Giver’s face. The believer, hearing this refrain, is moved to tears. They see the ocean of mercy even in the description of wrath. They understand that a God who did not care would not warn. The warning is proof of care. The believer answers: “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord, least of all the favour of Your warning. I hear the warning, I tremble, and I seek Your protection.” The refrain here is the ultimate expression of the surah’s central message: the overwhelming, all-encompassing mercy of Ar-Rahman, a mercy that persists even in the midst of judgment, a mercy that asks, one more time, “Will you not deny?”

=37=

The surah now describes one of the cosmic events of the Day of Judgment. The heavens will split apart, and they will become rose-red, like a stain of oil, like burnt or red-tanned leather.

The Arabic “fa idha inshaqqati as-sama’u fa kanat wardatan ka ad-dihan” paints a surreal and terrifying picture. The sky, that raised canopy, that solid blue dome that has sheltered humanity for millennia, will crack open. It will turn a deep, reddish colour, like a rose, or like red hide, or like the colour of oil.

The word “dihan” can mean red oil, or the dregs of oil, or tanned leather. The image is of a sky that has lost its familiar appearance and has become a writhing, coloured mass of destruction.

This is the end of the cosmic order that the surah has been describing. The same sky that was raised high, that held the sun and moon in their precise orbits, will now be rent asunder. The colour red evokes blood, fire, and intense heat. The comparison to oil suggests a liquid, flowing instability, a sky that is melting and boiling.

This is the backdrop against which the judgment will occur. The splendor of the heavens will turn to horror for the deniers.

For the believer, it is the birth pangs of a new creation, the death of the old world and the birth of the eternal one. The verse is a powerful contrast to the beautiful catalog of creation earlier in the surah. The same Lord who raised the sky in such beauty can bring it down in such terror. The purpose is to shake the heart, to remind it that the familiar world is not permanent, and that a day of radical transformation is coming. The believer looks at the blue sky with new eyes, appreciating its current beauty as a temporary gift, and preparing their soul for the day when that beauty will be replaced by the awesome spectacle of the divine judgment. The rose-red sky is a sign of the majesty and the wrath of the Lord, a visual warning that words cannot fully capture.

=38=

The refrain returns for the twelfth time, now following the apocalyptic image of the splitting sky. Once again, the question challenges the listener to find a denial in what has been described. The Day of Judgment, with its terrifying cosmic upheavals, is a manifestation of divine justice. Justice is a favour. The splitting of the sky is the herald of the final settlement of all accounts.

For the believer, it is the day when wrongs are righted, when tears are wiped away, when the faithful are separated from the faithless. The very event that terrifies the denier is a source of hope for the oppressed.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of ultimate justice? The denier, who has built his life on the assumption that there is no judgment, is being told that judgment is coming, and the sky itself will be the proof. The rosy colour of the sky is not a random effect; it is a divine signal that the time of mercy in the form of a warning has ended, and the time of mercy in the form of justice has begun. The refrain here is a powerful reminder that even the most frightening signs of the Last Day are part of the complete system of divine mercy. A world without an end, without a judgment, would be a world where evil triumphs forever. The splitting sky is the guarantee that evil will not triumph. It is a favour. The believer answers the refrain with a heart that trembles but is also full of gratitude. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. Even the splitting of the sky is a favour, for it brings the justice that Your mercy promised. I await that Day not with terror but with hope in Your mercy.” The refrain transforms the apocalypse from a scene of pure horror into a sign of the completion of mercy. It is the final note in the symphony, the chord that resolves all the previous melodies of favour into the ultimate harmony of divine justice.

=39=

The surah continues its description of the Day of Judgment with a specific detail about the process of reckoning. On that Day, no human or jinn will be asked about their sin.

The Arabic “fa yawma’idhin la yus’alu ‘an dhanbihi insun wa la jann” means that the guilty will not be subjected to an interrogation to determine their guilt. Why? Because their guilt is already manifestly clear. The records are complete, the witnesses have testified, the bodies bear the marks of their deeds, and the faces of the wicked are dark with shame. There is no need for a cross-examination. The judgment is swift and certain. This does not contradict other verses that mention questioning, such as “Then We will surely question those to whom the messengers were sent.” The questioning in those verses is a form of rebuke and establishing the proof, not an investigation to discover the unknown. Allah already knows everything. The verse here emphasises the efficiency and inevitability of the judgment. The criminals will not be able to give excuses, to hire advocates, or to plea-bargain. Their own existence will be a confession.

This is a fearsome description. In this world, a criminal can escape justice if there is not enough evidence. On that Day, the evidence is absolute and built into every atom of the criminal’s being. There is no need to ask about the sin because the sin is written on the face, on the limbs, and in the book of deeds.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder of the seriousness of sin. Nothing is hidden. No sin is so secret that it will escape the record. The believer is not complacent but lives with a constant awareness of that Day, seeking forgiveness for their sins before they are presented so openly.

The verse also provides a subtle comfort. The righteous will not be subjected to this humiliating exposure. Their sins will have been covered by repentance, and their faces will be radiant. The verse is a call to live in such a way that on the Day when no one is asked about their sin, one’s own account is among the blessed who are welcomed into mercy, not among the exposed who are driven to the Fire.

=40=

The refrain returns for the thirteenth time, after the verse about the absence of questioning. Once again, the question seems paradoxical after a description of judgment, but the logic holds. The fact that the criminals will not be asked about their sins is a favour in the sense that it demonstrates the perfection of divine justice. There will be no long, drawn-out trials, no uncertainty, no possibility of injustice. The judgment will be swift, accurate, and based on irrefutable evidence.

This is a favour for the entire creation, because it shows that the court of the Almighty is the most efficient and just court imaginable. For the believers, it is also a favour because their own judgment will be light and easy. The Prophet spoke of a believer whose record will be discussed privately, with his sins covered, and he will be told to simply acknowledge them and be forgiven. The harsh public exposure described in these verses is for the deniers. So the swift justice is a mercy for all.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” challenges the denier who might scoff at the idea of a judgment where no questions are asked. They might think it unfair, but it is the opposite: it is the ultimate fairness, where the evidence is so complete that questioning is redundant. The believer, hearing this refrain, reflects on the mercy of a judgment that is based on absolute knowledge. They are grateful that their Lord is not one who needs to investigate, but one who already knows and who judges with perfect wisdom. They answer the refrain with a renewed commitment to transparency in their own lives, knowing that what is hidden will one day be made manifest. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The Day of swift justice is a day I believe in, and I prepare for it with repentance and good deeds.” The refrain is a constant companion through the terrors of the apocalypse, a whisper of mercy that never fades, even as the heavens split and the fire rages.
