=41=

The surah continues its vivid portrayal of the Day of Judgment, now describing the arrest of the criminals. The wicked, the "mujrimun," will be recognised by their marks, by their appearance.

The Arabic "yu'rafu al-mujrimuna bi simahum" indicates that the guilty will have unmistakable signs on their faces and their bodies. Their faces will be blackened, their eyes downcast, their postures betraying their terror and shame. There will be no need for introductions or identification papers. The sin that was hidden in their hearts will be visible on their features.

This is a profound and terrifying truth: the inner state of a person cannot be concealed forever. On that Day, the righteous will also be recognised by their marks, the radiant light of faith shining on their faces. The wicked, by contrast, will be branded by the darkness of their deeds. Then comes the seizure: "they will be seized by their forelocks and their feet.

" The Arabic "fa yu'khadhu bi an-nawasi wa al-aqdam" describes a violent, humiliating arrest. The angels of punishment will grab them by the forelocks, the hair of the forehead, and drag them, while their feet are bound or pulled. The forelock is the front of the head, the seat of pride and arrogance. The feet are the foundation of the body, the means of movement. Seizing both the highest and the lowest parts of the person signifies total control and complete degradation. The criminal who walked arrogantly on his feet and lifted his head in pride will now be dragged like a worthless object to the Fire.

The mention of the forelock is particularly significant in the Quran. Elsewhere, Allah says He seizes by the forelock, a lying, sinful forelock. The forelock is where thoughts of arrogance and schemes of evil are hatched. It is the symbol of the rebellious will. Now, that rebellious will is literally in the grip of the divine enforcers. The believer, reading this, feels a profound humility. The body they inhabit now, the feet they walk with, the head they hold high, are all gifts that can become witnesses against them or instruments of their humiliation if misused. The believer prays to be among those whose faces are radiant, not among those seized by their forelocks and feet. The verse is a stark reminder that the dignity of the human form is a trust, and its violation on the Day of Judgment is the ultimate loss.

=42=

The refrain returns for the fourteenth time, directly after the graphic description of the seizure of the wicked. This is another instance of the profound Quranic technique of following a terrifying warning with the gentle, insistent question of mercy. The seizure of the criminals by their forelocks and feet is a manifestation of divine justice. Justice is a favour. The fact that the wicked are recognised and not mistaken for the righteous is a favour for the righteous. The fact that they are seized and not left to roam free is a favour for the entire moral order.

The question "which of your Lord's favours will you both deny?" challenges the listener to see even the harsh realities of the Hereafter as part of the complete system of mercy that Ar-Rahman has established. For the believer, the refrain is a reassurance. They hear about the terrible fate of the wicked, and their heart might tremble, but then the question comes, and they remember that this fate is for those who denied the favours. They, who are striving to acknowledge and be grateful for the favours, have hope in a different outcome. The refrain is a lifeline, a reminder that the door of mercy is still open in this world, even as the description of the punishment is being recited. The denier, hearing this, is given yet another chance.

The question implicitly says: this seizure, this humiliation, is the result of your denial. But you are not yet seized. You are still free. Will you continue to deny, or will you finally acknowledge the favours? The refrain is a powerful rhetorical device that prevents the listener from falling into despair or passive terror. It keeps the channel of invitation open. The believer answers with a heart full of humble hope: "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I seek refuge in Your mercy from being among those seized. I believe in Your justice, and I trust in Your compassion." The refrain is the thread of light woven through the dark tapestry of the Hereafter's warnings, guiding the soul back to the source of all mercy.

=43=

The scene shifts to the moment when the criminals are brought before the Fire. They will be told, in words that cut deeper than any flame, "This is the Hell which the wicked used to deny.

" The Arabic "hadhihi jahannamu allati yukadhdhibu biha al-mujrimun" is a declaration that connects their present torment directly to their past denial. The Fire is not a surprise; it is the very thing they were warned about repeatedly, and which they arrogantly dismissed as a myth. Now it stands before them, a blazing, undeniable reality.

The word "this" indicates immediate presence. It is no longer a distant threat; it is right here, right now, surrounding them.

The use of the term "the wicked," al-mujrimun, identifies them as the criminals, those who earned this fate through their own actions. The psychological torment of this moment is immense. In the world, they laughed at the idea of Hell. They called the Prophet a liar for warning about it. They asked mockingly when this punishment would come. Now, the punishment has come, and their own mockery is thrown back in their faces. They are being told, in effect: "You denied it, so now experience it.

" The word "yukadhdhibu" is a present tense verb, indicating their habitual, continuous denial in the worldly life. Their rejection was not a one-time mistake; it was a persistent stance. This verse is a powerful example of the Quran's concept of "tasting." The punishment is not merely physical; it is a full-sensory and psychological experience that includes the bitter taste of regret and the humiliation of being proven wrong.

For the believer, this verse is a stark warning to never be among those who deny the truths of the unseen. The Hell that the deniers mocked is real, and their mockery will be the source of their deepest shame on that Day. The believer hears this and renews their faith in the unseen, believing in Hell as if they can see it, and striving to avoid the deeds that lead to it. The verse is a mirror held up to the denier, reflecting the ugly face of their own disbelief back at them.

=44=

The punishment of the deniers is described further. They will circle between the Hellfire and scalding, boiling water.

The Arabic "yatufuna baynaha wa bayna hamimin an" paints a picture of restless, cyclical torment. They go from the flames to the water, and back to the flames, finding no relief in either. The Fire burns them from the outside. The "hamim" is water that has reached the extreme of heat, water that is itself a fire. In this world, water is the antidote to fire. If a person is burned, they seek cool water to soothe the pain. But in Hell, the water is as torturous as the fire. It is scalding, boiling, and when the damned try to drink it, it tears their insides apart. The alternation between the two forms of torment means there is no moment of respite. They are driven from one agony to another, and back again, in an endless cycle.

This is the recompense for their own restless circling in the world, going from one sin to another, from one false god to another, never settling on the truth. The word "yatufuna" is related to "tawaf," the act of circling. The deniers who refused to circle the Kaaba in worship of the One God will circle in Hell between fire and boiling water. The verse is a vivid, horrifying image designed to deter the soul from sin. The believer, upon hearing it, feels a deep aversion to the Hellfire and its torments. They cling more tightly to the hope of Paradise and increase in their good deeds.

The mention of boiling water is also a sign of the complete absence of mercy. In Paradise, the righteous will drink from cool, refreshing springs. In Hell, the water itself is an enemy. The contrast could not be starker. The believer thanks Allah for the water that is cool and sweet, recognising it as a favour that could have been otherwise, and prays to be given the water of Paradise and spared the "hamim" of the Fire. The verse is a powerful motivator for taqwa, for the God-consciousness that leads to the cool shade of the Gardens.

=45=

The refrain returns for the fifteenth time, now following the description of the alternation between fire and scalding water. This is perhaps the most striking juxtaposition in the surah so far. The horror of the punishment has been laid bare: the circling, the flames, the boiling water. And then, immediately, the gentle, insistent question: "Then which of your Lord's favours will you both deny? " The contrast is breathtaking. It is as if Ar-Rahman is saying: even now, even as I describe the fate of the damned, My mercy is still present, still offering you a way out. The description of Hell is a favour because it is a warning. The warning is a mercy. By telling you about the fire and the boiling water, I am giving you the knowledge that can save you from them. Will you deny this favour? The denier, by rejecting the warning, is denying a mercy. They are throwing away the map that shows the location of the minefield. The believer, hearing this refrain in this context, is overwhelmed by the mercy of it. They realise that the Quran does not describe Hell to satisfy a morbid curiosity or to instil a paralysing fear. It describes Hell to save. Every verse about punishment is an act of love, a desperate plea from the Most Compassionate to His creation: "Do not go there. Here is what it is like. Turn back.

" The question "which of your Lord's favours will you both deny?" is the voice of that love. The believer answers with tears: "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I hear the warning, I believe it, and I beg You to protect me from that which You have described." The refrain is the ultimate proof that the surah is not a surah of wrath, but a surah of mercy.

The name Ar-Rahman dominates everything, even the verses about Hell. The fire is real, but the mercy that warns about it is even more real, and the refrain is the persistent, merciful heartbeat that pulses through the entire surah.

=46=

The surah now makes a dramatic and beautiful turn. The terrifying descriptions of Hell give way to the exquisite descriptions of Paradise.

This is the reward for a specific group: those who feared standing before their Lord. The Arabic "wa li man khafa maqama Rabbihi jannatan" describes the one who is in awe, who genuinely fears the moment of standing in the presence of Allah for judgment. This fear is not a paralysing terror that leads to despair. It is a reverential awe, a deep awareness of the majesty of Allah and the seriousness of the reckoning. This fear motivated the believer to restrain their soul from desires, to obey the commands, and to live a life of accountability. The reward for this quality is "two Gardens. " The Arabic uses the dual form: "jannatan," two Gardens. This indicates a pair of gardens for each individual among this elite group. The scholars, based on the details that follow, have identified these two Gardens as the higher levels of Paradise, the Gardens of the Foremost, the Muqarrabun, those brought near. The fact that the reward is two Gardens, a pair, signifies abundance, completeness, and joy that is doubled. The fear of standing before the Lord, which seemed like a burden in this world, is transformed into a double portion of eternal bliss.

The verse establishes a principle: the fear of Allah is the key to the Gardens. The one who lives with the consciousness of the Day of Judgment, who allows that consciousness to shape their choices, will find that the very thing they feared becomes the gate to unimaginable happiness. The believer, hearing this, feels a surge of hope. The fear of Allah is not a negative, oppressive emotion. It is a protective, guiding light. It is the compass that points to Paradise. The believer asks themselves: do I have this fear? Do I live as if I will stand before my Lord? They renew their commitment to cultivating this awe, knowing that it is the seed from which the Gardens grow. The verse opens the door to the most beautiful passages of the surah, the descriptions of the twin Gardens that await the God-fearing.

=47=

The refrain returns for the sixteenth time, now following the announcement of the two Gardens for the God-fearing. This is a direct and joyful challenge. After the horrors of Hell, the surah presents the beauty of Paradise.

The question is now asked in a context of pure mercy and reward. "Which of your Lord's favours will you both deny? " Can you deny the favour of the two Gardens? Can you deny that Allah, out of His mercy, has prepared such a reward for those who fear Him? The two Gardens are the ultimate favour, the culmination of all the earlier favours. The sun and moon, the fruits and grains, the ships and the seas, were all signs pointing to this. The real home, the real feast, is here. The denier, who scoffed at the idea of Paradise just as they scoffed at Hell, is asked: will you deny this too? Will you deny that the Most Compassionate, who scattered stars across the sky and laid the earth for all beings, is capable of creating Gardens of eternal bliss for His grateful servants? The believer answers with a heart overflowing with joy and anticipation: "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I believe in Your Gardens. I long for them. Make me among those who enter them." The refrain here functions as a celebration. It is no longer a somber warning but a jubilant proclamation. The same question that echoed through the threats of Hell now echoes through the promises of Paradise, uniting the two halves of the surah. The mercy that warns is the same mercy that rewards. The consistency of the refrain reveals the unity of Allah's attributes. He is the Most Compassionate in both His punishment and His reward. The believer's heart dances to the rhythm of the refrain, feeling the nearness of the Lord who asks, after every description of beauty, "Will you not acknowledge this favour?" It is an intimate conversation between the Giver and the recipient, a conversation that the believer never wants to end.

=48=

The description of the two Gardens begins with their physical beauty. They are described as "dhawata afnan," meaning possessing abundant, spreading branches, laden with fruit and foliage. The "afnan" are the branches of trees, but the word also carries connotations of variety and abundance. The Gardens are not sparse or uniform. They are lush, diverse, filled with trees of every kind, their branches intertwining to create a canopy of shade and beauty. In the harsh desert climate of Arabia, a garden with spreading branches was the ultimate image of paradise on earth. The dense foliage provided relief from the scorching sun. The branches, heavy with fruit, promised delicious sustenance within easy reach.

The word "afnan" also has a linguistic connection to "funun," meaning arts or varieties. Some scholars have noted that the Gardens contain every kind of beauty, every variety of delight, spread out on every branch. The verse is short but evocative. It allows the imagination to wander through the shaded avenues of Paradise, to see the green leaves rustling in a gentle breeze, to smell the fragrance of countless flowers, to hear the birds singing in the branches.

This is the reward for a moment of God-fearing in the world. A single act of restraint, a single tear of repentance, a single prostration in the darkness of the night, is transformed into an eternity among the lush branches of the divine Garden. The believer, hearing this, feels a longing for that Garden. They know that the branches of this world, the material possessions and the temporary achievements, will wither and die. The branches of the Gardens of Ar-Rahman are eternal. The verse is an invitation to invest in the eternal garden, to plant the seeds of good deeds in the soil of this world so that they may blossom into the afnan of the Hereafter. The beauty described is not just for the eyes; it is a balm for the soul that has been wearied by the barrenness of a worldly life lived away from the remembrance of Allah.

=49=

The refrain returns for the seventeenth time, now prompted by the description of the lush branches. The question asks: can you deny the favour of these beautiful, spreading branches? The Quraysh knew the value of a green garden in a dry land. They prized their orchards in the valleys of Ta'if. The Quran takes that familiar blessing and elevates it to an eternal scale. The lush branches of Paradise are not just a garden; they are the Garden. They are a favour that no human effort could ever earn. They are a gift, pure and simple, from the Most Compassionate.

The question challenges the denier who sees a beautiful tree in this world and admires it but refuses to attribute it to Ar-Rahman. If you can appreciate a branch in this world, how can you deny the branches of the next? If you understand the pleasure of shade, how can you deny the eternal shade of Paradise? The believer answers with a heart that sees every beautiful tree in this world as a preview, a trailer for the coming attraction. The oak tree, the olive tree, the palm tree, all are signs, shadows of the afnan that await. "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. Every branch I see reminds me of Your promise. Make me worthy of the branches of Your Garden." The refrain here is a call to see the connection between the blessings of this world and the blessings of the next. The believer is not a killjoy who refuses to enjoy the beauty of nature. Rather, the believer enjoys it more deeply, because they see it as a gift that points to an even greater Gift. The refrain trains the heart to be constantly aware of the Giver, to live in a state of perpetual gratitude, to see the shadow and long for the substance. The lush branches are a mercy, and the question of the refrain ensures that mercy is not overlooked but celebrated.

=50=

The description of the two Gardens continues with another feature: water. In each Garden, there will be two flowing springs.

The Arabic "fihima 'aynan tajriyan" uses the dual form for both the Gardens and the springs, indicating a perfect symmetry and abundance. Springs are the source of life and refreshment. In Paradise, the springs flow perpetually, without ceasing, without the need for pumps or pipes. They flow by the command of Allah, emerging from the ground and winding through the Gardens, offering cool, pure water to the inhabitants. Water in the Quranic Paradise is one of the central motifs. There are springs of pure water, springs of milk, springs of honey, and springs of wine that does not intoxicate. The springs mentioned here are a general blessing, representing all the flowing waters of delight. The sound of flowing water is one of the most soothing sounds in nature. It calms the mind and refreshes the soul. The believer, hearing this verse, can almost hear the gentle gurgle of the heavenly springs. The fact that each Garden has two springs, making four in total for the two Gardens, indicates a lavish abundance. The inhabitants of Paradise will never thirst. They will never experience drought. The water that was a precious, sometimes scarce commodity in the desert, will be an overflowing, infinite blessing. The springs also represent the spiritual fountain of divine knowledge and love that will flow into the hearts of the believers. Just as the body is refreshed by water, the soul is refreshed by the nearness of Allah. The flowing springs are a symbol of the continuous, uninterrupted flow of divine mercy and pleasure. The believer, who restrained themselves from the impure sources of worldly pleasure, is rewarded with the purest, most delightful springs. The verse is an invitation to purify the soul in this world through repentance and good deeds, so that one may drink from the springs of Paradise in the next.

=51=

The refrain returns for the eighteenth time, now after the mention of the flowing springs. Water is life. The flowing springs of Paradise are a favour of the highest order. The denier might take water for granted in this world, but they cannot deny its necessity.

The question asks: if you can recognise the favour of a spring in this world, how can you deny the springs of the next? The Quraysh knew the value of a well, of a spring like Zamzam that sustained their city. They fought over water rights, and they understood that water was power. The Quran tells them that the real springs, the eternal springs, are in the hands of Ar-Rahman, and He gives them to those who fear Him. The challenge of the refrain is to acknowledge that the same Lord who provides the water of this world is the Lord who provides the springs of Paradise. To deny the latter is to be ungrateful for the former. The believer answers the refrain with a heart full of thirst, not for the water of this world, but for the springs of the Hereafter. "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I believe in Your springs. Make me drink from them, and let the water of this world remind me of the water of the next." The refrain is a bridge between the quenching of physical thirst and the quenching of spiritual thirst. The believer drinks a glass of cool water and remembers this verse, remembers the refrain, and says, "Which of my Lord's favours can I deny? This water is a mercy, and the springs of Paradise are a greater mercy. " The entire act of drinking becomes an act of worship, a meditation on the favours of Ar-Rahman. The refrain sanctifies the mundane. It elevates the ordinary experience of drinking water into a remembrance of the divine promise, a taste of the eternal springs that await the God-fearing. The mercy of the refrain is that it keeps the Hereafter alive in the heart, even in the simplest of worldly actions.

=52=

The description of Paradise's bounty continues with the fruits. In each of the two Gardens, there will be two kinds of every fruit.

The Arabic "fihima min kulli fakihatin zawjan" means that from every fruit, there is a pair, two varieties. This indicates a completeness and a richness of variety that surpasses anything in this world. In the earthly gardens, a tree bears one kind of fruit. In Paradise, the fruits are diverse beyond measure. If you desire an apple, you will find multiple varieties, each more delicious than the last. If you desire a fruit that does not even exist in this world, it will be there.

The pairing of fruits also suggests a harmony, a balance, a perfection of taste and appearance. The fruits of Paradise are not only delicious; they are beautiful to behold. They are free from blemishes, free from worms, free from any imperfection. And they are eternal, never going out of season.

The mention of fruit is a recurring theme in the Quranic descriptions of Paradise. It represents the delight of the senses, the reward of taste, the satisfaction of desire without any negative consequence. The believer, who restrained themselves from forbidden foods and drinks, who ate only what was lawful and good, is rewarded with an endless banquet of the most exquisite fruits.

The phrase "two kinds" also emphasises abundance. There is no scarcity in Paradise. Every fruit is doubled, every pleasure multiplied. The believer, reading this, feels a deep sense of anticipation. The temporary pleasures of the world's fruits are a mere shadow. The real fruit, the eternal fruit, is waiting. The verse is a powerful motivator to seek the sustenance of the Hereafter, to not be overly attached to the fruits of this world, which rot and fade, but to work for the fruits that never decay. The pair of Gardens, each with a pair of springs, each with a pair of every fruit, is a symphony of twos, a structure of perfect symmetry that reflects the divine perfection of the Creator.

=53=

The refrain returns for the nineteenth time, now after the mention of the paired fruits. The abundance and variety of the fruits of Paradise are an unmistakable favour.

The question challenges the listener: can you deny the favour of this eternal fruit? In this world, a person might enjoy a mango or a pomegranate and not think of the Giver. The Quran places that enjoyment in an eternal context. The same Lord who creates the delicious fruits of this earth has prepared even more delicious fruits in Paradise. To deny the latter is to be ungrateful for the former. The denier, who enjoys the fruits of the world but refuses to believe in the Hereafter, is living a contradiction. The fruits they eat every day are a daily sign, a daily test, a daily question: "Who gave you this? And if He gave you this, can He not give you more?" The refrain is that question, articulated with perfect clarity. The believer answers with a heart that tastes the mango and says, "This is a favour from my Lord, and the fruits of Paradise are a greater favour. I deny none of them." The believer's enjoyment is deepened, not diminished, by this awareness. The mango is not just a mango; it is a love letter from Ar-Rahman, a sample of the menu of Paradise. The refrain trains the believer to live in a state of constant spiritual awareness, where every bite of food is a reminder of the Giver, and every pleasure is a pointer to the ultimate Pleasure. The mercy of the refrain is that it turns eating into an act of worship. It saves the believer from the heedlessness of mere consumption and elevates them to the station of gratitude. The paired fruits of Paradise become a promise that makes the believer smile, a promise that says: the best is yet to come. The refrain is the key that unlocks this perspective, turning the surah into a continuous meditation on the relationship between the blessings of this world and the blessings of the next.

=54=

The description of the comforts of Paradise continues. The believers will be reclining on raised couches, on beds or thrones, whose linings are of rich brocade, of thick silk.

The Arabic "muttaki'ina 'ala furushin bata'inuha min istabraq" paints a picture of luxurious ease. The exterior of the furnishings is made of the finest, heaviest silk, a fabric that was the height of luxury in the ancient world and remains a symbol of wealth and comfort today. The believers are reclining, a posture of complete relaxation, security, and honour. There is no fear, no anxiety, no need to work or toil. They are at rest, surrounded by beauty and softness.

The second part of the verse adds to this ease: the fruits of the two Gardens are near, hanging low, within easy reach. The Arabic "wa jana al-jannatayni dan" means the plucked fruits, the harvest of the Gardens, are close, accessible. There is no need to climb a tree, to strain, to labour. The fruit bends to the hand of the believer.

This is a profound symbol of the complete service that Paradise provides. Everything is brought to the believer; the believer does not have to go to anything. The ease of Paradise is the opposite of the toil of the world. The believer, who strained in worship, who struggled against desires, who worked for the Hereafter, is now given a rest that is perfect and eternal. The rich brocade and the near fruit represent the fulfilment of all desires without any effort. The verse is a powerful contrast to the earlier description of the deniers who were seized by their forelocks and feet and dragged. The believers are reclining on silk; the deniers are dragged on their faces. The believers reach for fruit; the deniers alternate between fire and boiling water. The surah's structure highlights these contrasts, urging the listener to choose the silk and the fruit over the forelock and the flame. The believer, reading this, feels a deep longing for that divine ease, and a renewed energy to continue the struggle of faith, knowing that every difficulty in this world is an investment in the eternal rest of the next.

=55=

The refrain returns for the twentieth time, following the description of the rich brocade couches and the near fruits. The comfort, the luxury, the ease, these are favours from the Lord of all worlds. The brocade of Paradise, the silk that lines the couches, is a favour that the Quraysh could understand. They were merchants who traded in fine fabrics. They knew the value of silk and brocade. The Quran uses their own language of luxury to describe a luxury that surpasses their wildest dreams.

The question is: will you deny the favour of this eternal luxury? Will you trade the temporary silk of this world for the eternal silk of the next? The fruits that hang within easy reach are a favour that addresses the deepest human desire for effortless provision. The denier, who toils for the fruits of this world, is asked: can you not see that the same Lord who provides these fruits with toil can provide fruits without toil? The believer answers with a heart that rests in the divine promise. "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I believe in the couches of brocade, in the near fruits. I work for them with my faith and my deeds, and I trust in Your generosity to grant them to me." The refrain here is a breath of pure hope. After the terrors of Hell, the surah has been bathing the listener in the beauties of Paradise, and each refrain is a gentle nudge, a loving question that says: this is all for you, if you will only accept it and be grateful. The believer feels the love of Ar-Rahman in this persistent questioning. It is not an interrogation; it is a courtship, a wooing of the soul with descriptions of beauty and the repeated invitation to acknowledge the Giver of that beauty. The refrain is the voice of the Beloved asking, "Do you love Me? Do you see what I have prepared for you?" The believer's heart whispers back, "Yes, my Lord, I see, I believe, I love, and I deny none of Your favours."

=56=

The description of the delights of Paradise now turns to the companions that await the believers. In the two Gardens will be maidens, the "hur," with modest, restrained gazes.

The Arabic "fihinna qasirat at-tarfi" describes females who lower their gaze, who do not look at anyone except their spouses. Their gaze is chaste, full of modesty and devotion.

This is a quality of profound beauty, a spiritual beauty that complements the physical. They are not looking around, comparing, or desiring others. Their entire attention is focused on their partner.

The verse then adds a description of their purity: "lam yatmithhunna insun qablahum wa la jann," meaning no human or jinn has ever touched them before. They are untouched, pure, reserved exclusively for the believers who enter these Gardens.

The word "tamth" refers to the act of deflowering, of sexual contact. The maidens of Paradise are created directly by Allah for the believers, untouched by any previous relationship. This purity is a symbol of the perfect, untainted nature of all the blessings of Paradise. In this world, even the best of blessings are mixed with some imperfection, some previous ownership, some wear and tear. In Paradise, everything is fresh, new, and pure. The description of the maidens is not meant to be the sole focus of Paradise, but it is part of the complete package of eternal bliss that addresses the deepest human desires for love, intimacy, and companionship. The believing men and women will both be granted pure and loving spouses, as indicated by other Quranic verses that mention purified spouses for both genders. The verse is a promise of the fulfilment of desire in a way that is beautiful, pure, and eternal. The believer, hearing this, is inspired to guard their own gaze and chastity in this world, knowing that the reward for modesty is the company of the modest in the Hereafter. The untouched maidens are a favour that represents the perfect, exclusive love that awaits the people of Paradise.

=57=

The refrain returns for the twenty-first time, now after the description of the pure maidens. The companionship of Paradise, the love and intimacy that is perfect, untainted, and eternal, is a tremendous favour from Ar-Rahman.

The question challenges both humans and jinn: can you deny this favour? The desire for loving companionship is one of the deepest human needs, placed in the soul by the Creator Himself. The denier may seek to fulfil this desire in ways that are illicit or purely worldly. The Quran tells them that the true fulfilment, the pure and eternal fulfilment, is in the Gardens of the Lord. To deny this is to deny a favour that answers the heart's deepest longing. The believer answers with a heart that is filled with hope and a sense of purpose. "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. I believe in the pure companions of Paradise. I strive to purify my own desires in this world, to lower my gaze, to guard my chastity, so that I may be worthy of the pure companions of the next." The refrain here is a powerful motivator for moral behaviour. The young believer who struggles against the temptations of the flesh hears this verse and feels the encouragement of the divine promise. The struggle is difficult, but the reward is unimaginable. The refrain reminds them that the Lord who asks "which of your Lord's favours will you both deny?" is the same Lord who has prepared these favours as a reward for patience and chastity. The refrain is the gentle voice of the Beloved, reminding the struggling soul that the wait is worth it, that the reward is real, and that the effort is seen and will be compensated beyond measure. The believer's heart is strengthened, and their resolve is renewed. The refrain is not just a question; it is a promise, a reassurance, a loving whisper that makes the path of righteousness easier to walk.

=58=

The physical beauty of the maidens of Paradise is described in terms of precious stones. They will be like rubies and coral.

The Arabic "ka annahunna al-yaqutu wa al-marjan" compares them to rubies, which are among the most precious and beautiful of gemstones, and coral, which was already mentioned earlier in the surah as a treasure from the sea. The comparison to rubies evokes an image of intense, radiant beauty, a deep red lustre, a purity of colour that captivates the eye. A ruby is hard, brilliant, and rare. The comparison to coral evokes the soft, delicate beauty of the red coral, the smooth, polished surface, the organic elegance. Coral was highly prized by the Arabs for its use in jewellery and ornamentation. By comparing the maidens to both rubies and coral, the verse combines the qualities of precious stones: brilliance, purity, colour, rarity, and enduring beauty. These maidens are not subject to ageing, to blemishes, to the fading of beauty that affects all worldly beings. Their beauty is as permanent and flawless as a gemstone.

This description is not meant to objectify but to use the highest human standards of beauty, the beauty of precious stones, to point to a beauty that surpasses them. The stones are mere shadows. The real beauty is in Paradise. The verse invites the listener to imagine the most beautiful thing they have ever seen, the sparkle of a ruby, the smoothness of coral, and to know that the companions of Paradise are even more beautiful, because they are the direct creation of the Most Beautiful. The believer, hearing this, feels a sense of wonder. The beauty of this world, even in its most precious forms, is but a hint of the beauty of the Hereafter. The rubies and coral of the earth are signs. The real rubies and coral are the maidens of Paradise. The verse is a call to seek the eternal beauty, to not be fooled by the fleeting sparkle of the world, but to work for the beauty that never fades.

=59=

The refrain returns for the twenty-second time, after the comparison of the maidens to rubies and coral. The beauty of the companions of Paradise is a favour that speaks to the human appreciation of aesthetics. The Lord who created rubies and coral in this world, which are objects of human admiration and desire, has created something far more beautiful in the Hereafter.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of this beauty? The denier, who might spend their life collecting precious stones, adorning themselves with jewellery, and admiring the beauty of gems, is being told: the real gems are not in the vaults of the wealthy; they are in the Gardens of Ar-Rahman, waiting for the God-fearing. To deny this is to be content with the imitation while rejecting the original. The believer answers with a heart that sees through the glitter of the world to the true treasure. "I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The rubies and coral of this world are beautiful, and they are Your gifts. But the beauty of Paradise is greater, and I long for it. Make me among those who see it." The refrain here is a calibration of the believer's sense of value. The world's most precious things are declared to be mere metaphors for the real treasures. This reorients the believer's ambition. Why obsess over collecting worldly jewels that can be lost, stolen, or destroyed, when the jewels of Paradise are eternal? The refrain is a liberation from materialism. It does not deny the beauty of the world; it puts it in its proper place. The rubies and coral are signs, not goals. The believer enjoys them with gratitude but does not attach their heart to them. The heart is attached to the One who created the rubies, and to the Paradise where the true beauties dwell. The refrain is the key that unlocks this detachment, freeing the soul from the tyranny of worldly possessions and directing its desire toward the eternal.

=60=

The surah pauses the description of Paradise to insert a verse of profound wisdom and principle. This is a rhetorical question that contains its own answer, and the answer is a resounding no.

The Arabic "hal jaza'u al-ihsani illa al-ihsan" is one of the most beautiful and hope-inspiring verses in the entire Quran. It states a divine law: the reward for excellence is nothing less than excellence.

The word "ihsan" is used twice. The first "ihsan" refers to the deeds of the believers: their worship, their righteousness, their God-consciousness, their acts of kindness and beauty performed in this world. Ihsan is the highest level of faith, defined by the Prophet as "worshipping Allah as if you see Him.

" The second "ihsan" refers to the reward from Allah, which is Paradise in all its beauty, the vision of the Divine Face, and the eternal good pleasure. What did the believers do? They performed ihsan. What will they receive? Ihsan. Goodness given, goodness returned. The verse is a powerful reassurance that Allah is not unjust and is not stingy. The smallest deed of goodness will not be lost. The tear shed in repentance, the charity given in secret, the prayer offered in the night, the patience shown in hardship, all of this is ihsan, and it will be repaid with ihsan.

The verse also implies a delightful reciprocity. The believer's ihsan was imperfect, mixed with shortcomings, done with struggle. The reward of ihsan is perfect, pure, unending, and effortless. It is a trade where the gain is infinite and the cost is finite.

The placement of this verse after the descriptions of the Gardens is perfect. The listener has just heard about the lush branches, the flowing springs, the paired fruits, the silk couches, the pure maidens. Then the question is asked: is the reward for ihsan anything other than this ihsan?

The answer is that these Gardens are the natural, inevitable reward for those who did good. This verse is a fountain of hope for every believer who feels their deeds are inadequate. It reassures them that Allah's generosity far exceeds their efforts. The believer hears this and is filled with a desire to excel in ihsan, to beautify their worship and their character, knowing that the reward is a beauty beyond imagination.
