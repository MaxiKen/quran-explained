=61=

The refrain returns for the twenty-third time, immediately following the profound declaration that the reward for goodness is goodness. The verse “Is there any reward for goodness except goodness?” is itself a magnificent favour. It reveals a principle of divine generosity that should make every believer’s heart soar with hope.

The question of the refrain now asks: can you deny this favour? Can you deny that Allah, out of His pure mercy, has established a law where a finite act of human goodness is repaid with infinite divine goodness?

This is not a transaction of equals. It is a transaction of sheer grace. The believer gives a little, and receives everything. The believer struggles for a few years, and rests forever. The believer plants a seed of ihsan in the soil of this world, and harvests an eternal garden of ihsan in the next. The denier, who might complain that the world is unfair, is being told that the ultimate reality is perfectly fair, and in fact, tilted heavily in favour of the righteous. Is this not a favour? The believer answers with a heart that is overwhelmed by the generosity of Ar-Rahman. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The fact that You accept my small deeds and reward them with such immense bounty is a favour I can never deny. I am humbled by Your ihsan. ” The refrain here is placed at the peak of the surah’s description of Paradise, after the highest delights have been mentioned, and just before the surah moves to describe a second tier of Gardens. It serves as a summary and a climax. The principle of ihsan being repaid with ihsan is the key that unlocks all the doors of Paradise. The refrain is a moment of reflection, a deep breath before the surah continues to unfold more layers of divine mercy. The believer feels the love of Allah in this refrain, a love that asks, again and again, “Do you see how good I am to you? Will you not acknowledge it?”

=62=

The surah now introduces a second pair of Gardens, distinct from and lesser in rank than the first two. The Arabic “wa min dunihima jannatan” means that besides those two lofty Gardens, lower in degree and proximity, there are two other Gardens.

The first pair of Gardens, described with lush branches, flowing springs, paired fruits, silk brocade, and pure maidens, was the reward for the Foremost, the Muqarrabun, those who feared standing before their Lord at the highest level. This second pair is for a different group: the People of the Right, the Ashab al-Yamin, the general body of the believers who, while righteous, did not attain the highest ranks of the Foremost. The fact that even the “lower” Gardens are described as being two Gardens, a pair, indicates that the mercy of Allah is vast and encompasses all levels of the faithful. There is no one who enters Paradise and receives a meagre reward. Even the “lesser” Paradise is a pair of magnificent Gardens. This hierarchy within Paradise is a motivation for the believers to compete in good deeds. The Foremost have their special reward, but the People of the Right are not neglected.

The verse also subtly refutes any notion that there is only one level of bliss. The mercy of Ar-Rahman is graduated, perfectly tailored to the capacity and the deeds of each soul. The believer, hearing this, feels a healthy combination of hope and aspiration. Hope that even if they cannot reach the highest peaks, they can still attain these beautiful Gardens. Aspiration to strive harder, to be among the Foremost who are closer to the Divine presence. The “below” does not imply inadequacy. It simply describes a relative position in the vast, multi-layered landscape of Paradise. The believer is grateful that their Lord has prepared not just one home but many homes, and that His mercy has a place for everyone who believed.

=63=

The refrain returns for the twenty-fourth time, following the announcement of the second pair of Gardens. The existence of multiple levels of Paradise, the fact that the mercy of Ar-Rahman is so vast that it accommodates both the Foremost and the People of the Right, is a tremendous favour. The denier might ask: why are there different levels?

The answer is that this is part of divine justice and mercy. Those who stroked harder are given a greater reward, but no one who believed is left out.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of a merciful God who rewards every sincere effort, who overlooks the shortcomings, who gives Gardens even to those who were not the absolute best? The believer answers with a heart full of gratitude for the divine inclusivity. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The fact that You have prepared a place for me, even if I am not among the Foremost, is a mercy beyond my comprehension. I trust in Your vast generosity.” The refrain here is a celebration of divine grace. It reminds the believer that their hope should not be narrow. The mercy of Ar-Rahman is wider than the sins of the sinner, wider than the imagination of the saint.

The second pair of Gardens is a testament to that wideness. The believer hears the refrain and feels a sense of relief. The path to Paradise, while requiring effort, is not an impossible, elite-only path. It is a path of mercy, and its gates are many. The refrain is a voice of encouragement, urging the listener to keep walking, to not despair, to know that their Lord has prepared not one but two, not two but four Gardens, and each one is more beautiful than anything the world can offer.

=64=

The description of the second pair of Gardens begins with their colour: they are dark green, intensely green. The Arabic “mudhammatan” comes from a root that means deep green verging on black because of the intensity of its lushness.

This is a colour of extreme vitality and life. A garden that is dark green is one that is densely planted, heavily watered, and thriving. The shade is deep, the foliage is thick, the vegetation is exuberant. In the desert context of Arabia, such deep green was the rarest and most coveted of sights. It represented the ultimate oasis, a place where water and plant life were so abundant that the green reached the point of saturation. The colour is also soothing to the eye. It is a colour of tranquillity and rest. The Gardens of the People of the Right are not sparse or pale. They are richly, intensely green.

This is the first impression, the overwhelming visual beauty that strikes the one who enters. The believer, hearing this, can almost see the deep, velvety green, can almost feel the coolness of the dense shade. The verse is short, just two words in Arabic, but it paints a vivid picture. It is a promise of life, freshness, and perpetual spring. The dark green is a contrast to the barren, rocky landscapes of the world, and a symbol of the eternal vitality of Paradise. There is no autumn in these Gardens, no browning leaves, no withering. The green is constant, renewed, and perfect. The believer longs for that green, and in longing, works for the deeds that will admit them to its shade. The verse is a powerful example of how the Quran uses simple sensory imagery to evoke a profound spiritual desire. The colour green becomes a symbol of divine pleasure, a visual representation of the mercy of Ar-Rahman.

=65=

The refrain returns for the twenty-fifth time, now after the description of the deep green colour of the second Gardens. The intense, beautiful green is a favour from the Lord. It is a gift to the eyes and the soul.

The question asks: can you deny the beauty of this colour? The denier who walks through a green valley in this world and admires the scenery is experiencing a shadow of this favour. The real, eternal, perfect green is in the Gardens of Ar-Rahman. To deny the latter is to be blind to the Giver of the former. The believer answers with a heart that sees the colour green in a new light. Every green leaf, every blade of grass, every verdant field becomes a reminder of the Gardens waiting below the two higher Gardens. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The green of this world is beautiful, and it is a sign of Your mercy. The dark green of Paradise is a greater mercy, and I long to see it. ” The refrain here is a training of the spiritual eye. The believer learns to see the natural world not as a random collection of colours but as a palette of divine signs. The green of the earth points to the green of Paradise. The beauty of a forest or a garden is a foretaste, an appetiser, a promise. The refrain sanctifies the sense of sight. It transforms looking at nature into an act of remembrance. The believer walks through a garden in this world and hears the echo of the refrain: “Which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” The heart answers, “None, my Lord, none. ” The refrain is the thread that connects the earthly garden to the heavenly one, weaving the world and the Hereafter into a single tapestry of divine favour.

=66=

The description continues with the water features of the second Gardens. In each Garden, there will be two gushing springs.

The Arabic “fihima ‘aynani naddakhatan” uses a different word for the springs than was used for the higher Gardens. Here, the springs are “naddakhatan,” meaning gushing forth, pouring out with force and abundance, constantly overflowing. This contrasts with “tajriyan,” flowing, used for the higher Gardens. The gushing springs suggest an even more vigorous, unstoppable flow of water, a bubbling, effervescent abundance. The water is not merely flowing gently; it is bursting forth with energy and life.

This is a different kind of beauty, a dynamic, exuberant beauty. The believers in these Gardens will witness the joyful rush of water, a spectacle that delights the senses. The fact that each Garden has two such springs, making four in total, ensures that there is no corner of these Gardens that is not refreshed by the sight and sound of water. The gushing springs represent the overflowing, generous nature of divine mercy. It is not a trickle; it is a flood of blessings. The believer, who in the world may have experienced scarcity, thirst, or the careful rationing of water, will be surrounded by water that gushes without end. The springs are also a metaphor for the spiritual gifts that will gush into the hearts of the believers: knowledge, wisdom, love, and divine inspiration, constantly renewed, never stagnant. The verse is an invitation to come to the source, to drink from the spiritual springs of the Quran and the remembrance of Allah in this world, so that one may be worthy of the gushing springs of the next. The believer hears this and feels a thirst, not just for physical water, but for the spiritual water that will gush in Paradise, the water of divine closeness and eternal joy.

=67=

The refrain returns for the twenty-sixth time, prompted by the gushing springs. The abundance of water, the sight of it gushing forth, the sound of it bubbling, is a favour that addresses a deep human need and appreciation.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of these springs? The denier who enjoys a fountain or a waterfall in this world is experiencing a small, temporary version of this divine mercy. The real, eternal, perfect springs are in Paradise. The believer answers with a heart that is refreshed by the very thought. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. Every fountain I see in this world reminds me of Your promise. The gushing springs of Paradise are a favour I believe in, and I ask You to let me drink from them.” The refrain here is a meditation on the nature of divine provision. The water of this world can become stagnant, can dry up. The springs of Paradise gush forever. The believer’s trust is in the eternal spring, not in the temporary watering holes of the world. The refrain also serves as a contrast to the earlier description of Hell, where the wicked alternated between fire and boiling water. The believer is safe from that torment and is instead promised gushing springs. The contrast amplifies the gratitude.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” is now a reminder of salvation, a celebration of being among the saved, not the damned. The gushing springs are not just water; they are symbols of life, of mercy, of the overflowing love of Ar-Rahman. The believer’s heart gushes with praise in response to the gushing springs of the divine promise. The refrain is the harmony that unites the water of the world with the water of the Hereafter, the physical with the spiritual, the seen with the unseen.

=68=

The description of the second Gardens' vegetation begins. They contain fruits of all kinds, and specifically mentioned are date palms and pomegranates.

The Arabic “fihima fakihatun wa nakhlun wa rumman” highlights these three categories. Fruits in general represent the sweet, delightful produce of Paradise. The date palm is singled out because of its supreme importance in the Arab world. It is the tree of life, providing staple food, shade, and materials. Its presence in Paradise is a continuity of a familiar blessing, elevated to perfection. The pomegranate is singled out for its unique beauty and deliciousness. It is a fruit mentioned elsewhere in the Quran as a sign of Allah’s bounty. Its jewel-like seeds, its tough rind protecting the delicate interior, its rich, sweet-tart taste, make it a fruit of wonder.

The pairing of the humble date and the exotic pomegranate represents the range of provision: the staple and the luxury, the sustaining and the delightful. The mention of these specific fruits does not mean that other fruits are absent; it highlights these as examples of the perfection and variety that await. The believer, eating a date or a pomegranate in this world, experiences a sensory preview of Paradise. The taste is a sign, a message from the unseen. The verse invites the believer to be mindful while eating, to taste the fruit and to remember the promise of the Gardens where the fruit is eternal, unblemished, and far more delicious. The date palm and the pomegranate become not just food but objects of meditation, bridges between the physical and the spiritual. The believer’s enjoyment is deepened by this awareness. They eat with gratitude, with longing, with hope. The verse is a call to see the extraordinary in the ordinary, the eternal in the temporary, the mercy of Ar-Rahman in every bite of a sweet, juicy pomegranate or a chewy, nourishing date.

=69=

The refrain returns for the twenty-seventh time, following the mention of the fruits, date palms, and pomegranates. The specific and familiar fruits are a tangible favour. The date palm and the pomegranate were well-known to the Quraysh. They knew their taste, their texture, their value. The Quran takes these familiar blessings and elevates them to the status of divine signs and eternal promises.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of these fruits? You enjoy the date in this world. Do you deny that the same Lord who created this date can create a far better one in Paradise? The believer answers with a heart that tastes the sweetness of a date and thinks of the sweetness of Paradise. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. This date is a gift from You, a sample of what awaits. The dates of Paradise are a greater gift, and I long for them. ” The refrain is a spiritual exercise in eating. It transforms the act of consuming food from a purely physical act into an act of remembrance and anticipation. The believer who is trained by this refrain does not simply eat and forget. They eat and remember. They eat and hope. The date becomes a sacrament, a visible sign of an invisible grace. The mercy of the refrain is that it sanctifies the most ordinary human activities. It brings the Hereafter into the kitchen, into the marketplace, into the orchard. The believer lives in a world that is thick with signs, every fruit a promise, every meal a reminder.

The question “which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?” echoes in the believer’s mind as they go about their day, and the constant answer is: “None, my Lord, none. I see Your favours everywhere, and I am grateful.” The refrain is the thread that weaves the physical and the spiritual into a seamless garment of faith.

=70=

The description of the companions in the second Gardens is given. In these Gardens, there will be good, beautiful women.

The Arabic “fihinna khayratun hisan” uses two powerful adjectives. “Khayrat” means good, excellent in character and nature, virtuous, kind, pleasant. “Hisan” means beautiful, lovely, with a beauty that is both physical and spiritual. The combination indicates that the companions of Paradise are not one-dimensional; they possess both inner goodness and outer beauty. Their character is as beautiful as their appearance.

This is the complete package of companionship. In the world, one might find beauty without character or character without beauty, and both are imperfect. In Paradise, the two are united in perfection.

The verse also states that these companions are in “all Gardens,” meaning in all four of the Gardens mentioned. The higher Gardens have their pure maidens, and these lower Gardens have their good and beautiful mates. No believer is deprived of the blessing of perfect companionship.

The word “khayrat” is deeply significant. It points to the ethical dimension of Paradise. The companions are not just objects of beauty; they are persons of virtue, whose goodness adds to the joy of their company. The believer, reading this, is motivated to seek inner and outer purification. The companions of Paradise are khayrat, good. The believer should also strive to be khayr, good, so that they may be a fitting match for such companions. The verse is an invitation to cultivate goodness and beauty in one’s own soul, to become a person of character and virtue, so that one may be worthy of the company of the good and beautiful in the eternal Gardens. The believer’s spouse in this world, if they are among the righteous, will be reunited with them in Paradise in a perfected form, a relationship that began in the world and flourishes forever in the Hereafter.

=71=

The refrain returns for the twenty-eighth time, following the description of the good and beautiful mates. The blessing of loving, virtuous, and beautiful companionship is a deep human need, met in Paradise with infinite abundance.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of these perfect companions? The denier who seeks companionship in this world, who marries, who loves, who longs for a partner, is seeking a shadow of this divine gift. The real, perfect, eternal companionship is in the Gardens of Ar-Rahman. To deny this is to settle for the shadow and reject the substance. The believer answers with a heart that loves the good and beautiful in this world as signs and trusts. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The goodness and beauty I see in the believers here are reflections of Your attributes. The goodness and beauty of Paradise are pure and eternal. I believe in them and I strive to be worthy of them.” The refrain here is a call to purify one’s earthly relationships. The believer’s marriage, their friendships, their family ties, are all rehearsals for the eternal companionship of Paradise. They are arenas for practicing the virtues of goodness, kindness, and love. The believer does not despise the lawful pleasures of human relationship; they sanctify them by connecting them to the divine promise.

The question of the refrain reminds the believer that the best of this world is but a pale reflection of the next, and that the heart should be primarily attached to the eternal, not the temporary. The good and beautiful mates of Paradise are a favour that inspires the believer to be a good and beautiful person themselves, to treat their earthly companions with love and respect, and to guide their family towards the Gardens where their relationships will be perfected and eternalised.

=72=

The description of the companions in the second Gardens gains more detail. They are “hur,” maidens with wide, gorgeous eyes, a term that appears throughout the Quranic descriptions of Paradise. The “hur” are characterized by the intense beauty of their eyes, where the white is brilliantly white and the dark iris is deeply dark, a mark of striking beauty in Arab culture. They are “maqsuratun fi al-khiyam,” meaning they are guarded, reserved, kept within pavilions or tents.

The word “khiyam” refers to large, luxurious tents or pavilions, which in the Arab context were the dwellings of nobility. The maidens are not wandering the streets; they are in their private, elegant dwellings, reserved and exclusive for their spouses.

This image conveys a sense of preciousness, privacy, and honour. The pavilion is a place of intimacy and beauty, a home within the Garden. The hur being in pavilions suggests that the believers will have private residences where they enjoy the company of their spouses in an atmosphere of perfect comfort and seclusion.

The word “maqsurat” also implies that their gaze is restricted; they do not look at others, and others do not look at them. Their beauty is a treasure kept for the one they love.

This is a profound picture of marital fidelity and exclusive devotion, carried into eternity. The believer, who in this world is commanded to lower their gaze and guard their chastity, is rewarded with a spouse whose gaze is exclusively for them, who is guarded in a beautiful pavilion, a private paradise within Paradise. The verse is a powerful motivator for modesty in the world. The fleeting, illicit glances that the world offers are nothing compared to the eternal, exclusive beauty that awaits the patient. The believer treasures this promise and lets it strengthen their resolve to remain chaste and faithful.

=73=

The refrain returns for the twenty-ninth time, following the description of the gorgeous-eyed maidens in their pavilions. The privacy, the exclusivity, the beauty of the companions, these are profound favours that touch the deepest human longing for intimacy and belonging.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of these pavilions, this guarded beauty, this perfect private world? The denier who seeks privacy and intimacy in this world, who builds houses and creates private spaces, is seeking a reflection of this divine gift. The real home, the real private sanctuary, is the pavilion in the Garden of Ar-Rahman. The believer answers with a heart that values modesty, privacy, and the sanctity of the marital bond. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The pavilions of Paradise are the homes I truly long for. The maidens of Paradise are the promise that makes modesty in this world easy.” The refrain here is a call to elevate one’s concept of home. The believer’s house in this world is temporary, a waystation. The real home is the khiyam of Paradise. The believer decorates their worldly home with faith, prayer, and Quran, making it a preview of the pavilion to come.

The question of the refrain invites the believer to compare the best of this world’s privacy with the privacy of Paradise and to recognise that one is a mere shadow of the other. The believer does not despise the comfort of their earthly home, but they hold it lightly, knowing that a far greater comfort awaits. The refrain is a gentle detachment from the world, not by denying its beauty, but by pointing to a beauty that infinitely surpasses it. The believer’s heart is in the pavilion, even while their body is in the house. And they walk through the world with the quiet confidence of one who has a reservation in the most exclusive dwelling imaginable.

=74=

The purity and exclusivity of the companions in the second Gardens are emphasised with the same phrase used for the companions of the higher Gardens, though in a slightly different grammatical form. The Arabic “lam yatmithhunna insun qablahum wa la jann” means that no human or jinn has ever had intimate contact with them before their present spouses. They are untouched, pure, created specifically for the believers of these Gardens. This verse reinforces the theme of complete newness and perfection in Paradise. Everything is fresh, pristine, without any prior claim or blemish. The companions are not recycled; they are original creations, a direct gift from Ar-Rahman to the believers. This purity is a reflection of the complete purity of the entire environment of Paradise. There is no pollution, no decay, no history of sin or corruption.

The concept of “untouched” also appeals to a deep human desire for exclusive belonging. The spouse in Paradise is wholly and completely the partner’s, with no past relationships to compare or compete with.

This is a state of perfect marital bliss that is rarely, if ever, attainable in this world. The believer, hearing this, feels a renewed appreciation for the wisdom of the Islamic teachings on chastity and modesty. The fleeting pleasures of premarital or extramarital relationships are shown to be paltry and damaging compared to the eternal, pure companionship that Allah has reserved for the patient. The verse is a powerful deterrent against sexual immorality. It tells the young believer: the pleasure you are tempted by is temporary, and it comes with a cost. The pleasure that awaits you in Paradise is eternal, pure, and far greater. Choose the eternal. Be patient for the sake of Ar-Rahman, and He will reward you with that which no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no heart has imagined.

=75=

The refrain returns for the thirtieth time, after the description of the untouched maidens. The purity and exclusivity of the spouses in Paradise is a magnificent favour.

The question asks: can you deny the favour of this pure, untainted love? The denier who pursues impure relationships in this world is, in effect, denying the favour of the pure relationship promised in the next. They are trading a diamond for a piece of glass. The believer answers with a heart that values purity, that strives for chastity, that believes in the promise of Ar-Rahman. “I deny none of Your favours, my Lord. The pure companions of Paradise are a favour I hold in my heart as a motivation and a comfort. I guard my own purity in this world, seeking the purity of the next.” The refrain here is a powerful support for the believer’s moral struggle. The world is full of temptations, and the path of chastity can feel lonely and difficult. But the refrain comes like a gentle hand on the shoulder, a reminder that the Lord sees, that the Lord knows, and that the Lord has prepared something infinitely better. The believer feels the compassion of Ar-Rahman in this question. He is not a demanding tyrant who forbids pleasure without offering an alternative. He is the Most Merciful who forbids the impure and promises the pure, who says: be patient, and I will give you what will make you forget every sacrifice. The refrain is a whisper of hope in the ear of the struggling soul. It is a promise wrapped in a question, a question that expects the answer: “I deny none of it, my Lord, and I am waiting for Your promise with patient certainty.”

=76=

The description of the comforts of the second Gardens reaches its conclusion. The believers will be reclining on green cushions and beautiful, splendid carpets.

The Arabic “muttaki’ina ‘ala rafrafin khudrin wa ‘abqariyyin hisan” paints a final image of luxurious ease. The “rafraf” are cushions, pillows, or possibly the raised couches themselves, and they are green, the colour of freshness and life, the colour of the Gardens described earlier. The “abqariyy” refers to fine, ornate carpets, the kind that are woven with intricate patterns and rich colours, the masterpieces of the carpet weaver’s art.

The word is related to ‘Abqar, a legendary place known for its exquisite carpets. The believers are reclining, relaxed, at peace, surrounded by softness and beauty. The green cushions and splendid carpets are the finishing touches of their eternal home.

This is the scene of the ultimate rest, the sabbath of the soul, the reward for a lifetime of striving and worship. The believer, who may have spent their life on hard floors, on simple mats, on battlefields, is given a resting place of unparalleled comfort. The green colour links back to the dark green of the Gardens, creating a unified aesthetic of lushness. The splendid carpets indicate that even the floors of Paradise are works of art. Every detail is beautiful, every surface is luxurious. The verse is a powerful contrast to the earlier images of Hell, where the wicked were seized and dragged. The believers recline on cushions; the deniers are dragged on their faces. The believers rest on carpets; the deniers alternate between fire and scalding water. The surah’s structure highlights this stark contrast, urging the listener to choose the cushions and carpets of Paradise over the forelocks and flames of Hell. The believer, reading this, feels a deep sense of peace. The struggle is over. The rest is eternal. The green cushions and splendid carpets await.

=77=

The refrain returns for the thirty-first and final time, after the description of the green cushions and splendid carpets. This is the last occurrence of the refrain in the surah, and it marks the conclusion of the grand catalog of divine favours. The green cushions and splendid carpets, the final resting place of the righteous, are the capstone of the pyramid of blessings.

The question is asked one last time, with the full weight of the entire surah behind it. After everything that has been described, from the creation of the human and the teaching of the Quran, to the sun and moon, the earth and its fruits, the ships and the seas, the warning of Hell, the promise of the double Gardens, the pure companions, the lush branches, the gushing springs, the paired fruits, the silk brocades, the dark green shades, the pavilions, the cushions and carpets, after all of this, the question is asked: “Then which of your Lord’s favours will you both deny?

” The question reverberates in the heart. The believer, overwhelmed by the sheer abundance of mercy, can only answer with a tearful, joyful, resounding: “My Lord, I deny none of Your favours. Not one. Not the smallest. Not the greatest. All of them are true. All of them are gifts. All praise and gratitude are Yours, O Most Compassionate.” The thirty-one repetitions of the refrain have trained the believer’s heart to see the world and the Hereafter through the lens of divine mercy. The refrain has been a companion, a teacher, a questioner, a comforter. It has been the voice of Ar-Rahman speaking directly to the soul. With this final occurrence, the refrain completes its work. The denier is left speechless, unable to deny any favour without lying. The believer is left in a state of ecstatic gratitude, enveloped in the mercy of the Most Compassionate. The refrain is the melody of the surah, and as it sounds for the last time, it leaves an echo that will resonate in the believer’s heart forever.

=78=

The surah concludes with a magnificent verse of glorification, a doxology that perfectly seals the entire discourse. “Blessed is the Name of your Lord, the Possessor of Majesty and Honour.

” The Arabic “tabaraka ismu Rabbika dhi al-jalali wa al-ikram” is a celebration of the divine name itself. The word “tabaraka” means blessed, exalted, abundant in goodness, far above all imperfection.

The Name of the Lord is a treasure, a source of continuous, overflowing blessings. The entire surah has been an exposition of the blessings that flow from that Name. The Quran began with “Ar-Rahman,” and now it ends with “Rabbika dhi al-jalali wa al-ikram.” The “Rabbika” (your Lord) directly addresses the Prophet, and through him every believer, creating an intimate connection. The Lord of Majesty and Honour was mentioned earlier in the surah, in verse 27, where it was said that only His Face, full of Majesty and Honour, will remain. Now the surah ends by blessing His Name. Majesty inspires awe and fear; Honour inspires love and hope. The Lord combines both in perfect harmony. The believer is left in a state of reverent love. The surah that catalogued favours now declares that the source of all those favours, the Name of the Lord, is itself blessed above all.

The Name is the key to all the treasures described. By remembering the Name, reciting it, invoking it, the believer partakes in its blessings. The verse is also a subtle warning.

The Name of the Lord is full of Majesty. The one who denies the favours attached to that Name will face the Majesty in its aspect of wrath. The one who acknowledges the favours will experience the Honour in its aspect of generous reward.

The surah ends not with a description of a place, but with a celebration of a Person, the Lord. The ultimate goal of all the favours was to bring the servant to the Lord, to know Him, to love Him, to be in His presence. The Gardens are beautiful, but they are merely the outward radiance of His Honour. The Fire is terrible, but it is the shadow of His Majesty. The reality behind all the verses is the Lord, full of Majesty and Honour. The believer, having completed the recitation of this extraordinary surah, feels a profound sense of blessing. They have been taken on a journey through the cosmos, through history, through the soul, through Hell and Paradise, and they have been brought at last to the doorstep of the Divine Name. They are left in a state of worship, whispering the praise of the blessed Name, and preparing to prostrate to the One who is Ar-Rahman, the Most Compassionate.
