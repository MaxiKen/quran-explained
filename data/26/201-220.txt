=201=

This verse draws the final line for the deniers whose hearts have been described in the previous verse as vessels into which denial has been steeped. The pronoun “it” refers to the Quran, the clear revelation from the Lord of all worlds. The verse states with absolute certainty that these people will not believe until they witness the painful punishment with their own eyes.

This is not a prediction of a future change of heart; it is a declaration that their window for sincere, saving faith has closed. They have passed the point where the signs in the horizons and in their own souls could move them. Now, only the brute force of the impending torment will wring from them a recognition of the truth, but that recognition will come too late to benefit them.

The phrase “painful punishment” translates ‘adhâb alîm, a torment that is excruciating in its intensity. The verse echoes the fate of Pharaoh, who, as the waves closed over him, finally said, “I believe that there is no god but He in whom the Children of Israel believe. ” His declaration was rejected because it came at the moment of certain death. The same pattern repeats with every nation that was destroyed after a prolonged period of warning and mockery. They would not believe until the punishment was at their doorstep, and then their belief was worthless. This verse serves as a severe warning to the Quraysh and to all who postpone faith. There is a point of no return, known only to Allah, after which the heart is irreversibly sealed. The only safe course is to believe while the warning is still a call from the unseen, not a visible, crushing reality. The mercy of Allah is that He delays the punishment to allow time for repentance, but that mercy must not be mistaken for indifference or inability. The believer who reads this verse feels a sense of urgency, a fear of delaying repentance, and a deep gratitude for having been guided before the arrival of the painful punishment. It is a call to make the most of the present moment, for the future is not guaranteed.

=202=

The description of the punishment continues with two characteristics that make it particularly terrifying: it will come to them suddenly, baghtatan, and it will arrive while they are in a state of complete heedlessness, “when they least expect it.” The Arabic conveys the image of being seized unawares, ambushed by a reality they had dismissed as a fable. They are not waiting for the punishment; they are mocking it, challenging the Prophet to hasten it. Their daily lives are consumed by trade, by family, by the pursuit of pleasure, and by the ridicule of the believers. The thought of a divine reckoning does not cross their minds except as a joke. Then, in a single moment, the jest becomes the terrifying truth. This suddenness is a consistent feature of divine punishments described in the Quran. The people of Noah were drowned in a flood they never imagined could overwhelm the whole earth. The people of Lot were seized by a cry at sunrise while they were going about their morning affairs. The people of Shu‘aib were destroyed by a cloud they had welcomed as shade. In every case, the punishment came when they felt most secure, when the possibility of divine intervention seemed most remote. The psychological impact of this verse on the Meccans was meant to shatter their complacency. They walked past the ruins of ‘Âd and Thamûd, they traded with the descendants of the saved, and yet they lived as if they were exempt from the same laws. The suddenness of the punishment is a manifestation of divine justice. If they were given advance notice of the exact day, they might repent out of fear, not out of sincere submission, and the test of faith in the unseen would be compromised. The believer learns from this to live with a constant, healthy expectation of the meeting with Allah, to be always prepared, and to never feel secure that tomorrow will be like today. The surprise is only for those who are heedless; for the God-conscious, the signs are clear and the heart is ever watchful.

=203=

When the punishment seizes them suddenly, their first and desperate reaction will be a plea for a respite. They will cry out, asking if they can be given more time, a delay, a return to the worldly life.

This is the same cry that the deviant will utter in the fire, as mentioned earlier in the surah: “If only we could have a second chance, then we would be believers.” The Arabic verb yurja‘ûn implies being sent back, being given a reprieve. The plea is utterly futile. The time for action was the life they squandered. Now that the punishment has arrived, the door of repentance is slammed shut. The verse exposes the psychology of the procrastinating sinner. Throughout life, they assume there will always be more time—another day, another year, another chance. They postpone their repentance to some vague future when they will supposedly be more ready. But death and punishment do not wait for human schedules. The irony is that the very time they now beg for was given to them in abundance. They had years, decades, even generations to heed the warning. They used that time to mock the warners and to deepen their disbelief. Now they would give all the treasures of the earth for just a few more moments, but it will not be granted. This cry is also a proof of the justice of Allah. Their own words testify that they recognise their error and that they would choose differently if given the chance. But the test of life is precisely that one must choose without seeing the consequences, based on faith in the unseen. Once the unseen becomes seen, the test is over. The believer hears this verse and resolves to treat the present moment as the most precious gift, the only capital for earning the pleasure of Allah. The cry of the damned is a warning that echoes across the pages of the surah: do not be among those who will beg for time when time has run out.

=204=

The verse turns to address the Meccan disbelievers directly with a rhetorical question of astonishment and rebuke. The Quraysh, in their arrogance, would mockingly tell the Prophet, “When will this promise come to pass, if you are truthful?” They would challenge him to hasten the punishment, as if its delay was evidence of its falsehood. This verse exposes the sheer folly of such a demand.

The question “Do they really seek to hasten Our torment?” translates the Arabic a-fa-bi-‘adhâbinâ yasta‘jilûn, with the particle a-fa indicating both wonder and condemnation. It is as if Allah is saying: how utterly foolish are these people who call down upon themselves what should be their greatest fear? They treat the warning as a game, a matter of pride in out-arguing the messenger. The torment they mockingly demand is not a light thing; it is the destruction of civilizations, the overturning of cities, the drowning of armies.

The verse reminds the listener that the delay of punishment is a mercy, not a weakness. Allah’s forbearance is an opportunity for repentance. When a debtor asks for more time, the creditor shows patience; how much more so the Most Merciful, who gives His servants year after year to turn back to Him. But when they throw that patience back in His face and dare Him to act, they are playing with fire. The same question was posed to earlier nations, and when the punishment came, there was no one to turn it back.

For the believer, this verse instils a deep sense of awe and caution. One should never adopt a cavalier attitude toward divine warnings or speak of punishment as if it were a distant abstraction. The proper response is to seek protection from it, to hasten toward good deeds, and to be grateful for every day that passes without calamity as a gift of time to improve one’s standing before Allah.

=205=

The verse presents a hypothetical scenario to the Prophet, inviting him and the listeners to contemplate a profound truth. “Imagine,” or more literally, “Have you considered,” if Allah were to grant the deniers enjoyment for many years.

The Arabic fa-in matta‘nâhum sinîna, “even if We gave them enjoyment for years,” uses the word matta‘a, which implies providing them with the pleasures and comforts of life, allowing them to live long, prosperous, and seemingly untroubled lives. The scenario is not far-fetched; it is exactly what was happening. The Quraysh were enjoying the fruits of the sacred months, the trade caravans, the orchards of Taif, and the leadership among the Arabs. They interpreted their prosperity as a sign of divine favour and a proof that they were on the right path. Allah was granting them enjoyment, year after year, and they were mistaking this respite for approval.

The verse begins to dismantle this illusion. The enjoyment of life, no matter how long it lasts, is not an indication of ultimate salvation. It is a test, a period of istidrâj where the sinner is drawn gradually toward destruction by the very blessings they take for granted.

The word “years” emphasises the duration. Even a long life of a hundred years is nothing compared to eternity. The Prophet is being asked to consider the fate of such people when their enjoyment comes to an abrupt end, which is the subject of the next verse. The lesson for the believer is to constantly evaluate one’s own prosperity in the light of the Hereafter. Long life and abundant wealth are not goals in themselves; they are means that will be accounted for. The righteous person enjoys the blessings of Allah with gratitude and uses them as fuel for obedience, while the wicked person uses the same blessings to rebel, making the blessing itself a witness against them.

=206=

The hypothetical scenario continues to its decisive moment. After all those years of enjoyment, the inevitable arrives: the punishment that they had been repeatedly threatened with, the very torment they mocked and dared the Prophet to hasten, finally comes upon them.

The phrase “what they had been threatened with” refers to the divine punishment in this world or the next, the destruction that the warners had announced with such clarity. The sudden arrival of this punishment after the long period of ease is a stark contrast that highlights the deceptive nature of worldly life. For years, they ate, drank, traded, laughed, and built. The punishment seemed like a myth, a scare tactic. But when it came, all those years of enjoyment evaporated into nothingness, as if they had been a fleeting dream. This verse underscores the Quranic concept that the respite given to disbelievers is not neglect but part of a precise divine plan. The threat is not empty; it is deferred to an appointed time, and when that time comes, it is utterly irreversible. The disbelievers of Mecca, who had been enjoying their lives and mocking the Prophet for over a decade, were being warned that their enjoyment was no guarantee of safety. The same Lord who gave them those years of pleasure could, in a single moment, turn their world upside down. The believer reflects on this and learns to never feel secure from the plan of Allah. The good times are not a sign of immunity; they are a test, and the test ends when the threatened punishment arrives. The only way to meet that moment in safety is to have responded to the threat with faith and repentance during the years of enjoyment.

=207=

The verse concludes the hypothetical with a devastating rhetorical question. All those years of enjoyment, all the accumulated wealth, the delicious food, the beautiful gardens, the comfortable homes—would any of it be of the slightest benefit to them when the punishment descends?

The answer is an emphatic no. The Arabic mâ aghnâ ‘anhum, “what would it avail them,” is a formula used in the Quran to highlight the utter futility of worldly possessions against divine decree. The years they spent in luxury do not subtract a single moment from the torment. If anything, they add to the regret, because they must account for every blessing they wasted in disobedience. The pleasure is gone, and only the liability remains. This verse strikes at the core of the materialist delusion that equates prosperity with success. The people of ‘Âd had gardens and springs, the people of Thamûd had carved palaces, the people of Madyan had thriving trade. All of it vanished in an instant, and none of it could shield them from the punishment. The Quraysh, who prided themselves on their two annual trade journeys and the wealth they accumulated, were being told that the very things they considered the ultimate goods were worthless in the scales of the Hereafter. The only thing that benefits a person is a sound heart, as Abraham’s prayer declared. The believer reads this verse and feels a profound reordering of priorities. The enjoyment of this world is not forbidden, but it must not become the goal. It is a passing shadow, and its true value lies only in whether it was a means to draw closer to Allah or a distraction that led to ruin.

The question echoes through the ages, challenging every soul: when your appointed hour comes, what will all your years of enjoyment avail you if you stand before your Lord empty-handed?

=208=

After the dire warnings of sudden punishment, the surah now affirms a fundamental principle of divine justice. Allah states as a universal law that He has never destroyed any society, any qaryah, meaning any town or civilization, without first sending warners.

This is the mercy that precedes the might. The destruction of nations, as recounted throughout the surah, was never an arbitrary act of divine wrath. Each of those nations—the people of Noah, ‘Âd, Thamûd, Lot, Madyan, and Pharaoh—received messengers who spoke their language, who lived among them, who warned them with clear arguments, and who called them to repentance for extended periods. The warners were sent precisely so that no one could claim on the Day of Judgment that they were taken unawares, that they had no chance to know the truth. This verse dismantles any notion of injustice in the divine plan. The destroyed nations were not innocent victims; they were communities that had been given every opportunity to reform and had stubbornly refused. The sending of warners is a manifestation of the divine name ar-Rahîm, the Most Merciful. Warning is itself a form of mercy, even if the content of the warning is fearsome. By informing people of the consequences of their actions and calling them to change, Allah provides the means of escape.

The verse also establishes the function of the Prophet Muhammad as the warner to the Quraysh and, ultimately, to all humanity. He is the final link in the chain of warners, and his presence among them means that they have been given the complete proof. If destruction were to befall them now, it would be because they rejected the warner, not because they were neglected.

For the believer, this verse provides a profound sense of the justice of Allah and a deep appreciation for the sending of messengers. It is a mercy that God does not strike without warning, and the greatest warning is the revelation itself.

=209=

The verse continues and completes the statement of the previous one. The warners were sent with a specific purpose: to remind.

The Arabic word dhikrâ, “a reminder,” is central. The human soul, in its pure state, already knows its Lord, as all souls testified before birth. But the dust of worldly life causes forgetfulness. The warners come to reawaken that dormant memory, to polish the rust from the mirror of the heart, and to call people back to their original covenant with their Creator. The reminder is an act of pure goodness. It does not impose a new burden; it simply points to the truth that is already inscribed in the fitrah.

The verse then closes with a powerful declaration: “for We would never wrong anyone.” The Arabic wa mâ kunnâ zâlimîn, “and We are not ever unjust,” is a definitive denial of any possibility of divine injustice. The destruction of a people, when it occurs, is not an act of oppression but an act of pure justice, the recompense for their own persistent wrongdoing after the reminder has been clearly delivered. The combination of these two verses—warning before destruction, and the absolute negation of divine wrong—provides a complete answer to the question of why a merciful God would destroy nations. It is because He is also just, and His justice demands that those who knowingly and persistently rebel against the truth after it has been made crystal clear must face the consequences. The delay of punishment is mercy; its eventual arrival is justice. The Quraysh, hearing this, were being told that they had been sent a warner, the reminder was among them, and if they continued to reject, any punishment that came would be entirely their own doing.

For the believer, this verse is a comfort and a warning. It comforts because it assures that Allah is perfectly just and that no soul will be wronged. It warns because it makes clear that the presence of the warner removes all excuse, and with great light comes great responsibility.

=210=

The surah now returns to a direct defence of the Quran’s divine origin, refuting a specific accusation levelled by the Meccan disbelievers. They claimed that the Prophet Muhammad was a poet or a soothsayer, and that his recitation was inspired by devils, the shayâtîn, who brought him the words from the unseen. This verse categorically denies that claim. The Quran was not brought down by the devils. The emphatic particle mâ and the word tanzîl, “brought down” or “sent down,” connect this verse back to the earlier description of the revelation descending from the Lord of all worlds through the Trustworthy Spirit. The descent of the Quran is a divine operation, involving the highest levels of purity and holiness. The devils have absolutely no part in it. This refutation is crucial because it distinguishes the Prophet from the soothsayers and fortune-tellers who were known in Arabia and who claimed to receive information from jinn. The Quraysh, in their confusion and malice, tried to lump Muhammad into that category. But the content of the Quran itself is the strongest evidence against this accusation. The Quran calls to the worship of Allah alone, condemns idolatry and immorality, commands justice, and warns of the Day of Judgment. The devils, who are the enemies of humanity and the callers to evil, would never deliver a message that so comprehensively destroys their own kingdom. As the Quran says elsewhere, “They do not descend except upon every sinful liar.” The devils would not bring a book that curses them and guides people to salvation from their traps. The verse, therefore, is a logical and theological argument. The nature of the message proves its source. Light does not descend from darkness. The believer reads this and feels a renewed confidence in the divine purity of the Quran. It is not a mixture of truth and falsehood, nor a whispered inspiration from the unseen world of the jinn. It is the pure, undiluted word of the Lord of all worlds, carried by the noblest angel, received by the noblest human.

=211=

The verse adds two further layers to the refutation. First, it states that it is not befitting or appropriate for the devils to bring such a revelation.

The Arabic wa mâ yanbaghî lahum means that the act of bringing divine revelation is completely outside the nature, role, and capacity of the devils. Their entire orientation is toward misguidance, deceit, and evil. Bringing a book of pure truth that guides to righteousness is the antithesis of their being. It would be as incongruous as a thief building a bank. Second, the verse states that even if it were somehow appropriate, they do not have the ability to do so. Wa mâ yastatî‘ûn, “and they are not able. ” The devils lack the power and the access to bring down a revelation from the heavens. Their abilities, though real and sometimes terrifying to humans, are severely limited. They cannot penetrate the celestial barriers to steal the divine word in its pure form, as the next verse will explain.

This is a complete closure of the argument. The devils are both unwilling, in the sense that their nature rebels against such a pure task, and unable, because the heavens are fortified against them. The combination of moral incongruity and physical incapability leaves no room for the accusation. The Quraysh, who resorted to the slander of demonic inspiration because they could not rationally deny the Quran’s eloquence and truth, were being told that their own claim defies both logic and the known facts about the jinn. The believer learns from this the importance of defending the faith with reason and with knowledge of the unseen that the Quran provides. The devils are real, but their power is circumscribed, and their nature is evil. The Quran, which commands all that is good and forbids all that is evil, can only be from the source of all goodness, the Lord of all worlds.

=212=

The verse gives the specific reason why the devils are unable to bring down the Quran: they are barred, prevented, completely cut off from overhearing the divine discourse in the higher celestial assembly. The Arabic innahum ‘ani as-sam‘i la-ma‘zûlûn, “indeed they, from hearing, are removed,” indicates that the devils used to attempt to eavesdrop on the conversations of the angels in the heavens to gain snippets of knowledge about future events, which they would then whisper to their human allies among the fortune-tellers.

However, with the advent of the Prophet Muhammad and the revelation of the Quran, the heavens were guarded even more strictly. The devils are now driven away by flaming meteors whenever they attempt to ascend and listen. This concept is elaborated in other surahs, such as Surah Al-Jinn and Surah Al-Mulk. The “overhearing” refers to the devils’ attempts to snatch fragments of the divine decrees that the angels discuss. Before Islam, they had some success, and they would mix those fragments with a hundred lies and pass them to the soothsayers. But now, the protection is intensified, and the pure revelation of the Quran is completely safeguarded from their interference.

This is a powerful argument. The Meccans knew the lore of the jinn and the soothsayers. They knew that the soothsayers’ information was a garbled mix of truth and falsehood. The Quran, by contrast, is a book of pristine coherence, deep wisdom, and unassailable truth, with no trace of the chaotic, mixed nature of satanic whispers. The purity of the Quran is the proof that it did not come through the corrupted channels of the devils. The believer marvels at the divine protection of the revelation. The Quran that we recite today has been guarded not only in its transmission on earth but in its very descent from the heavens. It is the untouched word of Allah, and the devils, for all their cunning, could not lay a finger on it.

=213=

This verse marks a sharp transition from the argument about the Quran’s divine origin to a direct command to the Prophet and, by extension, to all believers. The particle fa, “so,” links the command to the preceding truths. Since the Quran is the revelation from the Lord of all worlds, since it was brought by the Trustworthy Spirit, and since it has nothing to do with the devils, the only logical and necessary response is to worship Allah alone. The command “do not ever call upon any other god besides Allah” is phrased in the strongest prohibitive terms, lâ tad‘u ma‘a Allâhi ilâhan âkhar, “do not invoke with Allah any other god. ” The word tad‘u, from du‘â’, encompasses both worship and supplication. It means do not direct any form of prayer, request, sacrifice, or devotion to any being other than Allah. The verse singles out the Prophet as the primary addressee, even though he is divinely protected from shirk, to emphasise the severity of the sin. If the Prophet, with his lofty station, is warned in such terms, how much more cautious should ordinary believers be? The consequence of violating this command is stated plainly: “or you will be one of the punished.” The threat of being among the punished, al-mu‘adhdhabîn, is a direct reference to the destroyed nations and the inmates of Hell whose stories fill the surah. This verse is the core message of all the prophets: pure, uncompromising monotheism. It ties the entire surah together. Every prophet from Noah to Shu‘aib called their people with the same essential message: fear Allah and obey me, which means worship Him alone. The believer who recites this verse is reminded that tawhid is not just a theological concept but a life-or-death matter. It is the only safeguard against the painful punishment. The verse calls for a constant vigilance over the heart, ensuring that no hidden idol—whether wealth, status, ego, or love of praise—shares the worship that is due only to the Creator.

=214=

After the command to maintain pure monotheism, the Prophet is given a specific instruction for his mission. He is to warn the people, delivering the message of the Quran to all, but he must begin with his closest relatives, his ‘ashîrah, his clan.

The Arabic wa andhir ‘ashîrataka al-aqrabîn, “and warn your closest kindred,” establishes a principle of priorities in da‘wah. The mission is universal, but it starts at home. The closest family members have the greatest right to be warned because of the ties of blood and affection, and because their acceptance or rejection has a profound impact on the wider community.

This command was dramatically fulfilled when the Prophet climbed Mount Safa in Mecca and called out to the various clans of Quraysh, warning them of the impending punishment if they did not believe. According to authentic hadith, when they gathered, he said, “If I were to tell you that there is an enemy army behind this mountain about to attack you, would you believe me?” They replied, “Yes, we have never known you to lie. ” He then said, “Then I am a warner to you before a severe punishment.” This event is the living tafsir of this verse. The command to begin with relatives does not imply exclusivity; it establishes a method. The family is the training ground for da‘wah. If a prophet’s own family rejects him, it is a great trial, but if they accept, they become a core of strong support.

For the believer, this verse teaches that one’s religious responsibility begins within one’s own household. It is not enough to be a caller to the world while neglecting the spiritual state of one’s spouse, children, and parents. The warning should be delivered with love, wisdom, and persistence, following the model of the Prophet, who never gave up on his uncle Abu Talib despite his refusal to embrace Islam until his death.

=215=

After the command to warn, the Prophet is instructed on how to treat those who respond to the warning and embrace faith. The Arabic wa akhfid janâhaka li-man ittaba‘aka min al-mu’minîn, “and lower your wing to the believers who follow you,” uses a beautiful metaphor drawn from the image of a bird that gently lowers its wing to shelter and protect its young. The “wing” symbolises mercy, humility, gentleness, and protection. The command is to be gracious, kind, and approachable to the community of believers, especially those who are weak or poor, like many of the early Muslims were. This verse directly counters the criticism of the elites who complained that the Prophet was surrounded by the “lowest of the low.” Allah tells him to treat those followers not with contempt or impatience but with tender care. This instruction mirrors the command given to Moses and other prophets. The lowering of the wing is an act of tawâdu‘, of humility. The Prophet, though he is the leader and the messenger, is not to be arrogant or distant. He is to be a compassionate shepherd to his flock. The verse sets the standard for all Islamic leadership. A true leader is a servant to the believers, not a tyrant over them. The believer learns that the community of faith is bound by a special love and tenderness. We are to lower our wings to one another, to be gentle with each other’s faults, to support the weak, and to create an atmosphere of mercy within the ummah. The sternness is for the enemy and for the deniers; the gentleness is for the household of faith. This verse, placed right after the command to warn the relatives, balances the gravity of the warning with the beauty of the community it seeks to build. The warning may be fearsome, but the community of the warned who accept is a community of love, humility, and mutual care.

=216=

The verse provides the instruction for the scenario where the warning is rejected and the relatives or others disobey the messenger. The Prophet is told that if they persist in their disobedience after the clear message has been delivered, he is to publicly declare his disassociation from their deeds.

The Arabic fa-in ‘asawka fa-qul innî barî’un mimmâ ta‘malûn, “then if they disobey you, say: indeed I am innocent of what you do.” This is the principle of barâ’ah, the disavowal of shirk and its people. The messenger’s duty is to deliver the warning; he is not responsible for forcing people to believe. Once the message has been conveyed, those who reject it bear their own burden, and the messenger is absolved of any guilt for their continued misguidance. This verse is a source of immense relief for the Prophet. It releases him from the crushing weight of responsibility for the choices of others, a weight that nearly consumed him, as mentioned at the beginning of the surah. He is not to grieve himself to death; he is to disavow their deeds and entrust the matter to Allah. This declaration of innocence is not a personal insult but a theological and legal separation. It means that the bond of faith, which is the strongest bond, is severed by their persistent disobedience.

This principle was exemplified by Abraham when he disowned his father and his people’s idolatry. For the believer, this verse provides a balance between love for family and loyalty to the truth. One should warn and care for relatives with all tenderness, but if they choose disbelief and refuse to change, the believer must ultimately dissociate from their sins, while still maintaining the hope that they may one day turn back.

The final judgment is with Allah, and the believer’s soul is not tainted by the sins of others.

=217=

Having been instructed to warn, to be gentle to the believers, and to disavow the disobedient, the Prophet is now told the source of all his strength and comfort: complete reliance on Allah. The command “Put your trust” translates wa tawakkal, which means to place one’s full trust, reliance, and dependence upon Allah after taking all necessary human measures. The two divine names that follow are chosen with perfect precision. The Prophet is to trust in al-‘Azîz, the Almighty, whose power can overcome every enemy, shatter every opposition, and protect him from all harm. This name is a direct answer to the threats of the Quraysh and the apparent might of the deniers. He is also to trust in ar-Rahîm, the Most Merciful, whose mercy will guide him, comfort him, forgive his lapses, and shower blessings upon him and the believers.

The pairing of might and mercy has been the signature refrain of the entire surah, sealing the story of every destroyed nation. Now it becomes the personal anchor for the Prophet. Trust in Allah, or tawakkul, is the heart of Islamic spirituality. It is not passive resignation but an active, confident reliance on the One who controls all outcomes. The Prophet, having done his duty of warning, entrusts the results to the Almighty and the Most Merciful. This verse is a lifebuoy for every believer facing trials, rejection, or the daunting task of conveying truth. When human resources are exhausted, when the hearts of others remain sealed, the only refuge is the Lord who is both able to change the situation and merciful in His dealings. The believer recites this verse and feels a weight lifting, a calming assurance that the battle is not theirs alone but is in the hands of the Best of Guardians.

=218=

The verse now describes the One in whom the Prophet is to place his trust, adding layers of intimacy and reassurance. Allah is the One who sees him when he rises.

The Arabic alladhî yarâka hîna taqûm, “who sees you when you stand up,” is a beautiful and personal affirmation of divine watchfulness. The “rising” is understood, particularly from the context of the next verse, to refer to the Prophet’s standing for the night prayer, the tahajjud. In the stillness of the night, when the world sleeps and no human eye is watching, the Prophet would rise from his bed, perform ablution, and stand in long, devoted prayer before his Lord. This verse tells him that Allah sees him in that most private and sincere moment of worship. The divine gaze is not a surveillance of fear but a gaze of love, appreciation, and companionship. The Prophet is never alone in his night vigils; the Lord of the heavens and the earth is fully aware of him. This knowledge transformed the night prayer from a duty into an intimate conversation. The believer who reads this verse is filled with a sense of being seen by the Beloved. The night prayer, which is a sunnah so highly recommended, becomes a secret meeting between the servant and the Lord, a meeting that no one else knows about but that is recorded and cherished by the All-Seeing.

The verse also connects back to the command at the beginning of the surah not to grieve. The Prophet’s rising at night is a manifestation of his dedication, and Allah sees it. The trial of the day is washed away in the prayer of the night, under the watchful, loving eye of the Almighty, Most Merciful.

This is a deep source of comfort and motivation for all who struggle to rise before dawn to meet their Lord.

=219=

The divine watchfulness is further detailed. Allah does not only see the Prophet when he rises in solitude but also sees his movements, his taqallub, among those who prostrate.

The Arabic wa taqallubaka fî as-sâjidîn, “and your moving about among those who prostrate,” paints a dynamic picture. It refers to the Prophet’s movements during the congregational prayer: his standing, bowing, prostrating, and sitting, all in the company of the believers who pray behind him.

The word sâjidîn, “those who prostrate,” is a beautiful term for the believers, highlighting their most distinctive and humbling act of worship. The verse tells the Prophet that Allah sees him in his leadership of the prayer, in his care for the community, and in his participation with them in the worship of the One God. This encompasses all his public religious activities, his teaching, and his guiding of the nascent ummah. The inclusion of the fellow worshippers is significant. The Prophet is not an isolated mystic; he is the imam of a community. Allah sees his interaction with them, his patience with their questions, his endurance of their weaknesses, and his love for them. This verse brings immense comfort. The Prophet, who faced rejection from his clan and mockery in the streets, had a sanctuary in the prayer, and in that sanctuary, he was surrounded by the sâjidîn, and the Lord of all worlds was watching over them all.

For the believer, this verse elevates the status of the congregational prayer. Our movements in the mosque, our standing shoulder to shoulder, our collective prostration, are all under the loving gaze of Allah. This awareness should transform the quality of our prayer, filling it with humility, sincerity, and a profound sense of being in the divine presence, not just individually but as part of the blessed community that prostrates.

The verse concludes this section of reassurance with two foundational divine attributes. Allah is indeed the All-Hearing, as-Samî‘, and the All-Knowing, al-‘Alîm. The All-Hearing hears the recitation of the Prophet in the night, the whispered supplications, the cries of the oppressed believers, and the insults of the deniers. Nothing escapes His hearing. The All-Knowing knows the intentions of the heart, the sincerity of the one who rises to pray, the struggles of the believers, and the secret plots of the enemies. He knows the fatigue of the Prophet’s feet during the long night vigils, and He knows the reward that awaits him. The combination of perfect hearing and perfect knowledge means that Allah is completely aware of every aspect of His servant’s state, both the outward and the inward.

This is the foundation of tawakkul. One can only truly trust in a Lord who hears every prayer and knows every need. If Allah were not All-Hearing, the Prophet’s night prayers might go unheard. If He were not All-Knowing, the hidden sincerity of the worshippers might go unnoticed. But He is both, so every moment of devotion, every tear shed in the darkness, every prostration made in humility, is fully registered and will be magnificently rewarded. This verse is an anchor for the believer who feels alone or unheard. The world may not listen, but the All-Hearing listens. The world may not understand your pain, but the All-Knowing knows it completely. This knowledge transforms worship from a mere ritual into a deep, personal connection with a Lord who is perfectly attentive and perfectly understanding. It is the ultimate comfort for the Prophet and for every soul that turns to its Creator.
