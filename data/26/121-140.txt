=121=

The refrain returns to mark the end of Noah's narrative. "In this" refers to the entire story just recounted: the centuries-long call of a patient prophet, the rejection by the arrogant elite, the threat of stoning, the prayer for decisive judgment, the building of the Ark under mockery, the divine salvation of the believers and the paired creatures, and the comprehensive drowning of the rest. Every element of this history is a sign, an âyah, pointing to the immutable laws of Allah. The sign is that the truth, no matter how weak it appears, is ultimately victorious, while falsehood, no matter how entrenched and powerful, is ultimately swept away. The sign is also that divine patience is vast, but it is not infinite. When the warning is persistently ridiculed, the punishment descends.

Yet the refrain observes the same tragic reality: most of them would not believe. Here "them" refers firstly to the Quraysh who are hearing the Quran. They are being told the story of Noah in vivid detail, yet most of them will not take the lesson. The human heart, when it turns away, can hear a hundred cautionary tales and still remain unmoved. This refrain is a mirror held up to the Meccans. They are walking in the footsteps of Noah's people, and if they do not change course, their end will be the same. The verse is also a comfort to the Prophet. He is not to grieve himself to death because the majority refuses to believe; this is simply the pattern of history. His duty is to convey, and the results are with Allah. The verse creates a rhythm in the surah, a repeated pulse of warning and lament that drives the message deep into the listening heart.

=122=

The twin attributes seal the Noah narrative, just as they sealed the stories of Moses and Abraham. After the drowning of the "rest," one might be overwhelmed by the sheer terror of divine punishment. The verse balances that by immediately reminding that the same Lord is also ar-Rahîm, the Most Merciful. The Almighty who drowned the deniers is the Most Merciful who saved Noah and the believers, who taught him how to build the Ark, who kept the believing community safe amidst the raging floodwaters. "Your Lord" personalises the relationship for the Prophet Muhammad, anchoring him in the certainty that the Lord who was with Noah in the Ark is with him in Mecca.

The pairing of might and mercy is not a contradiction but a perfect harmony. The might ensures that justice is done and that the oppressors do not escape. The mercy ensures that the righteous are protected and that the door of repentance remains open for any who wish to enter it.

For the believer, this verse is a source of profound security. The world may be filled with Noah-like challenges: long years of calling, mockery, and apparent failure. But the Lord is Almighty, able to change the situation in an instant. And He is Most Merciful, aware of every tear, every supplication, every moment of patient endurance. The Noah story, with this divine signature, becomes not just a tale of destruction but a story of hope. It teaches that the Ark of salvation is always prepared for those who board it with faith, and the Captain of that Ark is the Lord of all worlds Himself.

=123=

The surah now moves to the story of the people of ‘Âd, another ancient civilisation destroyed for their rejection of the truth. As with Noah, the verse says they rejected the messengers, using the plural, even though only one messenger, Hûd, was sent to them. This affirms the principle that to reject one prophet is to reject them all, for the message of monotheism is one. The people of ‘Âd were the successors of Noah's people, a civilisation that rose to great power and prosperity. They inhabited the region of al-Ahqâf, the winding sand dunes, in the southern part of the Arabian Peninsula. They were known for their impressive physical stature, their advanced architecture, and their immense strength. Allah had blessed them with abundant gardens, springs, and livestock. But prosperity led to arrogance, and arrogance led to idolatry and tyranny. They began to worship idols and to oppress the weak. The verse encapsulates their entire spiritual condition in a single act: they rejected the messengers. This rejection was not a passive disbelief but an active turning away, often accompanied by mockery and persecution of the one calling them to the truth. For the Quraysh, the mention of ‘Âd was a powerful reminder. The ruins of ‘Âd were known to the Arabs, passed on their trade routes. They could see with their own eyes the physical remains of a once-mighty nation that had been obliterated. The lesson was clear: the Quraysh were repeating the same sins of arrogance, idolatry, and rejection, and if they persisted, they could expect the same outcome. The story of ‘Âd is a warning written in the landscape of Arabia itself.

=124=

Like every messenger in this surah, Hûd is introduced with the affectionate title "their brother." He was not a stranger or an angel descending from the sky but a man from among them, known to them. His lineage connected him to the people; he was Hûd ibn Shâlikh, from the noble line of ‘Âd. The brotherhood highlights the mercy and relatability of prophethood. The messenger is one of the family, deeply concerned for his kin. Hûd's opening call is identical to that of Noah, Moses, and Abraham: "Will you not fear Allah?

" This is the foundational question of every prophetic mission. The people of ‘Âd, like all corrupt societies, had lost their fear of the Creator. They feared poverty, they feared defeat by enemies, they feared the loss of status, but they did not fear the One who held their very souls. Hûd's question is designed to awaken the dormant conscience. It assumes that the natural human disposition, the fitrah, recognises that the Creator deserves reverence.

The question is gentle but devastating. It implies that their entire way of life—their idol worship, their oppression, their heedlessness—is a symptom of a missing core: the fear of Allah. If they could only recover that fear, everything else would fall into place. For the Quraysh, hearing these same words from their own "brother" Muhammad, the question was just as piercing.

The repetition of this identical opening line across the surah hammers home the unity of the divine message. The call never changes, because the spiritual disease it addresses is always the same: the loss of God-consciousness.

=125=

Hûd, following the prophetic pattern, establishes his personal credibility before his people. He tells them that he is a trustworthy messenger, an amîn. This was a quality they had witnessed in him throughout his life. Before his prophethood, Hûd was known among ‘Âd for his honesty, his integrity, and his sound judgment. No one could point to a lie he had told or a trust he had betrayed. By invoking this shared history, Hûd removes the possibility of an ad hominem attack. His people cannot accuse him of being a fraud seeking personal gain, because his entire life testifies against such an accusation.

This is the same dynamic that played out with the Prophet Muhammad, whose nickname al-Amîn was a testament to his character decades before revelation. The declaration of trustworthiness is also a subtle challenge: if you admit I am truthful in everything I have ever said, then why do you doubt me now when I speak on behalf of the Lord? The only logical response is to at least consider the message seriously. The fact that they refused to do so reveals that their rejection was not based on reason or a genuine doubt about the messenger's character. It was based on arrogance, on attachment to their false gods and their worldly privileges.

This verse teaches that the character of the caller to truth is a crucial part of the message. Without trustworthiness, even the clearest truth can be dismissed. The prophets were not only bearers of revelation; they were living embodiments of its moral demands.

=126=

Hûd delivers the twin command that is the heart of the prophetic call. "Fear Allah" is the internal, spiritual foundation. "Obey me" is the external, practical manifestation of that fear. The two are inseparable. A person cannot claim to fear Allah while living in disobedience to the messenger He sent. Hûd's call to obey him is not a call to follow a human opinion but to submit to the divine guidance that he carries. His instructions are not his own inventions; they are the revealed remedy for the specific diseases of his people. For ‘Âd, obedience to Hûd meant abandoning their idols, reforming their tyrannical behaviour, and turning to the worship of Allah alone with sincere repentance. The command is simple, direct, and non-negotiable. It cuts through the complexities and rationalisations that people build around their sins. The believer learns from this the proper relationship between the inner state of the heart and the outward actions. Fear of Allah is not a vague, emotional sentiment; it is a force that drives one to align every aspect of life with the prophetic model. The Quraysh, who claimed to revere Allah but refused to obey His messenger, were living examples of the severance of these two commands. Hûd's words exposed their inconsistency and demanded a holistic submission that they were unwilling to give. The verse stands as a timeless call to integrate belief and action, conscience and conduct.

=127=

Hûd, like Noah before him, declares his complete independence from any material incentive. He asks for no payment, no salary, no elevation in social status, no share of their wealth. This announcement silences the cynical accusation that prophets invent their message for worldly gain. Hûd has nothing to gain from them; his sustenance and his ultimate recompense are exclusively with Allah.

The phrase "Lord of all worlds" is significant. It ties his own reward to the very Being whose universal lordship he is calling them to acknowledge. If he were a false prophet, he would be seeking reward from the lords of this world—from kings, from the wealthy, from the powerful. But he turns away from all that and looks only to the Lord of all worlds.

This is the mark of a sincere caller. His motivation is not the applause of the crowd or the accumulation of riches but the pleasure of his Creator.

The verse also teaches the believer a principle of da‘wah: one should never make the acceptance of the message conditional on receiving something from the people. The caller to truth must be financially and emotionally independent, relying solely on Allah. This gives the message a purity and a power that cannot be matched by any movement driven by worldly incentives. Hûd's words are a declaration of freedom: he is not a servant of the people's opinions or a seeker of their favours; he is a servant of the Lord of all worlds, and that is enough for him.

=128=

Hûd now moves from the general call to taqwâ to a specific critique of his people's behaviour. He questions their practice of building a landmark, an âyah, on every elevated location.

The word translated as "landmark" can also mean a tall structure or a monument. The people of ‘Âd, in their immense wealth and engineering skill, had developed a habit of constructing imposing buildings on hilltops and high places. Hûd's question exposes their motive: they did this "in vanity," for no beneficial purpose, but merely to display their power, to show off their architectural prowess, and to indulge in competitive boasting. The high places were chosen so that their monuments could be seen from afar, a constant advertisement of their greatness.

This is the behaviour of a people who have become intoxicated with their own achievements and have forgotten the source of their blessings. The sin here is not architecture itself but the arrogance and wastefulness behind it. They built not to provide shelter, not to serve a community need, but to stroke their own egos. It was a form of idolatry, where the human ego and its creations were worshipped instead of Allah. For the Quraysh, who also prided themselves on their grand houses and their custodianship of the Kaaba, this critique hit close to home. The believer learns that the material achievements of a civilisation are not signs of divine favour if they are built on a foundation of arrogance and heedlessness.

The question challenges every generation to examine its monuments: are we building for the sake of Allah and the benefit of humanity, or are we building monuments to our own vanity?

=129=

Hûd continues his critique by pointing to their magnificent castles and fortified structures. The word masâni‘a refers to elaborate, well-constructed buildings, often with water cisterns and other advanced features. The people of ‘Âd were master builders, using the abundant resources Allah had given them.

However, Hûd exposes the psychological delusion behind their construction. They built as if they were going to abide in this world eternally, as if death had no power over them. Their architecture was not just a matter of providing comfort; it was a statement of perceived immortality. They behaved as if their mighty fortresses could protect them from the vicissitudes of time and from the decree of Allah.

This is the mentality that prosperity often breeds: a false sense of security and permanence. When a person or a nation feels invincible, they stop thinking about the end, about the grave, about the judgment. They pour all their energy into this world, forgetting the Hereafter entirely. Hûd's words are a sharp reminder that no castle can keep death out. The greatest empires, with the strongest walls, have crumbled to dust. The Quraysh, who were growing wealthy from the trade caravans and building up Mecca, were falling into the same trap. This verse is a timeless warning against the illusion of worldly permanence. It calls the believer to invest in the eternal abode, to build with deeds, not just with bricks, and to remember that the most fortified castle in this world is, in reality, a tent that will soon be folded up.

=130=

Hûd's critique now turns to the violence and tyranny that accompanied the arrogance of ‘Âd. The Arabic word batashtum, translated as "act so viciously," conveys the idea of seizing with extreme force, striking with cruelty, and using overwhelming power without mercy. When the people of ‘Âd attacked others, whether in war, in punishment, or in oppression, they did so with a savage ferocity. Their physical strength, which was legendary, was not tempered by justice or compassion. They were bullies on a civilisational scale, using their might to subjugate and terrorise.

This is the natural fruit of a society that has lost the fear of Allah. Without divine restraint, power becomes a tool of pure oppression. The strong devour the weak, and mercy is seen as a weakness. Hûd's question exposes this moral rot. He links their architectural vanity with their vicious conduct: both stem from the same root of arrogance and heedlessness. A people who build monuments to themselves will inevitably trample over other human beings to satisfy their egos. For the Quraysh, this was a mirror. Their own society was plagued by the oppression of the weak, the exploitation of orphans, and the blood feuds of tribal warfare. The message of the Prophet was a call to replace the law of the jungle with the justice of Allah.

This verse reminds the believer that true strength lies in self-restraint and mercy. A Muslim is strong, but that strength must be governed by taqwâ, using power only in the service of justice and never as a tool of wanton aggression.

=131=

For the third time in Hûd's speech, the central command is repeated. After laying out his credentials, declaring his sincerity, and exposing the specific sins of his people—their vanity in building, their delusion of immortality, and their viciousness—he circles back to the solution: fear Allah and obey me.

The repetition is not redundancy; it is a powerful rhetorical and spiritual device. Each repetition builds on the previous one, adding weight and urgency.

The first call was general; the second was tied to his trustworthiness; now this third call is the conclusion of his diagnosis. He is essentially saying: the only cure for the diseases I have just described—your arrogance, your materialism, your brutality—is to develop taqwâ and to follow the guidance I bring. Without this, all the building and all the power are meaningless and will lead to destruction. The structure of Hûd's speech mirrors the way the Quran itself works: it alternates between warning, argument, and the call to the core of faith. The believer learns that admonishing and advising others is not a one-time statement but a continuous, layered process. The message must be repeated, connected to real-life examples, and always brought back to the foundational need for God-consciousness and prophetic obedience. For the Quraysh, this repeated refrain echoed in their ears, inviting them, again and again, to the same simple, life-changing truth that their ancestors had rejected.

=132=

Hûd now adds a compelling reason for taqwâ, an argument from gratitude. He calls his people to fear the One who has provided them with the specific blessings that they know and recognise.

The phrase "the good things you know" refers to the tangible, undeniable favours that filled their daily lives. Hûd is appealing to their own experience and memory. They did not need to look far to see the generosity of Allah; it was all around them.

This is a classic Quranic argument: the God who gives you these blessings is the One who deserves your worship and your fear. To enjoy Allah's gifts while giving one's devotion to idols is the height of ingratitude.

The verse also plants a seed of reflection. It asks the people to consider: where did these good things come from? Did the idols provide them? Did they create themselves? The only rational answer is that the generous Creator provided them. And if He provided them, He can also take them away. The fear of Allah, then, is not just an abstract duty but a logical response to His kindness. It is the fear of losing the blessings, and more importantly, the fear of meeting the Giver in a state of thanklessness. For the Quraysh, who enjoyed the blessings of the sacred sanctuary, the trade routes, and the abundant provision, this argument was directly applicable. The Prophet Muhammad was saying to them exactly what Hûd said: fear the One who has provided you with all that you enjoy. The believer reads this and is reminded to constantly connect the blessings in their life to the Bestower, letting that connection nourish their fear, love, and gratitude.

=133=

Hûd begins to list the specific blessings, starting with two of the most valued assets in any agrarian and tribal society. Cattle—which included camels, cows, sheep, and goats—were the backbone of wealth. They provided milk, meat, hides, and transport. The people of ‘Âd were particularly known for their large herds. Children, especially sons, were considered a source of strength, honour, and continuation of the family line. By mentioning these two together, Hûd covers both economic and social capital. Allah gave them the wealth of livestock and the wealth of progeny. These were the very things they boasted about and relied upon. Hûd reminds them that these gifts are not the products of their own power or the favour of idols but the direct provision of the Lord of all worlds.

The verse also subtly echoes a theme that runs through the surah: on the Day of Judgment, neither wealth nor children will avail. What is a blessing in this world can become a trial if it leads to arrogance and forgetfulness of the Giver. Hûd is calling his people to enjoy these blessings with gratitude and to use them as reasons to draw closer to Allah, not as reasons to feel self-sufficient.

For the believer today, the verse is a call to see children and material provisions not as personal achievements to be proud of, but as trusts from Allah that should increase our humility and our obedience. The response to a blessing should be taqwâ, not tyranny.

=134=

The list of blessings continues with the agricultural wealth of ‘Âd. Gardens and springs represent a lush, well-watered paradise. The region where ‘Âd lived, though now a desolate desert of sand dunes, was once a fertile land with abundant orchards and flowing water. The gardens provided fruits, shade, and beauty. The springs gave life to the land and the people. Hûd reminds his people of this idyllic environment, a direct result of Allah's mercy. The combination of cattle, children, gardens, and springs paints a picture of a complete and ideal worldly life: wealth, family, food, and water. The people of ‘Âd had everything that a human being could materially desire. And yet, they had turned away from the Provider. Their very prosperity became a veil. They looked at the blessings but forgot the Bestower. Hûd's enumeration of these specific blessings is a form of therapy for the soul. It forces them to connect the dots. Every time they drank from a spring, every time they ate from a garden, they were experiencing the mercy of Allah, and every experience should have been a reminder to worship Him alone.

This is the essence of Quranic gratitude: to see the blessing, to attribute it to Allah, and to let that attribution translate into worship. For the believer, this verse is an invitation to look at the "gardens and springs" in one's own life—the food on the table, the roof over one's head, the health of one's body—and to let those daily mercies become daily motivations for deeper faith and greater obedience.

=135=

After reminding them of the blessings, Hûd expresses his deep concern for what awaits them if they persist in their ingratitude and rejection. He says, "I truly fear for you the torment of a tremendous day.

" The word "truly" emphasises the sincerity and gravity of his fear. This is not a rhetorical threat; it is a genuine, heartfelt anxiety for their wellbeing. Hûd, like all the prophets, was not an enemy of his people but a compassionate warner. His fear for them was a reflection of divine mercy channelled through his prophetic heart. The "tremendous day" refers to the Day of Judgment, or specifically to the day of punishment that would strike them in this world—a destruction by a fierce, screaming wind that lasted for seven nights and eight days, as described in other surahs. That day was tremendous in its scale of devastation and in its significance as a sign. Hûd's warning ties the blessings they enjoy to the punishment they face. The same Lord who provided the cattle and the gardens can send the wind that strips everything away. The blessings are not a guarantee of safety; they are a test. If the test is failed, the punishment is terrible. The lesson for the believer is the importance of maintaining a healthy balance between hope and fear. We should hope in Allah's mercy when we see His blessings, but we should also fear His justice when we recognise our own sins. Hûd's fear for his people is a model for every believer who cares about the fate of others. True love for someone means fearing for their spiritual state and warning them with compassion, not remaining silent out of a desire to be liked.

=136=

The reply of ‘Âd is one of the most chilling expressions of spiritual stubbornness in the Quran. They tell Hûd that his warning has absolutely no effect on them.

The Arabic expression sawâun ‘alaynâ, "it is all the same to us," conveys complete indifference. They are not even angry or argumentative; they are dismissively apathetic.

This is a deeper level of rejection than active opposition. They are saying: your words are so weightless to us that they do not merit any consideration at all. Whether he speaks or remains silent, their course will not change. This attitude is the result of hearts that have been completely sealed by prolonged indulgence in sin and arrogance. They have heard the truth, but it has become like background noise, unable to penetrate their consciousness.

This is a terrifying spiritual state. When a person reaches the point where the warning of a prophet becomes a matter of indifference, they are on the precipice of destruction. The verse is a warning to all who hear the Quran: do not let the word of Allah become something you are numb to. The fact that the Quraysh listened to the Quran and then turned away with a shrug was a sign of a grave spiritual sickness. The believer should strive to keep their heart soft and receptive, so that the reminders of Allah always stir something within—fear, hope, love, or resolve. Indifference to divine warning is the death of the soul long before the death of the body.

=137=

The people of ‘Âd add a second layer to their dismissal. They claim that what Hûd is preaching—worship of one God, a Day of Judgment, moral accountability—is not a divine revelation but just the customs and stories of the earlier generations.

The word "tradition" translates khuluq, which can also mean the inventions or fables of the ancients. They are essentially saying: this is nothing new, nothing special, just recycled ideas from people who lived long ago. This accusation is a classic defence mechanism. By labelling the message as "old tales," they avoid engaging with its truth claims. The same accusation was hurled at the Prophet Muhammad by the Quraysh, who called the Quran "tales of the ancients." It is ironic, because the people of ‘Âd were themselves the predecessors of the Quraysh, and their rejection of the truth as "old tales" is now recorded as a "tale" for the Quraysh to learn from. The verse exposes the circular nature of this excuse. Every generation of disbelievers dismisses the truth as the outdated myths of the past, yet the truth remains constant and unchanging. The believer learns that the antiquity of a message is not a sign of its falsehood but of its divine origin. The truth does not change with fashion; it has been the same since the time of Noah. The dismissal of truth as "tradition" is often a lazy way of avoiding the hard work of sincere reflection and the harder work of changing one's life.

=138=

The arrogance of ‘Âd reaches its zenith with this bold and defiant statement. They do not merely doubt the warning; they flatly deny the possibility of punishment. "We will never be punished" is a declaration of absolute immunity. They believed that their power, their fortified castles, their massive bodies, and their advanced civilisation put them beyond the reach of any divine retribution. They had no concept of a God who could hold them accountable. Their idols certainly could not punish them. They thought that the universe was indifferent to their actions, that might made right, and that there was no higher authority to answer to.

This is the logical endpoint of a materialistic worldview that has lost all sense of the transcendent. When a people believe they are the highest power, they consider themselves invincible.

This statement was not just a prediction; it was a challenge to the heavens. It was an invitation to the very punishment they denied. Hûd had warned them of a tremendous day, and they had laughed it off. The Quran records their boast so that every generation can see the folly of such arrogance. The very next verses will show how quickly their declaration was proven wrong. The lesson for the believer is never to feel secure from the plan of Allah. The moment a person thinks they are beyond divine reach, they have adopted the mindset of ‘Âd. True security lies not in one's own power but in the mercy and protection of the Almighty.

=139=

The verse delivers the swift and just conclusion. They rejected Hûd, persisting in their denial and their arrogant belief that they were beyond punishment. The consequence is stated with stark brevity: "and so We destroyed them.

" The Arabic fa-ahlaknâhum indicates a complete and utter annihilation. The destruction of ‘Âd came in the form of a barren, furious wind that screamed for days, ripping up their bodies, flattening their castles, and burying their gardens under the sand. The mighty civilisation that believed it would never be punished was reduced to a cautionary tale whispered among the desert tribes. The refrain then returns, marking the end of the narrative. "Surely in this is a sign." The story of ‘Âd, their arrogance, their specific sins, their dismissal of the warning, and their catastrophic end, is a clear sign of the law of divine justice. The ruins of their dwellings were visible to the Meccan caravans as they travelled north. The sign was physically present, but the refrain observes, with a tone of sorrow, that most of them would not believe. The Quraysh could see the ruins, they could hear the story recited in their own language, and still the majority of their hearts remained unmoved. The verse is a profound commentary on the limits of external evidence when the inner heart is resistant. It is a call to the individual believer to be among the few, not among the many who see the signs and yet remain blind.

=140=

The story of Hûd and ‘Âd closes with the familiar divine signature. The Lord who destroyed the arrogant builders of castles is the Almighty, whose power is beyond any human fortification. The Lord who sent Hûd with a clear warning, who provided the cattle, children, gardens, and springs, and who preserved the story as a guidance for later generations, is the Most Merciful. The mercy is evident in the warning itself, which was an offer of repentance. It is evident in the fact that the believers among ‘Âd, those few who followed Hûd, were saved from the destruction. It is evident in the lesson that the story provides to all subsequent humanity, inviting them to avoid the same fate. The verse, as always, brings the narrative back to the personal relationship with the Prophet. "Your Lord" is the Lord who stands with the messenger, whether it is Hûd facing the arrogance of ‘Âd or Muhammad facing the arrogance of Mecca. The attributes reassure the Prophet that his Lord is fully in control. The might of the Quraysh, their wealth, their castles, and their large families, are no more permanent or protective than the might of ‘Âd. And the mercy of Allah is his constant companion, guiding him, comforting him, and promising him a victory that will unfold in due time. The believer who reads this verse internalises the balance: fear the Almighty, and hope in the Most Merciful, for in that balance lies the path of the sound heart.
