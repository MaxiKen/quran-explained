=61=

The chase has ended, and the two groups now stand within sight of each other. On one side, the Children of Israel, exhausted from their night-long flight, find themselves hemmed in by the sea. On the other, Pharaoh’s mighty army, fresh and well-equipped, blocks any retreat.

The term “came face to face” captures the terrifying intimacy of the moment: they could see the faces of their pursuers and the spears that glinted in the sunrise. The companions of Moses, meaning the Israelites who had followed him out of Egypt, are overcome by a natural human panic. Their cry, “We are overtaken for sure,” is not an expression of disbelief but an outburst of fear in the face of seemingly inevitable destruction.

The Arabic construction conveys certainty: they saw no way out. They were not merely pessimistic; they were stating what appeared to be an objective fact. The sea before them, the army behind them—by all material calculations, they were doomed. This moment of raw fear is recorded in the Quran not to condemn the Israelites but to highlight the contrast between human vulnerability and prophetic trust. Even the best of communities can feel overwhelmed when trials close in from every side.

The verse also sets the scene for the magnificent response of Moses, who, as a true leader, does not join the chorus of despair. He sees what they cannot see: the unseen help of the Lord. The panic of the companions of Moses is a mirror for every believer who has ever felt trapped by circumstances, whether by financial ruin, illness, or persecution. The apparent dead end is often the very place where Allah’s miraculous intervention is closest. The cry of the Israelites is not the final word; it is the dark backdrop against which the light of divine assurance shines all the more brilliantly.

=62=

Moses responds with a statement of absolute certainty that has echoed through the ages as a model of prophetic faith. The word “Absolutely not!” translates the forceful Arabic kallâ, which completely negates the fear just expressed. Moses does not say, “I hope we will not be overtaken,” or “Perhaps there is a way.” He rejects the very possibility with complete conviction. His basis is not a hidden military tactic or a secret escape route but the living reality of divine accompaniment: “My Lord is certainly with me.

” The Arabic inna ma‘iya rabbî carries the weight of unshakeable assurance. Notice the beautiful intimacy: Moses does not say “with us,” as if he is merely including himself in the collective. He says “with me,” because he is the prophet, the recipient of revelation, and he knows personally that his Lord will not abandon him. Then he adds, “He will guide me.

” The Arabic sa-yahdîn means Allah will show him the way out, the specific path of deliverance. Moses does not yet know how deliverance will come, but he knows that the One who commanded him to leave Egypt will not lead him to destruction. He trusts the Guide. This verse is a profound lesson in the psychology of faith under pressure. While the community may panic, the leader anchored in revelation sees the situation through the lens of divine promises. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, experienced a similar moment in the cave of Thawr when Abu Bakr feared they would be captured, and he said, “Do not grieve; indeed Allah is with us.” Both statements draw from the same well of certainty.

For believers today, this verse is a wellspring of comfort: when all apparent means fail, the presence of the Lord and His guidance remain, and no army, no sea, no obstacle can stand against them.

=63=

The answer to Moses’ declaration of trust comes immediately in the form of divine inspiration. Allah instructs him to strike the sea with his staff, the same staff that had become a serpent and devoured the magicians’ illusions. Moses obeys, and the unimaginable occurs: the sea splits. The water does not merely recede slowly or dry up; it divides into immense walls, each section described as being “like a huge mountain. ” The Arabic term at-tawd al-‘azîm paints a picture of towering, solid masses of water held upright by the command of Allah, forming a dry path through the depths. This was not a gradual natural phenomenon; it was a direct, miraculous intervention that defied all laws of nature. The imagery is deeply symbolic: the very thing that was the source of their terror, the impassable sea, becomes the instrument of their salvation. The staff of Moses, which represented prophethood and divine authority, was the key that unlocked this miracle. This teaches that the means of deliverance often lie in the very things Allah has already placed in our hands, combined with obedience to His command. The splitting of the sea is also a prefiguration of divine judgment, as the waters that stood as walls for the believers would soon collapse upon the pursuers. The vastness of the sea parting into mountain-like columns also reflects the majesty and limitless power of Allah. Nothing is too great for His command. A mere touch of a wooden staff upon the water, done by a prophet acting on revelation, was sufficient to divide the mighty sea.

For the believer facing a seemingly insurmountable problem, this verse is a reminder that the Creator of the sea can part it with a single word, and He can part the seas of difficulty in our lives as well.

=64=

The Arabic phrase azlafnâ thamma al-âkharîn means “We caused the others to draw near there,” meaning Pharaoh and his army were brought to that exact location. This verse reveals the active hand of Allah in positioning the oppressors for their destruction. It was not mere chance or the skill of Egyptian trackers that led them to the parted sea. Allah drew them, motivated them, and allowed them to witness the miracle of the dry path, so that their doom would be sealed by their own arrogant decision to pursue the Israelites into it. The tyranny of Pharaoh had reached its fullness, and the cup of his people’s sins was now overflowing. Allah’s plan was to gather them all in one place, at one moment, to execute a judgment that would become a sign for all generations. This concept of divine “drawing” is often seen in the Quran when disbelievers are led step by step toward their destruction without realising it, a process called istidrâj. They are given temporary success and confidence, which only serves to increase their eventual ruin. The pursuers saw the parted sea and, instead of recognising the mighty hand of God and turning back in repentance, their hearts were so hardened that they plunged in after their prey. The believer learns from this that a respite given to oppressors is not a sign of divine neglect. Allah is gathering them, drawing them toward the appointed moment when their power will be shattered. It is a call to patience and trust, knowing that the Lord who parted the sea is also the Lord who orchestrates the entire sequence of events to deliver His servants and establish His justice.

=65=

The verse shifts from the fate of the pursuers to the salvation of the believers. Allah says He delivered Moses and all those with him completely, leaving no one behind.

The phrase “all together” translates ajma‘în, emphasising that not a single one of the Children of Israel who followed Moses was lost in the sea. Every man, woman, and child who placed their trust in Allah and stepped onto the dry seabed made it safely to the other shore. This was a complete and undivided salvation. It underscores the mercy and faithfulness of Allah toward His servants. When He delivers, He does so thoroughly. The deliverance of the Israelites stands as one of the greatest acts of collective salvation in history, a physical and spiritual liberation that became the founding narrative of a nation. It was a deliverance from slavery, from tyranny, and from the threat of imminent slaughter. The dry path through the sea was the birth canal of a free people.

This verse also highlights the role of Moses as the divinely appointed leader through whom salvation came. The people were saved not because of their own righteousness, as later events in the desert would show their weaknesses, but because they followed the prophet and trusted in his Lord. For the believers of every era, this is a lesson: our ultimate deliverance from the trials of this world and the punishment of the Hereafter lies in following the guidance brought by the messengers. The complete salvation of the Israelites is also a foreshadowing of the Day of Judgment, when the believers will be led to Paradise and not a single one of them will be left behind in the fire. The Lord who split the sea for Moses will open the gates of mercy for the faithful.

=66=

The verse is stunning in its brevity and finality. After the Israelites had safely crossed, the “others”—Pharaoh and his army—were drowned.

The Arabic verb aghraqnâ signifies complete, overwhelming submersion. The waters that had stood as solid walls by the command of Allah collapsed upon the pursuing army, swallowing them entirely. There was no escape, no survivor, no partial victory for the oppressors. The destruction was total. This short sentence delivers a powerful message about the fate of tyranny when it reaches its term. Pharaoh, who had claimed to be the highest lord, who had enslaved a nation and murdered their sons, who had called Moses a madman and chased the believers to the edge of the sea, met his end beneath the very waves that had been turned into a path of deliverance for the righteous. The irony is immense. The sea, a symbol of chaos and danger, became an instrument of divine justice, obeying its Creator by drowning those who had rebelled against Him. The brevity of the verse also conveys a sense of divine disdain. There is no lengthy description of the drowning, no dramatic dwelling on their suffering, because their fate, though terrible, is merely the just recompense of their deeds and not the main focus. The focus is on the lesson. This drowning became a historical sign that the Israelites would recount for generations, and it stands as a warning in the Quran for all who follow the path of Pharaoh. For the Prophet Muhammad and his companions, facing the persecution of the Quraysh, this verse was a promise that the “others” who oppressed them would similarly face a divine reckoning, and the believers would witness the day when the forces of falsehood are swept away.

=67=

The refrain returns, marking the end of the Pharaoh narrative. Allah declares that in this entire episode—the flight, the pursuit, the parting of the sea, the salvation, and the drowning—there is a clear sign. The sign is not just in the miracle of the sea but in the whole pattern of events. The rise and fall of Pharaoh, the victory of the oppressed, the divine response to Moses’ trust, and the catastrophic end of the tyrant are all signs pointing to the reality of Allah’s power, the truth of prophethood, and the certainty of divine justice.

However, the refrain completes itself with the sobering observation: yet most of them would not believe. Here, “them” refers to the people of Mecca, the Quraysh who were hearing this story recited by the Prophet. Despite its clarity, despite its powerful logic and its parallels with their own situation, the majority would still refuse to accept faith. The Quran thus draws a direct line from Pharaoh’s court to the Quraysh leadership. Both were presented with overwhelming signs, and both allowed pride and worldly interests to blind them. This repetition of the refrain after each major story serves to hammer home the theme of human stubbornness. It also consoles the Prophet, reminding him that rejection is not a sign of his deficiency but a recurring historical pattern. The sign is there, luminous and undeniable, but faith is a gift that only enters a heart that is open and humble. The verse is both a promise to the believers that the truth is firmly established and a lament over those who choose to remain blind.

=68=

The twin attributes of power and mercy again seal the narrative. The drowning of Pharaoh’s army is a manifestation of Allah’s might, al-‘Azîz, the Almighty whose will cannot be opposed. The deliverance of Moses and his followers is a manifestation of His mercy, ar-Rahîm, which envelops those who believe and follow His messengers. The combination is essential for a complete understanding of the divine act. The same water that was a mercy to the Israelites was a punishment to the Egyptians. The attributes are not contradictory but harmoniously expressed in a single event.

The verse also directs the Prophet’s attention to the personal nature of his Lord. “Your Lord” is the same Lord who split the sea for Moses, and He will not abandon you. The Almighty, who crushed the greatest tyrant of his age, is fully capable of dealing with the petty tyrants of Mecca. The Most Merciful, who led the Israelites through the depths to freedom, will lead you and your companions to victory and respite.

The repetition of this verse after each prophet story in the surah creates a rhythm of reassurance that is deeply calming to the believing heart. It frames all of history as the outworking of these two divine names. The believer can face the Pharaohs of the world with courage, knowing that his Lord is both powerful enough to destroy the oppressor and merciful enough to save the oppressed. The story of Moses ends here in this surah, but its echoes will continue to reverberate, because the attributes of Allah that made the story possible are eternal and unchanging.

=69=

The surah now transitions from the story of Moses to the story of Abraham, and it does so with a direct command to the Prophet Muhammad. The instruction “Relate to them” indicates that the stories of the prophets are not merely historical records; they are living tools of da‘wah, to be recited to the people as arguments, warnings, and sources of reflection. The story of Abraham was particularly relevant to the Quraysh because they claimed descent from him through Ishmael and prided themselves on following his legacy.

Yet, they had completely distorted his pure monotheism into idol worship. By retelling the story of Abraham, the Quran essentially tells the Quraysh: look at your own father, the one you claim to honour, and see how he broke the idols, challenged his people, and stood alone for the worship of the One true God. You are not his followers; you are the spiritual descendants of those he opposed.

The command also serves to strengthen the Prophet himself. Abraham, like Moses, faced a powerful and hostile society, including his own family. He was alone and vulnerable, yet he stood firm. His story is a comfort and a model.

The Arabic word naba’ means a significant piece of news or a story of great import. It is not a trivial tale but a narrative heavy with lessons. The Prophet is to recite it with the weight it deserves, so that the Meccans might see themselves in its mirror and so that the believers might find in Abraham a pattern of unwavering commitment. The stage is thus set for a new cautionary tale, one that revolves not around a tyrant like Pharaoh but around the intellectual and spiritual struggle against idolatry within one’s own community and family.

=70=

The story begins in the middle of a confrontation. Abraham, in his youth, approaches his father and his people with a simple yet devastating question. He asks them what it is that they worship.

The question is not asked out of ignorance but as a rhetorical challenge meant to provoke reflection. The phrase “besides Allah” is implied in the context, as Abraham is calling them to abandon their idols. By asking “What is that you worship,” he forces them to articulate the nature of their objects of devotion.

The question demands that they look at their idols not as familiar household gods but as things that need to be defined and justified. The inclusion of his father first is significant. Abraham’s father, Azar, was a maker of idols, a man deeply invested in the system of shirk both financially and culturally. Abraham does not exempt his own family from the call to truth. He begins with those closest to him, demonstrating that loyalty to Allah supersedes all tribal and familial bonds. This was a direct lesson for the Meccan Muslims who were estranged from their polytheist parents. Abraham’s manner is respectful yet uncompromising. He addresses them directly, calling them to account for their actions.

The question sets the stage for the dialogue that follows, where the emptiness of idol worship will be progressively exposed. It teaches that the call to monotheism must often start with a simple, clear question that challenges the unexamined assumptions of a society. The believer is encouraged to ask, with wisdom and gentleness, the foundational questions that lead people to rethink what they truly serve and why.

=71=

The answer of Abraham’s people is remarkably candid and reveals the depth of their attachment to their idols. They admit openly that they worship idols, using the Arabic word asnâm, which refers to physical statues carved from wood or stone. They do not attempt to philosophise or to claim that these idols are intermediaries to a higher God, as some later polytheists did. Their worship is direct and undiluted.

The second part of their response is even more revealing: “to which we are fully devoted.” The word ‘âkifîn implies a continuous, devoted attendance. They sit before their idols, they tend to them, they adorn them, they offer sacrifices to them, and they spend their days in their service. This devotion is not a passive cultural habit; it is an active, emotional, and time-consuming commitment. The people are essentially saying, “Our lives revolve around these statues.” This honest, if misguided, devotion exposes the tragedy of shirk. Human beings are created with an innate need to worship and devote themselves to something. When that devotion is misdirected toward lifeless objects, it becomes a bondage that consumes the heart and mind. Abraham’s people were enslaved not by a tyrant like Pharaoh but by their own attachment to stone. Their answer also reveals the power of tradition and community. They all did it together; it was the collective norm. It would take immense courage to stand against such a unified culture. For the Quraysh, who also spent their days in devotion to their idols around the Kaaba, this verse was a mirror held up to their own practices. It asked them implicitly: is your devotion any different from the devotion of Abraham’s people, the very people he was sent to reform?

=72=

Having heard their confession of devotion, Abraham begins a logical deconstruction of idol worship. His first question targets the most basic attribute of any being worthy of worship: the ability to hear. He asks whether these idols can hear them when they supplicate.

The question is devastating in its simplicity. The idols are made of stone and wood; they have no ears, no consciousness, no life. When the people call upon them in times of distress, when they pray for help, for children, for healing, for rain, their prayers fall upon deaf, insensate matter. The idols do not hear a single word. Abraham is drawing their attention to a glaring contradiction. They claim to be devoted to these idols, yet the idols are utterly oblivious to their existence. How can a worshipper have a relationship with a god that cannot hear? This argument is one that resonates with the natural human intellect. The Quran frequently uses it to expose the absurdity of idol worship. The idols cannot hear, they cannot see, they cannot respond. The believer, by contrast, worships the All-Hearing, the Ever-Near, who hears every whisper and answers every sincere call. Abraham’s question is gentle but piercing. He is not insulting the idols; he is simply asking his people to apply the most basic test of divinity. For the Quraysh, this was a challenge to think about their own idols, Hubal, Al-Lat, Al-Uzza, and Manat. Could they hear?

The question left only one answer, and that answer would either lead to the truth or expose the stubbornness of a heart that refuses to see.

=73=

Abraham moves from the passive attribute of hearing to the active attributes that define a true god. He asks whether the idols can bring them any benefit or cause them any harm. These two categories encompass everything that human beings seek from their deities. People worship because they hope for benefit: provision, health, victory, children, success. They also worship out of fear of harm: disease, defeat, poverty, disaster. A true god must have the power to bestow benefit and avert harm. Abraham asks his people to look honestly at their idols and acknowledge what they already know deep in their hearts: these statues can neither benefit nor harm anyone. They cannot give a morsel of food or take it away. They cannot heal a sickness or cause one. They cannot grant a child or prevent death. The idols are utterly powerless. By asking these two questions in sequence—hearing, then benefit and harm—Abraham systematically dismantles any conceivable justification for worship. If a being cannot hear, it cannot respond. If it cannot respond, it is incapable of affecting your life in any way. So what is the basis of your devotion? The argument is irrefutable, and it lays bare the emptiness of idol worship. This line of reasoning is timeless. Modern idols may not be statues of stone, but the test remains the same. Can the things people devote their lives to—wealth, status, other human beings, ideologies—truly hear their deepest cries, and can they bring ultimate benefit or ward off ultimate harm? They cannot. Only the Lord of all worlds holds that power. Abraham’s questions are a universal call to examine what we truly place our trust in and to direct our worship exclusively to the One who is All-Hearing and All-Powerful.

=74=

Cornered by the undeniable logic of Abraham’s questions, his people have no rational defence. They cannot claim the idols hear, benefit, or harm. So they answer with a candid “No,” admitting the impotence of their gods. But immediately, they retreat to the one justification they have left: tradition. They declare that they found their forefathers doing the same, and that is reason enough for them to continue. This response is the classic refuge of those whose beliefs have no foundation in reason or revelation. When evidence fails, they appeal to the authority of ancestors. The argument is not “It is true” but “It is what we have always done. ” This mentality is one of the greatest obstacles to faith. The Quran repeatedly warns against blind adherence to ancestral customs, as it chains the mind and seals the heart against the truth. Abraham’s people were willing to admit their idols were powerless, yet they were not willing to break from the path of their fathers. The comfort of conformity, the fear of social ostracism, and the weight of inherited identity outweighed the clear truth. This verse is a mirror for all societies. How many beliefs, practices, and systems of thought persist solely because “we found our forefathers doing the same”? The Quraysh gave the same answer to the Prophet Muhammad: they worshipped what their fathers worshipped. The response of Abraham’s people teaches that the attachment to tradition can be so strong that people will knowingly embrace falsehood rather than face the discomfort of change. It also highlights the courage required to stand for truth, a courage that Abraham exemplified and that all true believers must cultivate.

=75=

Abraham does not accept their appeal to tradition as the final word. He presses them to reflect more deeply, using the powerful rhetorical device of drawing their attention to the objects of their worship and asking them to truly consider them.

The phrase “Have you really considered” translates a-fa-ra’aytum, which is an invitation to look with insight, to use the faculties of reason that Allah has given them. He is asking them to pause and to examine the idols not as inherited furniture but as candidates for divinity. What are these things that you have been worshipping all your lives, and which your ancestors worshipped before you? He wants them to move from passive acceptance to active contemplation. This method of da‘wah is crucial. It does not heap insults on the idols but simply asks people to think. Abraham is confident that if they really look, they will see the absurdity.

The question also prepares the ground for his own declaration in the following verses, where he will contrast those false gods with the true Lord. The word “worshipping” connects the discussion to the fundamental human act of devotion. It is not a matter of mere intellectual assent; it is about where their love, fear, and hope are directed. Abraham’s approach demonstrates that the battle against falsehood begins with awakening the mind. When a person truly considers what they are serving, the cracks in the foundation of shirk become visible.

For the believer engaged in calling others to truth, this verse offers a model: do not simply condemn; ask questions that lead people to examine their own beliefs critically, with you as a guide pointing them toward the inevitable conclusion.

=76=

Abraham completes his question by including himself and his people across time, linking the present generation with their forefathers. He does not just say “what you worship” but “you and your ancestors,” making them see that the chain of tradition they are so proud of is actually a chain of shared error. The inclusion of the ancestors broadens the scope of the challenge. They had appealed to their forefathers as an authority. Abraham forces them to put those same forefathers under scrutiny. Were the ancestors not human beings like yourselves, capable of error? Just because they did something for generations does not make it true.

This is a bold move, especially in a society that revered its ancestors almost to the point of worship. Abraham is essentially saying that the accumulated generations of devotion do not add an ounce of truth to a falsehood. A lie remains a lie, even if it is a thousand years old. For the Quraysh, this was a deeply subversive idea. Their entire social and religious order was built on ancestral precedent. Abraham, their own claimed forefather, was now being used as an example to show that a true son of noble lineage may need to stand against the corruption that his descendants fell into. The lesson for all believers is that truth is not determined by age, popularity, or lineage. Each generation must reconnect with the original, pure message of monotheism and not blindly inherit the deviations that have crept in over time. Abraham stands as the father of those who break free from the shackles of ancestral conformity to worship Allah alone.

=77=

Abraham now makes a clean and radical break. After forcing them to consider the idols, he declares his absolute dissociation. “They are all enemies to me.

” The word “enemies” is extraordinarily strong. It means that these idols, and by extension the system of false worship they represent, are not neutral objects. They are sources of harm, misguidance, and opposition to the truth. Abraham personifies the idols as active enemies because they stand between his people and the worship of the one true God. His declaration is a statement of loyalty and warfare: he is on the side of Allah, and whatever opposes Allah is his enemy. But he immediately makes a crucial exception: “except the Lord of all worlds.” This exception is the core of his faith. He does not reject all worship; he redirects it to the One who is truly worthy. The Lord of all worlds is not an enemy but the ultimate Friend, the Creator, the Sustainer, the Merciful. This verse encapsulates the essence of Islamic monotheism, which is often summarised in the phrase al-walâ’ wa al-barâ’, allegiance and disavowal. A believer aligns themselves with Allah and His allies and dissociates from false gods and their followers. Abraham’s words were not just a private belief; they were a public proclamation that severed his ties with the prevailing religion of his community. For the Prophet Muhammad and his companions, this was a model to emulate. They too were to declare the idols of the Quraysh as enemies and to cling solely to the Lord of all worlds, even if that meant becoming outcasts in their own city.

The verse teaches that faith cannot be a mixture. One must take a clear stand, and that stand begins with the recognition of who the true Lord is.

=78=

Having declared his allegiance to the Lord of all worlds, Abraham now begins to describe why this Lord alone deserves worship. He starts with the most foundational relationship: creation. The Lord created him. Abraham recognises that his very existence is not an accident, not a gift of idols, not a product of his parents’ power, but a direct act of the Creator. This personal acknowledgment—created me—makes the relationship intimate. The One who brought him into being from nothingness is the One who knows him best and has the sole right to be worshipped. Then Abraham adds, “He alone guides me.” The guidance here encompasses all forms of guidance: the innate natural disposition, the intellectual ability to reason, and the specific prophetic guidance that Abraham received. Just as his body was created by Allah, so his spiritual and moral direction comes from Allah alone. The idols, which could not even guide themselves, were utterly incapable of guiding a human being.

This verse establishes that the Creator is also the Guide, and these two functions cannot be separated. For the Quraysh, who believed in Allah as a Creator but turned to idols for guidance and intercession, this logic was a direct challenge. If you admit that Allah created you, then surely He is the One who should guide you. Why would you seek guidance from that which did not create anything? Abraham’s simple, personal testimony is a masterclass in da‘wah. He does not launch into abstract theological arguments; he speaks from his own direct experience of the divine. He knows his Lord as Creator and Guide, and he invites his people to know Him in the same way.

=79=

Abraham continues to enumerate the blessings of his Lord, moving from creation and guidance to the most basic and continuous necessities of life: food and drink. He says that it is Allah who provides him with what he eats and drinks.

The Arabic verbs yut‘imunî and yasqîn, meaning “He feeds me” and “He gives me drink,” are in the present tense, indicating an ongoing, repeated action. Every meal, every sip of water, is a direct provision from the Lord of all worlds.

This is not a mere acknowledgment of a distant creator who set the world in motion and then left it; it is a recognition of constant, personal care. The idols, which are themselves dependent on human hands for their upkeep, can neither feed nor provide drink to anyone. Abraham’s argument is rooted in the observable reality that his people already experienced. When they were hungry, they turned to the earth that Allah had made fertile; they did not ask the idols to create food from nothing. Abraham is simply connecting the dots they had failed to connect. The provision of food and drink is one of the most intimate ways in which Allah’s mercy touches human life. It sustains us, gives us pleasure, and is a constant reminder of our dependence. By highlighting this, Abraham teaches a profound lesson in gratitude and monotheism. The One who feeds you is the One who deserves your thanks and worship. To eat from Allah’s provision and then bow to a stone is the height of ingratitude.

For believers today, this verse serves as a reminder to see every meal as a sign, to connect the sustenance on the table to the Sustainer, and to ensure that the worship we offer goes only to Him.

=80=

Abraham adds another deeply personal dimension to his testimony: healing. He acknowledges that when he falls ill, it is Allah alone who cures him. The wording is subtle and beautiful. He does not say, “He makes me sick and then heals me,” though Allah is the ultimate cause of all things. Instead, he attributes sickness to the natural human condition and attributes healing directly to his Lord.

This is an exquisite display of adab, of proper etiquette with Allah. Abraham focuses on the mercy and the blessing. Sickness is a state of vulnerability. The strongest human being becomes weak, and no idol, no doctor, no medicine can ultimately guarantee a cure. The healing process, whether through the body’s own systems or through remedies, is entirely dependent on the will and power of the Creator. Abraham’s people likely had their own rituals of healing, perhaps invoking their idols or using magical charms. Abraham shatters these superstitions by declaring that the source of all healing is the Lord of all worlds. This verse is a powerful reminder for believers in every age. When we are ill, our first and primary recourse should be to the One who holds the cure in His hand. We use medicine as a means, but we place our trust not in the medicine but in the Healer. Abraham’s testimony moves from the general (creation) to the specific and personal (guidance, provision, healing), building a complete picture of a life entirely enveloped by the care of Allah. The believer who reflects on these verses learns to see every aspect of their existence as a direct link to their Lord, a reason to love Him, and a proof that He alone is worthy of worship.
