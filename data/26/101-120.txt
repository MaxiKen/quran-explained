=101=

The litany of loss continues. After admitting they have no intercessor, the deviant in the fire add that they also have no close friend.

The Arabic word hamîm denotes a warm, intimate companion, a soulmate whose presence brings comfort. In the world, people rely on their circles of trusted friends, their confidants, their partners in crime and in life. The polytheists of Mecca, like people everywhere, had deep bonds of friendship that gave them a sense of security. They encouraged one another in disbelief, backed each other up in arguments against the Prophet, and found solace in their shared worldview. In the fire, all those bonds evaporate. The Quran elsewhere states that on that Day, friends will be enemies to one another, except the God-fearing. The very relationships that were a source of strength become a source of nothing but absence and isolation.

The word "nor" piles one devastating void upon another: no intercessor, no friend. Every pillar of support that a human being leans on in this life is systematically removed.

This is the ultimate realisation of individual accountability. The soul stands alone before its Creator, stripped of every attachment. The lesson for the believer is to evaluate the basis of friendships. Are our close friendships rooted in mutual love for Allah and shared commitment to the truth? If so, they will benefit us in the Hereafter. If they are based merely on worldly interests, shared sins, or tribal solidarity in falsehood, they will vanish when we need them most. The cry of the damned serves as a warning to prioritise the friendship of the righteous and to be a true friend who sincerely cares about the ultimate fate of those we love, rather than just being a companion in worldly pleasures. It also reminds us that the ultimate Friend is Allah Himself, and a believer should never feel completely alone, because the Most Merciful is closer to them than their jugular vein.

=102=

The culmination of their regret is expressed in a desperate, impossible wish. They yearn for a single opportunity to return to the worldly life, convinced that this time they would be among the believers.

The word "if only" translates the Arabic law, a particle of wistful yearning for something that cannot be. The phrase "a second chance" speaks to the deep human desire for a do-over when the consequences of our choices become undeniable. They are not asking for wealth, comfort, or even a reduction of the present torment; they are asking for a return to the very life they once squandered, so that they might finally use it correctly. Their declaration that they would be believers is, in their minds, absolutely sincere. In the face of the fire, unbelief is no longer a viable option.

Yet, the Quran repeatedly affirms that even if they were sent back, they would return to the very things they were forbidden. Allah knows that their nature, without a fundamental change wrought by sincere faith before death, would revert to its old patterns. Their wish is therefore a tragic impossibility, a thought that only compounds their misery because it can never be fulfilled. This verse is one of the most potent warnings in the Quran against procrastination in matters of faith. The "second chance" must be seized now, in this life, where every day is a new opportunity for repentance. The believer listens to this cry and feels an urgent responsibility to use the breath they currently have, to believe, and to do righteous deeds. It is a reminder not to delay turning to Allah until some vague future, because the moment of death may arrive before that imagined "tomorrow." The wish of the damned is an echo that calls the living to act.

=103=

The refrain returns, punctuating the lengthy and terrifying depiction of Judgment Day and the fate of the deviant. "In this" refers to the entire preceding narrative of Abraham and the eschatological scenes that followed his prayer. The sign is in the story of a single man standing against idolatry, in his logical dismantling of false gods, in his beautiful supplications, and in the stark depiction of the afterlife that confirms the truth of his message. All of this serves as an unmistakable âyah pointing to the reality of monotheism, the necessity of a pure heart, and the certainty of divine judgment.

Yet, despite the clarity of these signs, the refrain observes with a tone of sorrow and inevitability that most people will not believe. The Meccans who heard these verses had the story of Abraham, their own claimed forefather, presented to them as a mirror. They saw in it the absurdity of idol worship and the lofty station of a man who submitted fully to Allah. They also heard the chilling consequences of rejecting that path. But the message did not penetrate hearts that were sealed by pride, economic interest in the pilgrimage trade, and blind loyalty to ancestral tradition. The verse is both a conclusion to the Abraham segment and a transition. It reminds the Prophet and the believers that the pattern is unchanging: the truth is luminously clear, yet the majority will turn away. This refrain tempers the enthusiasm of the caller to faith, grounding him in the reality that his task is to deliver, not to convert en masse. The success of the mission is not measured by numbers but by the clarity of the proof and the sincerity of the effort.

=104=

The Abraham narrative cycle closes with the same divine seal that ended the Moses story. "Your Lord" is the Lord of Abraham, the Lord of Moses, and the Lord of Muhammad. He is al-‘Azîz, the Almighty, whose power was demonstrated in the destruction of the idolaters and in the scenes of Judgment where no wealth or friend can avail. This might is a source of comfort for the Prophet: the seemingly powerful Quraysh are nothing before His force. He is also ar-Rahîm, the Most Merciful, who listened to Abraham's intimate prayers for wisdom, righteousness, forgiveness, and a place in the Garden of Bliss. That mercy is what the Prophet and his followers can hope in.

The pairing of these two names after the terrifying imagery of the fire demonstrates the perfect balance of divine justice and divine grace. The believer is never left with only fear; they are always reminded that the same Lord who will hurl the idols and the wicked into Hell is the One who will bring Paradise near to the God-fearing and forgive the flaws of those who turn to Him in repentance. This verse is a lifeboat of hope after a storm of warnings. It teaches that the correct response to the stories of destruction is not despair but a turning toward the Lord who is both able to punish and ready to forgive. The surah will now move on to the next prophet, Noah, and this refrain will continue to punctuate the narratives, reinforcing the unchanging nature of the divine attributes throughout human history. The believer can rest in the knowledge that the same Almighty and Most Merciful Lord who was with Abraham is with them, provided they walk the path of Abraham.

=105=

The surah shifts to the story of Noah, the first messenger sent to a community that had fallen into widespread idolatry. The verse states that the people of Noah rejected the messengers. The plural "messengers" is used, even though Noah was the only prophet sent to them directly, because rejecting one messenger is tantamount to rejecting all messengers. The message of all the prophets is one and the same: worship Allah alone. To deny Noah was to deny the entire chain of prophethood that his call represented. This phrasing also foreshadows the Quraysh's rejection of Muhammad, which is essentially a rejection of the entire prophetic tradition. The people of Noah were the first great civilisation to deviate into shirk after the flood, according to Islamic tradition. Their sin was not merely private belief but a systemic, societal defiance of the truth. Noah spent centuries calling them, day and night, publicly and privately, with immense patience.

Yet, the more he called, the further they fled. The verse encapsulates their entire history in a single, damning sentence. It serves as a parallel to the Quraysh, who were also rejecting a messenger from among themselves who was calling them back to the pure faith of their ancestors. The story of Noah is a story of ultimate patience meeting ultimate stubbornness. It sets the stage for the tragic end that awaits those who persist in denial, regardless of the length of the respite they are given. The believer is reminded that the rejection faced by the Prophet in Mecca was not new or unique; it was a test that the most patient of prophets had endured before him, and its outcome was always decided by the Lord of all worlds.

=106=

The verse introduces Noah with the tender title "their brother." This signifies that the messenger was not an outsider, a foreigner, or an alien being. He was one of them, born among them, known to them for his truthfulness and integrity long before his prophethood. This brotherhood is a bond of mercy; the messenger is not sent to condemn from a distance but to rescue his own family from destruction. Noah's opening call is exactly the same as that of every messenger in this surah: "Will you not fear Allah?" The call to have taqwâ is the first and most essential step. It is an invitation to develop a shield of God-consciousness that protects from His punishment. Noah's question implies that their current state—their idol worship, their corruption, their oppression of the weak—is a result of a complete lack of this fear. They live as if there is no one watching, no one to whom they will return. The rhetorical question is designed to awaken the dormant fitrah, the innate human sense that there is a Creator who deserves reverence. It is gentle yet piercing. For the Quraysh, hearing this was a reminder that the Prophet Muhammad, their own "brother" from the tribe of Quraysh, was asking them the very same question. The parallel was unmistakable. Noah's call is the timeless call of all prophets, and the negative response he received is a mirror for every community that hears the call and turns away. This verse encapsulates the essence of da‘wah: it begins with a call to the heart, to that foundational awareness of the Divine that makes all subsequent guidance possible.

=107=

Noah, like every prophet in this surah, establishes his personal credibility before his people. He declares that he is a trustworthy messenger.

The Arabic word amîn, translated as trustworthy, was a quality that Noah's people had witnessed in him for centuries before his mission. They knew he was not a liar, not a cheat, and not a man seeking personal gain. By invoking this shared history, Noah dismantles any suspicion that he has suddenly fabricated a message for worldly motives. The trustworthiness of the messenger is the logical foundation of accepting the message. If the conveyor is known to be completely honest, then the only question is whether the message itself is from Allah. This declaration also implicitly challenges his people: you know me; you know my character; can you honestly accuse me of lying? The Quraysh faced the exact same dilemma with Muhammad, whom they themselves had nicknamed al-Amîn long before revelation. They could not credibly accuse him of falsehood, so they resorted to calling him a madman or a bewitched poet, accusations that contradicted their own lifelong experience of his character. Noah's emphasis on his trustworthiness also highlights that prophethood does not negate human character but builds upon it. Allah chooses those who are already recognised for their virtue.

For believers today, this verse is a reminder that the character of the one who calls to Allah must be beyond reproach. Trust is the currency of da‘wah. Without it, even the clearest truth can be dismissed. Noah modelled the integrity that every caller to truth must strive to embody, anchoring his claims not in arrogance but in the known reality of his life.

=108=

Noah follows his declaration of trustworthiness with a double command. "Fear Allah" is the spiritual foundation; "obey me" is the practical implementation. The obedience to the messenger is not an obedience to a man for his own sake, but obedience to the one who sent him. The messenger is the living embodiment and the authoritative expounder of divine guidance.

In the context of Noah's people, obedience to him meant abandoning idolatry, reforming their corrupt practices, and following the path of monotheism that he called them to. The pairing of taqwâ and obedience is central to the Quranic concept of faith. Inner consciousness of Allah must be paired with outward submission to His messenger. One without the other is incomplete. A person cannot claim to fear Allah while refusing to obey the prophet He sent.

This is a direct challenge to the common pre-Islamic and even modern idea that one can have a personal spirituality without adhering to the practical guidance brought by the prophets. The verse also contains a profound leadership lesson. Noah does not say, "fear Allah and obey your own whims about what that means." He points to himself as the divinely appointed guide. Authority in religion is not a matter of democratic consensus or personal interpretation detached from revelation; it flows from Allah through His chosen messengers. For the Meccans, this was the stumbling block: obeying Muhammad meant abandoning the authority of their chiefs and the customs of their forefathers. The call to obey the messenger, when that messenger is one of your own, known to be trustworthy, is the most reasonable demand in the world. To refuse it is to allow pride and habit to override both logic and faith.

=109=

Noah continues to remove any potential barrier between his people and the acceptance of the message. He tells them he asks for no material reward, no payment, no increase in status, no worldly compensation whatsoever for delivering this message.

This is the hallmark of a true prophet. He has nothing to gain from them personally. His motivation is pure: the sincere desire for their salvation and obedience to his Lord.

The phrase "my reward is only from the Lord of all worlds" is a powerful declaration of independence from the creation. Noah is not an employee of the people; he is a servant of the Creator. His provision, his protection, his ultimate recompense, are all in the hands of Allah. This frees him from the pressure of pleasing the crowd or compromising the message to gain popularity or wealth. It also silences any accusation that he is calling to a new religion in order to become a king or to extract wealth from the masses, a common slander against prophets. The Quraysh similarly accused Muhammad of seeking kingship or wealth. By declaring "my reward is only from Allah," the Prophet reassured his people that his message was not a business transaction.

This principle is vital for all who carry the legacy of the prophets, from scholars to ordinary believers who call others to the truth. The reward they seek should be solely from Allah. When the heart is attached to divine reward, it can withstand rejection, poverty, and slander. It also preserves the purity of the message, because the caller has no incentive to soften the truth to please those who might otherwise give him material support. Noah's words are a model of disinterested sincerity.

=110=

The command is repeated for emphasis. In the concise style of the Quran, the repetition of "fear Allah and obey me" serves to drive home the central demand after the messenger has established his credentials and clarified his motives.

The first time, it was introduced alongside his trustworthiness. Now, it follows the declaration that he seeks no reward. The structure is: I am trustworthy, so obey me; I seek no worldly gain, so obey me.

The repetition reinforces the idea that there are no excuses left. Noah's sincerity is beyond doubt; his character is unimpeachable; his call is purely for their benefit. What then remains to prevent them from fearing Allah and obeying His messenger? The rhetorical power of this repetition would have struck the listeners of the Quran. The Quraysh, hearing this, were forced to confront their own refusal to obey the Prophet Muhammad, despite knowing he was truthful and seeking no reward from them. The verse is a masterclass in persuasion, but it is also a divine warning.

The repetition implies a narrowing window of opportunity. Noah called his people with this same message for centuries, and the essential command never changed. It is simple, clear, and non-negotiable. The believer learns that the core of the religion is taqwâ—a living consciousness of Allah—and that this consciousness is not a free-floating spirituality but is anchored in obedience to the prophetic guidance. Without obedience, there is no real taqwâ. The verse is a call to action, not just an intellectual assent. Fear Allah, and let that fear manifest in following the path laid down by the messenger, without hesitation, without bargaining, and without delay.

=111=

Noah's people, unable to challenge his character or his motives, resort to a classic argument of the arrogant against the truth: they attack his followers. They ask how they can possibly believe in Noah when his only adherents are the "lowest of the low." This phrase refers to the poor, the socially marginalised, the slaves, and those without status or influence. In the class-conscious society of Noah's time, as in Meccan society, a person's worth was measured by their lineage, wealth, and social standing. The elites looked with contempt upon the downtrodden who had accepted Noah's message, seeing their poverty as evidence of their lack of intelligence or divine favour. Their argument, in essence, is: if this religion were true, the noble and the wise among us would be the first to embrace it, not these rabble.

This is the logic of privilege. It assumes that truth follows social hierarchy. The same objection was hurled at the Prophet Muhammad, as the Quraysh chiefs pointed to the poor companions like Bilal, Ammar, and Suhayb, asking why they should stoop to be on the same level as such people. The Quran repeatedly exposes this as a profound spiritual sickness. The truth does not bend to the artificial rankings of this world.

In fact, the poor and the humble are often closer to accepting the truth because their hearts are less hardened by the arrogance that comes with wealth and power. Noah's response in the next verse dismantles their snobbery. The verse is a timeless warning to never judge the truth by the social status of its followers. A person's dignity lies in their piety, not in their bank account or lineage.

=112=

Noah's response is wise and measured. He does not defend the social status of his followers by claiming they are secretly noble or that they will soon become powerful. Instead, he deflects the entire premise of the question. He says, in essence, that their private deeds and their inner worth are not something he has full access to. The "what they do" may refer to their occupations, which the elites deemed lowly, or to their inner spiritual state, which Noah cannot judge with absolute certainty. He implies that the judgment of a person's worth is not his province. His task is not to vet people based on their social standing but to deliver the message to all. The deeper meaning is that even if his followers had once been sinners, their outward lowliness does not negate the validity of their faith. Noah places the focus back on the message itself, not on the sociological profile of its adherents. This reply teaches a profound lesson in humility for all callers to truth. We are not the gatekeepers of salvation, and we should never look down on those who respond to the call, no matter how insignificant they appear in the eyes of the world. The Quraysh chiefs wanted the Prophet to expel the poor believers so that they might dignify his gathering. Allah forbade this, telling the Prophet to remain patiently with those who call upon their Lord morning and evening. Noah's answer is an early expression of that principle. Faith is not a country club; it is a hospital for souls, and those who know they are sick are often the first to seek the cure. The believer learns to value people for their faith, not for their worldly résumé.

=113=

Noah continues his rebuttal by firmly placing the ultimate judgment of his followers' worth where it belongs: with Allah alone. He says their account, their inner reality, their final fate—all of that is the concern of his Lord. Noah is not their judge; he is their warner. He refuses to set himself up as an arbiter of human souls, a role reserved for the Creator.

The second part of the verse, "if you had any sense," is a sharp rebuke to the elites. He essentially tells them: if you only used your intellect, you would realise that the truth of a message has nothing to do with the social rank of those who follow it. The true measure is the message itself. By focusing on the followers rather than the content of the call, they are betraying their own lack of reason. This verse is a powerful defence of human dignity in religion. It dismantles all class-based objections to faith. The believer learns that no one is to be barred from the mercy of Allah because of their social status, and no one is to be assumed righteous because of their wealth or nobility. The only scale that matters is the scale of taqwâ, and only the Lord of all worlds can read that scale accurately. This was a direct lesson for the Meccan chiefs, whose "sense" was clouded by their obsession with status. It remains a lesson for all times: when we see a diverse community of believers, rich and poor, literate and illiterate, we should see the beauty of Allah's mercy, not a reason for contempt.

=114=

Noah makes his position unambiguously clear. He will never drive away the believers who have accepted his message, no matter how much the elites demand it as a condition for their own belief. This declaration demonstrates the prophet's solidarity with the weak and his complete loyalty to the community of faith. The believers, regardless of their worldly station, are his brothers and sisters, bound by the unbreakable tie of monotheism. To expel them would be a betrayal of the very message he was sent to deliver, a message that declares all human beings equal before their Creator. The elites were essentially asking Noah to create a two-tier religion: one for the nobles, one for the commoners. Noah refuses outright. This same trial was faced by the Prophet Muhammad when the Quraysh leaders proposed that he set aside a special day for them and another for the poor believers. Revelation came down firmly prohibiting such a division. The refusal to expel the believers is not merely a matter of social policy; it is a matter of faith. Respecting the dignity of every soul that turns to Allah is part and parcel of fearing Allah. This verse is a charter for the inclusive nature of Islamic brotherhood. The mosque should be a place where the CEO and the janitor stand shoulder to shoulder, and no one is made to feel inferior. Noah's courageous stand in the face of a powerful elite teaches the believer that true faith often demands that we stand with the marginalised and reject the terms of the arrogant, trusting that the reward and honor come from Allah, not from the high and mighty of this world.

=115=

Noah concludes his rebuttal by restating his sole mandate. His role is singular and clear: he is a warner.

The word "only" emphasises that he has no other agenda. He is not a social climber seeking to build a political movement of the poor against the rich. He is not a reformer with a worldly economic program. He is a prophet with a clear warning of divine punishment if they do not repent, and a bearer of glad tidings of divine mercy if they do. The "clear warning" is the message of monotheism and the consequences of rejecting it. By defining his role so precisely, Noah removes any ambiguity. The elites cannot accuse him of inciting class warfare or seeking power for the downtrodden. He is simply delivering a divine ultimatum. The ball is now in their court. They have heard the warning, they have no rational objection, and they are left only with their arrogance. This focus on the core mission is a crucial lesson for anyone engaged in da‘wah. It is easy to get sidetracked into secondary battles about social status, politics, or personalities. The prophet keeps his eyes on the prize: the salvation of souls through the delivery of the divine message. The clarity of the warning leaves no room for an excuse on the Day of Judgment. Those who heard Noah and rejected him could never say they were confused about his purpose. Similarly, the Quraysh could not claim they did not understand what Muhammad was calling them to. The warning was clear, and so was their responsibility.

=116=

The elites, their intellectual arguments completely refuted and their social snobbery exposed, now abandon dialogue and resort to raw intimidation. They deliver a direct, brutal ultimatum: stop your preaching, or we will kill you by stoning. Stoning was a particularly cruel form of execution, one that involved the whole community, thereby making everyone complicit and terrorising any sympathisers. This threat marks the point where the struggle moves from the arena of ideas to the arena of life and death. It is the classic response of a tyranny that has lost the argument. When falsehood cannot win with words, it tries to silence the truth with force.

The phrase "O Noah" is laced with a mocking familiarity; they address him as they might a stubborn fool who needs to be brought to his senses by the strongest possible means. Noah faced this threat with a patience that is legendary, continuing his call for centuries. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a direct parallel. He too had been threatened with death, and his companions had been persecuted. The Quraysh had already discussed plans to assassinate him. This verse comforted him with the knowledge that he walked the same path as Noah, and that the Lord who saved Noah from his enemies would also deliver him. The believer learns that threats and persecution are not signs of failure but often signs that the truth is striking at the heart of the system of falsehood. The response to such threats is not to despair or to compromise the message, but to turn to Allah and to continue the mission with trust, as Noah did.

=117=

Faced with an imminent death threat, Noah does not turn to his followers to mount a defence. He does not panic or despair. He turns directly to his Lord in supplication. His prayer is a statement of fact, delivered with a heavy heart but with complete honesty. He tells Allah that his people have truly rejected him.

The word "truly" confirms that he has exhausted every avenue, and their rejection is now categorical and final. This is not an impatient complaint but a report from a servant to his Master. It is the prayer of a prophet who has spent centuries calling his people with love and wisdom, only to be met with deaf ears and now with a death threat. The rejection is not just of Noah personally but of the divine message he carries. This moment marks a turning point. The door of da‘wah, after immense forbearance, is about to close, and the door of divine judgment is about to open.

The verse teaches the believer the proper response to prolonged and entrenched rejection: to turn to Allah, to lay out the reality of the situation, and to seek His judgment. Human effort has reached its limit, and now one must rely entirely on the divine will. Noah's prayer is filled with a quiet sorrow that resonates with anyone who has ever loved someone and watched them persist in a path of self-destruction despite years of sincere counsel. It is a prayer of surrender, not of weakness.

=118=

Noah's prayer becomes specific. He asks Allah to judge between him and his people with a decisive, final judgment.

The Arabic word fataha, translated as "judge decisively," carries the connotation of opening a matter and settling it conclusively. Noah is essentially saying: O my Lord, the case has been presented, the proofs have been delivered, and the defendants have rebelled. Now, it is time for Your verdict to be enacted.

This is a weighty prayer. A prophet only resorts to it when the cup of his people's sins is full to the brim. Noah also includes a plea for deliverance for himself and the believers who are with him. He does not ask for his own salvation alone but remembers the community of faith that has gathered around him. This demonstrates the pastoral heart of the prophet. Even in his moment of seeking judgment against the oppressors, his love for the believers shines through.

The verse teaches that it is permissible to pray for an end to an unjust situation and for Allah to manifest His judgment when all other means have been tried. It is not a prayer of personal vengeance but a prayer for divine justice and the protection of the righteous. The Prophet Muhammad would later face a similar moment, praying to Allah to deal with the Quraysh after years of persecution. This prayer of Noah is a powerful reminder that ultimate justice is in the hands of Allah, and the patient believers who are oppressed can call upon Him to bring about the decisive day that separates truth from falsehood, saving those who believed and dealing with those who denied.

=119=

The divine response to Noah's prayer was swift and comprehensive. Allah says "So We saved him," with the "So" indicating the direct consequence of his supplication. Noah and the believers with him were saved by means of the Ark, a massive vessel that he had built under divine instruction, despite the mockery of his people. The description "fully loaded" refers to the fact that the Ark carried not only the human believers but also a pair of every animal species, as detailed in other surahs. The Ark was packed to capacity with the seeds of a new creation that would repopulate the earth after the flood.

The word "fully loaded" also conveys a sense of the Ark's completeness and sufficiency as an instrument of divine mercy. Everything that was destined to survive was safely on board. The salvation was a physical, tangible event, a dramatic rescue from the cataclysmic flood that drowned the rest. This teaches that Allah's help, when it comes, is thorough and leaves no believer behind. The Ark itself is a symbol of faith in action. Noah built it over many years, enduring the jeers of the disbelievers, trusting in a flood that no one else could imagine. His salvation was not a passive miracle but required long, obedient labour. For the Prophet Muhammad and his companions, who were a small, mocked community, the story of the Ark was a promise that their own "vessel" of faith would carry them to safety, and that the flood of persecution would eventually recede, leaving them as the inheritors of a new era. The believer learns that salvation requires both trust in Allah and diligent effort in carrying out His commands, even when the world laughs.

=120=

The verse provides the stark, unadorned conclusion to the story of Noah's people. After the Ark was secured and the believers were safe, Allah drowned all the rest.

The word "afterwards" establishes the sequence: salvation first, then destruction. The faithful are never mixed with the condemned in the divine punishment. The "rest" refers to all those who remained behind, the mockers, the idolaters, the elite who had threatened Noah, and the masses who had followed them. The flood was a complete and utter annihilation, leaving no survivors.

This is the terrible fulfilment of the judgment Noah had prayed for. The verse is brief, but its weight is immense. It confirms that the threat of the prophets is never empty. The warning that was mocked for centuries became the undeniable, drowning reality. The brevity also underscores the matter-of-fact nature of divine justice. There is no need for elaboration; the destruction of a corrupt civilisation is simply the logical outcome of their persistent rebellion. For the Quraysh, this was a chilling warning. The same Lord who had drowned the people of Noah could drown them, or destroy them by other means, if they continued to reject and persecute His messenger. For the believers, it was a source of comfort and hope. The long night of oppression under the Quraysh would not last forever. The "rest" who now seemed so powerful and numerous would meet their end, while the small community of believers on the "Ark" of faith would be saved. The verse is a timeless reminder that the respite given to oppressors is part of the divine plan, but it is never permanent.
