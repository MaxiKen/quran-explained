=181=

Shu‘aib now delivers the specific economic reforms that are the practical core of his message to the people of Madyan. The first command is to give full measure. This simple instruction strikes at the heart of their corruption. The merchants of Madyan had developed the habit of using two sets of measures: one for buying, where they took in more than their due, and one for selling, where they gave less. The command to give full measure demands complete honesty in all transactions, ensuring that the customer receives exactly what they paid for, without any hidden shortfall.

The second part, “cause no loss to others,” translates the Arabic phrase lâ takûnû min al-mukhsirîn, which means do not be among those who cause loss or diminish the rights of others. This broadens the prohibition beyond just measurement to encompass all forms of economic harm: cheating, fraud, misrepresentation, and exploitation. Shu‘aib is calling for a marketplace built on justice and mutual goodwill, where no one is deceived or unfairly disadvantaged.

This teaching is revolutionary because it roots economic justice not in secular regulation but in the fear of Allah. The believer gives full measure not merely because it is good business practice but because the Lord of all worlds is watching and will hold him accountable. The verse is a timeless charter for business ethics in Islam. It teaches that a Muslim’s honesty must be impeccable, even when no human authority is checking. The Quraysh, whose own trade depended on fair dealings during the sacred months and in the markets of Arabia, were being called to the same standard. The believer today is reminded that every transaction is an act of worship or an act of disobedience, depending on whether the scales are just. Integrity in the small things is the foundation of a sound heart.

=182=

Shu‘aib adds a second, closely related command that emphasises precision and fairness in weighing. The “even balance” translates al-qistâs al-mustaqîm, which refers to a scale that is straight, accurate, and unwaveringly just. If the previous verse dealt with volume measures, this verse deals with weights.

The use of both measure and weight covers the two main methods of quantifying goods in trade. The balance must be even, meaning it is not tilted in favour of the seller through some hidden mechanism or clever manipulation. The command is absolute: the scale itself must be a tool of justice, a physical manifestation of the moral principle of fairness.

This verse reveals the extraordinary attention that Islam pays to the minutiae of daily life. A scale that is slightly off may seem like a small matter, but over thousands of transactions, it represents a mountain of stolen wealth and a multitude of oppressed souls. Shu‘aib’s people were not being accused of grand theft but of systematic, small-scale cheating that had become culturally normalised. The prophet’s call is to reject that normalisation and to hold oneself to a standard of meticulous honesty.

For the believer, the even balance is a metaphor for all of life. In every interaction, one should weigh with justice, giving others their full due of respect, kindness, and fairness. The scale on the Day of Judgment will weigh deeds with absolute precision, and those who tampered with earthly scales will find their own deeds wanting. This verse calls the believer to live a life of such integrity that if one’s own scale were examined by the angels, it would be found perfectly even.

=183=

Shu‘aib’s third instruction broadens the scope of economic justice further. “Do not defraud people of their property” translates the Arabic wa lâ tabkhasû an-nâsa ashyâ’ahum, which literally means “do not diminish people’s things.

” The word bakhas implies a subtle, unjust reduction of something’s true value. It covers not only outright theft but also underpaying for goods, undervaluing someone’s labour, and any form of transaction where one party unfairly loses value. It is a comprehensive prohibition of all forms of economic exploitation.

The verse then adds a second, even broader command: “Nor go about spreading corruption in the land.” This elevates the issue from individual ethics to societal health. Cheating in trade is not a private sin; it is an act of fasâd, of spreading corruption, because it erodes trust, destroys the fabric of mutual respect, and creates an environment where the strong prey on the weak.

The phrase “in the land” indicates that economic corruption has ripple effects that extend far beyond the marketplace, affecting the entire social and moral ecosystem. When cheating becomes rampant, the whole society becomes diseased. Shu‘aib’s diagnosis is that the economic sins of Madyan are not isolated; they are symptoms of a deeper spiritual corruption that has spread throughout the land. For the Quraysh, who prided themselves on their trade but were also guilty of usury, exploitation of orphans, and tribal economic warfare, this verse was a direct indictment. The believer learns that economic justice is not an optional add-on to faith but a core component of it. A Muslim must be a force for fairness, not a contributor to the corruption that tears communities apart.

=184=

Shu‘aib concludes his economic instructions by directing his people’s gaze to the ultimate source of authority: the Creator. He calls them to fear the One who created them and the earlier generations that came before them.

This is a powerful rhetorical move. After laying out the specifics of honest trade, he grounds those commands in the most fundamental truth of all: that they owe their very existence to Allah.

The mention of “earlier peoples” is particularly pointed. The people of Madyan lived in the shadow of the destroyed civilizations of Noah, ‘Âd, and Thamûd. Their ruins were around them, and their stories were known in tribal memory. Shu‘aib is reminding them that the same Lord who created their forefathers and then destroyed them for their sins is the Lord who is watching them now. The fear of Allah is not an abstract concept but is tied to the historical evidence of divine justice that they can see with their own eyes.

The verse also implies that the economic corruption of Madyan is of a piece with the spiritual corruption of the earlier nations, and that the same fate awaits them if they do not reform. For the Quraysh, this was an eerily direct warning. They too were a trading people, they too were surrounded by the ruins of the destroyed nations, and they too were being called by a prophet to fear the One who created them and the earlier peoples. The believer learns to connect the dots between creation, history, and personal conduct. The fear of the Creator should manifest in the fairness of the scale, and the ruins of the past should serve as a sobering reminder of the stakes involved in obedience and disobedience.

=185=

The response of Shu‘aib’s people to his clear, logical, and compassionate call is the same tired accusation that was hurled at every prophet: he must be bewitched. They claim that Shu‘aib is a victim of sorcery, his mind clouded by some spell. This accusation is particularly absurd in Shu‘aib’s case because his message was so eminently rational. What is irrational about giving full measure and weighing with justice? What kind of spell produces such lucid moral instructions? The charge of being bewitched is the last refuge of a corrupt society that has no logical answer to the prophetic critique. By labelling Shu‘aib as insane or under a spell, they could dismiss his words without having to engage with their content. It is a psychological defence mechanism. If he is bewitched, then his call to economic justice is not a reasonable demand but a symptom of his delusion, and they can continue their cheating with a clear conscience.

This verse reveals the deep spiritual sickness of the people of Madyan. They were so attached to their dishonest gains that they were willing to slander the most honest man among them rather than give up a fraction of their illicit profits. For the Quraysh, who made the same accusation against the Prophet Muhammad, this was a mirror. The believer learns that when truth threatens vested interests, the response is often an attack on the bearer of truth rather than a fair consideration of the message. The prophet is called crazy because a sane person in a corrupt society looks out of place. The believer should take courage: if one is called bewitched for standing for justice, one is in the best of company.

=186=

The people of Madyan add a second and third layer to their rejection. First, they state that Shu‘aib is merely a human being like them, implying that a true messenger should be an angel or a being of a superior nature.

This is the same objection raised by the people of Noah, ‘Âd, and Thamûd. The insistence that a prophet must be more than human is a pretext, a way of setting an impossible condition so that they can feel justified in their rejection. Deep down, what they could not accept was that a man from among themselves, whom they had known all their lives, could hold such moral authority over them. Their pride could not stomach submitting to an equal. Second, they directly call him a liar.

This is a significant escalation. They had already called him bewitched, and now they explicitly deny his truthfulness. This accusation flies in the face of Shu‘aib’s lifelong reputation for honesty. Their words are not based on any evidence but on pure arrogance and a desire to discredit him by any means necessary.

The phrase “we think you are indeed a liar” reveals that their judgment is based on conjecture and emotion, not on a rational examination of his claims. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a familiar pain. His own people, who had called him al-Amîn, now called him a liar, a madman, and a sorcerer. The believer learns that when one speaks truth to power, one must be prepared for the assault on one’s character. The accusations are not a reflection of one’s actual worth but a reflection of the corrupt hearts that cannot bear the light.

=187=

The people of Madyan, having exhausted their repertoire of insults, now issue a brazen challenge. They dare Shu‘aib to bring down a punishment from the sky if he is indeed truthful.

The phrase “deadly pieces of the sky” translates kisafan min as-samâ’, referring to fragments or chunks of the heavens collapsing upon them. This is not a sincere request for a sign but a blasphemous mockery, a taunting of the divine power. They speak with the arrogance of those who feel completely secure, believing that no such punishment could possibly occur. Their words are reminiscent of the Quraysh who challenged the Prophet to cause the sky to fall on them or to bring a painful punishment. The challenge is the epitome of spiritual heedlessness. They are essentially daring the Almighty, through His messenger, to destroy them.

This is the final stage of a hardened heart: it moves from ignoring the warning, to mocking the warner, to openly challenging the Lord. Shu‘aib, who had warned them with such tenderness, now hears them asking for the very destruction he has been trying to save them from. The verse is a chilling reminder of the human capacity for self-destructive arrogance. The believer learns never to adopt a posture of challenge toward Allah, never to treat divine warnings lightly, and never to mock the concept of punishment, for the One who created the sky is fully able to cause it to fall, and His forbearance should not be mistaken for impotence.

=188=

Shu‘aib does not take the bait of their mocking challenge. He does not call down a punishment in a fit of anger, nor does he try to defend his honour. Instead, he turns the matter over to Allah with a statement of profound trust. “My Lord knows best whatever you do” is a declaration that places the judgment where it belongs. Shu‘aib has delivered the message; now the response of his people, and the appropriate recompense for their actions, is in the hands of the All-Knowing.

The phrase “knows best” implies that Allah’s knowledge of their deeds is perfect and complete, encompassing not only their outward acts of fraud and mockery but the hidden intentions of their hearts. Shu‘aib is effectively saying: I have done my duty; your account is with Him, and He will deal with you in the way His perfect wisdom and justice dictate. This response is a model of prophetic composure and reliance. The messenger does not need to take personal revenge; the Lord of all worlds is sufficient as a witness and a judge.

The verse also subtly warns the people of Madyan that their challenge has been heard, not by a powerless man, but by the Lord who knows all that they do. The pieces of the sky may not fall at their command, but the punishment will come at the time and in the manner that Allah chooses.

For the believer, this verse is a source of peace. When faced with mockery and hostility, one can entrust the matter to Allah, whose knowledge is perfect and whose justice is certain. The response to insults is not retaliation but serene reliance on the One who sees and knows all.

=189=

The consequences of their rejection unfold with swift finality. They persisted in denying Shu‘aib, so the punishment seized them. The instrument of their destruction was a “deadly cloud,” a dense, dark canopy that appeared in the sky. The people of Madyan, who had been suffering from a prolonged heat wave, initially welcomed the cloud, thinking it would bring shade and rain. But the cloud was not a bearer of mercy; it was a bearer of fire and a raging wind or a scorching heat that annihilated them.

The phrase “day of the deadly cloud” translates yawm adh-dhullah, the day of the shadow, which was in reality the day of the punishment that came under the guise of a shade. This is the supreme irony: they had challenged Shu‘aib to bring pieces of the sky down upon them, and instead, a piece of the sky came upon them in the form of a cloud that destroyed them. The verse comments, “That was really a torment of a tremendous day,” echoing the earlier warnings of Hûd and Sâlih. The punishment of Madyan was not a minor localised event; it was a cataclysm of immense scale, a day of overwhelming terror that wiped out a civilization.

The word “tremendous” underscores the severity of the divine justice. For the Quraysh, this was another historical precedent that they could not afford to ignore. The cloud that they might look to for rain could, by the command of Allah, become the cloud of their own destruction. The believer learns never to feel safe from the plan of Allah and to treat His warnings with the utmost seriousness.

=190=

The familiar refrain returns to punctuate the narrative of Shu‘aib and Madyan. “In this” refers to the whole story: the economic corruption, the clear and reasoned call of Shu‘aib, the accusations of bewitchment and lies, the arrogant challenge to bring down the sky, and the ironic destruction by the deadly cloud. Every element of this history is a sign, a luminous proof of the laws of divine justice. The sign is that economic integrity is a non-negotiable part of faith. The sign is that a prophet’s warning, no matter how eloquent and reasonable, will be met with mockery by those whose hearts are attached to their ill-gotten gains. The sign is that the punishment, when it comes, often arrives in a form that echoes the very nature of the sin or the challenge. They asked for pieces of the sky, and a cloud from the sky became their doom.

Yet the refrain observes, with a tone of sorrow and inevitability, that most of them would not believe. The Meccans hearing this story had the ruins of Madyan as a physical sign, just as they had the ruins of ‘Âd and Thamûd. The stories were recited in their own language, with unmatched eloquence. But the majority still turned away. This refrain is a structural heartbeat that keeps the surah grounded in its central theme: the abundance of signs does not guarantee belief, because belief is a matter of the heart, and most hearts choose the comfort of familiar falsehood over the demanding truth. The believer is called to be among the few who see and respond.

=191=

The story of Shu‘aib closes with the divine signature that has sealed every prophetic narrative in this surah. The Lord who sent the deadly cloud upon Madyan is al-‘Azîz, the Almighty, whose power is beyond any human resistance. The fraudulent scales of the merchants, the mocking words of the deniers, the solid buildings of their city—none of it could stand against the might of Allah when His decree descended. But the verse balances this display of power with ar-Rahîm, the Most Merciful. The mercy is seen in the sending of Shu‘aib himself, the “orator of the prophets,” whose beautiful and clear warnings were a prolonged opportunity for repentance. It is seen in his patience with his people, in his refusal to call down punishment immediately when they mocked him, and in his trust that his Lord knew best. It is seen in the salvation of Shu‘aib and the believers who followed him, who were gathered safely away from the destruction. The mercy is also in the preservation of the story in the Quran, a warning and a guidance for all who will listen.

The pairing of might and mercy is the theological climax of each narrative. It reminds the believer that the Lord is not to be approached with either hopeless fear or presumptuous complacency. He is the Almighty, so fear Him; He is the Most Merciful, so hope in Him. “Your Lord” keeps the message personal and intimate for the Prophet and for every believer who recites these verses. The Lord of Shu‘aib is your Lord, with the same attributes, the same power, and the same mercy. What He did for Shu‘aib, He can do for you.

=192=

The surah now transitions from the accounts of the destroyed nations to a powerful affirmation of the Quran’s divine origin. After recounting story after story of prophets who brought messages from the same Lord, the logical conclusion is drawn: this Book that you are hearing, O people of Mecca, is not a human invention or a tale from the past. It is a revelation, a tanzîl, something sent down directly from the Lord of all worlds.

The word “certainly” leaves no room for doubt. The Quran is not the product of Muhammad’s mind, nor is it a collection of ancient fables, nor is it taught to him by some human informant. It is divine communication, pure and unaltered, from the same Lord who sent Noah, Hûd, Sâlih, Lot, and Shu‘aib. The title “Lord of all worlds” ties this revelation to the universal theme that has echoed through the surah. Every prophet introduced his message by pointing to the Lord of all worlds, and now the Quran itself is identified as coming from that very source. This verse is a direct challenge to the Quraysh’s attempts to explain away the Quran as poetry, sorcery, or folklore. The internal consistency of the prophetic stories, the profound moral teachings, and the inimitable eloquence all testify that this is no human speech. The believer hearing this verse feels a surge of certainty and gratitude. The Book that is recited is not a mere text; it is a living tether to the Creator, a rope of light descending from the heavens to guide humanity out of darkness. The appropriate response to such a Book is not casual reading but deep reverence, careful study, and complete submission.

=193=

The verse specifies the means by which the revelation was delivered. It was brought down by the Rûh al-Amîn, the Trustworthy Spirit, who is the angel Gabriel, peace be upon him. The title “Trustworthy Spirit” is doubly significant. First, “Spirit” indicates that Gabriel is a being of pure light and spirit, not a material creature weighed down by earthly limitations. He is from the highest realms, and his nature is holiness. Second, “Trustworthy” emphasises that Gabriel is absolutely faithful in his delivery of the divine message. He does not add, subtract, or alter a single word. The chain of transmission is thus perfectly secure: from the Lord of all worlds, through the Trustworthy Spirit, to the heart of the Prophet.

This description refutes the Meccan accusation that the Prophet was receiving the Quran from devils or jinn. The devils are treacherous and untrustworthy; they lie, deceive, and steal fragments of heavenly discourse. Gabriel is the opposite: he is the archangel of revelation, entrusted with the most sacred task in the universe. His trustworthiness is a guarantee that the Quran is pristine.

The mention of Gabriel also connects this revelation to the previous scriptures. Gabriel was the angel who brought revelation to Moses, to David, to Jesus, and to all the prophets. The Quraysh who were familiar with Jewish and Christian traditions knew of Gabriel. The verse thus places the Quran squarely within the grand tradition of divine revelation, carried by the same noble messenger who had served all the prophets before. The believer reciting this verse should feel the weight and majesty of the Quran, knowing that it travelled from the Throne of the Most High, carried by the greatest of the angels, to reach the earth.

=194=

The revelation was not brought down to a mountain or deposited as a book on the ground; it was sent directly into the heart of the Prophet Muhammad. The heart is the centre of human consciousness, the seat of understanding, emotion, and will. By placing the Quran in the heart, Allah ensured that the Prophet did not merely learn it as external information but absorbed it into the very core of his being. The revelation became fused with his personality, his thoughts, and his emotions.

This is why the Prophet’s character was the Quran, as Aisha, may Allah be pleased with her, described him. The deep integration of the message into his heart made him the perfect conveyor and exemplar of its teachings. The purpose of this inner revelation is then stated: “so that you may be one of the warners.” The Prophet is a mundhir, a warner, like Noah, Hûd, Sâlih, Lot, and Shu‘aib. The stories that have just been recounted are not just history; they are the very substance of his warning to the Quraysh. The pattern is now complete. A prophet receives revelation in his heart, and that revelation compels him to go to his people with a warning of divine punishment if they do not repent, and glad tidings of mercy if they do. This verse is deeply personal for the Prophet. It is a divine confirmation of his mission and a reminder that the weight in his heart is not a burden of his own making but a sacred trust.

For the believer, it is a reminder that the Quran should not merely be on our tongues or in our bookshelves; it must descend into our hearts, transforming us from within, so that we too may become conveyors of its warning and its mercy, each according to our capacity.

=195=

The Quran was revealed in a clear Arabic language, the mother tongue of the Prophet and of the Quraysh to whom he was first sent. The word “clear” translates mubîn, the same word used to describe the Book itself in verse two. The clarity of the language is a mercy, because it makes the message accessible to its immediate audience without the need for translation. The Quraysh could not claim that they did not understand the Quran; it was recited to them in the purest, most eloquent form of their own language. The clarity of the Arabic also means that the meanings are precise and lucid, not ambiguous or garbled. This verse refutes any notion that the Quran is a foreign, mysterious, or confused text. It is a direct communication, in plain language, meant to be understood and acted upon. The revelation being in Arabic does not diminish its universality. The Lord of all worlds chose Arabic as the vessel for His final message, and then made it incumbent upon all people, Arab and non-Arab, to learn its meanings and follow its guidance.

The Arabic language has a depth and richness that make it uniquely suited to convey the nuances of the divine message. The verse also carries a subtle challenge. The Quraysh prided themselves on their mastery of the Arabic language, their poetry, and their oratory.

Yet the Quran, in their own language, surpassed everything they could produce, leaving them speechless. Its clarity exposed the muddled nature of their idolatrous beliefs.

For the believer, this verse invites a deeper engagement with the Arabic of the Quran, to taste the original beauty and precision of the divine word, and to recognise that every word, every letter, is divinely chosen for a purpose.

=196=

This verse provides a powerful external validation of the Quran’s truth. Allah reveals that the coming of the Quran and the Prophet Muhammad was foretold in the earlier scriptures, the Torah, the Gospel, and the other revealed books. This was not a new, unheralded religion; it was the culmination of a long line of prophecy. The scholars among the Children of Israel and the Christians who were sincere in their study of their scriptures knew of the descriptions of the final prophet. The Quran itself, in other surahs, quotes the unlettered prophet described in their books. This foretelling served multiple purposes. It was a proof for the Meccans who could consult the People of the Book if they doubted. It was a comfort to the Prophet, confirming that his coming was part of a divine plan that spanned millennia. It also challenged the Jews and Christians of Arabia to recognise the truth that they had been awaiting and to follow the final messenger. The verse underscores the unity of revelation. The same Lord of all worlds who sent the Torah and the Gospel has now sent the Quran. The message is essentially one, and the final Book confirms and completes what came before.

For the believer, this creates a sense of connection to the entire prophetic heritage. Islam is not an isolated faith but the universal and eternal truth that all the prophets preached. The foretelling in previous scriptures is a sign that Allah’s knowledge encompasses all time, and His promise to guide humanity has been faithfully kept from the days of Adam to the mission of Muhammad, peace be upon him and upon all the prophets.

=197=

The verse poses a sharp rhetorical question to the Meccan disbelievers. Is it not enough of a sign for them that the learned scholars of the Children of Israel recognise the truth of the Quran? Reports indicate that certain Jewish rabbis in Arabia, like Abdullah ibn Salam, who was a respected scholar of the Torah, recognised the signs of prophethood in Muhammad and embraced Islam. Others, though they did not believe out of pride, admitted privately that the descriptions matched.

The question exposes the stubbornness of the Quraysh. They had no access to the previous scriptures themselves; they were an unlettered people. But they had neighbours, the Jews of Medina and elsewhere, who possessed the ancient knowledge and who were testifying, or at least could be asked, about the foretelling of a prophet in Arabia. The fact that the knowledgeable among the Children of Israel could recognise the Quran was a powerful testimony that should have shaken the Quraysh’s denial.

Yet, they ignored this evidence. The verse implies that their rejection was not due to a lack of proof but to an unwillingness to accept the truth, driven by tribal pride, economic interests, and the fear of losing power.

For the believer, this verse is a reminder of the value of knowledge and the importance of seeking truth from its authentic sources. The recognition by sincere scholars of other faiths is a bridge of understanding and a confirmation of Islam’s truth. It also teaches that the rejection of disbelievers is often a matter of the will, not the intellect. The proof was sufficient, and the scholars had recognised it, but the deniers still turned away. The believer should take comfort that the truth is well-attested and that the objection of the stubborn is not an indicator of any weakness in the evidence.

=198=

The next verses present a hypothetical scenario that further exposes the irrationality of the Meccan rejection. Allah says that even if He had revealed the Quran to a non-Arab, a man from a foreign race and tongue, the Quraysh would still not have believed. This argument dismantles their objection that the Prophet was merely a man from among themselves. They claimed that a true messenger should be an angel or at least a foreign dignitary. Allah’s response is that a change in the messenger’s ethnicity or background would not change their hearts. Their rejection is not based on the messenger’s qualifications but on their own ingrained arrogance. The hypothetical also highlights the wisdom of sending an Arab messenger to an Arab people. A non-Arab messenger would have given them the excuse of a language barrier or cultural unfamiliarity. By sending Muhammad, an Arab of the Quraysh, speaking their own tongue, Allah removed every possible excuse. The verse is a psychological insight into the mind of the disbeliever. The excuses are never the real reason; they are just rationalisations for a heart that has decided to disbelieve. Change the circumstances, and new excuses will be invented.

For the believer, this verse teaches that the acceptance of truth is fundamentally a matter of the heart’s openness to Allah. External factors can be facilitators, but they cannot create faith. It also teaches that one should not exhaust oneself trying to satisfy every objection of the disbelievers, because the objections are often not sincere. The truth has been presented in the clearest possible way; the rest is in the hands of the One who guides hearts.

=199=

The hypothetical scenario continues. Suppose the Quran had been revealed to a non-Arab, but that foreign messenger miraculously recited it in fluent, eloquent Arabic. Even then, the deniers of Mecca would not have believed.

The image is striking: a man from a far-off land, who does not know a word of their language, suddenly standing before them and reciting the most sublime Arabic ever heard. Such a sign would be undeniably supernatural, bypassing the entire issue of the messenger’s local background.

Yet, Allah declares with certainty that they still would not believe. This is a profound statement about the nature of hardened disbelief. Disbelief is not a logical conclusion reached after examining evidence; it is a spiritual state that twists all evidence to fit its preconception. If a foreigner recited the Quran in perfect Arabic, they would attribute it to some other form of sorcery or claim he had been secretly tutored. The human capacity for denying what is obvious, when the heart is corrupted, is almost limitless. This verse is a comfort to the Prophet and to all callers to truth. You can present the most watertight argument, the most dazzling miracle, and there will still be those who reject it. Your success is not measured by universal acceptance but by the clarity of your delivery and your steadfastness in the face of rejection.

The verse also highlights the miracle of the Quran’s language. The fact that an unlettered Arab, Muhammad, recited it was a sign enough, but even a non-Arab reciting it would be a sign that would be dismissed. The believer learns to remain firm in faith and to understand that the guidance of individuals is in Allah’s hands, not in our rhetoric.

=200=

This verse provides a chilling explanation for the phenomenon of persistent disbelief described in the previous verses. Allah states that He allows denial to enter and settle deep into the hearts of the criminals, the mujrimîn.

The Arabic verb salaknâhu, “We cause it to enter,” indicates that the denial is not an external force but a disposition that Allah permits to take root in the heart as a consequence of the person’s own choices. The “wicked” are those who have persistently chosen sin, arrogance, and rejection over guidance. When a person repeatedly turns away from the truth, their heart becomes calloused, and Allah, in accordance with His law of cause and effect, allows the darkness of denial to penetrate deeper until it becomes a settled characteristic.

This is not a random or unjust act by Allah; it is the spiritual equivalent of a sickness that deepens because the patient refused the medicine. The verse explains the otherwise baffling reality of how clear signs, like those recounted throughout the surah, can be rejected by the majority. The denial has become part of their inner makeup. This understanding is crucial for the believer. It instills a fear of the consequences of small, repeated rejections of the truth. Every time one hears a reminder and dismisses it, one is inviting the denial to steep further into the heart. Conversely, every time one accepts and acts upon a truth, the heart becomes more receptive. The verse is also a warning to the Quraysh: their persistent mockery and rejection are not innocent; they are transforming their hearts into vessels of denial, leading them toward the painful punishment that awaits the wicked. The only cure is sincere repentance that turns the heart back to its pure state before the denial becomes permanent.
