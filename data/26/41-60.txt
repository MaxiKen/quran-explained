=41=

The magicians finally arrive, and before they perform, they address Pharaoh with a very practical concern. They ask whether they will receive a substantial reward, an ajr, if they prove victorious over Moses. This question reveals several things about their worldview and the nature of the regime they served. First, the magicians were professionals, not necessarily fanatical devotees of Pharaoh’s religion. Their craft was their livelihood, and they saw this contest as a high-stakes commission. Their request for a reward indicates that they were not initially driven by any ideological commitment to the truth of Pharaoh’s gods but by worldly gain. They were prepared to deploy their skills for the highest bidder. Second, the phrase “if we prevail” shows that even the most confident practitioners of magic harboured no absolute certainty; they entertained the possibility of defeat, even if they considered it remote.

The question also demonstrates the transactional nature of Pharaoh’s court. Loyalty was bought, not inspired.

This detail exposes the hollowness of the tyrannical system. In contrast, Moses and Aaron asked for no reward from Pharaoh; their reward was with the Lord of all worlds. The magicians’ request, therefore, sets up a powerful contrast between those who work for the fleeting riches of this world and those who labour for the eternal reward with Allah. The entire event would soon turn this worldly motivation on its head, as the magicians themselves would abandon all hope of Pharaoh’s reward the moment the truth became clear to them.

The question also served to make Pharaoh’s subsequent promise binding, ensuring that the contest would be conducted with full public accountability. For the believer, this verse serves as a mirror: when we undertake a task, do we seek reward from creation or from the Creator?

=42=

Pharaoh responds with a twofold promise designed to motivate the magicians to give their absolute best. First, he confirms the material reward with a simple “yes,” giving them what they asked for. But he goes further, adding a far more alluring incentive: they will be granted closeness to the throne, becoming members of his inner circle. In the court of an absolute monarch like Pharaoh, proximity to the ruler was the highest worldly aspiration. It meant power, prestige, influence, and security.

The phrase “those closest to me” translates al-muqarrabîn, a term that signifies not just physical nearness but a position of honour and intimacy. Pharaoh was essentially offering them entry into the elite class of Egypt, a complete transformation of their social standing. He understood human psychology well. By coupling material wealth with the promise of status, he thought he had secured their absolute loyalty. No one, in his calculation, would abandon such a glittering prize.

Yet, the irony that the Quran highlights through the unfolding narrative is profound. Within moments of witnessing the truth, these same magicians would throw Pharaoh’s reward back in his face and accept a far greater closeness: nearness to the Lord of all worlds. They would trade the transient honour of a tyrant’s court for the eternal honour of prostration before Allah. Pharaoh’s promise, which he believed would bind them to him, became the very means by which their subsequent faith would shame him publicly. They had come seeking the reward of a king but found the reward of the King of kings, and they realised that the closeness Pharaoh offered was worthless compared to the mercy of the One who created them.

=43=

The time for the contest has arrived. Moses steps forward, completely composed, and gives the magicians the first move. He tells them to cast whatever they intend to throw.

This is not arrogance but the calm confidence of a prophet who knows that the truth he carries cannot be overcome by any illusion, no matter how elaborate. By allowing them to go first, Moses ensures that the full extent of their art is displayed before his sign appears.

This sequence is deliberate. If Moses had cast his staff first and it became a serpent, the magicians might have refused to perform, claiming the contest was unfairly structured or that they were not given a chance. By letting them exhaust all their tricks first, he leaves no room for excuses.

The phrase “whatever you wish to cast” also subtly hints at the boundlessness of Allah’s power versus the limitations of human sorcery. The magicians can bring whatever they want; it will make no difference. This teaches a profound lesson in the methodology of truth. Falsehood is often given the stage first, allowed to parade its attractions and arguments. Only then does the truth manifest, not by imitating falsehood but by exposing its essential hollowness. Moses’ instruction is also an echo of the divine command to him earlier: to cast his staff. Now he invites the magicians to do their casting, but the result will be a miracle that no human hand could produce. The believer should take heart from this: when falsehood seems to dominate the arena, let it display itself fully, and then watch how easily the truth, when it appears, swallows it whole.

=44=

The magicians respond to Moses’ invitation with a theatrical display. They cast down their ropes and staffs, which, through their sorcery, were made to appear as slithering serpents filling the ground. The visual effect must have been overwhelming, as the Quran elsewhere describes that their magic “bewitched the eyes of the people and struck terror into them.” To accompany their performance, they swore an oath by Pharaoh’s might, declaring their inevitable victory. This oath is particularly revealing. They did not swear by God or by any of the traditional Egyptian deities; they swore by the ‘izza of Pharaoh, by his honour and power. This shows the extent to which Pharaoh had successfully centred his own person as the ultimate source of authority and even of sacredness. The magicians, at this moment, are fully invested in the political and ideological system of Egypt. Their confidence is expressed in the emphatic phrase “it is we who will surely prevail,” leaving no room for doubt in their minds or in the minds of the spectators. The entire scene is a powerful portrait of falsehood at its peak: massive, intimidating, and swearing by the power structures of this world.

Yet, the very fact that the Quran records this boast is part of the lesson. How often does worldly power appear invincible, swearing by its armies, its wealth, its technology?

The next verse demonstrates how fragile such boasts are when faced with a single sign from the Lord of the heavens and the earth. The oath by Pharaoh’s might would be answered not by a greater display of might from the same category, but by the devouring truth.

=45=

After the magicians had done their utmost, Moses cast his staff. The Quran describes the result with the particle fa-idhâ, “and behold,” which signals a sudden, astonishing transformation. The staff became a real, living serpent, and it did not merely lie among the magicians’ ropes; it actively and rapidly swallowed them, consuming every single object of their illusion.

The Arabic word for what the staff devoured is mâ ya’fikûn, which means “that which they were faking” or “the falsehood they produced. ” This choice of words is crucial. It identifies the magicians’ display not as reality but as illusion, as a fabrication. A real serpent consuming fake serpents becomes a vivid, tangible parable. Truth devours falsehood. It does not merely compete with it; it annihilates it. The magicians, being experts in their craft, immediately understood that what they had witnessed was not magic. Magic produces illusions, but it cannot physically consume solid objects like the ropes and staffs that had actually been thrown. The serpent that emerged from Moses’ staff was no trick; it was a genuine creation of Allah, a miracle that altered the very essence of the wood. The psychological impact on the magicians was seismic. They had seen the peak of their art effortlessly destroyed. This moment is one of the most dramatic defeats of falsehood in the entire Quran. It teaches that no matter how sophisticated or widespread deception becomes, it is, at its core, hollow. When the truth arrives with divine permission, falsehood vanishes, not gradually, but in an instant. The believer should never fear the towering illusions of the world, for they are all, ultimately, just what the magicians’ ropes were: things waiting to be devoured by the truth.

=46=

The reaction of the magicians is instantaneous and complete. They do not argue, they do not negotiate, they do not look to Pharaoh for permission. They simply fall into prostration.

The Arabic word for prostration, sujjadan, indicates placing the forehead on the ground in the most profound act of submission. This physical movement was not merely a reflex; it was a declaration of faith with their entire bodies. These were men who had spent their lives mastering a craft that relied on the deception of the senses. When they saw a reality that shattered all their knowledge, they recognised its source immediately. They knew the difference between magic and miracle, and they knew that the creator of a real serpent could only be the Lord of all worlds. Their prostration was a direct challenge to Pharaoh, whose divinity demanded prostration to himself. The very act of bowing to anyone other than Pharaoh was an act of high treason. But the truth they had witnessed made the wrath of Pharaoh irrelevant. This moment is one of the most hopeful and instructive in the Quran. It shows that sincere people, even those deeply immersed in falsehood, can be transformed in an instant when they encounter a clear sign from their Lord. The magicians were not prophets, not scholars, not righteous men prior to this; they were practitioners of the occult.

Yet, the clarity of the truth overrode all their previous allegiances. It is a reminder to never write off any human being as beyond guidance. The One who guided the magicians of Pharaoh can guide anyone, and often those who have known falsehood most intimately recognise the truth most sharply when it finally appears.

=47=

While still in prostration, or immediately upon rising from it, the magicians give voice to their new faith. The declaration is precise and echoes the exact message that Moses and Aaron had proclaimed. They say, “We now believe in the Lord of all worlds.

” The word “now” carries immense weight. It marks the sharp boundary between their past and their present, between disbelief and belief, between serving Pharaoh and serving Allah. They do not say they believe in Moses or Aaron as personal leaders, though that is a consequence; they place their faith directly in the Lord of all worlds, the very being whose existence Pharaoh had sarcastically questioned. Their words are a public confession that invalidates the entire religious foundation of Pharaoh’s kingdom. The title “Lord of all worlds” was the signature of Moses’ message, and by adopting it, the magicians signal that they have fully understood and accepted the universality of Allah’s sovereignty.

This is also a lesson in the content of true faith. It begins with recognising the Creator and Sustainer of all existence, not with loyalty to a human leader or a tribal deity. The magicians’ testimony is all the more powerful because they were the very people whom Pharaoh had relied upon to defeat Moses. Their conversion turns the day of presumed victory into a catastrophic defeat for the state’s ideology. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a source of strength. Even the most skilled opponents of the truth can become its most courageous bearers when Allah opens their hearts.

=48=

The magicians now explicitly connect their belief in the Lord of all worlds to the messengership of Moses and Aaron. By saying “the Lord of Moses and Aaron,” they acknowledge that the God they have just submitted to is the same God who sent these two men. This clause does two things. First, it publicly affirms the prophethood of Moses and Aaron, which was the very point Pharaoh was trying to suppress. Second, it clarifies that their faith is not in a vague, abstract deity but in the God who revealed Himself through these specific messengers, with the signs they just witnessed.

The mention of Aaron is significant. Aaron had stood alongside Moses, yet the magicians recognise both as prophets. They do not differentiate or deny the role of the brother. This shows the depth of their understanding, even in the first moments of faith. They understood that the miracle they saw came from the Lord who commissioned both messengers, and that the message Moses delivered, including the demand to free the Children of Israel, was from the same source as the power that transformed the staff.

This verse also serves as a profound lesson for believers: true faith in Allah requires belief in His messengers and in the truth they brought. The magicians could have simply said, “We believe in the God who gave this power,” but they deliberately tied that belief to Moses and Aaron. In doing so, they fully accepted everything that the messengers had said. Their testimony becomes a model for how a person should enter Islam: with a clear acknowledgment of Allah’s lordship and the truth of His messengers, without ambiguity or compromise.

=49=

Pharaoh’s rage erupts in a torrent of threats and accusations. His first exclamation is one of wounded pride: “How dare you believe in him before I give you permission?” This reveals the core of his tyranny. He considered himself the ultimate authority not only over bodies but over souls and beliefs. Faith, for him, was a matter of state permission, not of personal conviction. The very idea that anyone could transfer their allegiance from him to the Lord of all worlds without his consent was the ultimate affront. Immediately, he attempts to explain away their conversion by spinning a conspiracy theory: Moses must be their master who taught them magic, and they had all planned this in advance. This was a desperate attempt to salvage the narrative in front of the gathered crowds. If the people believed that the magicians’ prostration was a genuine recognition of the truth, the entire regime would be undermined. But if Pharaoh could sell the story that it was a pre-arranged plot, he might limit the damage. The accusation is, of course, completely illogical, since the magicians had come from all over Egypt and had never met Moses before that day, and they had initially asked Pharaoh for a reward. After the accusation comes the brutal sentence: crucifixion combined with the amputation of opposite hands and feet, a punishment of extraordinary cruelty designed to maximise pain and humiliation.

The phrase “soon you will see” is the language of a vengeful tyrant. Yet, the magicians’ response in the next verse shows that the threat that would terrify any ordinary person had lost its power over hearts that had just tasted true faith.

=50=

The magicians’ reply is one of the most stunning displays of faith in the entire Quran. They dismiss Pharaoh’s terrifying threat with a single phrase: “That would be no harm.

” The Arabic construction conveys absolute indifference. Crucifixion and mutilation, the worst that Pharaoh could do to their bodies, they considered no real loss at all. The reason is given in the second half: “Surely to our Lord we will all return.” Their perspective had been completely reoriented. A few moments earlier, they were negotiating for worldly reward and closeness to Pharaoh. Now, they see death as merely the doorway to meeting their Lord. They understand that the suffering of this world, no matter how severe, is temporary, while the pleasure of Allah is eternal.

This is the fruit of yaqîn, of certain faith. When a person truly believes in the return to Allah and the reality of the afterlife, the worst punishments of this world shrink to insignificance. The magicians are not being reckless or suicidal; they are expressing the highest level of trust in their Lord. They are also making a theological point: Pharaoh’s threats do not constitute real harm because real harm is what affects one’s standing with Allah. Physical torture cannot touch the soul that is secure in faith. This verse became a timeless source of strength for believers facing persecution. The early Muslims in Mecca, enduring torture and social boycott, could see in the magicians a model of unwavering resolve. When Bilal was dragged on the burning sand, he too declared “Ahad, Ahad”—the One, the One—showing that the Lord to whom we return is worth any price.

=51=

The magicians add a humble confession and a hopeful plea. They express their earnest hope that their Lord will forgive their sins.

This is remarkable, because they are not claiming to be pure or righteous; they acknowledge that they have a past filled with sin. Their previous life as magicians, serving Pharaoh and practicing sorcery, was a life of disobedience.

Yet, the moment they believed, they did not despair of Allah’s mercy. They turned to Him with hope, trust, and the recognition that He is forgiving.

The phrase “as we are the first to believe” likely means they were the first among their people, or the first from Pharaoh’s court and the gathered assembly, to believe in the message of Moses. They were the first to cross the line, setting an example for any others whose hearts might be swayed. This hope for forgiveness and this sense of pioneering faith are deeply instructive. A believer should never feel that their previous sins are too great for Allah to forgive. The magicians had spent their lives in the service of a tyrant who claimed to be God, and yet, with a single prostration of sincere faith, they became recipients of divine mercy.

The verse also illustrates the proper attitude of a new believer: a combination of hope for forgiveness, fear of past misdeeds, and a desire to be among the vanguard of faith. The magicians’ words are recorded in the Quran for all time, honouring their faith and serving as a model for every sinner who turns to Allah in repentance.

=52=

The scene now shifts from the public confrontation to the secret planning of the exodus. Allah inspired Moses to depart with the Children of Israel under the cover of night.

The phrase “My servants” is a term of immense honour and love. The oppressed slaves of Egypt are elevated to the status of being directly attributed to Allah. This signals a complete reversal of their condition. In Pharaoh’s eyes, they were mere chattel; in Allah’s eyes, they are His chosen servants, and He is now their protector and liberator. The instruction to leave at night was a strategic command from the All-Wise, who knew that Pharaoh would not let them go willingly. The night journey provided cover and an initial head start. The warning “for you will surely be pursued” informed Moses that the escape would not be the end of the trial. Pharaoh would not simply let them walk away. The pursuit was certain, but so was the deliverance.

This verse begins a sequence that teaches profound lessons about how divine help arrives. First, the believers must take the practical steps—leave, move, act. Then, the enemy responds with full force. Then, when all seems lost, the ultimate miracle occurs. Moses was not told that the pursuit would be stopped before it began; he was told it would happen, and he was to trust in the plan.

This is the balance between reliance on Allah and taking appropriate means. The night of the exodus was a night of hope and fear, of silent footsteps and whispered prayers, a night that would lead to the dawn of freedom and the destruction of tyranny.

=53=

When Pharaoh discovered that the Israelites had fled, his response was immediate and organised. He dispatched mobilizers, a term that refers to heralds or military recruiters, to all the cities of Egypt. The purpose was to assemble his army and to rally the people for a massive pursuit.

The use of the word “mobilizers” indicates that this was a call to arms, not merely a message delivery. Pharaoh was not going to let a large labour force, a nation of slaves, simply walk out. The economic and political implications of their departure were enormous.

Moreover, Pharaoh’s pride, already deeply wounded by the public defeat at the hands of Moses, would not allow this final insult. His mobilizers fanned out to every city, calling upon able-bodied men to join the chase. The rapid and comprehensive nature of this mobilisation demonstrates the might of the Egyptian state. They had a standing infrastructure for quickly assembling forces.

Yet, this very efficiency was being used by Allah to gather the entire military elite of Egypt into one place for their collective destruction. The verse shows that the earthly power that seems so intimidating is actually being marshalled toward its own doom by the very One it defies. Pharaoh thought he was sending men to recapture slaves; in reality, they were marching toward the sea that would become their grave.

For the believer, this is a reminder that when the forces of falsehood mobilise against the truth, it may appear frightening, but it is often the prelude to a divine intervention that will rewrite the story completely.

=54=

As the army assembles, Pharaoh addresses his forces, and his words are recorded to expose his psychology. He first belittles the Israelites by describing them as “a handful of people,” a small and insignificant group.

The Arabic term shirdhimatun qalîlûn conveys contempt; they are a mere ragtag band, hardly worth the effort of a full military campaign. This belittling served two purposes. First, it boosted the morale of his troops by minimising the threat. Second, it masked the deep unease that must have been gnawing at Pharaoh. After all, this “handful” had just walked out of Egypt after a series of devastating signs, and their leader had bested the magicians. The tyrant must resort to propaganda to convince himself and his followers that the enemy is weak. This psychological tactic is common among oppressors facing a movement of truth: they portray the believers as a marginal, insignificant minority, not worthy of serious concern.

However, in the divine scale, the few are often the majority when they are with Allah. The battle of Badr would later demonstrate that a small band of believers, armed with faith, can overcome a vastly superior force. Pharaoh’s dismissal of the Israelites as a mere handful sets the stage for the ironic twist: this handful will be saved, and the mighty army of the oppressor will be drowned. The believer learns never to judge by numbers, for victory comes from Allah, not from human calculations of strength.

=55=

Pharaoh continues his address, adding another layer of motivation. He admits that this small group of Israelites has caused intense anger among the Egyptians.

The Arabic verb yughîdûnanâ means “they have enraged us” or “they have provoked our fury to the extreme.” This is a candid admission of the psychological impact Moses and his people had on Pharaoh’s regime. Their escape, after all the humiliations Pharaoh had suffered, was a final straw that could not be tolerated. The anger was real, and it was dangerous. A tyrant in a rage is a force of destruction, driven not by reason but by wounded pride. Pharaoh’s words were intended to channel this collective fury into the military campaign ahead. If the army was angry, they would fight with vengeance. But the verse also exposes the spiritual state of the oppressor. Their anger is rooted in arrogance and the refusal to submit to the truth. The believers had done nothing but worship their Lord and demand their freedom, yet this was enough to “enrage” the system. This dynamic repeats itself in every age: the truth, simply by existing and refusing to bow to falsehood, provokes the wrath of those invested in the status quo. The believer should not be surprised or disheartened when peaceful adherence to faith enrages the powerful; it is a sign that the truth is disturbing the foundations of falsehood. Pharaoh’s rage was about to lead him and his army straight into the sea, a lesson that uncontrolled anger against Allah’s servants is a path to self-destruction.

=56=

After admitting his anger, Pharaoh reassures his forces by declaring their collective state of readiness. “We are all on the alert” translates the Arabic innâ la-jamî‘un hâdhirûn, meaning they are a fully prepared and watchful army, leaving nothing to chance.

The word “all” emphasises their numerical strength and unity. Pharaoh is projecting confidence: yes, we are angry, but we are also disciplined and ready.

This statement was meant to counter any lingering fear that the God of Moses might have some trick in store. It was a declaration that human vigilance and military might would be sufficient to crush this insignificant rebellion. The irony, of course, is that their state of alertness could not protect them from the will of the Almighty. They were alert to the movements of a slave caravan, but they were utterly blind to the power of the One who controls the sea. Their watchfulness was limited to the material realm, and it gave them a false sense of security.

This is the perpetual condition of those who rely solely on worldly means. They may have intelligence, surveillance, and overwhelming force, but they cannot perceive the divine plan unfolding right before their eyes.

The verse teaches that while believers are commanded to be vigilant and prepared, ultimate security comes only from Allah. The alertness of Pharaoh’s army was a match for human fugitives but utterly useless against the command of the Lord of the heavens and the earth.

=57=

The narrative now reveals the divine purpose behind the entire sequence. Allah says that He “lured” or “drove out” Pharaoh and his army from their gardens and springs.

The Arabic verb akhrajnâhum, “We caused them to exit,” indicates that their departure from their lands of luxury was not merely their own decision but part of Allah’s plan. They were drawn out of their comfortable estates, their orchards, and their flowing springs—the symbols of their worldly power and prosperity—to pursue the Israelites. This was not a mere military campaign; it was a divine trap. The tyrants, who had oppressed others for so long and luxuriated in their wealth, were now being removed from the very blessings they had taken for granted.

The mention of “gardens and springs” contrasts sharply with the desert route the Israelites were taking. Pharaoh and his chiefs left behind the best of Egypt to chase a group of escaped slaves, believing they would quickly return with captives and spoils. They had no idea they were leaving their homes for the last time. This verse illustrates a profound sunnah of Allah: when He intends to destroy oppressors, He often draws them out of their positions of security and comfort, leading them step by step toward their doom while they remain completely unaware. The believer should reflect that no amount of worldly luxury can protect a person from Allah’s decree, and the very things people cling to can be taken away in a single march toward a sea that will not part for them.

=58=

The list of what they were lured away from continues, piling up the images of their lost opulence. “Treasures” translates kunûz, which refers to stored wealth, gold, silver, and priceless heirlooms accumulated over generations. “Splendid residences” refers to their magnificent palaces and mansions, built with the finest craftsmanship and situated in the most desirable locations. The detail is not incidental. It serves to emphasise the totality of their loss and the completeness of the divine recompense. Everything they had built, hoarded, and prided themselves upon was left behind forever in a single day’s pursuit.

This is a powerful meditation on the fleeting nature of worldly wealth and the deceptive security it provides. Pharaoh’s court had likely felt invincible within those walls, surrounded by treasures that symbolised their power.

Yet, none of it could save them or accompany them to their watery grave. The Quran often juxtaposes the fate of the oppressors with the material blessings they enjoyed, not to condemn wealth itself but to show that wealth without faith and justice is a mirage. The very treasures they had amassed would soon become the inheritance of the people they had enslaved. This teaches that the wealth of the unjust is never truly secure; it is held in trust by the Lord of all worlds, and He can transfer it to the righteous whenever He wills. For the believers who were with the Prophet in Mecca, this was a promise: the Quraysh’s splendid residences and treasures would not protect them from the consequences of their disbelief.

=59=

The verse delivers the final verdict on the material legacy of Egypt’s elite. With the brief phrase “So it was,” the Quran indicates that the matter was decreed and carried out with certainty. The wealth, the gardens, the springs, the treasures, and the splendid residences that the Egyptians left behind were given by Allah as an inheritance to the Children of Israel.

This is the ultimate reversal. The slave nation, which had been forced to build the monuments and toil in the fields, now became the heir to the land. Allah uses the word awrathnâhâ, meaning “We caused them to inherit it,” a term that denotes a permanent transfer of ownership by divine decree. This event is a testament to a recurring promise in the Quran: that the meek and oppressed, who remain steadfast in faith, will eventually inherit the earth. It was not a matter of the Israelites seizing the land by their own power; it was a gift from Allah after they had endured years of suffering with patience.

This verse also serves as a warning to any society that builds its prosperity on the backs of the oppressed. The Egypt of Pharaoh seemed eternal, yet in a single night, its power was shattered and its wealth redistributed. For the early Muslims, who had been driven from their homes in Mecca and whose wealth had been confiscated, this was a comforting assurance. The Lord who gave the gardens of Egypt to the Israelites could certainly give the believers better than what they had lost. The principle is timeless: after hardship comes ease, and after patience comes inheritance, provided one holds fast to faith.

=60=

The exodus narrative reaches its climax as Pharaoh’s army finally catches up with the Israelites. The pursuit, which had been predicted by Allah, culminates at sunrise. The timing is significant. As the sun rose, the Egyptians, with their chariots and weapons glinting in the morning light, spotted the Israelite encampment near the sea. For Pharaoh’s forces, this moment must have seemed like the perfect conclusion to their chase. The early light gave them visibility, and the sea lay ahead of the fugitives, cutting off their escape. The Israelites, for their part, saw the dust cloud of the approaching army and felt the terror of being trapped between the water and the sword. Sunrise, a time normally associated with hope and new beginnings, here became the backdrop for a moment of extreme trial for the believers and of impending judgment for the oppressors. The detail of sunrise also underscores the meticulous precision with which Allah’s plan unfolded. Every detail, down to the hour of the day, was under His control. The army that had sworn by Pharaoh’s might was now marching confidently toward what they thought was a final massacre. They could not see the divine trap that had been set for them. The verse sets the stage for one of the most famous miracles in religious history: the parting of the sea. It teaches the believer that the darkest, most constricted moments—when the enemy is at your back and an impassable obstacle stands before you—are precisely the moments when Allah’s help is nearest. The sunrise that spelled doom in the eyes of the Israelites was actually the dawn of their complete deliverance.
