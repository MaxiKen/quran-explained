=221=

The surah now returns to the theme of the devils, offering a final, powerful contrast between the source of the Quran and the source of the soothsayers’ information. The rhetorical question “Shall I inform you” engages the listener directly, building suspense. The Quraysh had been accusing the Prophet of being inspired by devils. Allah now turns the tables and asks if they want to know the true recipients of satanic descent.

The question implies that they have been looking in the wrong direction. They think the devils descend upon the truthful, trustworthy Prophet, but the reality is completely different. The devils have their own distinct clientele, a group of people who are the polar opposite of the Prophet. This verse introduces a sharp, clear dichotomy: the pure revelation descends upon the pure heart of the messenger through the Trustworthy Spirit; the unclean whispers descend upon a very different kind of person.

The question is also a rebuke. The Quraysh, who should have been able to distinguish between truth and falsehood, were blindly accusing the Prophet of something that, upon a moment’s reflection, was manifestly absurd. The nature of the Prophet’s character and the content of his message were the clearest evidence that he was not in league with the devils. The devils do not call to monotheism, they do not command justice, and they do not warn of Hellfire. The believer learns from this verse the importance of looking at the character of a person and the fruit of their message to discern their source. The devil’s disciples are known by their fruits, and so are the disciples of the Most Merciful.

The question invites us to use our intellect and our spiritual insight to see the truth.

=222=

The verse provides the answer to the question. The devils descend upon every sinful liar.

The Arabic affâk, translated as “sinful liar,” refers to someone who is a habitual, compulsive liar, one whose speech is thoroughly mixed with falsehood. The word carries a connotation of slander and fabrication.

The second characteristic is athîm, “sinful,” meaning someone deeply immersed in sin, whose moral compass is completely broken. The combination of being a persistent liar and a grievous sinner describes the exact opposite of the Prophet Muhammad, whose entire life before and after revelation was defined by the titles al-Amîn, the trustworthy, and as-Sâdiq, the truthful. The devils seek out people who are already steeped in falsehood and sin because they are natural allies. There is a resonance between the evil of the devils and the evil of the human sinner. The devils find a welcoming home in the heart that is darkened by lies and immorality. They whisper their suggestions, and the sinful liar eagerly accepts and transmits them. This was the profile of the soothsayers and fortune-tellers of pre-Islamic Arabia. They were known to be unscrupulous individuals who would mix a grain of stolen truth with a mountain of lies and sell it for profit. The Quraysh, by associating the Prophet with such people, were not only insulting him but were wilfully ignoring the obvious moral chasm between the two. The believer learns a profound lesson about spiritual vulnerability. Lying and persistent sin are not just acts of disobedience; they open the door of the heart to satanic influence. The soul that is habituated to falsehood becomes a receiver for the devil’s broadcasts. Conversely, the soul that is sanctified by truth and righteousness becomes a receiver for divine light.

=223=

The verse continues the description of those upon whom the devils descend. They are people who listen eagerly, “giving an attentive ear,” to the devils’ whispers.

The Arabic yulqûna as-sam‘a, “they cast their hearing,” implies an active, deliberate effort to eavesdrop or to receive information from the unseen world through illicit means. The soothsayers would claim to have jinn who brought them news from the heavens. But the truth of the matter is that what they receive is a mixture of a tiny fragment of overheard truth and a vast amount of deliberate lies. The devils would steal a word from the angels and then add a hundred falsehoods to it. The soothsayer then transmits this mixture, and people are impressed by the one true statement while being deceived by the ninety-nine lies. The verse says that “mostly” they pass on sheer lies.

The Arabic wa aktharuhum kâdhibûn, “and most of them are liars,” indicates that even if there is a grain of truth in what they say, their speech is overwhelmingly composed of falsehood. This is the starkest contrast with the Quran, which is truth through and through, with no falsehood in it from any direction. The Prophet’s recitation is consistent, coherent, and unfailingly true, while the soothsayer’s speech is a jumble of garbled predictions, flattery, and deception. The verse thus provides a clear criterion for distinguishing the divine from the demonic. The believer is warned against paying attention to fortune-tellers, astrologers, and any who claim knowledge of the unseen through channels other than the pure revelation. The source is tainted, and the product is overwhelmingly lies. The only safe source of knowledge about the unseen is the Quran and the authentic Sunnah. All other claimants are, in reality, merely passing on the whispers of the devils, dressed up in the language of mystery.

=224=

The surah now addresses the other major accusation against the Prophet: that he was a poet. The Quraysh called the Quran poetry and Muhammad a poet. This verse refutes that by drawing a sharp line between the Prophet and the poets, and between the Quran and poetry. The verse states, “As for poets, they are followed merely by deviants. ” The Arabic ash-shu‘arâ’u yattabi‘uhum al-ghâwûn, “the poets—the deviant ones follow them.

” The word ghâwûn is the same word used earlier to describe the people of the fire. It means those who are astray, who are lost in error and misguidance. The followers of poets are characterised by their deviation from the truth. This does not mean that all poetry is evil, as the next verse will clarify with the exception of righteous poets. It means that the kind of poets being referred to—the pre-Islamic poets who were the propagandists of idolatry, the satirists who attacked the believers, the panegyrists who praised tyranny, and the romanticisers of vice—are leaders of misguidance, and their followers are the misguided. The Prophet, whose character was the embodiment of guidance, could not possibly be numbered among such people. His words guide to the straight path; their words lead to deviation. This verse is a powerful rebuttal. The Quraysh had a deep cultural reverence for poetry. Poets were the celebrities of the age, the shapers of public opinion. By declaring that the followers of such poets are deviants, the Quran was demolishing a false cultural idol. The believer learns to evaluate art and speech not by its aesthetic appeal alone but by its moral and spiritual direction. Does it guide to Allah, or does it lead away from Him? The beauty of the words is not the criterion; the truth and righteousness of the message are.

The verse also implies that the quality of followers reflects the quality of the leader. The Prophet’s followers were the righteous, the truthful, the God-fearing; the poets’ followers were the heedless and the astray.

=225=

The verse gives a further characteristic of these misguided poets. The Arabic a-lam tara annahum fî kulli wâdin yahîmûn, “Have you not seen that they roam in every valley,” paints a vivid picture. The “valley” is a metaphor for the various subjects and styles of poetry. The poet wanders aimlessly through every field of speech, with no fixed direction or commitment to truth. He might compose a poem praising a patron lavishly one day, and the next day satirise the same person viciously. He might glorify wine and debauchery in one verse, and then speak of honour and courage in another, not out of conviction but out of whatever the occasion demands.

The word yahîmûn comes from hayamân, which means to wander lost in love, to be crazed, to roam without restraint. The poet is driven by his emotions and his imagination, not by principle or divine guidance. He rants, he exaggerates, he fabricates. This stands in total contrast to the Quran, which has a single, unwavering message, a consistent moral framework, and a sublime purpose. The Quran does not rant; it speaks with measured, divine wisdom. The poets’ wandering in every valley indicates a lack of inner compass, a soul tossed about by whims and social currents. The Prophet, by contrast, was steadfast, his character and message unchanging from the first revelation to the last. The verse is not a blanket condemnation of the poetic art but a critique of a particular type of poet whose speech is untethered from truth and morality. The believer learns that one’s speech should be anchored in truth and sincerity, not in the shifting winds of popular opinion or personal gain. The tongue is a gift that should be used to traverse the straight path, not to wander aimlessly through the valleys of falsehood.

=226=

The verse delivers the final blow to the credibility of the poets who were being compared to the Prophet. The Arabic wa annahum yaqûlûna mâ lâ yaf‘alûn, “and that they say what they do not do.

” This is the ultimate indictment. The poets are hypocrites in their art. They praise bravery but are cowards in battle. They extol generosity but are miserly with their own wealth. They speak of honour but engage in treachery. Their words are grand, but their actions are small and contradictory.

This is a direct contrast to the Prophet Muhammad, whose entire life was a perfect harmony between what he said and what he did. He commanded prayer, and he spent the night in prayer. He commanded charity, and he gave until his family had nothing left. He taught patience, and he endured persecution with unmatched forbearance. His wives described his character as the living Quran. The disconnection between word and deed is a hallmark of the ungodly poet and, indeed, of all insincere preachers. The Quran, in Surah As-Saff, rebukes the believers with the same question: “O you who believe, why do you say what you do not do? It is most hateful to Allah that you say what you do not do. ” The verse strikes at the heart of all pretension. It calls for integrity, for a life where the tongue and the limbs are in complete alignment. The poets who were the enemies of Islam were masters of beautiful words that meant nothing in practice. The Prophet was the master of truthful words that were perfectly embodied in a living example. The believer takes this verse as a constant check on their own sincerity. Before speaking, one must ask: do I do this? Am I living this truth, or am I merely reciting it? The greatest da‘wah is a life that matches the tongue.

=227=

The surah ends with a crucial exception that prevents the previous verses from being a blanket condemnation of poetry. The “except” introduces the righteous poets, those who combine their artistic gift with sincere faith and good deeds.

The first quality is belief: they have iman. This is the foundation.

The second is doing good deeds: their faith manifests in righteous action. The third is remembering Allah often: their hearts and tongues are moist with the dhikr of Allah, and their poetry reflects this remembrance, serving a spiritual purpose rather than being a vehicle for vanity. The fourth quality is specific to their context: they use their poetry to avenge the believers after being wrongfully slandered. This refers to the Muslim poets like Hassan ibn Thabit, Abdullah ibn Rawahah, and Ka‘b ibn Malik, who used their talents to defend the Prophet and the Muslim community against the vicious satirical attacks of the Quraysh poets. They responded to slander with truth, to lies with eloquence, and they upheld the honour of the faith. Their poetry was not a frivolous wandering but a weapon in the struggle for truth, wielded with justice.

The surah then concludes with a final, ominous warning: “The wrongdoers will come to know what evil end they will meet.” The wrongdoers are those who slandered the believers, who accused the Prophet, and who rejected the warning. They will soon discover, whether in this world or the next, the terrible destination that awaits them. This ending ties the entire surah together. The stories of the destroyed nations, the arguments for the Quran’s divine origin, the refutation of false accusations, and the command to the Prophet all lead to this point. The wrongdoers will know, but by then it will be too late. For the believers, the surah offers a path of pure monotheism, trust in the Almighty and Most Merciful, and a community of sincerity and remembrance.

The final note is one of divine justice, leaving the listener with a sense of awe and a renewed determination to be among the righteous, not the wrongdoers.
