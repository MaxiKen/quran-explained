=141=

The surah now turns to the story of Thamûd, the third of the great Arabian civilizations destroyed after their rejection of divine guidance. As with the previous nations, the verse states that they rejected the messengers, using the plural, even though only one prophet, Sâlih, was sent directly to them. This affirms the essential unity of the prophetic message and the fact that rejecting one prophet is tantamount to rejecting the entire chain of messengers stretching from Noah onward. Thamûd were the successors of ‘Âd, inheriting the land and building a new civilization. They inhabited the region of Al-Hijr, in the northwestern part of Arabia, where they carved magnificent homes out of the rock faces of mountains. They were blessed with gardens, springs, and an advanced material culture. But like ‘Âd before them, prosperity led to arrogance, idolatry, and social corruption.

The Quran presents the ruins of Thamûd as visible signs for the Meccan caravans that passed by their dwellings on the route to Syria. The people of Thamûd were known for their intelligence and skill, yet they used these gifts to beautify their worldly life while denying the Hereafter. Their rejection of the messengers encapsulates their spiritual state: they saw the truth, understood the call of Sâlih, but chose to turn away. The verse serves as a stark warning to the Quraysh, who were following the exact pattern. The ruins of Thamûd were a familiar sight, a silent testimony to the fact that a civilization that rejects its warner, no matter how advanced, is destined for annihilation. The lesson for every generation is that intellectual and material achievements, divorced from faith, become the very instruments of a people’s downfall. The rejection of the messengers is not merely a historical event; it is a spiritual posture that repeats itself whenever a community places its own desires above the revealed truth.

=142=

Sâlih is introduced with the affectionate title “their brother,” a term that underscores his deep connection to Thamûd. He was not a foreign conqueror or a distant angel but one of their own, born among them, known to them for his wisdom and integrity. The brotherhood of the prophet is a profound Quranic theme; it signifies that divine mercy sends a guide from within the community, someone who shares their language, their culture, and their lived experience, so that the message is not alien but familiar. Sâlih’s opening call is identical to that of Noah, Hûd, and every other messenger in this surah: “Will you not fear Allah? ” This question is the doorway to all spiritual reform. It seeks to awaken the dormant conscience, to ignite that innate sense of the Creator’s majesty and the soul’s accountability. The people of Thamûd, like all corrupt societies, had lost this fear. They feared poverty, they feared tribal enemies, they feared the loss of their carved palaces, but they did not fear the One who gave them life. Sâlih’s question is both a diagnosis and a cure. It gently exposes the root cause of their transgressions—the absence of taqwâ—and simultaneously offers the remedy by calling them to recover it. For the Quraysh, hearing these words from their own “brother” Muhammad, the call was identical.

The repetition of this exact phrasing across the surah reinforces the timeless and unchanging nature of the prophetic mission. The believer learns that the first step in calling others to the truth is not a detailed critique of their behaviour but a simple, profound invitation to reconnect with the fear of Allah, which alone can motivate genuine change.

=143=

Following the prophetic pattern established throughout the surah, Sâlih immediately grounds his call in his personal credibility. He declares himself to be a trustworthy messenger.

The word amîn carries a weight of meaning that his people understood perfectly. Sâlih had lived among them for many years before his prophethood, and his character was an open book. They knew him to be truthful in speech, faithful in trust, and sound in judgment. He had never lied to them, never cheated anyone, and never sought personal advantage. By invoking this shared history, Sâlih removes any basis for an ad hominem attack. The people cannot credibly accuse him of being a fraud, a sorcerer, or a madman seeking power, because his entire life testifies against such accusations.

This is a crucial element of prophetic da‘wah. The messenger’s moral authority is the platform from which the message is delivered. Without trustworthiness, even the clearest truth can be rejected on the grounds of suspicion about the messenger’s motives. Sâlih’s declaration is also an implicit challenge: if you acknowledge that I have always been truthful in worldly matters, then why do you doubt me when I speak on behalf of the Lord of all worlds? The only honest response is to give the message serious consideration. For the Prophet Muhammad, who was also known as al-Amîn, this verse was a direct parallel and a divine vindication. The Quraysh could not deny his trustworthiness, and their rejection of his message was thus exposed as a rejection rooted not in reason but in pride. The believer learns that the foundation of all successful da‘wah is personal integrity; one must live a life that lends weight to one’s words.

=144=

Sâlih delivers the twin command that forms the heart of every prophetic mission. “Fear Allah” is the inner transformation, the cultivation of a constant awareness that one is standing before the Creator, which breeds both love and awe. “Obey me” is the outer manifestation of that inner state, the practical submission to the guidance that the messenger brings. The two are inseparable, and their order is significant. True obedience to the messenger can only flow from a heart that is filled with the fear of Allah. Obedience without taqwâ becomes hollow ritual or begrudging compliance; taqwâ without obedience becomes a vague spirituality that lacks concrete form. Sâlih is calling his people to a complete reorientation of their lives. They are to abandon the worship of idols, to reform their corrupt social practices, and to follow the divine law that he conveys.

This command is simple, clear, and non-negotiable. It cuts through the complexity of their intellectual objections and cultural attachments.

The repetition of this identical command across the surah—by Noah, by Hûd, by Sâlih, and later by Lot and Shu‘aib—emphasises that the core of the religion does not change with time or place. The Quraysh were being called to the same thing: fear Allah, and obey His messenger.

The verse teaches the believer that the spiritual life is not a menu from which one can pick and choose. It is a unified package of inner God-consciousness and outer submission to the prophetic model. The refusal of Thamûd to obey Sâlih, despite his clear trustworthiness, stands as a warning against the arrogance that disconnects belief from action.

=145=

Sâlih, like every sincere prophet, declares his complete independence from any worldly compensation. He asks for no payment, no salary, no increase in status, no share of their gardens or palaces. This announcement is a powerful refutation of the typical slander that prophets are motivated by greed or a desire for kingship. Sâlih has nothing to gain from them personally; his sole motivation is the sincere desire for their salvation and his unwavering obedience to his Lord.

The phrase “my reward is only from the Lord of all worlds” is a declaration of complete reliance on Allah. It frees the messenger from the pressure to please the crowd, to soften the message, or to favour the rich and powerful in hopes of donations. His provision, his protection, and his ultimate recompense are all in the hands of the One who sent him.

This is the hallmark of a true caller to truth. When the heart is attached solely to the reward of Allah, it cannot be bought or intimidated by the creation.

This verse also highlights the stark contrast between the messenger and the corrupt elites who were motivated entirely by worldly gains. The people of Thamûd were obsessed with their orchards, their carved homes, and their material prosperity. Sâlih stands before them empty-handed, asking for nothing but their own good, pointing them to the Lord who will reward them far beyond anything they can imagine.

For the believer engaged in any form of da‘wah, this is the model: to serve without expecting anything from people, to look only to Allah for recompense, and to let that sincerity be a proof of the message’s truth.

=146=

Sâlih now moves from establishing his own integrity to challenging the core assumption of his people’s lifestyle. They lived as if their present state of prosperity and safety was permanent and guaranteed. The rhetorical question “Do you think you will be forever left secure” is designed to shatter this illusion.

The Arabic phrasing implies a sense of being left alone, unbothered, with no accountability and no end. The people of Thamûd behaved as if they were immune to death, to disaster, and to divine judgment. They had settled into their carved mountain homes, their gardens, and their springs, with a sense of absolute permanence. Sâlih’s question is a sharp wake-up call. It asks them to consider the possibility that this security is not self-generated but a temporary grant from the Lord, and that it can be revoked at any moment.

The word “forever” is the key. No worldly state is permanent. Civilizations rise and fall, health turns to sickness, and life inevitably ends. The illusion of permanent security is one of the most dangerous deceptions that prosperity brings. It causes people to delay repentance, to neglect the Hereafter, and to become arrogant. Sâlih is echoing the Quranic principle that Allah grants respite to test whether people will be grateful or arrogant. The Quraysh, enjoying the security of the sacred sanctuary and the prosperity of the trade routes, were in the exact same spiritual condition. This verse is a timeless call to every human being who feels comfortably settled in their worldly life: do not be deceived. The blessings you have are not a guarantee of eternal safety; they are a test that demands gratitude and accountability.

=147=

Sâlih continues his question by specifying the blessings that had lulled his people into their false sense of security. The gardens of Thamûd were lush orchards filled with fruit trees, and their springs provided abundant fresh water. These two elements together represent a paradise on earth. Water in the Arabian Peninsula is life itself, and gardens are the pinnacle of settled civilization. The people of Thamûd had transformed their arid valley into a blooming oasis, and they attributed this success to their own skill and to their idols, forgetting the true Provider. Sâlih’s mention of gardens and springs is an echo of the blessings described for ‘Âd, and it will recur with the later prophets in the surah.

The repetition creates a pattern: Allah gives abundant material blessings, the people become intoxicated with them, they forget the Giver, and they are then destroyed. The gardens and springs are not evil in themselves; they are signs of Allah’s mercy. The evil lies in the human heart’s response to them—taking them for granted, using them as a justification for arrogance, and treating them as ends in themselves rather than means to know and worship the Creator.

For the believer, the material comforts of life should trigger an increase in gratitude, humility, and fear of losing the One who provided them, not a sense of invulnerability. Sâlih’s words invite his people, and all readers of the Quran, to look at the springs that water their gardens and ask: who caused this water to flow?

The answer, if honest, leads directly to the worship of the Lord of all worlds.

=148=

The catalogue of Thamûd’s blessings continues, painting an even richer picture of their material prosperity. Crops of various kinds—grains, vegetables, and fodder—indicate a thriving agricultural economy. The date palm, the queen of desert trees, is singled out for special mention, with the description that their fruit is “tender,” meaning perfectly ripe, soft, and easy to eat.

The Arabic word hadîm conveys the idea of fruit that is at its peak of ripeness and quality. This detail is not merely poetic; it underscores the extraordinary generosity of Allah. He did not give them just any palm trees but palm trees whose fruit was of the highest quality, a delight to the senses. The blessing was comprehensive and exquisite. Sâlih is reminding them that every mouthful of that tender date was a direct gift from the Lord, a tangible experience of divine mercy. The tragedy of Thamûd was that they consumed these blessings daily without letting the pleasure of the taste lead them to the pleasure of the Giver. They remained stuck at the level of physical enjoyment, their hearts hardened to the spiritual reality behind the fruit.

The verse also subtly contrasts the transient tenderness of the date with the harshness of the punishment that would soon descend. The soft, sweet fruit that they took for granted would be replaced by the deafening cry that annihilated them.

For the believer, this verse teaches the art of seeing Allah’s artistry in the details of creation. The perfection of a ripe fruit is a sign, and the correct response to such a sign is to praise the One who crafted it and to obey His commands, for the same hand that gives the tender fruit can also send the storm.

=149=

Sâlih now mentions the most famous hallmark of Thamûd’s civilization: their remarkable skill in carving elaborate homes and monumental structures directly out of the solid rock of the mountains. The phrase “with great skill” translates fârihîn, which also carries the connotation of being exceedingly happy, proud, and exuberant in their craftsmanship. They were masters of stonework, and their dwellings were not mere shelters but works of art, testaments to human ingenuity and power. The ruins of these rock-hewn structures can still be seen in Madâ’in Sâlih in northwestern Saudi Arabia, standing as silent witnesses to the Quranic account. Sâlih does not condemn the skill itself; it was a gift from Allah. What he questions is the attitude that accompanied it. They carved their homes with a sense of arrogant self-sufficiency, as if they, by their own hands, had created eternal security. The mountain homes represented their perceived invincibility. They believed that living inside the very rock made them immune to any force. But Sâlih’s question implies that no human construction, no matter how skilful, can protect against the decree of Allah. The mountain that they carved for safety would become the very place where they perished.

This is a powerful meditation on the limits of technology and architecture. A civilization can reach the pinnacle of engineering, but if it loses its connection to the Creator, all its strength is an illusion. For the Quraysh, who took pride in their building and their custodianship of the Kaaba, this was a direct warning. The believer learns to appreciate human skill and artistry as a gift from Allah, while never placing ultimate trust in physical structures, for the only true fortress is the protection of the Almighty.

=150=

After this detailed exposition of their material blessings and the delusion of security they bred, Sâlih returns to the central command. The repetition of “Fear Allah, and obey me” now carries the accumulated weight of all the preceding arguments. He is essentially saying: look at the gardens, the springs, the crops, the palm trees, the mountain homes. All of these are from Allah. The only rational and grateful response to such overwhelming generosity is to fear the One who provided it and to obey the messenger He sent to guide you.

The placement of this command after the enumeration of blessings is a masterful rhetorical structure. It ties the call to obedience directly to the experience of gratitude. Every blessing they enjoy becomes a reason to submit. Their refusal to obey becomes, by extension, a monstrous act of ingratitude.

The verse also serves as a structural marker within Sâlih’s speech, gathering the threads of his argument before he delivers the specific warning against following the transgressors. The Quraysh, hearing this, were being told that their own blessings—the sacred house, the trade, the safety—were not reasons for pride but reasons for fear and obedience. The believer learns that the remembrance of blessings should never lead to complacency but should always drive one back to the core duties of taqwâ and obedience. The more Allah gives, the more reason we have to bow. Sâlih’s repetition is not redundant; it is a deepening, an insistence that the path is clear and the only question is whether the heart will yield.

=151=

Sâlih adds a specific negative command that complements the positive call to obey him. He warns his people not to follow the orders of the musrifîn, the transgressors, those who exceed all bounds in sin and corruption. This reveals the social dynamic at play in Thamûd. The society was not a collection of isolated individuals; it was led by an elite class of arrogant chiefs who set the tone of idolatry, oppression, and heedlessness. These transgressors were the ones who rejected Sâlih and pressured the masses to follow suit. Sâlih’s warning is a direct challenge to their authority. He is telling the ordinary people that obeying these leaders is a path to destruction, and that their ultimate allegiance must be to Allah and His messenger, not to the corrupt influencers of their society.

The term musrifîn is particularly poignant; it denotes those who are wasteful and excessive, who have gone beyond all limits in their indulgence and tyranny. They are the very opposite of the balanced, God-conscious life that Sâlih calls to. The command is timeless. In every age, there are powerful voices—political, cultural, economic—that call people away from the path of the prophets. The believer must learn to discern these voices and have the courage to refuse them, even when that refusal comes at a social or material cost. For the early Muslims in Mecca, the transgressors were the chiefs of Quraysh who demanded that they abandon Muhammad and return to the religion of their forefathers. Sâlih’s warning was their warning: do not obey those who are heading toward ruin, no matter how powerful they seem.

=152=

Sâlih further characterizes the transgressors by describing their actions. They spread corruption, fasâd, throughout the land. This corruption is comprehensive: it includes the spiritual corruption of idolatry, the moral corruption of vice and oppression, the economic corruption of fraud and exploitation, and the environmental corruption of arrogance that views the earth as a mere playground. They are agents of decay, turning what should be a land of peace and justice into a land of chaos and suffering.

The second part of the description, “never setting things right,” indicates that their entire orientation is toward destruction, not reform. They do not engage in islâh, in the work of improving, reconciling, and healing. Their influence is purely negative. Any society dominated by such people is on a fast track to collapse. Sâlih’s words draw a sharp line between the mission of the prophet, which is to set things right and establish justice, and the work of the transgressors, which is to tear things down. This verse provides a clear criterion for evaluating leaders and movements. Do they spread corruption or do they work for genuine reform? Do they increase injustice and moral decay, or do they call to the fear of Allah and the establishment of His law?

For the believer, the command not to follow the corruptors is a call to be an agent of islâh in one’s own sphere. It is not enough to avoid personal sin; one must actively resist the spread of corruption and contribute to the betterment of society, following the prophetic model. Sâlih’s warning is a diagnosis of the disease, and his call to obey him is the prescription for the cure.

=153=

The response of Thamûd to Sâlih’s clear, reasoned, and compassionate call is a textbook example of how hardened hearts deflect the truth. Instead of engaging with his arguments about their blessings, their false sense of security, or the corruption of their leaders, they attack his person. They accuse him of being “bewitched,” a victim of magic or sorcery that has clouded his mind. This accusation is deeply ironic. It is the very same tactic that Pharaoh used against Moses, and it was used against Noah and Hûd before. The charge of being bewitched is a convenient way to dismiss a message without having to think about it. If Sâlih is under a spell, then his words are not his own, and there is no need to take them seriously. It also subtly implies that he is a victim, someone to be pitied or feared, rather than a prophet to be obeyed. This accusation reveals the arrogance of the elite. They could not deny Sâlih’s personal integrity or the logic of his arguments, so they had to invent a reason to discredit him. The Quraysh hurled the exact same accusation at the Prophet Muhammad, calling him a bewitched poet. The Quran records these repetitions to show the comforting pattern that prophets throughout history faced identical slanders. The believer learns that when the truth is powerful, the opposition often shifts from argument to character assassination. The response should not be to despair but to recognise the tactic for what it is—a sign of intellectual bankruptcy—and to continue delivering the message with patience and clarity, trusting that Allah sees all.

=154=

Thamûd add a second layer to their rejection. They acknowledge that Sâlih is merely a human being, not an angel, and they use this as an argument against his prophethood.

This is the same objection raised by nearly every disbelieving nation: how can a man who eats, drinks, and walks in the markets be a messenger from God? They could not comprehend that divine mercy would choose a human from among them, rather than sending an angelic being. Their materialistic worldview could not accept that the vehicle of revelation could be an ordinary human body. Then, they issue a challenge: “bring forth a sign if what you say is true.” This demand appears, on the surface, to be a reasonable request for proof.

However, in the context of their hardened hearts, it was not a sincere inquiry but a stalling tactic and a form of mockery. They had already rejected Sâlih’s moral and spiritual proofs, and they assumed that any physical sign he brought would be impossible. They were like Pharaoh’s court, demanding a spectacle while already determined not to believe. Sâlih, by Allah’s permission, will answer their challenge with a sign that is perfectly suited to their psychology and their sins. The she-camel that emerges from the rock is not just any miracle; it is a test tied directly to their pride in their material resources and their oppression of the weak.

The verse teaches the believer to be careful when asking for signs from Allah. The request can come from a sincere seeking heart, or it can come from a place of arrogant challenge. The latter often leads to a sign that becomes a final ultimatum, after which disbelief leads directly to destruction.

=155=

In response to their challenge, Sâlih, by the power and permission of Allah, produces the requested sign. From the mountain rock, a massive, unique she-camel emerges, a living miracle. Sâlih presents her with the simple statement, “Here is a camel.” He does not boast or elaborate on the miraculous nature of her creation; the camel herself is the proof. He immediately establishes a law for the community regarding this camel: she will have a designated day to drink from the water sources, and the people of Thamûd will have their own designated day. The water of the valley was a shared resource, and the camel, as a sign from Allah, was to be given her fair share. This arrangement was a direct test of their sense of justice, their gratitude, and their obedience. The camel was not merely a spectacle; she was a living trust placed in their midst, a test of their willingness to coexist with a clear sign of Allah’s power. The sharing of water on alternate days was a reasonable and just arrangement. It did not deprive them completely; it merely required them to show consideration for the miracle that had been granted to them. This test targeted their greed, their arrogance, and their tendency to oppress. They had been exploiting resources for themselves alone, and now they were asked to share with a creature that represented the divine presence among them. The appointed day also meant that any harm done to her on their day or hers would be a direct violation of the sacred pact. The sign was clear, and the rules were simple. The stage was set for the final act of the drama of Thamûd.

=156=

Sâlih issues a dire warning alongside the gift of the she-camel. The instruction is absolute: “do not ever touch her with harm.

” The word “ever” implies that any harm, at any time, no matter how small, will trigger the promised destruction. The prohibition is not just against killing her but against any act of aggression, harassment, or denial of her rights. The consequence of disobedience is spelled out with terrifying clarity: they will be seized by the torment of a tremendous day.

The phrase echoes the warning of Hûd to ‘Âd, linking the two nations in their shared fate. The “tremendous day” refers to the day of the punishment that would annihilate them, a day of an overwhelming cry and an earthquake. The clarity of the warning leaves no room for ambiguity. It is a mercy, in a sense, because it tells them exactly what the red line is and exactly what the penalty will be. The test is thus perfectly fair. The people of Thamûd have been given a sign they asked for, a clear set of rules, and a direct warning. Their response will be purely a matter of their free will.

This is the divine pattern: when a people demand a sign and it is given, the consequences of rejecting that sign are swift and absolute, because the level of proof has reached its maximum. For the believer, this verse instills a profound respect for the signs of Allah. Whether it is the she-camel of Sâlih, the sanctity of the Prophet’s mosque, or the sacred months, the limits set by Allah are not to be trifled with. To violate them knowingly is to invite a punishment from which there is no escape.

=157=

Despite the clear warning, the people of Thamûd, led by their most wretched members, proceeded to hamstring and kill the she-camel. The Quran elsewhere specifies that the one who actually committed the act was the most miserable among them, but the whole community is held responsible because they consented, participated, or failed to stop it.

The phrase “becoming regretful” captures their emotional state after the deed was done. The regret, however, was not the regret of sincere repentance. It was the regret of fear, the sinking realisation that they had crossed the red line and that the promised punishment was now inevitable. Their regret did not undo the act, nor did it lead them to turn back to Allah in submission. It was a worldly, desperate regret, like that of someone who destroys their own future and then laments when it is too late.

This detail is psychologically profound. Sins, especially great ones, often produce a temporary high of defiance, followed by a crash of remorse. But remorse alone, without the turning of the heart back to Allah, is worthless. The people of Thamûd regretted because they sensed the impending doom, not because they truly repented. Their regret became a part of their punishment, a bitter taste in the moments before the storm.

For the believer, this verse is a warning to treat the limits of Allah with utmost seriousness. Do not wait until after the sin to feel regret; let the fear of regret prevent the sin in the first place. And if one does fall, the regret must be channeled immediately into tawbah, into a genuine return to Allah, before the window of opportunity closes.

=158=

The consequence arrives with concise finality. After they killed the she-camel, the punishment—a devastating shriek, an earthquake, and a thunderous blast from the sky—seized them.

The Arabic verb akhadhathum, “overtook them,” conveys the sense of being suddenly seized and enveloped by something inescapable. They were in their carved homes, the very structures they thought made them invincible, when the punishment reduced them to lifeless bodies kneeling in their dwellings. The refrain returns, marking the end of the Thamûd narrative. “Surely in this is a sign.” The story of the she-camel, the clear warning, the arrogant violation, and the catastrophic punishment is all a luminous sign of the laws of divine justice. The ruins of their mountain homes stood as a physical sign that the Meccans could see.

Yet, the refrain observes, most of them would not believe. The human pattern repeats: the sign is clear, the ruins are visible, the story is told in eloquent Arabic, and still the majority turns away. This refrain, now familiar, becomes a drumbeat of warning and sorrow throughout the surah. It serves to console the Prophet and to challenge the listener. The believer who hears this story is called to be among the few who do believe, who take the lesson to heart, and who live in the constant awareness that the Lord who destroyed Thamûd is the same Lord who watches over all deeds, and that His mercy and His might are both near.

=159=

The Thamûd narrative closes with the divine signature that has sealed every story in this surah. The Lord of Sâlih, who is also the Lord of Muhammad, is al-‘Azîz, the Almighty. The might that shattered the arrogant rock-carvers, that turned their skilled craftsmanship into a tomb, is a might that no human strength can oppose. This attribute is a source of immense comfort for the Prophet and the believers who face the persecution of powerful elites. The Quraysh, with their wealth and their stone houses, are no match for the Almighty who levelled the mountain homes of Thamûd. But the verse does not end with might. It immediately pairs it with ar-Rahîm, the Most Merciful. The mercy is evident in Sâlih’s patient call, in the provision of the she-camel as a sign, in the clear warning that was a chance for repentance, and in the salvation of Sâlih and the believers from the destruction. The mercy is also in the fact that the story is preserved in the Quran as a guidance and a healing for those who come after.

The pairing of might and mercy teaches the believer to never lose hope, because the same Lord who can destroy is the Lord who forgives, who loves to forgive, and who rewards the smallest act of faith with immense gardens. The personal address “your Lord” keeps the relationship intimate and direct. The Prophet is not dealing with a distant, impersonal force but with a Lord who is his protector, his guide, and his friend. The lesson for every believer is to hold these two divine names in constant balance, fearing the might and yearning for the mercy, walking the straight path between them.

=160=

The surah now transitions to the story of Lot, the nephew of Abraham, who was sent to the cities of the plain near the Dead Sea. As with all the previous narratives, the verse declares that the people of Lot rejected the messengers, using the plural even though Lot was the single prophet living among them. This establishes the consistent principle that to reject one messenger is to stand in opposition to the entire chain of prophethood, for the message is one. Lot’s people, the inhabitants of Sodom and Gomorrah, were guilty of a sin that had never been committed by any nation before them: widespread, open, and defiant homosexuality, coupled with highway robbery, corruption, and the rejection of divine guidance. Their rejection was particularly brazen because they had the example of Abraham, their neighbouring prophet and relative, and they had Lot living among them, a man known for his righteousness. The verse encapsulates their spiritual state as a rejection, setting the stage for Lot’s call and the horrific punishment that would soon engulf them. For the Quraysh, this was another chapter in the book of destroyed nations, a warning that the moral corruption that eats away at a society’s foundations, when coupled with the denial of the messengers, leads to a ruin that turns the land itself upside down. The story of Lot is particularly significant as it addresses moral depravity in a direct manner, showing that the prophetic mission is not only about theology but also about the sanctity of the natural order and the family. The believer learns that silence in the face of widespread moral corruption is not an option when one is in a position to warn, and that the fate of societies that normalise what Allah has forbidden is a catastrophe that serves as a sign for all the worlds.
