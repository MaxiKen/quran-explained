=81=

Abraham’s testimony now reaches the ultimate realities of human existence: death and resurrection. He declares that it is Allah alone who will cause him to die. This simple statement contains profound layers of meaning. Death is not an accident, not a victory for any so-called god of the underworld, and not the end of existence. It is a transition that occurs by the precise decree of the Creator. By acknowledging that Allah causes him to die, Abraham places his very lifespan entirely in divine hands. He does not fear death as an extinction but as a meeting with the One who gave him life.

The second part of the verse, “and then bring me back to life,” asserts the certainty of resurrection. Abraham, like all the prophets, was a preacher of the afterlife. This belief is the bedrock of moral accountability. Without it, the injustices of this world would remain unresolved. Abraham’s people, steeped in idolatry, either denied the resurrection or had a vague, corrupted notion of it. He confronts them with the clear truth: the Lord who created you once can recreate you.

The sequence is deliberate: death comes first, then new life. This is the pattern that the Quran repeatedly emphasises. Just as dry land is revived by rain, so will the dead be raised. Abraham’s inclusion of this in his personal testimony makes the abstract doctrine intensely personal. He is not speaking of a distant event; he is speaking of his own death and his own resurrection. The idols, which his people worshipped, would eventually crumble to dust, incapable of causing death or granting life. They were themselves dead matter. Abraham’s statement is thus a confident proclamation of where true power lies and an invitation for his people to prepare for the day when they too will be raised and questioned.

=82=

Abraham completes his testimony with an expression of humble hope. He does not claim to be sinless. Instead, he looks forward to Judgment Day and expresses a deep, yearning hope that his Lord will forgive his faults.

The Arabic word khatî’atî, translated as “my flaws,” refers to his slips, his human imperfections. Prophets are protected from major sins and from persisting in error, but as human beings, they may still commit minor lapses for which they seek Allah’s forgiveness. Abraham’s hope is not a presumptuous guarantee; it is balanced by a reverent fear of his Lord and an acute awareness of His mercy. This combination of hope and fear is the hallmark of true faith.

The verse also firmly places the locus of forgiveness with Allah alone. The idols his people worshipped could neither harm nor benefit, let alone forgive sins on a Day of terrible reckoning.

The concept of Judgment Day is central: it is the day when every soul will stand before the Lord of all worlds and give an account. Abraham, despite his lofty station as the intimate friend of Allah, approaches that day with deep humility. This serves as a profound lesson for every believer. No matter how many good deeds one performs, one enters Paradise only through the mercy and forgiveness of Allah.

The verse also connects back to the surah’s theme: the stories of destroyed nations show what happens when forgiveness is spurned, but here Abraham models the opposite posture, one of hopeful repentance. For the Quraysh, who claimed to follow the faith of Abraham but had no concept of personal accountability before Allah, this verse was a sharp reminder that their forefather’s religion was built on hope in divine mercy, not on the worship of stone idols.

=83=

The scene now transitions from Abraham’s dialogue with his people to his intimate supplication before his Lord. This prayer is a beautiful sequence of requests that reveal the priorities of a prophet’s heart. He begins by asking for wisdom.

The Arabic word hukm encompasses far more than intellectual knowledge. It means sound judgment, prophetic authority, the ability to discern truth from falsehood, and the capacity to rule justly between people. Abraham, who had already displayed immense wisdom in his debate with his people, still asks for more. This teaches that wisdom is a gift from Allah, not a human acquisition, and even the greatest of men are in constant need of divine increase. A believer should never feel self-sufficient in knowledge but should perpetually turn to Allah to open the doors of understanding.

The second part of the prayer, “and join me with the righteous,” is particularly moving. Abraham does not ask merely to be righteous in isolation; he asks to be united with the community of the righteous, the sâlihîn. This reflects a deep longing for blessed companionship, for a place among those who have purified their souls and drawn near to Allah. It is a prayer for both personal virtue and for inclusion in the caravan of the pious that stretches across time.

For the believer, this teaches that we should not only seek our own salvation but also long for the company of the good, in this life and the next. The righteous are those whose deeds are in accordance with divine guidance, and being among them is one of the greatest joys of Paradise. This prayer is a direct refutation of the individualistic, tribal loyalties of Abraham’s people; the only bond that ultimately matters is the bond of righteousness.

=84=

Abraham’s prayer turns to the legacy he will leave after his death. He asks Allah to grant him a truthful and honoured mention among the later generations, those who will come after him.

The Arabic lisâna sidqin fi al-âkhirîn literally means “a tongue of truth among the later ones,” meaning that people far into the future will speak of him with reverence, love, and truthfulness. This is not a request for worldly fame or empty celebrity but for a lasting spiritual legacy that guides others toward the truth. Abraham wanted his life and his message to continue bearing fruit long after he had returned to his Lord. This prayer was magnificently answered. Abraham is revered by billions across the Abrahamic faiths. In Islam, his name is invoked in the daily prayers of every Muslim, as they send blessings upon the Prophet Muhammad and upon Abraham in the tashahhud. The Hajj pilgrimage, one of the pillars of Islam, is a reenactment of his life and the life of his family. The Quran is filled with his stories, and he is described as an ummah, a nation in himself. This verse is a profound lesson for those who work for the cause of truth. One should not only be concerned with immediate results but also with planting seeds that will flourish in generations to come. The most lasting legacy is not wealth or monuments but a life of sincere devotion that inspires others to follow the path of monotheism. Abraham’s prayer teaches us to aim high in our supplications, to ask Allah for things that benefit humanity long after we are gone, and to understand that true honour comes from Allah alone.

=85=

After asking for wisdom, righteousness, and a lasting legacy, Abraham turns his gaze to the ultimate destination: the Garden of Bliss. He prays to be among its inheritors.

The Arabic phrase warathati jannati an-na‘îm means “the inheritors of the Garden of Bliss.” The concept of inheritance implies a permanent possession, a rightful transfer of ownership. Paradise is not a temporary visit but an eternal home that the righteous will inherit by Allah’s mercy.

The word “Bliss” translates na‘îm, which signifies perfect, unending delight, a pleasure that knows no fatigue, no decrease, and no sorrow. Abraham’s heart, despite all his trials and his towering faith, was not free from the need for this divine assurance. He longed for the final home of peace. This prayer balances his earlier requests for wisdom and righteous companionship in this world with the recognition that this life is merely the planting ground, and the harvest is in the Hereafter. It is a powerful reminder that all the blessings of this world, including wisdom and a good reputation, are only truly valuable if they lead to the Garden. The believer learns to keep the afterlife as the central goal of every supplication. The idols of Abraham’s people could offer no such inheritance; their worshippers would find nothing but the fire. Abraham’s prayer is thus an act of complete dissociation from the false gods and a complete turning toward the Lord of all worlds, in whose hands lies the gift of Paradise. Every believer who recites this verse is invited to make Abraham’s prayer their own, yearning for that ultimate bliss that only the Most Merciful can bestow.

=86=

Abraham’s prayer now turns to a deeply personal and painful concern: his father. He asks Allah to forgive his father, acknowledging at the same time that his father is among those who are astray. This supplication shows the tender heart of the prophet and his concern for his closest relative, even though that relative was an idolater who opposed his message. Abraham loved his father and wished for his salvation.

This is natural human affection, and Islam encourages a believer to desire guidance for their family. However, the prayer is coupled with the truthful acknowledgment of his father’s state: he was dâll, misguided, lost in the worship of idols. Abraham does not pretend his father is on the right path; he seeks divine mercy for someone who desperately needs it. Later, in Surah At-Tawbah, it is clarified that once it became clear to Abraham that his father was an enemy of Allah and died in that state, he dissociated himself from him. This verse, however, captures a moment of hope, a plea before the final outcome was sealed. For the Prophet Muhammad, who also had beloved relatives like his uncle Abu Talib die upon disbelief, this verse was a poignant reminder of the limits of intercession and the importance of complete submission to Allah’s decree regarding those who reject faith. The lesson for believers is that while we should pray for the guidance of our misguided loved ones and show them compassion, ultimate forgiveness for those who die in shirk rests solely with Allah’s judgment, and the prophetic example is to disavow the disbelief while continuing to call to the truth as long as life remains.

=87=

Abraham’s supplication ascends to a plea concerning the most terrifying aspect of the Hereafter: public disgrace. He asks his Lord not to humiliate him on the Day when all of creation will be raised from their graves. The Day of Resurrection is described as a day of exposure. Every hidden deed, every secret thought, will be laid bare before the Lord of all worlds and before the assembled multitudes of humanity. Disgrace on that day is not a minor embarrassment; it is a catastrophic ruin, a shame from which there is no shelter. Abraham, who had faced the mockery of his people and the threat of the fire with unshakeable courage, was deeply afraid of the disgrace that comes from standing before Allah with sins un-forgiven. This prayer reveals the profound humility of a prophet. He did not rely on his own status or deeds to protect him from humiliation; he threw himself entirely on the mercy of his Lord.

The phrase “the Day all will be resurrected” emphasises the universality of the event. No one, high or low, will be absent. The tyrants, the oppressed, the prophets, the idolaters—all will stand together. In that assembly, the only true honour is the honour granted by Allah. Abraham’s prayer teaches the believer to live with a constant awareness of that Day and to seek refuge in Allah from the disgrace that results from a life lived in disobedience. It instills a healthy fear that drives one toward repentance and good deeds, not toward despair.

=88=

Abraham continues to describe the very Day he fears, highlighting the futility of the worldly assets that people rely upon. He states that on that Day, neither wealth nor children will avail a person in the slightest. Wealth, which people spend their lives amassing, and children, who are considered a source of support and a continuation of one’s legacy, will both be utterly useless before the divine tribunal. The rich cannot buy their way out of punishment, and the children cannot intercede or bear the sins of their parents. Every soul will stand alone, accountable only for its own deeds. This verse dismantles the two greatest pillars of worldly security. Abraham’s people, like the Quraysh and like people of every age, prided themselves on their material prosperity and their numerous offspring. They believed that these were signs of divine favour and that they would provide protection. Abraham shatters this illusion. The only currency on that Day is a heart that is sound with faith and deeds.

This teaching is a direct challenge to the materialism and tribalism that often cloud the truth. For the believer, it is a call to reorder priorities. Wealth and children are trusts from Allah and can be sources of blessing if used in His service, but they are not the ultimate measure of success. The real investment is in what one sends forward of righteous actions. This verse from Abraham’s prayer serves as a powerful reminder to detach the heart from the fleeting and to attach it to the eternal.

=89=

Abraham now states the single condition for salvation on that Day. It is not wealth, lineage, or worldly power, but coming to Allah with a pure heart.

The Arabic term qalb salîm, a sound heart, is one of the most profound concepts in the Quran. It refers to a heart that is free from shirk, associating partners with Allah. It is a heart that is purified from hypocrisy, envy, malice, and the love of sin. It is a heart that is whole and healthy, entirely devoted to its Creator, filled with sincerity, love, fear, and hope in Him alone. This heart is the container of faith, and its purity is the result of a lifelong struggle of repentance and purification. Abraham, who had smashed the idols and argued for monotheism, had a heart that was completely sound in its devotion. He is the model for all who seek to come to Allah successfully. This verse provides the ultimate answer to the questions raised throughout the surah. Why do most people not believe despite the signs? Because their hearts are sick. What is the common thread among all the destroyed nations? A heart corrupted by shirk and sins. What is the key to inheriting the Garden of Bliss? A heart made sound through faith and submission.

This teaching refocuses the entire spiritual life on the inner state. Outward deeds are essential, but their life and value stem from the condition of the heart. The believer is thus invited to engage in constant self-auditing, asking: In what state is my heart? Is it attached to the world or to Allah? Is it harbouring idols of pride, love of status, or greed? The journey to Allah is a journey of the heart back to its sound, primordial nature.

=90=

The scene shifts from Abraham’s prayer to a panoramic description of Judgment Day as a divine reality. The verse states that Paradise, in all its splendour, will be brought near to the muttaqîn, the God-fearing.

The Arabic verb uzlifat, meaning “brought near,” creates a vivid image. The vast, unimaginably beautiful Garden is not a distant dream that the righteous must travel to with difficulty; it is drawn close to them as an act of honour and welcome. They do not have to search for it; it comes to meet them.

This is the culmination of a life spent in fear of Allah’s displeasure and hope in His mercy. The God-fearing are those who lived with an acute awareness of divine watchfulness, who restrained themselves from sin and hastened to good. The nearness of Paradise is both a physical reality and a spiritual embrace. It signifies that the toil, the patience, and the striving of the worldly life are over, and the eternal reward is now immediately present. The imagery is one of joy, relief, and divine hospitality. This verse serves as a powerful motivator for the believers, contrasting their future with the horrific fate of the deviant that follows in the next verse. It also links back to Abraham’s prayer to be among the inheritors of the Garden of Bliss. The God-fearing are those who will inherit that nearness. The believer is encouraged to cultivate taqwâ in every aspect of life, knowing that the result is not a delayed, arduous reward but a Garden that rushes to meet its beloved inhabitants.

=91=

In stark contrast to the nearness of Paradise for the righteous, the Hellfire will be made plainly visible to the deviant, the ghâwîn. The word “displayed” translates burrizat, which means to be exposed, brought out into the open, made inescapably present. The deviant cannot look away; they cannot pretend it does not exist. The very thing they mocked and denied is now an overwhelming, terrifying reality right before their eyes.

The term ghâwîn refers to those who strayed from the path of guidance, who followed their whims and the deceptions of Satan, and who rejected the calls of the messengers. Their deviation was not an innocent mistake but a willful turning away from the truth. Now, the consequence of that deviation is visually confronting them, perhaps while they are still on the plain of judgment, filling them with a dread that is the first taste of the punishment to come. The psychological torment of seeing their destination before they are cast into it is part of the divine justice. The verse creates a scene of absolute horror on one side and absolute bliss on the other, with humanity divided between them. This juxtaposition is a recurring Quranic method of shaking the heart awake. The believer sees this verse and clings tighter to guidance, while the wavering are warned to turn back before the fire is no longer just a warning but a displayed reality. It is a reminder that the unseen truths of revelation will one day become the most tangible, undeniable facts of existence, and that moment is precisely the moment when belief is too late to benefit.

=92=

The deviant do not only see the fire; they are confronted with a question. The voice, whether from the angels or from Allah directly, asks them about the beings they used to worship in the worldly life.

The question is not a request for information but a severe rebuke and a form of humiliation. It forces them to look around for the gods they had relied upon, only to find them absent.

The phrase “those you used to worship” encompasses everything they had set up as partners with Allah: the stone idols, the human beings they deified, the jinn they served, and even their own desires that they obeyed. The question exposes the utter bankruptcy of their lifelong devotion. In the world, they had felt so secure in their worship, building temples, performing rituals, and waging war for the sake of these false gods. Now, in the moment of their greatest need, these gods have vanished.

The question is designed to crush their false hopes definitively. There will be no last-minute rescue, no intercession from the idols. The silence that follows the question is deafening. For the Quraysh, who proudly served the idols around the Kaaba, this was a direct and terrifying warning.

The verse teaches that anything worshipped besides Allah will abandon its worshipper on the Day of Judgment. The only One who will remain is the Lord of all worlds, and the only relationship that will endure is the one built on sincere monotheism. This should cause every believer to examine their own heart and ensure that no hidden idol—whether of wealth, ego, or other people—has taken a place that belongs only to Allah.

=93=

The question from the previous verse is now elaborated upon, intensifying the humiliation. The false gods are specified as those worshipped “besides Allah,” meaning in exclusion or in partnership with Him. Then the rhetorical question is posed: can these gods help you now, or can they even help themselves?

The answer, obviously, is a resounding no. The idols are themselves being dragged into the fire. They are powerless, insensate objects that cannot save anyone from the divine punishment.

This is the ultimate exposure of the lie of shirk. The polytheist believed that these gods could intercede, provide, or protect. The reality on the Day of Judgment is that they cannot even muster the power to save their own forms from the flames. This argument mirrors the exact logic that Abraham used with his people earlier in the surah: they cannot hear, they cannot benefit or harm. What was a logical deduction in this world becomes a physical, witnessed truth in the Hereafter.

The verse also demolishes the notion that some of these worshipped beings, like Jesus or the angels, would be pleased with the worship they received. They are shown to be utterly separate from their worshippers, incapable of providing the aid that was falsely attributed to them. The lesson for the living is stark: place your reliance and worship only on the One who has absolute power to help you, both in this life and in the Hereafter. The idols of modernity—be they human gurus, political ideologies, or material possessions—will be exposed in the same way. The believer learns to ask themselves daily: am I leaning on something that, when I need it most, will be utterly unable to help even itself?

=94=

The scene of humiliation reaches a physical climax. The idols, the very objects of worship, will be violently thrown into the Hellfire.

The Arabic verb kubkibû, “hurled headlong,” carries an image of being repeatedly shoved and tumbled face-first, without any dignity or respect. These stones and statues, which people had revered and before which they had prostrated, are now treated as nothing more than fuel for the fire, rubbish to be disposed of. They are cast in along with the ghâwîn, the deviant who worshipped them. The worshipper and the worshipped share the same fate.

This is a profound act of divine justice and a tangible demonstration of the falseness of those gods. The idol worshipper believed the idol was a source of honour and salvation; instead, the idol becomes a companion in disgrace. The verse destroys any lingering notion that the idols were harmless symbols. They are active participants in the spiritual crime, as they were the focal points of the greatest sin, and their destruction is a witness against those who carved and served them.

The term “deviant” includes not only the polytheists of old but all those who misguided themselves and others, the leaders of disbelief and the followers alike. This image serves as a severe warning to anyone who creates or promotes false ideologies that lead people away from the worship of Allah.

For the believer, it reinforces the duty to smash the idols of the heart and to warn others against placing their ultimate hope in anything other than the Creator.

=95=

The scope of the punishment widens further. Not only are the idols and the human deviants hurled into Hell, but also the soldiers of Iblis, that is, the army of Satan.

The phrase junûdu iblîs includes all those among the jinn and humans who enlisted in his service, who carried out his work of spreading misguidance, whispering evil, and beautifying sin. They are gathered together with their leader, Iblis, and with the idols that were their tools.

The phrase “all together” emphasises that no one from this army of falsehood is left behind. There is no escape, no forgotten regiment. The collective punishment of all the elements of disbelief—the false gods, the misguided worshippers, and the satanic inspirers—is a picture of complete and utter justice. It shows that the system of disbelief is a unified whole, and it will be dismantled as a whole. The soldiers of Iblis may have appeared successful and powerful in the world, leading nations and crafting ideologies, but their end is the same as the stones they fooled people into worshipping. This verse provides immense comfort to the believers who are oppressed or outnumbered by the forces of falsehood. The apparent might of Satan’s army is temporary. Its final gathering place is the fire, while the believers, however weak and few in this life, will be gathered in the Gardens of Bliss. The lesson is to recognise that the true battle is not against flesh and blood but against the whispers and armies of the devil, and that the only way to avoid being mustered into his forces is to enlist wholeheartedly in the service of the Most Merciful.

=96=

Once inside the fire, the scene of suffering is not silent. The deviant will engage in heated disputes and mutual recriminations.

The word “cry” can refer to both wailing from pain and shouting in argument. They dispute with their idols, the very things they once adored.

This is the ultimate irony. The idols, which had no power to hear, are now personified, or perhaps the dispute is with the devils who inspired the worship of those idols. The relationships of the dunya, built on shared falsehood, shatter into accusations and blame. The leaders and the followers, the gods and the worshippers, all turn on each other, each trying to shift the weight of responsibility. This dispute is a form of added torment, a psychological agony layered on top of the physical pain. The unity of disbelief was always a fragile alliance of convenience, and in the fire, it completely disintegrates. This verse provides a profound insight into the nature of false communities. They may appear united in this world under banners of nationalism, tribalism, or shared ideologies, but if their bond is not grounded in the truth, it will become a source of mutual hatred on the Day of Judgment.

For the believer, it is a call to build relationships on the basis of faith and sincere love for the sake of Allah, for these are the bonds that last. The argument in the fire also serves as a warning against blindly following others. Each person must take personal responsibility for their faith, because the excuses offered in the dispute will not avail them in the slightest.

=97=

The disputing deviant utter an oath, swearing by Allah Himself, the very God they had refused to worship sincerely. In this moment of terrible clarity, they finally acknowledge the truth that they had denied for a lifetime.

The phrase “By Allah” indicates the seriousness of their confession. They are not lying or making an excuse; they are stating a fact that has now become as clear as the sun. They admit that they were fî dalâlin mubîn, in clear, manifest error. The error was not a minor misjudgment but a vast, obvious straying from the path. The veil of worldly desires, pride, and satanic deception has been lifted, and they see their lives for what they truly were. The tragedy, of course, is that this confession comes at a time when it is utterly useless for changing their fate. Their recognition of the truth does not benefit them, because the test is over.

This is a recurring theme in the Quran: the disbelievers will beg for a second chance, they will admit their wrongdoing, they will even swear by Allah, but the door of repentance is closed after death. This verse stands as a desperate warning to the living. Do not wait for the certainty of the grave to acknowledge the truth. True wisdom is to recognise the manifest error of shirk and sin now, while repentance is still accepted. The believer reads this and is filled with a sense of urgency to correct their course and to help others see the clarity of the truth before the day when seeing it clearly comes too late.

=98=

The deviant continue their confession, specifying the nature of their clear error. They acknowledge that their great crime was to make their false gods equal to the Lord of all worlds.

The Arabic nusawwîkum bi-rabbi al-‘âlamîn means they set them up as equals, treating them with the same reverence, love, fear, and obedience that is due only to the Creator. This is the very essence of shirk. They did not necessarily believe that the idols had created the heavens and the earth, but in their practical worship, their devotion, and their obedience, they placed created things on the same level as the Creator. This confession is a direct echo of the message of every prophet in the surah, who all called to the worship of the Lord of all worlds. The deviant now admit that the prophets were right all along.

The phrase “Lord of all worlds” is especially poignant. They had narrowed their worship to local, tribal, or naturalistic deities, ignoring the universal Lord whose dominion encompasses everything. Their confession affirms the core Quranic doctrine that the greatest injustice is to ascribe partners to Allah.

The verse teaches that the ultimate criterion of success or failure is monotheism. A person may have many good deeds, but if they meet Allah with shirk, those deeds are rendered vain. The confession of the damned is a backhanded testimony to the truth of the message that the Prophet Muhammad was sent to deliver. The Quraysh, who heard this verse, were being told that their own idols, which they treated as equals to Allah, would one day be the subject of their own bitter regret and admission of error, unless they repented.

=99=

The dispute in the fire continues as the deviant search for someone to blame for their predicament. They declare that it was only the wicked who led them astray.

The term al-mujrimûn, “the criminals” or “the wicked,” refers to the leaders of disbelief, the satans among mankind and jinn, the elites who called them to idolatry, the poets and priests who glorified falsehood, and the tyrants who enforced it. This verse shows the human tendency to shift responsibility, even in the fire.

However, the Quranic context makes it clear that this blame is not an excuse. They are stating a fact: the wicked did call them, and they followed. But they also bear the full responsibility for following. The Quran elsewhere states that the followers will plead, “Our Lord, we obeyed our chiefs and great ones, and they misled us from the way,” only to be told, “Enter the fire along with the others. ” The wicked are indeed guilty of leading others astray and will bear a double punishment: for their own misguidance and for the misguidance of those they led. This verse serves as a powerful warning to anyone in a position of influence. The wicked leaders, the corrupt scholars, the celebrity preachers of falsehood, the social media influencers who normalise sin—all will bear the burden of their own deeds and a share of the burden of those they misled, without diminishing the burden of the followers. The lesson for the believer is to be extremely cautious about whom they take as guides and to always verify what they follow against the clear revelation, so that they are not among those who will cry out against the wicked on that day.

=100=

The lament continues with a devastating realisation. The deviant admit that on that day, they have absolutely no intercessors. In their worldly life, the polytheists had believed that their idols would intercede for them with Allah. They claimed, “We only worship them that they may bring us closer to Allah. ” Now, when they need that intercession most desperately, they find none. The idols are themselves in the fire. The angels, the prophets, and the righteous, whose intercession is real and can occur by Allah’s permission, are not available for the disbelievers. The intercession on the Day of Judgment is only for the believers, and even then, it only occurs with Allah’s leave and for those with whom He is pleased. The deviant have no share in it.

The word “none” is absolute. No family member, no tribal chief, no earthly ally, and no false god can speak on their behalf. The stark loneliness of that moment is a major part of the punishment. Every soul is isolated with its deeds. This verse reinforces the Quranic call to abandon the false concept of intercession tied to shirk and to seek instead a direct relationship with Allah. The believer understands that the way to secure the intercession of the Prophet Muhammad on the Day of Judgment is not to worship him or call upon him as a partner with Allah, but to live by the monotheism he brought, to love him, and to follow his Sunnah, which leads one into the mercy of Allah. The cry of the deviant echoes across the surah as a warning: secure your intercessor now through faith, for on that day, no negotiation and no rescue will be possible for those who die in shirk.
