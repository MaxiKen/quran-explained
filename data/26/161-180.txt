=161=

Lot is introduced with the same affectionate title, “their brother,” signifying that he was not an outsider passing judgment but one of their own, deeply connected to them by blood and shared life. Lot was the nephew of Abraham, and he had accompanied his uncle on his journey of faith. He settled among the people of Sodom, a city whose inhabitants had sunk into depravity unprecedented in human history. His opening call is identical to that of every messenger in this surah: “Will you not fear Allah? ” This simple question is the root of all reform. The people of Lot had completely lost the sense of divine presence and accountability. They indulged their lusts openly, they waylaid travellers, they mocked righteousness, and they lived as if no higher power was watching. Lot’s question is an invitation to rediscover that primordial fear, that healthy shame before the Creator, which alone can restrain a human being from descending into the worst of behaviours. It is significant that Lot begins with the fear of Allah, not with a direct condemnation of their specific sin. He targets the spiritual root before addressing the moral fruit. If they could recover the consciousness of Allah, the reformation of their actions would follow naturally. For the Quraysh, who also engaged in shameful practices—burying daughters alive, cheating in trade, and indulging in tribal pride—the question was equally piercing. The lesson for every caller to truth is to prioritise the revival of taqwâ in the hearts of people, for without it, any attempt at moral reform is like painting a house whose foundation is crumbling.

=162=

Lot, following the established prophetic pattern, grounds his message in his personal integrity. He declares himself to be a trustworthy messenger, an amîn. Lot had lived among the people of Sodom for many years before his prophethood. He was known for his upright character, his hospitality, and his protection of the weak in a society that preyed on the vulnerable. His people could not point to a single lie he had told or a single trust he had betrayed. This made his warning all the more compelling and their rejection all the more inexcusable. By reminding them of his trustworthiness, Lot is essentially saying: you know I am not a liar, you know I am not seeking personal gain, and you know I have always been sincere with you.

Therefore, when I now tell you that I am a messenger from the Lord of all worlds, you have every reason to believe me. This appeal to lived character is a hallmark of prophetic da‘wah. The messenger does not appear from nowhere making grand claims; he emerges from the fabric of the community, his life an open book of truthfulness. The Quraysh faced the same reality with Muhammad al-Amîn. Their inability to deny his character forced them to attack his message with irrational labels like “madness” or “bewitchment. ” For the believer, this verse is a reminder that the first step in calling others to Allah is to live a life of such integrity that when one speaks of the divine, the words carry the weight of a trustworthy life behind them.

=163=

Lot delivers the identical twin command that forms the core of every prophetic mission in this surah. The fear of Allah is the inner transformation that realigns the heart with its Creator, and obedience to the messenger is the outer submission that translates that inner state into concrete action. For the people of Lot, obeying him meant abandoning their abominable practices, repenting sincerely, and restructuring their society according to the divine law that Lot conveyed. It meant acknowledging that their natural desires were gifts from Allah to be channelled within the bounds of the sacred institution of marriage, not to be perverted into acts that defy the natural order. The command is simple but radical. It demanded a complete overturning of their entrenched culture of vice.

The pairing of taqwâ and obedience is particularly significant here. The sin of Lot’s people was not merely a failure of obedience; it was a symptom of a complete loss of the fear of Allah. They had normalised the abnormal because their hearts had become insensible to the divine gaze. True reform, therefore, could not be a superficial adjustment of behaviour but had to begin with a spiritual awakening. For the Quraysh, who also had cultural practices that contradicted the prophetic message, this verse underscored that obedience to the messenger was not optional. The believer learns that faith is not a private sentiment but a public commitment that reshapes every aspect of life, and that the messenger is the authority who defines the shape of that life.

=164=

Lot declares his complete disinterest in any material compensation, following the sincere pattern of all the prophets. He asks for no payment, no elevation in status, no share of the city’s wealth. His motivation is purely the fulfillment of his duty to his Lord and his genuine care for the salvation of his people. In a society where everything had become transactional, where even hospitality had been twisted into a trap for the unwary, Lot’s disinterested sincerity shone as a stark contrast. He wanted nothing from them but their own good.

The phrase “My reward is only from the Lord of all worlds” is a profound statement of reliance and freedom. Lot is not an employee of the people of Sodom; he is a servant of the Creator of the universe. His provision, his protection, and his ultimate recompense are in hands far higher than any human authority. This frees him from any need to compromise the message to please the powerful or to soften the truth to gain popularity. The corrupt elites could offer him nothing that would tempt him, because his eyes were fixed on a reward beyond their imagination.

For the believer engaged in any form of moral or spiritual advocacy, this verse is a purification of intention. One must constantly check the heart: am I seeking the approval of people, or am I seeking the reward of Allah? The prophet’s example teaches that the most powerful da‘wah is that which is offered freely, with hands empty of worldly expectation, pointing only to the One who rewards the sincere in this life and the next.

=165=

Lot now directly confronts the defining sin of his people, the act for which they became a byword throughout history. The question is sharp, exposing the unnaturalness and the moral perversion of their behaviour.

The Arabic phrase adh-dhukrân, “fellow men,” emphasises that they were turning away from the natural, divinely created order and pursuing sexual gratification with their own gender. Lot’s question is not a mere expression of disapproval; it is a call to reason and to the innate human nature, the fitrah, that knows this act is a deviation. The verse is explicit, and its inclusion in the Quran demonstrates that religion does not shy away from addressing even the most sensitive moral issues when they threaten the fabric of society. Lot’s people were not engaged in private, hidden sins; they had institutionalised their vice, performing it openly in their gatherings and even assaulting travellers who passed through their lands.

The question “Why do you lust” probes the psychology behind the act. It was not a matter of love or commitment; it was raw lust, divorced from the purposes for which Allah had created intimacy: procreation, tranquility, and bonding within the sacred structure of marriage.

For the believer, this verse is a clear statement of the Islamic position on homosexual acts, which are viewed as a transgression of the limits set by the Creator. It also provides a model for addressing moral issues: with a clear question that appeals to the innate sense of right and wrong, rather than with mockery or hatred. The goal of the prophet was always reform and salvation, not mere condemnation.

=166=

Lot continues his question by highlighting what they were abandoning. They were leaving their wives, the spouses whom Allah had created for them, within whom the natural fulfillment of desire and the blessing of offspring are found.

The phrase “that your Lord has created for you” roots the marital relationship in the divine creative order. Marriage between a man and a woman is not a social construct but a sacred gift from the Creator, designed for the harmony of the soul and the continuation of the human race. By turning away from this divine gift and pursuing forbidden avenues, the people of Lot were committing a double crime: they were engaging in a prohibited act and simultaneously neglecting the lawful means that Allah had graciously provided. Lot’s words are filled with a kind of sorrowful bewilderment. He sees them spurning the wholesome and pure for the unnatural and corrupt. Then he delivers the final verdict: “In fact, you are a transgressing people. ” The word ‘âdûn denotes those who persistently and deliberately cross the boundaries. This was not a case of occasional weakness or secret sin; it was a culture of transgression, a communal identity built around defiance of divine law.

The verse teaches that the failure to channel desires within the halal leads inexorably to the haram. It also establishes the Quranic principle that the remedy for societal vice is not the eradication of desire but its proper orientation toward the spouses that Allah has created.

For the believer, this is a call to honour and cherish the institution of marriage, to see it as a shield and a blessing, and to recognise that the breakdown of the family is a sign of a society that has lost its way, following the path of Lot’s people toward transgression.

=167=

Cornered by Lot’s clear and irrefutable moral challenge, his people abandon any attempt at intellectual debate. Their response is a naked threat: stop preaching, or be cast out of the city. The threat of expulsion was the ultimate weapon of a society that could not tolerate the presence of a righteous voice. Lot was not asking for political power; he was merely calling them to fear Allah. But even that was too much for a community that wanted to indulge its desires without any reminder of accountability. The threat reveals the totalitarian nature of a culture of vice. It cannot coexist with truth; it must silence or expel it.

The phrase “O Lot” is laced with a patronising familiarity, as if they were giving a stubborn friend one last warning. They did not want to kill him immediately, perhaps out of some residual tribal respect or because they feared his uncle Abraham, but they could not bear his presence.

This is a common pattern: when falsehood is deeply entrenched, it seeks to banish the truth rather than reform itself. The Quraysh faced a similar ultimatum from the Meccans, who offered to expel the Prophet or even kill him. The believer learns that standing for truth often leads to social ostracism. The threat of being labelled an outcast, of losing one’s community, is one of the most powerful tools of social pressure. Lot’s response, which follows, shows the courage required to face such a threat. One must be willing to be expelled by the people if that is the price of remaining loyal to the Lord of all worlds. True belonging is to the community of faith, not to a society of transgression.

=168=

Lot’s reply is a powerful blend of personal conviction and prophetic courage. He does not negotiate, he does not soften his stance, and he does not express fear. He declares openly that he is among those who profoundly despise their deeds.

The Arabic word qâlin, translated as “despise,” carries the meaning of intense abhorrence and loathing. Lot is not merely disapproving; he feels a deep, inner revulsion toward what they do. This revulsion is not born of personal prejudice but of a soul that is aligned with the divine standard of purity. His declaration serves several purposes. First, it shatters any hope they might have that he would ever compromise or quietly accept their practices. Second, it distances him completely from their sin, which is essential for a prophet who must be morally uncompromised. Third, it is an act of da‘wah itself, because by expressing his own strong feeling, he is holding up a mirror to their deadened consciences, hoping that some spark of natural human shame might be reignited. Lot’s loathing is directed at the “practice,” the action itself, not at the people as irredeemable beings. The distinction is crucial. He hates the sin, but he is still there, calling the sinners to repentance.

The verse teaches the believer that it is not enough to privately avoid sin; one must sometimes publicly disavow it, especially when it has become normalised. However, this disavowal should be accompanied by a sincere desire for the sinners’ guidance, not by self-righteous contempt. Lot’s courage in speaking this truth directly to the faces of those who had just threatened him with expulsion is a model of prophetic integrity.

=169=

Having openly declared his position, Lot turns immediately to his Lord in supplication. This is the consistent pattern of the prophets: they deliver the message with courage, and then they entrust the outcome to Allah. Lot’s prayer is specific: he asks for salvation for himself and his family from the consequences of the people’s deeds. The “consequences” refer primarily to the divine punishment that he knows is imminent, but it also includes the moral and spiritual contamination of living in such a corrupt environment. Lot has done all he can; now he places the matter entirely in the hands of his Lord. The prayer reveals his deep pastoral care for his family. He does not ask for his own salvation alone but includes his household. His family consisted of those who had believed in him, primarily his two daughters, and his wife, who, as the Quran reveals elsewhere, was an exception and did not believe. Lot’s prayer, in its simplicity and urgency, is a model for every believer who finds themselves surrounded by widespread evil. We are to do our utmost to call to what is right, but we must also constantly turn to Allah for protection—protection from the punishment that may befall the wrongdoers, and protection from the subtle ways in which a corrupt environment can erode our own faith. The prayer is an acknowledgment that ultimate safety lies not in human strength or social standing but in the direct intervention of the Most Merciful. Lot’s plea is answered in the following verses with a dramatic and decisive rescue.

=170=

The divine response to Lot’s prayer is immediate and comprehensive. Allah says, “So We saved him,” with the “So” indicating the direct connection between the sincere supplication and the act of deliverance. The salvation was complete; Lot and his entire family were taken out of the city before the punishment descended. This was a physical, miraculous rescue, carried out by angels who visited Lot in the guise of handsome young men, were threatened by the mob, and then revealed their true identity and the impending doom.

The phrase “all of his family” highlights the scope of the mercy. Every believing member of his household was included. This teaches a beautiful lesson about the blessings of a righteous patriarch. Lot’s faith was a means of salvation for his daughters, who were among the few believers in the city. It also underscores the Quranic theme that the believers are not left to perish with the disbelievers. When divine judgment arrives, a clear separation is made. The salvation of Lot’s family is a parallel to the salvation of Noah and his followers in the Ark, and to the salvation of Sâlih and the believers from Thamûd. The one who calls to the truth and remains steadfast is never abandoned. The Lord who commands the warning also orchestrates the rescue. For the Prophet Muhammad and his companions, this was a reassuring promise. The small, beleaguered community of believers in Mecca, surrounded by a corrupt society threatening them with expulsion or death, could trust that the same Lord who saved Lot and his family would provide a way out for them. The believer learns that no matter how dark the night, the dawn of divine deliverance is certain for those who hold fast to their faith and call upon their Lord with sincerity.

=171=

The comprehensive salvation has one tragic exception: an old woman, Lot’s wife. The Quran does not even dignify her with the title of “his wife” in this context, calling her an “old woman,” perhaps to indicate her spiritual decrepitude and her separation from the bond of faith that tied Lot to the rest of his family. She was among those who remained behind, one of the gâbirîn, the doomed who were to be destroyed. Her crime was not that she committed the abomination herself, but that she was complicit with the people of Sodom. She sympathised with them, she betrayed her husband’s mission, and she tipped off the mob about the visiting guests. Her heart was with the transgressors, not with the prophet in her own household. Her fate is a deeply sobering lesson. Biological proximity to a prophet does not guarantee salvation. Faith is a personal matter, not an inheritance. The wife of Lot and the wife of Noah are both cited in the Quran as examples of those who disbelieved and were not helped by their righteous husbands. Conversely, the wife of Pharaoh, who believed in Moses despite being married to the greatest tyrant, is an example of faith triumphing over a corrupt environment. The lesson is that each soul is responsible for its own choice. No family tie, no matter how close, can substitute for personal conviction and taqwâ.

For the believer, this verse is a call to ensure that one’s own faith is genuine and that one’s ultimate loyalty is to the truth, even if it means standing apart from the closest family members. The exception of the old woman serves as a stark warning against the complacency of assuming that association with the righteous is enough.

=172=

After Lot and the believers were safely removed, the destruction fell upon the rest. The phrase “utterly destroyed” indicates a complete annihilation, leaving no survivors among the people of Sodom and the surrounding cities of the plain. The destruction of Lot’s people was unique in its nature, as the next verse explains. The verse is brief, but its weight is immense. The entire civilization, with all its perversion, its wealth, its fortified cities, and its arrogant inhabitants, was wiped out. The “rest” included the mob that had threatened Lot, the elites who had institutionalised vice, and the general populace that had accepted and normalised the transgression. Their destruction is a manifestation of the divine name al-‘Azîz, the Almighty, whose might is beyond any resistance.

The verse teaches that there is a point of no return for collective evil. When a society becomes so saturated in corruption that the righteous voice is either expelled or silenced, and when the call to fear Allah is met with threats and mockery, the divine judgment descends. The destruction of Sodom is not just a historical event; it is a permanent sign and a perpetual warning. For the Quraysh, who passed by the ruins of Lot’s people on their trade journeys to the Levant, the evidence was there before their eyes. The Dead Sea region, a desolate and barren landscape, was a silent testimony to the truth of Lot’s warning and the finality of divine justice. The believer is reminded to never feel safe from the plan of Allah and to work actively to prevent society from crossing the threshold that invites collective punishment.

=173=

The verse describes the specific instrument of destruction. A rain of brimstone, stones of baked clay and fire, was poured down upon the cities of Lot’s people. The Quran elsewhere adds that the cities were turned upside down, meaning an earthquake accompanied the rain of fire, creating a scene of total devastation. The “rain” was not a natural shower but a deliberate, targeted punishment from the sky, each stone marked for its intended victim. The exclamation “How evil was the rain of those who had been warned!” is a divine commentary inserted into the narrative to drive the lesson home. It is an expression of lament and warning. The rain was evil not just because it brought death but because it was the direct consequence of their persistent rejection after clear warning. The ones who had been warned—the people to whom Lot had called for years—received not the life-giving rain of mercy but the deadly rain of destruction.

This contrast is powerful. Rain is normally a symbol of blessing, reviving the earth and bringing crops. But for a people who had corrupted the earth, the sky itself became a source of vengeance.

The verse teaches that the very elements of nature are soldiers of Allah, obedient to His command. The air that they breathed, the sky that gave them sunlight, all turned against them when the divine decree was issued.

For the believer, it is a humbling meditation on the fragility of human security. The same rain that falls gently on the fields can, by the command of Allah, become a lethal storm. The only protection is to be among those who heeded the warning, not among those who became a lesson for others.

=174=

The refrain returns, marking the conclusion of Lot’s narrative. “In this” refers to the entire story: the unnatural sin, the courageous call of Lot, the threat of expulsion, the angelic rescue, the salvation of the believers, the betrayal of Lot’s wife, and the cataclysmic destruction by the rain of brimstone. All of this is a multifaceted sign, an âyah, that points to the reality of divine justice, the truth of prophethood, and the seriousness of moral corruption. The sign was not only in the narrated story but in the physical remains of the cities, which lay on the route of the Meccan caravans. The Quraysh could see the evidence with their own eyes, yet the refrain observes, with a tone of sorrow and inevitability, that most of them would not believe. The human heart, when it becomes accustomed to sin and attached to worldly comfort, can look directly at the ruins of a destroyed civilization and still convince itself that the same fate will not befall them.

This is the tragic mystery of disbelief that the surah repeatedly highlights. The verse is both a lament and a challenge. It laments the stubbornness of the majority and challenges the individual listener to be among the few who see the sign, take the warning to heart, and turn to their Lord in repentance and obedience.

The repetition of this refrain is a structural genius that ties the disparate stories of the surah into a unified message: the signs are everywhere, on the earth and in history, but only the believers have the eyes to see them.

=175=

The divine signature seals the story of Lot as it sealed the stories of Moses, Abraham, Noah, Hûd, and Sâlih. The Lord who rained brimstone on Sodom is al-‘Azîz, the Almighty, whose power overturned cities and wiped out an entire civilization. The Lord who sent Lot with a clear warning, who saved him and his believing family, and who preserves his story in the Quran as a guidance for all time is ar-Rahîm, the Most Merciful. The mercy is evident in the details: the angels who visited Lot, the urgency with which they rushed him out of the city before dawn, the clear instruction not to look back, and the salvation of his daughters. Even the destruction itself is a form of mercy for the wider world, as it purges a deeply corrupt society and establishes a deterrent for future generations.

The pairing of might and mercy is the theological backbone of this surah. The believer is never allowed to dwell on divine punishment without being reminded of divine mercy, and vice versa. The Lord is not a wrathful tyrant who delights in destruction, nor is He a lenient figure who overlooks all transgressions. He is the Almighty who punishes the guilty with justice, and the Most Merciful who protects the righteous with care. For the Prophet, facing the corruption of the Quraysh, this was a reaffirmation that his Lord was fully capable of both delivering the believers and dealing with the transgressors. The personal address “your Lord” keeps the relationship intimate. The believer is invited to rest in the knowledge that the Almighty and Most Merciful is their personal Lord, their protector, and their ultimate destination.

=176=

The surah moves to the story of Shu‘aib and his people, known as the residents of the Forest, or As-hâb al-Aykah. The “Forest” refers to a thicket of trees in the region of Madyan, in northwestern Arabia near the Gulf of Aqaba. This community is also referred to in the Quran as the people of Madyan. They were an Arab tribe who were successors to the civilizations of ‘Âd and Thamûd, and they inherited the land but not the lessons. The verse states that they rejected the messengers, using the plural in keeping with the surah’s pattern. Shu‘aib is often called the “orator of the prophets” because of his eloquent and persuasive speech, yet his people, like the others, turned away. Their primary sins were economic: they were cheaters in trade, giving short measure and weight, and they were corruptors on the earth, spreading fraud and dishonesty. They also engaged in the idolatry and the denial of the Hereafter that were common to the other destroyed nations. Their rejection was not passive; they actively threatened Shu‘aib and the believers with expulsion and death. For the Quraysh, who were themselves a commercial society whose trade routes passed through the region of Madyan, this story was a direct parallel. The sins of Madyan—fraud, exploitation, and economic injustice—were also present in Meccan society. The story of Shu‘aib is thus a warning that commercial prosperity built on dishonesty and the rejection of divine guidance is a house of cards that will collapse under the weight of divine justice. The believer learns that the prophetic message encompasses not only theology and personal morality but also economic justice and business ethics. The marketplace is as much a testing ground for taqwâ as the mosque.

=177=

Shu‘aib is introduced as a prophet to his people with the same opening call that has echoed through every narrative in this surah. “Will you not fear Allah?” This question is the universal key to all reform. Shu‘aib’s people, the merchants and traders of Madyan, had become so consumed by greed and the pursuit of profit that they had lost all fear of the divine reckoning. Their scales were rigged, their promises were broken, and their dealings were filled with deceit, all because they did not live with the consciousness that Allah was watching. Shu‘aib’s question is an attempt to awaken that dormant consciousness. If they could only fear Allah, they would naturally give full measure, speak the truth, and deal justly. The fear of Allah is the internal regulator that no external audit can replace. A person who truly fears the Lord of all worlds will not cheat, even if they are certain no human eye will ever detect the fraud, because they know that the divine ledger is perfect and the Day of Judgment is real.

The question is gentle but profoundly challenging. It implies that their economic corruption is a symptom of a deeper spiritual disease. For the Quraysh, whose trade was the lifeblood of their city, the same question was being posed through the mouth of the Prophet Muhammad. The believer learns that the first step in addressing any societal ill, from fraud to fornication, is to call people back to the foundational awareness of Allah. Without that anchor, every other reform is a fragile artifice.

=178=

Shu‘aib establishes his personal credibility, following the unwavering prophetic pattern of this surah. He declares himself to be a trustworthy messenger. The people of Madyan knew Shu‘aib well. He had lived among them all his life, and his honesty was beyond reproach. In a society rife with fraud, Shu‘aib was known as a man of absolute integrity. This made him the ideal messenger to a people whose primary sin was dishonesty in trade. His life was a living refutation of their practices. When he called them to give full measure and to not defraud, his words carried the weight of a man who had never cheated anyone in his entire life. The unspoken argument is: if you admit that I am truthful in all my worldly dealings, then why do you doubt me when I speak on behalf of the Lord? The trustworthiness of the messenger is the logical foundation of the message. The Quraysh, who also knew the Prophet Muhammad as al-Amîn, faced the same unanswerable question.

This verse teaches that the character of the reformer is an integral part of the reform. One cannot call others to honesty while being dishonest oneself. The prophetic model is to be the change one wishes to see, so that the message is not just words spoken by the tongue but a reality embodied in the life. Shu‘aib’s declaration is a reminder to every believer, particularly those in business, that their upright conduct is a form of da‘wah, a silent testimony that prepares the ground for the spoken word of truth.

=179=

Shu‘aib delivers the twin command. “Fear Allah” is the inner cure for the greed and dishonesty that plagued Madyan. “Obey me” means to follow the specific economic and social reforms that Shu‘aib, as the messenger, was bringing: giving full measure, weighing with justice, not defrauding people, and not spreading corruption. Obedience to the messenger is the practical outworking of taqwâ. A person cannot claim to fear Allah while continuing to cheat in the marketplace. The command ties the spiritual and the economic together in an unbreakable bond. For the people of Madyan, this was a direct challenge to their entire way of life. Their wealth was built on deceit; obeying Shu‘aib would mean a radical restructuring of their business practices and, likely, a reduction in their profits. It was a test of whether they valued the pleasure of Allah more than the accumulation of wealth. For the Quraysh, the same test was present. Obeying the Prophet meant abandoning the fraudulent practices of the pre-Islamic trade, treating the poor with justice, and recognising that the Kaaba’s sanctity was from Allah, not from the idols they had placed there.

The verse teaches that faith is not compartmentalised. The fear of Allah must be present in the marketplace, in the ledger books, and in every transaction. The believer is called to a holistic obedience that leaves no corner of life untouched by the prophetic guidance.

=180=

Shu‘aib, like every sincere prophet, declares his complete financial independence from his people. He asks for no payment, no commission, and no increase in his own trading profits in exchange for his guidance.

This is particularly significant in the context of Madyan, where everything was measured in terms of material gain. By stating that his reward is only from Allah, Shu‘aib completely undercuts any accusation that he is a competitor in the marketplace or a man seeking to enrich himself through a new religion. He is not after their money; he is after their salvation. His sustenance is in the hands of the Lord of all worlds, not in the hands of the merchants of Madyan. This freedom from worldly incentives is what gives the prophetic voice its unmatched power. The corrupt elites could not buy him, and they could not threaten him with poverty, because his reliance was entirely on the One who owns the treasures of the heavens and the earth. The verse is a timeless lesson for religious scholars, preachers, and anyone engaged in da‘wah. The moment the caller to truth becomes financially dependent on the audience, the message risks being compromised. The prophetic model is to work for one’s livelihood by one’s own hands or to rely solely on Allah’s provision, while delivering the message free of charge. Shu‘aib, who was likely a successful merchant himself, exemplifies this balance. His hands were clean, his motives were pure, and his trust was in the Lord of all worlds. This sincerity left his people with no excuse, as their rejection could not be attributed to any suspicion about his intentions.
