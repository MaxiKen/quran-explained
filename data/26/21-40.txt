=21=

Moses continues his honest reply to Pharaoh, picking up where his previous admission left off. Having acknowledged that he acted without guidance at that time, he now explains the sequence of events that led to this very moment.

The first part, “So I fled from you when I feared you,” refers to his escape from Egypt after the unintended killing of the Egyptian. The fear he mentions was not the trembling of a coward; it was the rational fear of a man facing a powerful, unjust regime that had already demonstrated its willingness to slaughter the innocent. He left not out of guilt for a deliberate crime but to preserve his life so that he might live to serve a greater purpose. This honest recounting strips Pharaoh’s narrative of its sting. Moses was not an ungrateful fugitive hiding from justice but a man fleeing state-sanctioned murder. Then comes the transformative declaration: “Then my Lord granted me wisdom and made me one of the messengers. ” The Arabic word for wisdom here is hukm, which encompasses sound judgment, prophetic authority, and the deep understanding of divine law. This gift completely reshaped Moses. The man who once reacted in the heat of the moment was now a measured, divinely guided leader. Being made a messenger was not a title he claimed for himself; it was an appointment from the Lord of all worlds.

The phrase deftly connects this wisdom to the very message he now delivers. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a powerful parallel: he too had lived an ordinary life before the revelation came, and the Quraysh similarly tried to discredit him by pointing to his past. The lesson for believers is profound: divine selection is not based on a flawless past but on Allah’s sovereign choice and the transformative power of His guidance. No human being’s history can disqualify them from becoming a vessel of truth if Allah wills to grant them wisdom and a mission.

=22=

Moses now dismantles Pharaoh’s opening argument with a single, brilliant rebuttal. Pharaoh had tried to shame him by recalling the upbringing in the palace, framing it as a generous favour that Moses had betrayed. Moses turns this supposed favour on its head. He asks how it can possibly be called a favour when the only reason he ended up in the palace was because Pharaoh’s regime had instituted a genocidal policy of killing the male children of the Israelites. The very act of throwing infant boys into the river was the cause of his being placed in the basket and later found by Pharaoh’s household. So the “favour” was the accidental byproduct of a monstrous crime. Moses exposes the deep moral corruption of a tyrant who wants gratitude for merely allowing one child to live while systematically murdering countless others.

The phrase “you have enslaved the Children of Israel” broadens the indictment. The upbringing was not an isolated act of kindness; it occurred within the context of a vast, brutal system of enslavement and oppression. Moses connects his personal story to the collective suffering of his people, refusing to let Pharaoh separate the “palace child” from the slave nation. This rhetorical move transforms him from a former dependant into a liberator speaking on behalf of the oppressed. It also nullifies Pharaoh’s emotional manipulation entirely. The courtiers who had nodded along with Pharaoh’s accusation are now confronted with the ugly reality they have all been part of.

The verse teaches an enduring principle: when oppressors sentimentalize isolated acts of “mercy” to mask systemic injustice, the truth-teller must expose the full picture. One cannot be bribed into silence by crumbs from the tyrant’s table.

=23=

Having failed to discredit Moses personally, Pharaoh shifts his attack to the message itself. His question is not an honest inquiry from a seeking heart; it drips with sarcasm and condescension. He asks as if the concept of a Lord of all worlds is utterly novel, obscure, and perhaps even laughable. In Pharaoh’s worldview, he himself was the divine lord of Egypt, and the various nations each had their own local deities. The idea of one singular Lord who transcends all boundaries and holds absolute authority over every realm was a direct threat to his self-proclaimed divinity and to the polytheistic order that upheld his throne. By asking “what is,” rather than “who is,” Pharaoh may even be feigning that such a being is a nonsensical category, a mere abstraction.

The question was posed publicly, before his chiefs, to rally them in scoffing at Moses. It is a classic tactic of the arrogant: when faced with a profound truth, reduce it to an absurdity by asking for a definition while refusing to see the evidence already provided. Moses had already proclaimed the Lord of all worlds; the existence of that Lord was obvious from creation itself. Pharaoh’s question was therefore a stalling technique and a mockery.

Yet, Allah allowed this question to be recorded because Moses’ response, which follows, becomes one of the most majestic descriptions of God in the Quran. It demonstrates that even the worst blasphemer’s mockery can be turned into an occasion for proclaiming the truth. For the believers, it is a reminder that not every question deserves an answer in the same spirit, but sometimes the best response is to declare the magnificence of Allah, leaving the mockers with no refuge but silence.

=24=

Moses answers with a definition that encompasses the entire cosmos. He does not enter into a philosophical debate about the nature of divinity; he simply points to the observable universe. The Lord of all worlds is the One who owns, sustains, and governs the heavens and the earth and everything that exists between them.

This description was undeniable even to Pharaoh and his court, for they could not claim to have created the sky above them or the ground beneath their feet. The phrase “everything in between” includes the air they breathed, the birds in the sky, the rivers and mountains, and every living creature. Moses is essentially saying: look around you; everything you see is under the dominion of one Lord, and that is the Lord I am calling you to.

The closing clause, “if only you had sure faith,” is deeply instructive. The Arabic yaqîn means certainty, a faith that is so firmly rooted that it sees the truth as clearly as the eye sees the sun. Moses implies that the problem is not a lack of evidence but a lack of the inner quality that allows a person to accept the evidence and build upon it. He is not insulting them but diagnosing their condition. If they had this certainty, they would immediately recognise that the heavens and the earth cannot be the domain of a human king who eats, drinks, and dies. This approach mirrors the Quran’s own method of calling people to faith through reflection on creation. It also taught the Prophet Muhammad how to respond to the Quraysh’s similar scoffing about Allah’s oneness. Instead of being drawn into endless verbal traps, he was to point to the signs in the universe and invite people to cultivate the certainty that opens the heart to truth.

=25=

Pharaoh turns away from Moses and addresses his courtiers, the high-ranking chiefs who surrounded his throne. The question is meant to be incredulous. He is not seeking information; he is staging a performance to rally his inner circle in unified contempt. By asking “Did you hear what he said? ” Pharaoh implies that Moses’ words are so outrageous, so beyond the pale of acceptable discourse, that the mere act of hearing them should shock any sensible person. He wants his chiefs to nod, to sneer, to confirm that they too find this talk of a universal Lord to be preposterous.

This is the behaviour of a tyrant who rules through group consensus and psychological manipulation. He isolates the messenger by making him appear as an outsider spouting nonsense that no reasonable person would entertain. The tactic is to foreclose any genuine reflection by creating an atmosphere of collective ridicule. If the entire court laughs, who among them would dare to consider that Moses might be speaking the truth?

Yet, in the divine narration, this moment is preserved to show the sheer arrogance of power. Pharaoh does not even engage Moses directly now; he acts as if Moses is not worthy of a direct reply.

The verse also serves a narrative purpose: it sets the stage for Moses’ additional clarification in the next verse, which is a direct response to this courtly sneer. The Prophet Muhammad experienced identical treatment; the Quraysh leaders would turn to one another and mock his message, signalling that no one needed to take it seriously. This story comforts those who call to truth that the mockery of the powerful is not a sign of their own weakness but a sign of the hearers’ spiritual bankruptcy.

=26=

Undeterred by Pharaoh’s theatrical dismissal, Moses adds another layer to his description. He brings the universal declaration down to the personal and historical level. The Lord of the heavens and the earth is not some remote, abstract principle; He is your Lord, the One who created you and sustains you, and He is the Lord of your forefathers, the generations that came before you.

This statement was a direct challenge to the legitimacy of Pharaoh’s claim to divinity. Pharaoh’s own father and grandfather were not considered living gods; they were mortals who had died. By invoking their forefathers, Moses exposes the novelty and absurdity of Pharaoh’s self-deification. He also appeals to whatever remnants of ancestral tradition might have acknowledged a supreme Creator. Many ancient peoples, including the Egyptians, had residual beliefs in a high god above the pantheon of lesser deities. Moses reaches back to that primordial truth. He essentially tells Pharaoh: the One I call you to is not a new god I have invented; He is the God who sustained your lineage before you ever sat on that throne, and to Him you will return. This verse reinforces the Quranic principle that the call to monotheism is a call to return to the original, uncorrupted faith that all human souls inherently recognise. For the Quraysh, who prided themselves on the religion of their forefathers, this logic was piercing. The Prophet was sent to call them back to the Lord of Abraham and Ishmael, the true God of their own ancestors, not to an alien deity. Moses thus leaves no escape: if they have any reverence for their forefathers, let them worship the One who was their true Lord.

=27=

Faced with a truth he cannot refute, Pharaoh escalates from subtle dismissal to overt personal attack. He turns again to his chiefs and describes Moses as “your messenger,” using a tone dripping with sarcasm. He does not acknowledge Moses as a genuine messenger but mockingly refers to him as the one they claim has been sent to them. The accusation of insanity is the weapon of last resort for those whose arguments have collapsed. Throughout history, prophets have been called madmen by their opponents. It is a convenient label because it relieves the hearer from having to engage with the content of the message. If the messenger is insane, his words need not be weighed; they can simply be dismissed as the ravings of a disturbed mind. Pharaoh’s goal is to isolate Moses completely and to ensure that none of his own courtiers take him seriously.

Yet, the irony is that Moses had just spoken with the utmost clarity and coherence, linking the creation of the heavens and the earth to the one true Lord and connecting it to their own ancestral history. His words were the epitome of reason. Calling a perfectly rational person insane is itself a form of insanity born of arrogance. This verse demonstrates the psychological self-defence mechanism of the hardened disbeliever: when the truth becomes undeniable, one must attack the bearer of truth to preserve one’s own false sense of security. The Prophet Muhammad was similarly called a madman and a poet possessed by jinn. This parallel reassured him and the believers that such insults are not evidence of any flaw in the messenger but hallmarks of the opposition faced by every true caller to Allah.

=28=

Moses completely ignores the insult. He does not defend his sanity, for his words are their own defence. Instead, he continues to layer attributes upon the Lord of all worlds, adding yet another dimension of His sovereignty. He is the Lord of the east and the west. This phrase encompasses all directions, all places where the sun rises and sets, across every season and every latitude. It includes not only the lands of Egypt but the lands beyond, the distant empires, the uncharted territories, and the entire expanse of the globe. By using this expression, Moses is telling Pharaoh that his dominion is a tiny speck in the vast kingdom of Allah. The rising and setting of the sun are daily, visible signs that are entirely beyond human control. No pharaoh, no matter how mighty, can command the sun to rise from the west or to alter its course. The One who governs these celestial phenomena is the true Lord.

The closing remark, “if only you had any sense,” is a pointed yet dignified challenge. The word ta‘qilûn comes from ‘aql, meaning intellect, the faculty that restrains a person from foolishness. Moses is essentially saying that if they would only use the minds that Allah gave them, they would see that a being who cannot control the sunrise cannot possibly be a god. This verse is a masterclass in da‘wah: it responds to mockery not with anger or matching insults but with further, deeper truth. It calls people to reflect, while simultaneously exposing that their rejection is not grounded in reason but in a refusal to think clearly.

=29=

Reason having failed him completely, Pharaoh now drops all pretence of dialogue and resorts to the raw exercise of power. His threat is stark and unambiguous: if Moses persists in calling to a god other than Pharaoh, the consequence will be imprisonment.

The word masjûnîn implies being cast into a dungeon, cut off from the world, silenced. This threat reveals the true nature of Pharaoh’s regime. It is not a kingdom based on consent or even on a claimed divine right; it is a tyranny that criminalises the worship of the true God. Pharaoh’s statement “besides me” is the ultimate expression of his self-deification. He does not even try to argue that Moses’ God does not exist; he simply asserts that loyalty to any other sovereign, heavenly or earthly, will not be tolerated. The demand for exclusive allegiance, which belongs to Allah alone, is here usurped by a mortal man.

This is the peak of transgression. For Moses, this threat was real and terrifying in human terms. Pharaoh had vast dungeons and a network of spies and enforcers.

Yet, the divine narrative frames this threat as a turning point that leads directly to the display of the signs. The tyrant’s ultimatum is met with a calm counter-challenge.

This verse teaches that when the dâ‘î faces the clenched fist of power, he must remember that the Lord of all worlds is with him. Imprisonment, exile, or even death cannot stop the truth. The Quraysh would later make similar threats against the Prophet Muhammad, and the believers in every age who face oppressive regimes can draw strength from Moses’ unwavering stand in the face of such intimidation.

=30=

Moses does not flinch. His response is a question that places Pharaoh in an impossible dilemma. He asks, in essence, if Pharaoh would still carry out his threat to imprison him even if he presents a clear, undeniable proof of his prophethood.

The phrase “clear proof” translates the Quranic term mubîn, the same word used to describe the Book itself in verse two. Moses is about to bring something that makes the truth plainly manifest. By framing it as a question, Moses exposes Pharaoh’s injustice. If Pharaoh says yes, he will imprison him regardless of the proof, then he admits that his rule is based on brute force rather than truth. If he says no, then he must allow the proof to be presented and be bound by its outcome.

The question also puts the courtiers on notice: what is about to happen is a test of Pharaoh’s sincerity. Moses is not asking for a debate on equal terms; he is offering to demonstrate a divine sign that would, for any sincere person, settle the matter. The calm confidence of this question is itself a sign of prophethood. A normal man facing imprisonment would plead or compromise. Moses, armed with the knowledge that Allah is with him, instead raises the stakes. He will not be silenced by threats; he will bring forth the evidence that shames tyranny. This moment teaches believers that when faced with ultimatums against their faith, they should respond not with fear but with a confident reliance on the truth they carry, trusting that Allah’s signs will vindicate them.

=31=

Pharaoh takes the bait, exactly as Allah willed. His pride and his confidence in his magicians’ ability to counter anything Moses could produce drive him to issue the challenge. He demands that Moses bring forth the proof, adding the condition “if what you say is true.” This phrase, common in the Quran when disbelievers challenge prophets, is thick with arrogance. It implies that Moses is currently a liar and that the burden of proof is on him. Pharaoh believes that by calling Moses’ bluff, he will either expose him as a fraud or watch his magicians easily replicate whatever trick he performs. The court, the chiefs, and later the gathered crowds are all watching. This public demand sets the stage for one of the greatest confrontations between truth and falsehood in human history. It is important to note that Pharaoh does not set any conditions for what constitutes an acceptable proof; he simply thinks no such thing exists. His demand, though hostile, is nevertheless the moment when the door of the unseen opens. Allah’s wisdom decreed that the signs would be shown at the tyrant’s own request, thereby establishing the maximum possible evidence against him.

This verse reminds us that arrogant challenges to divine authority never end well for the challenger. When a person mockingly says, “Show me a sign if you are truthful,” they may not realise that the sign, when it comes, will leave them without excuse. Moses will now, with the permission of his Lord, respond to the demand in the most spectacular fashion.

=32=

The moment of the sign arrives. Moses casts his wooden staff to the ground, and instantly, before the eyes of Pharaoh and his entire court, it transforms into a living, moving serpent.

The Arabic word used here is thu‘bânun mubîn, which specifically denotes a large, clearly visible snake, a genuine serpent that inspires awe and terror. The description “real” is crucial because it distinguishes this miracle from the illusions of magicians. A magician might use hollow tubes, hidden strings, or sleight of hand to make a rope appear to slither, but this staff underwent a complete physical transformation. It had scales, a forked tongue, and the ability to move and even devour. The suddenness of the transformation is captured in the word “behold,” which translates the particle fa-idhâ, used in the Quran to mark an abrupt and astonishing change of state. Moses himself was initially frightened by this sign when it was first shown to him at the sacred valley, and Allah had to reassure him. Now, in the court of Pharaoh, the same sign is displayed publicly. This miracle addressed the specific expertise of the Egyptians, who were deeply immersed in the arts of sorcery and serpent-charming. By turning a staff into a real serpent, Allah demonstrated a power that no magician could match, for creating life or altering the essence of a thing belongs to Him alone. The stage was set for the contest with the magicians, and this first sign was a direct challenge to the foundations of their craft, a harbinger that what was to come was not of their realm.

=33=

As if one sign were not enough, Moses performs the second miracle that Allah had granted him. He inserts his hand into the opening of his garment, near his collar, and when he draws it out, it radiates a brilliant, shining whiteness.

The Arabic word baidâ’ means intensely white, but without any negative connotation of disease such as leprosy. It was a dazzling, luminous light, a supernatural glow that was beautiful to behold.

The phrase “for all to see” indicates that this was not a subtle or private display; the hand was visible to everyone present, shining with a light that testified to its divine origin. This sign, like the staff, was given to Moses as a personal proof, one that he carried with him at all times, embedded in his own body. It symbolised purity, divine selection, and the light of revelation. For the Egyptians, whose magicians relied on external objects and incantations, a sign emanating from the very flesh of a man was something completely outside their paradigm. The two signs together—the staff that became a living creature and the hand that became a source of light—encompassed both the external world and the human self. They demonstrated that the Lord of all worlds has power over matter, life, and the human body.

Yet, as the next verse reveals, Pharaoh’s response is not to submit but to reinterpret these clear signs through the only lens his pride would allow: magic. The believer is reminded that even the most obvious miracle will not convince a heart that is determined to reject, unless Allah opens that heart.

=34=

Witnessing the undeniable transformation of the staff and the radiant hand, Pharaoh’s mind immediately searches for a way to neutralise their impact. He cannot deny what his eyes have seen, so he must redefine it. He turns to his chiefs and labels Moses a skilled magician.

The word ‘alîm in this context means exceptionally knowledgeable, a master of the craft. By framing Moses’ signs as high-level magic, Pharaoh attempts to strip them of their divine significance and fit them into a category his court already understood and, he believed, could counter.

This is the classic response of the arrogant when confronted with the miraculous: they call it sorcery, trickery, or advanced technology. It allows them to maintain their worldview without the discomfort of admitting that they have witnessed the power of the Creator. Pharaoh’s statement also serves a political purpose. He is rallying his inner circle, preventing any of them from being swayed. If the chiefs accept that Moses is merely a magician, then the solution is simple: find better magicians. This interpretation sets the entire apparatus of the state against Moses, not as a prophet to be heard but as a rival sorcerer to be defeated in a public contest.

The verse reveals the psychology of disbelief. The evidence could not be clearer, yet the filter of pride and vested interest transforms a serpent into an illusion. The Prophet Muhammad would hear the same accusation: the eloquence and truth of the Quran were called magic. This parallel comforted him, for it showed that he was walking the well-trodden path of the prophets, facing the identical arsenal of false labels.

=35=

Pharaoh adds another layer to his accusation, one designed to stir fear and nationalistic fervour. He claims that Moses’ magic is not merely a display of skill but part of a calculated plot to drive the Egyptians out of their own land. By this, he paints Moses as a political subversive, an agent of chaos whose religious message is a cover for territorial ambitions. The idea of being exiled from one’s homeland is a primal fear, and Pharaoh exploits it masterfully. He shifts the conversation from theological truth to national security. Suddenly, the issue is not whether there is one Lord of all worlds, but whether the Egyptians will lose their property, their status, and their country to the Israelites. This tactic is used by tyrants in every age: when they cannot win an argument on principle, they manufacture a threat to the people’s identity and safety, uniting them against a common enemy. After sowing this fear, Pharaoh asks his council, “So what do you propose? ” This consultation is a calculated move. It gives the chiefs a sense of ownership in the decision and ensures collective responsibility for whatever is done next. It also tests their loyalty and forces them to step forward with a plan that matches the gravity of the “threat” he has described. The chiefs’ response, as we will see, is to propose the gathering of all the land’s greatest magicians. The entire exchange shows how the apparatus of a corrupt state mobilises to protect falsehood: by redefining truth as sorcery, the prophet as a foreign agent, and the conflict as a battle for survival.

=36=

The chiefs, now fully aligned with Pharaoh’s narrative, offer their counsel. They advise a strategy of postponement combined with a nationwide recruitment drive. “Let him and his brother wait” means that they should not immediately move against Moses and Aaron in a way that might appear hasty or fearful. Instead, they will buy time.

The word “wait” implies that Moses and Aaron will be kept under watch but not yet imprisoned or harmed, while the state prepares its ultimate response. The second part of the plan is to dispatch mobilizers, essentially heralds or recruiters, to every city in the land. These messengers would travel throughout Egypt with one mission: to find and bring back every skilled magician. This shows the high level of organisation in Pharaoh’s kingdom and the seriousness with which they viewed the challenge. They were not content with the court magicians alone; they wanted to gather the absolute best from every corner of the realm. This also indicates that the practice of magic was widespread and institutionalised. The chiefs believed that a contest of magic was the ideal arena to discredit Moses, because it was a field in which Egypt excelled. They would overwhelm him with a vast assembly of experts. The plan was designed not only to defeat Moses but to turn the defeat into a grand spectacle that would reaffirm Pharaoh’s authority and discourage any future dissent. Unbeknownst to them, their elaborate planning was merely setting the stage for an even greater display of Allah’s power.

=37=

The objective of the mobilizers is clarified: they are to bring to Pharaoh every skilled magician. The word kull sahharin ‘alîm, every knowing magician, indicates that no one of notable expertise was to be left out. The plan was to assemble an army of sorcerers, the very best the kingdom could produce. This reveals the pride of Egypt in its occult arts. Magic was not a fringe superstition in Pharaoh’s court; it was a respected craft, deeply intertwined with the religious and political establishment. By gathering all the top magicians, they aimed to present an unassailable force. Quantity and reputation were meant to guarantee victory. There is a tragic irony here: the more magicians they gathered, the greater the scale of the miracle that would defeat them, and the more witnesses there would be to the truth. Allah’s plan was working through their own schemes.

The verse also speaks to a universal truth about falsehood. It often gathers its resources, its experts, and its institutions, believing that sheer numbers and worldly skill can overcome the truth.

Yet, as the Prophet Muhammad was told, if all of mankind and jinn gathered to produce something like the Quran, they could not. The mobilisation of the magicians was a display of worldly power, but it was powerless against a single sign from the Lord of the heavens and the earth.

For the believer, this is a reminder never to be intimidated by the assembled might of falsehood, for the outcome is not determined by numbers but by the One who controls all things.

=38=

The narrative compresses time, indicating that Pharaoh’s orders were carried out efficiently. The magicians from all over Egypt were gathered together.

The phrase “at the set time on the appointed day” highlights that this was a highly organised event. A specific date and hour were fixed, likely chosen to coincide with a major festival or public gathering, as mentioned in other surahs, so that the maximum number of people could witness the spectacle. The day was a “day of adornment,” a holiday when people would be free from work and ready to assemble. The gathering was not a quiet, private test; it was a deliberate public showdown designed to humiliate Moses before the entire nation. The magicians, no doubt, arrived with their ropes, staffs, and paraphernalia, confident in their craft and eager for the promised reward from Pharaoh. The atmosphere would have been one of intense anticipation. Pharaoh’s propaganda machine had been at work, and the people were expecting a definitive victory for the state and its gods. This moment is a reflection of how the forces of disbelief often seek to turn the contest between truth and falsehood into a theatrical display. They believe that by controlling the stage, the timing, and the audience, they control the outcome. But as the following verses will show, the One who controls the true stage—the entire earth and the heavens—had other plans. The appointed day would not be the day of Moses’ defeat but the day of the magicians’ own unexpected transformation, a day they would fall in prostration to the Lord of all worlds.

=39=

The scale of the event is further emphasised. The people, meaning the general populace, were actively summoned and encouraged to attend.

The question “Will you join the gathering?” was likely proclaimed by heralds throughout the city. It was an invitation but also a call to national unity. The phrasing turns the contest into a communal event where everyone’s presence was expected. The underlying message was: come and witness how the state deals with those who challenge our gods and our way of life. By framing it as a gathering for all, Pharaoh’s regime aimed to create a massive public consensus against Moses. In the psychology of crowds, people are often swayed by the spectacle and the collective emotion. A public defeat of the prophet would make it socially and politically impossible for anyone to take his message seriously thereafter.

The verse shows how tyrants use mass media—in their context, public proclamations and festivals—to manufacture consent and marginalise the voice of truth. Yet, from the divine perspective, this very mass gathering was the means by which the truth would be broadcast most widely. The more people who came, the more witnesses there would be to the miracle that was about to unfold. The very instruments that Pharaoh used to solidify his power were being arranged by Allah to magnify the impact of His sign. This teaches believers that they need not fear the large platforms of falsehood; when Allah wills, those platforms become the stage for His truth.

=40=

The verse reveals the stated purpose of the public gathering and the hope of the people. They were being called to attend so that, when the magicians proved victorious, they would all collectively follow them.

The word “follow” implies adherence to their way, their religion, and their interpretation of reality. In the Egyptian context, the magicians were seen as the custodians of the established religious order. If they defeated Moses, it would prove, in the eyes of the masses, that their gods and their arts were superior, and that Moses was indeed an impostor. The conditional “if they prevail” shows that, despite their overwhelming confidence, they still framed it as a possibility, but one they fully expected to materialise. The entire event was a calculated exercise in reaffirming the status quo. The people were to be spectators who, at the end, would throw their support behind the winners. There was no genuine openness to the possibility that Moses might be truthful; the outcome was presupposed. This mentality is a hallmark of a society that has vested its identity in falsehood. It does not say, “Let us see which side presents the truth and follow that.” It says, “Let us follow the side we already believe in, once they confirm our bias. ” Allah exposes this mindset so that the readers of the Quran can recognise it in themselves and in the societies around them. The believer is called to follow the truth wherever it leads, not to cheer for a predetermined victor. The magicians, however, will confound everyone, for they will witness a truth that shatters their presuppositions, and their response will be the very opposite of what the crowd expected.
