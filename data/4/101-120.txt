=101=

“When you travel through the land, there is no blame upon you for shortening the prayer if you fear that the disbelievers may attack you. Surely the disbelievers are to you a clear enemy.”

The traveller does not leave prayer behind. Instead, Allah makes its form lighter. Qasr shortens the four-unit obligatory prayers—Dhuhr, Asr, and Isha—to two units under the conditions explained by the Sunnah and Islamic law. Fajr remains two and Maghrib three.

The verse mentions fear because the first journeys often passed through hostile territory, but the Prophet, peace be upon him, continued shortening prayer during safe travel. When Umar asked why the concession remained after security, the Prophet described it as a charity Allah gave and told believers to accept His charity. The established schools differ over details such as minimum travel distance, duration of stay, and whether shortening is recommended or obligatory, but agree that legitimate travel brings a real concession.

“Through the land” reminds the traveller that movement itself creates disruption: schedules change, resources become uncertain, and fatigue grows. Islamic worship is firm without being brittle. The same surah allowed tayammum when water is unavailable or harmful; now it preserves salah through shortened form.

The enemy is “clear” in this setting because armed opponents could exploit the worshippers’ concentration. Yet fear must not become a general assumption that every non-Muslim traveller or neighbour is an enemy. The verse addresses belligerents in a conflict, while 60:8 commands kindness and justice toward those who do not fight or expel believers.

Modern travel by aircraft or fast transport does not automatically remove the concession. Ease of vehicle is only one factor; the revealed legal cause is recognised travel. A believer should learn how to determine prayer times, direction, purification, and combining prayers where valid before beginning a journey.

The phrase “no blame” removes the fear that accepting ease reflects weak devotion. Refusing a concession from pride can misunderstand the Giver. The purpose is not to escape prayer but to remain attached to it on the road.

Verse 102 now addresses a more dangerous setting: congregational prayer in the presence of an enemy. Even there, believers pray in organised groups while carrying arms and maintaining watch.
=102=

“When you, O Prophet, are among them and lead them in prayer, let one group stand in prayer with you while carrying their weapons. After they have prostrated, let them take position behind you, and let the other group that has not yet prayed come and pray with you, taking their precautions and weapons. The disbelievers wish that you would neglect your weapons and equipment so they could assault you all at once. There is no blame if you lay down your weapons when troubled by rain or illness, but remain vigilant. Surely Allah has prepared a humiliating punishment for the disbelievers.”

This is salat al-khawf, prayer under active threat. Worship is neither abandoned nor performed carelessly. The community divides: one group prays while another guards, then they change. Authentic reports describe several forms the Prophet used according to battlefield conditions, showing flexibility around the verse’s purpose.

Weapons are carried because the enemy hopes for one moment of collective inattention. Salah is reliance on Allah, but reliance does not mean presenting the enemy an avoidable opening. The verse repeats the command to take precautions from 4:71 and demonstrates it inside worship itself.

Details of fear prayer belong to organised communal defence under lawful authority, not private militancy. Where danger is extreme, the Quran elsewhere permits prayer on foot or riding (2:239). Jurists derive forms according to actual ability, always preserving the prayer as far as possible.

The concession regarding rain and illness is precise. Wet conditions can make equipment burdensome, and sickness can make carrying weapons harmful. Arms may be set down, but vigilance remains. Removing one means does not mean removing all caution. This balance applies broadly to risk: accommodation for weakness should be real without pretending the danger has vanished.

The verse reveals an enemy tactic without authorising indiscriminate suspicion. The combatants planning an assault are the subject. Non-combatants, treaty partners, and those offering peace were protected in verses 90-91. Quranic war ethics must be read as one structure.

Prayer in this scene preserves moral identity. Soldiers remain servants standing in rows before Allah; force does not make them autonomous. Their Lord sees intention and conduct and will judge any abuse committed under the banner of defence.

The humiliating punishment belongs to Allah. Believers may resist lawful enemies, but they may not imitate divine punishment through torture or excess. Having prayed amid danger, they are told in verse 103 to continue remembering Allah in every bodily position and to return to the complete regular prayer when safety returns.
=103=

“When you have completed the prayer, remember Allah while standing, sitting, and lying on your sides. When you are secure again, establish the prayer in full. Indeed, prayer is prescribed for believers at appointed times.”

The fear prayer ends, but remembrance continues. Standing, sitting, and lying cover the human body’s ordinary states. Dhikr is therefore not confined to a mosque or one posture. After danger, adrenaline, and organised defence, the tongue and heart remain connected to Allah.

This echoes the people of understanding in 3:191, who remember Allah standing, sitting, and on their sides while reflecting on creation. A believer’s circumstances change; remembrance travels through them. Words such as tasbih, tahmid, takbir, istighfar, and Quranic supplication can steady a heart after fear.

“When secure, establish prayer” means return from emergency arrangements to its normal complete form. Concessions follow their causes. Ease is not carelessness, and caution is not a permanent excuse once safety returns. The believer accepts both the shortened form in hardship and the full form in stability from the same Lawgiver.

Kitaban mawquta describes prayer as a written obligation tied to fixed times. Salah is not performed whenever spirituality happens to feel convenient. Dawn, midday, afternoon, sunset, and night place appointments with Allah across the day. The Sunnah explains the beginning and end of each time and allowances for combining in recognised circumstances.

Fixed times create discipline but also mercy. A prayer has a window rather than one impossible instant. Travellers, patients, workers, caregivers, and students can plan within those windows and use lawful concessions. Neglecting prayer because life is busy reverses the verse: prayer is meant to structure life.

Danger did not erase the obligation, which shows its centrality. Yet the altered battlefield form shows Allah’s knowledge of capacity. Communities should help people pray through safe spaces, schedules, accessible washing, and informed guidance rather than adding needless obstacles.

After worship under fire, believers are told not to become weak in pursuing the hostile force. Both sides experience pain, but believers possess a hope from Allah their opponents do not share. Prayer and remembrance become strength for responsible action, not retreat from it.
=104=

“Do not weaken in pursuing the hostile people. If you are suffering, they too are suffering as you are, but you hope from Allah what they cannot hope for. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

The verse addresses a community wounded by conflict yet still responsible for security. Pain can create the illusion that only one side is tired and that the enemy’s strength is untouched. Allah corrects that perception: they suffer too. Comparable hardship does not produce comparable hope.

Believers hope for Allah’s pleasure, forgiveness, reward, and support. That hope does not make wounds imaginary or grieving sinful. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was injured, companions were killed, and families mourned. Courage is not absence of pain; it is refusal to let pain erase purpose.

“Do not weaken” is not an endless command to pursue anyone labelled an opponent. The qawm are the belligerents in the passage, and pursuit belongs to legitimate leadership, strategy, treaties, and the limits governing warfare. If peace is established or authority orders withdrawal, personal vengeance cannot continue under this verse.

The comparison also prevents romanticising the enemy as superhuman. Propaganda can magnify opposing power and conceal its costs. Believers verify reality, take precautions, and remember that every human force has limits. Their additional resource is not fantasy but hope in the Lord who knows outcomes.

Hope must remain ethically disciplined. A person cannot commit atrocities and call the expectation of victory “hope from Allah.” Divine support is sought through obedience, patience, justice, consultation, and lawful means. Wrongdoing can turn apparent victory into spiritual defeat.

Al-‘Alim knows who truly suffers and what each side intends. Al-Hakim commands action at the proper time and places reward wisely. Leaders who treat people as expendable cannot hide behind inspirational language before these names.

Beyond combat, the verse helps anyone tired in a just struggle. Others also bear costs, but the believer expects meaning and reward beyond what the visible result supplies. Still, the immediate context should not be erased into metaphor.

The surah now moves from battlefield judgment to courtroom judgment. The Book was revealed in truth so the Prophet could decide between people by what Allah showed him—without becoming an advocate for the treacherous merely because they claimed membership in his community.
=105=

“Indeed, We have revealed to you the Book in truth so that you may judge between people according to what Allah has shown you. And do not be an advocate for the treacherous.”

Reports connect this passage with a theft involving a man from the Banu Ubayriq, often identified as Tu‘mah ibn Ubayriq. Stolen armour was hidden and suspicion directed toward an innocent person, while the offender’s relatives defended him and appealed to the Prophet, peace be upon him. Revelation prevented tribal loyalty and persuasive argument from turning falsehood into judgment.

The Book came bil-haqq: carrying truth and sent for a real purpose. Judgment is “between people,” not only between Muslims. The innocent person in the reported incident was said in many accounts to be Jewish. Justice does not ask whether the wrongly accused belongs to the judge’s religious group before protecting him.

“According to what Allah has shown you” joins revelation to careful adjudication. The Prophet considered evidence presented to him, while Allah corrected what hidden facts could have made appear convincing. Ordinary judges receive no revelation and must use testimony, documents, expertise, due process, and legal standards with humility about unseen reality.

An advocate is not condemned for representing someone accused. Justice requires defence and fair hearing. The prohibition is knowingly becoming khasim for betrayal—using skill, influence, or selective facts to secure an unjust result. A lawyer may test evidence and insist on rights without lying, hiding unlawfully, or helping a client continue fraud.

The verse places principle above communal reputation. Some may have thought exposing a Muslim thief would embarrass the believers while blaming an outsider protected the group. Revelation teaches the opposite: a community is honoured by correcting its own, not by sacrificing an innocent person to appearances.

The Prophet’s protection from endorsing the false claim demonstrates divine care for his judicial mission. It does not teach that every leader who quotes scripture shares his protection. Institutions require accountability precisely because later judges can err.

The next verse instructs the Prophet to seek Allah’s forgiveness. Whether this concerns his initial inclination based on the apparent evidence or teaches continued humility, even the protected Messenger is directed toward istighfar before the Forgiving, Merciful Lord.
=106=

“And seek Allah’s forgiveness. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The command to the Prophet, peace be upon him, follows the warning not to advocate for traitors. He had not knowingly endorsed injustice; he was judging from testimony and argument available before revelation clarified the matter. Yet Allah trains him and the community in immediate return whenever judgment approaches error.

Prophetic istighfar does not imply betrayal or major sin. The prophets are protected in conveying revelation, and Allah corrects their human judgments so error is not established as religion. Their frequent seeking of forgiveness displays perfect servanthood, gratitude, humility, and awareness of Allah’s greatness.

The Prophet sought forgiveness many times each day despite his honoured status. If he continually returned, judges, scholars, parents, and leaders have even greater need. Authority can make admitting error feel costly; istighfar breaks pride before the error hardens into policy.

Seeking forgiveness should produce correction. If an innocent person was accused, his name must be cleared. If property was taken, it must be returned. Saying astaghfirullah while preserving the benefit of injustice is not the complete response the passage teaches.

The names al-Ghafur and al-Rahim make correction possible. Fear of losing reputation often drives cover-ups. Believing that Allah forgives allows a person to admit, repair, and grow. Mercy is not protection of the mistake but rescue from persisting in it.

The verse also guards people from despair over honest misjudgment. Human beings can be deceived despite reasonable care. Responsibility lies in using sound process, staying open to new evidence, and correcting promptly. Allah knows the difference between a sincere mistake and intentional defence of treachery.

Istighfar after decisions can become a healthy practice: ask whether bias, haste, or missing facts affected the outcome, then invite review where needed. Spiritual humility and procedural accountability support one another.

Verse 107 returns to the underlying case and broadens it: do not argue for people who betray themselves. Treachery first damages the traitor’s own soul, and Allah does not love one who persists in betrayal and sin.
=107=

“Do not argue on behalf of those who betray themselves. Surely Allah does not love anyone who is habitually treacherous and sinful.”

The thief betrayed the victim, the community, and trust, yet Allah says such people betray “themselves.” Every sin places its deepest wound in the sinner’s own record. The stolen item may leave the victim’s hand, but guilt settles upon the one who chose it.

The plural broadens the ruling beyond one incident. Family members, lawyers, officials, friends, and religious leaders must not turn loyalty into a campaign for known falsehood. Defence of a person’s lawful rights differs from defence of the betrayal itself. Justice can insist on due process while refusing lies.

Yukhtanun suggests repeated or concealed betrayal. The closing description khawwanan athima intensifies both treachery and sin. Allah does not love that settled character. A person who falls once and repents is not being told that reform is impossible; persistence and protective deception are the danger.

Communities sometimes imagine solidarity requires denying internal wrongdoing. Victims are pressured into silence because exposure might “harm the cause.” The verse reverses that logic. Shielding betrayal harms the cause before Allah, while fair accountability may purify it.

This applies to financial theft, academic cheating, abuse, forged credentials, and institutional cover-ups. One should verify accusations carefully, as verse 94 demanded, but once evidence is clear, personal closeness cannot make deception innocent. Neither factional condemnation without process nor factional defence against evidence is Quranic justice.

The phrase “Allah does not love” reaches beyond legal consequence. A person may escape court and still lose the greatest good: divine love. Repentance seeks not merely reduced penalty but restored relationship with Allah.

An advocate should also avoid becoming the next traitor. Selectively hiding an entrusted fact, coaching a false story, or attacking an innocent accuser can add fresh sins to the original one. Influence carries a share, as verse 85 taught.

Why do people attempt such strategies? They fear exposure before people more than presence before Allah. Verse 108 exposes this irrational calculation: they hide from human beings but cannot hide from Him, who is with them while they plan words He does not approve.
=108=

“They hide their deeds from people, but they cannot hide them from Allah. He is with them when they spend the night plotting words that displease Him. And Allah fully encompasses everything they do.”

Shame before people can be healthy if it restrains wrongdoing, but here it has become distorted. The offenders use secrecy to preserve reputation while continuing the act. Human eyes become more real to them than Allah’s knowledge.

Again the night appears as the setting of concealed planning, just as in verse 81. Darkness offers practical privacy, never metaphysical privacy. Allah is “with them” through His complete knowledge, hearing, sight, and power. This does not mean His essence is contained in the room; nothing in creation contains the Creator.

They plan qawl—an account, testimony, accusation, or defence—that He does not approve. Words can become organised injustice. A false narrative may be rehearsed until every participant tells the same story, but coordination does not make it true.

Allah is Muhit, encompassing their action from every side. He knows the stolen object, the first suggestion to blame an innocent person, who agreed reluctantly, who led the cover-up, and every consequence. Human investigators reconstruct fragments; Allah possesses the whole.

Modern secrecy uses passwords, disappearing messages, offshore structures, and private groups. Security and confidentiality can be lawful, but no technology changes the verse. Before writing what one would fear to see in the divine record, a believer should remember who is present through knowledge.

The solution is not public self-exposure for every private sin. Islam encourages a person whom Allah has concealed to repent rather than advertise wrongdoing. But concealment cannot be used to deny another’s rights or continue harm. Where others were injured, safe and lawful accountability is part of repentance.

The verse also comforts the falsely accused. Even when a coordinated story convinces the public, Allah encompasses what happened. That knowledge does not remove the need to seek justice, but it ensures truth is not metaphysically lost.

Verse 109 asks the defenders to consider the final court. They may argue successfully in worldly life, but who will argue with Allah for the traitors on Resurrection Day, and who could become their protecting trustee?
=109=

“Here you are, arguing on their behalf in the life of this world. But who will argue with Allah for them on the Day of Resurrection, or who will be their defender?”

Worldly advocacy can succeed because judges hear limited evidence. The Prophet warned that disputants may present their cases with unequal eloquence; if he awards someone a right belonging to another based on what he hears, the recipient is taking a piece of Fire. A favourable verdict cannot transform false property into lawful property before Allah.

The rhetorical questions leave no candidate. No advocate can confuse the One who witnessed the event and knows every intention. No wakil can manage affairs independently against His judgment. Relationships that seemed powerful in the world dissolve at the point where only truth matters.

The verse addresses the defenders, not only the original offender. “Here you are” makes them see their present position. They may not have stolen anything, but advocacy has joined them to the cause. Before continuing, they should imagine standing beside the same client when the opposing Judge is Allah.

This does not demean the legal profession or prohibit intercession. Verse 85 praised good intercession. Defending due process, preventing torture, and ensuring evidence is tested are good works even for a person accused of serious wrong. The sin is knowingly arguing that betrayal is innocence or shifting guilt onto someone blameless.

Families face this question when protecting an abusive relative, organisations when hiding financial misconduct, and political groups when excusing their own side. Short-term reputation is purchased with a case that reopens on Resurrection Day.

The safest advocacy sometimes consists of telling the wrongdoer the truth, helping him return property, and supporting sincere repentance rather than helping him “win.” Losing a false case in this world may save him from losing the final one.

The passage could end in exposure and fear, but verse 110 opens mercy immediately. Whoever commits evil or wrongs himself and then seeks Allah’s forgiveness will find Him Forgiving and Merciful. The door is open to the thief, defenders, and every reader willing to stop and return.
=110=

“Whoever commits evil or wrongs themselves and then seeks Allah’s forgiveness will find Allah All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The invitation is broad: whoever. Su’ can be harm done outwardly, while wronging oneself includes every sin’s inward consequence. The distinction ensures neither interpersonal wrongdoing nor private disobedience is excluded from hope.

“Then seeks forgiveness” marks a turn. Istighfar is not a phrase used to continue safely in the same conduct. It includes recognition, regret, abandonment, resolve, and—where people’s rights are involved—restitution or a sincere attempt to secure pardon. The theft narrative makes that practical meaning impossible to ignore.

The promise says the repentant person “will find” Allah Forgiving and Merciful. One may expect disgust, permanent identity as a traitor, or rejection after exposure, yet the Lord identifies what the returning servant will encounter. His forgiveness covers, and His mercy restores and guides.

This verse should protect communities from defining people forever by a past sin after genuine reform. Accountability can remain, trust may need rebuilding, and legal consequences may continue, but repentance changes a person’s standing with Allah. Humans should neither guarantee acceptance nor close a door He opened.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that all children of Adam sin and the best sinners are those who repent. A believer’s superiority is not never needing forgiveness but repeatedly returning before the heart hardens.

Delay is dangerous. The sequence says evil, then forgiveness—not evil, years of justification, and perhaps repentance at death. Verse 18 already warned against waiting until the final moment.

Victims should not be forced to reconcile because the offender says he repented. Allah’s forgiveness and a victim’s legal or emotional process are related but not identical. Safety and rights remain valid. The offender proves sincerity by accepting that reality without manipulation.

Verse 111 now states individual responsibility from another angle: whoever earns sin earns it only against himself. Blaming the innocent does not transfer guilt; defending the guilty does not remove it.
=111=

“Whoever earns a sin earns it only against themselves. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

Kasaba presents sin as an acquisition. The action may appear to gain money, pleasure, status, or escape, but what the person truly adds to his account is ithm. The visible profit can hide the deeper purchase.

“No bearer of burdens will bear the burden of another” is a repeated Quranic principle (6:164). Family, tribe, leader, and follower do not merge into one moral person. Each has a share according to choice. This makes inherited guilt and collective punishment unjust, while still allowing responsibility for helping another’s sin.

The offender in the surrounding story tried to place suspicion on someone else. Human accusation can shift social blame, but it cannot shift divine ownership of the deed. A lie creates an additional sin for the liar; it does not remove the original one.

The principle also protects a person from unhealthy shame over another adult’s conduct. Parents, spouses, or communities may feel responsible for everything someone close chooses. They must examine any real negligence, but they do not automatically carry an independent person’s sin. Guidance and control are not the same.

Conversely, “only against himself” does not deny harm to victims. Theft harms the owner and murder harms a family. It means the moral burden and final ruin return to the sinner; Allah is not diminished, and the victim does not inherit the offender’s guilt.

Al-‘Alim knows exactly who earned what, including coercion, assistance, repentance, and concealed motive. Al-Hakim assigns consequence with wisdom. Human records may confuse roles, but His judgment never does.

A person should therefore stop investing in sin simply because immediate returns appear good. Repentance is a divestment from an account whose final balance is loss.

Verse 112 describes an even worse attempt to transfer blame: someone earns a fault or sin and throws it upon an innocent person. He then carries both a shocking slander and a manifest sin.
=112=

“Whoever earns a fault or sin and then blames it on an innocent person has burdened themselves with a monstrous slander and a blatant sin.”

Khati’ah can indicate a fault or offence, while ithm emphasises sinful guilt. Whatever the original category, accusing a bari’—someone free of it—adds a new and grave wrong. The guilty person tried to unload blame but instead “carried” more.

Buhtan is a lie so shocking that it leaves the innocent person stunned. It attacks honour, safety, relationships, and sometimes freedom. The Quran used the same word in verse 20 for false allegations designed to recover a woman’s dowry. Slander often serves material interests.

False accusation can be explicit or manufactured through planted evidence, selective editing, coordinated testimony, or insinuation designed to let audiences draw the intended conclusion. Digital media makes the “throwing” image vivid: one click can cast guilt onto a person before verification catches up.

Repentance requires more than deleting the post or privately regretting the statement. The accuser should correct the record among those reached, restore resulting losses where possible, and seek the victim’s pardon without pressuring them. A lie travelled publicly; repair cannot remain conveniently private.

Listeners carry responsibility too. Verse 94 commanded verification, and 24:15 condemns passing accusations from tongue to tongue without knowledge. Refusing to forward uncertain claims protects both the innocent and the listener’s own account.

The verse does not prevent reporting credible abuse or crime. Protecting victims may require disclosure to responsible people. The duty is truth, evidence appropriate to the setting, confidentiality where needed, and avoidance of knowingly accusing the innocent. Fear of “slander” must not be weaponised to silence genuine complaints.

The one who framed the innocent in the revelation’s context nearly influenced judgment. Verse 113 explains that Allah’s favour, revelation, and teaching protected the Prophet from the group that intended to mislead him. Their plot ultimately returned against themselves.
=113=

“Had Allah’s favour and mercy not been upon you, a group of them would have tried to mislead you. But they mislead none but themselves, and they cannot harm you at all. Allah has revealed to you the Book and wisdom and taught you what you did not know. Allah’s favour upon you is truly immense.”

The group’s presentation was persuasive enough to create a risk at the level of outward evidence. Allah’s favour intervened through revelation, protecting the Prophet, peace be upon him, from establishing their deception as judgment. Their attempt therefore became proof of the very truth they opposed.

“They mislead only themselves” does not mean their plot could not hurt an innocent person socially. It means its deepest and final consequence returned to them, while Allah protected the Messenger’s mission. Schemes against revelation cannot make falsehood part of the religion.

The verse is evidence of prophetic protection in conveying and applying divine guidance. It also preserves his humanity: he judged evidence, did not independently know the unseen, and needed what Allah taught. Calling him Messenger does not make him a deity; the immense favour is explicitly from Allah.

The Book and hikmah are named together. The Book is the Quran; wisdom includes the prophetic understanding and Sunnah that teach its living application. Prayer, adjudication, character, and public life were not left to text without an authorised teacher.

“And taught you what you did not know” is honour and humility at once. The Prophet received knowledge no human study could reach, yet it remained taught knowledge. Every scholar after him should be even quicker to acknowledge dependence and limits.

The favour was immense because it protected an innocent person, corrected a community, preserved judicial integrity, and gave future generations principles against tribal defence and false accusation. Revelation’s corrections are mercy, not embarrassment.

A believer should welcome evidence that interrupts a preferred story. Defensiveness asks how to protect the group; gratitude asks how Allah is protecting the group from injustice.

The passage now examines private conversation. Much secret counsel carries no good, especially after the conspiracy just described. Exceptions are counsel that directs charity, recognised goodness, or reconciliation—and even those acts reach immense reward only when seeking Allah’s pleasure.
=114=

“There is no good in much of their private conversation, except when one encourages charity, what is right, or reconciliation between people. Whoever does this seeking Allah’s pleasure, We will grant them an immense reward.”

Najwa is confidential conversation. Privacy itself is not condemned; the content and purpose determine its value. In the surrounding case, private counsel coordinated betrayal. The verse says much secret talk lacks good because secrecy easily feeds gossip, faction, vanity, and plans speakers would not defend openly.

Three exceptions turn privacy into service. Charity may need discreet organisation to preserve a recipient’s dignity. Ma‘ruf includes every recognised good: preventing harm, arranging care, advising someone privately, or planning beneficial work. Reconciliation often requires confidential conversations so each side can speak honestly without public escalation.

Even an outwardly good topic needs the right intention. Seeking mardat Allah—Allah’s pleasure—distinguishes service from control, reputation, or gathering secrets. A mediator can speak about reconciliation while enjoying influence; a fundraiser can organise charity for personal branding. The promised immense reward belongs to sincerity joined to good action.

The verse offers a test for private chats and closed meetings: if the messages became public, would they reveal charity, good, and repair, or mockery, suspicion, and division? Confidentiality may still be necessary, but the heart should be able to place the conversation before Allah.

This does not require inviting outsiders into personal or professional secrets. Medical, legal, marital, and security privacy can be an amanah. “No good” targets idle or harmful counsel, not responsible confidentiality.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that private conversation excluding a third person should be avoided when it saddens them. Even harmless content can wound through the way secrecy is performed. Islamic manners protect belonging as well as information.

Reconciliation must not conceal abuse or erase rights. Sulh is good when just; pressure placed on a victim to protect an institution is another secret conspiracy. The Quran’s earlier requirement to judge with justice remains.

Verse 115 contrasts good counsel seeking Allah’s pleasure with conscious opposition to the Messenger and departure from the believers’ path. Once guidance is clear, choosing another road has grave consequences.
=115=

“Whoever opposes the Messenger after guidance has become clear to them and follows a path other than that of the believers, We will leave them to what they have chosen and burn them in Hell—what an evil destination!”

Yushaqiq means placing oneself on the opposite side, creating a split against the Messenger. The warning concerns opposition after guidance has become clear, not every question, misunderstanding, or mistake made before evidence. Knowledge increases responsibility.

The Messenger’s path and “the path of the believers” are joined. The first believing community learned directly from him and embodied revelation collectively. Scholars cite this verse among evidence for the authority of genuine consensus: the ummah cannot knowingly agree upon a path contrary to revealed guidance. Consensus is a technical conclusion established by qualified scholarship, not whatever is popular online or common in one culture.

“We leave them to what they chose” is a frightening form of punishment. A person insists on a direction until guidance is no longer felt as invitation, and Allah allows the chosen attachment to govern him. Getting what desire demanded can be worse than an immediate obstacle.

The verse does not authorise persecution of ordinary disagreement. Islamic jurisprudence contains valid differences because evidence, language, and circumstances can be understood through more than one sound route. Following a recognised interpretation is not automatically splitting from the Messenger. The threat is deliberate rejection of clear guidance and abandonment of the believers’ way.

Nor should factions define themselves as “the believers” and use the phrase to condemn everyone outside their organisation. The path is measured through Quran, authentic Sunnah, and the inherited body of the ummah—not loyalty to a modern group.

Hell is the end because separation from the Messenger’s guidance separates a person from the road to Allah. Yet before death, someone who recognises the error may repent and return. The warning describes persistence, not a locked door for the living.

Verse 116 now repeats the surah’s foundational warning: Allah does not forgive death upon unrepented shirk, though everything below it remains under His will. Opposing the Messenger reaches its furthest form when worship itself is directed away from the One who sent him.
=116=

“Surely Allah does not forgive associating partners with Him, but He forgives anything less than that for whomever He wills. Whoever associates partners with Allah has certainly strayed far away.”

This repeats the principle stated in verse 48, now after warnings about opposing the Messenger. Repetition marks shirk as the decisive danger beneath many deviations. Worship belongs to Allah alone, and no prophet, angel, saint, idol, spirit, ruler, or desire shares His divine right.

The verse concerns someone who dies upon shirk without repentance. It does not close forgiveness during life. The first Muslims included former idolaters whose sincere faith erased the past. Allah says that those who repent, believe, and act rightly can have evil deeds transformed into good (25:70).

Everything below shirk is subject to Allah’s will. He may forgive through mercy, repentance, trials, accepted deeds, or intercession He permits; He may punish with perfect justice. A major sinner cannot presume safety, but another Muslim cannot declare that Allah must reject him.

“Strayed far” describes distance from the purpose of creation and the straight path requested in prayer. Shirk may begin with an apparently small exaggeration—granting a created being unseen powers, directing supplication as worship, or obeying someone as if entitled to override Allah absolutely. Tawhid protects dignity by freeing the servant from worship of servants.

Clear monotheism does not permit injustice toward people of other faiths. The same revelation commands covenant, fair judgment, and kindness to peaceful neighbours. One can reject a belief without attacking the safety or dignity of its adherents.

A believer should also fear subtle riya, dependence, and self-worship without recklessly declaring them equal to major shirk. The Prophet called showing off “minor shirk” to awaken hearts, while legal judgments distinguish levels carefully.

The next verses expose pagan worship beneath its symbols. The idols bore feminine names, but those invoking them were in reality responding to a rebellious Satan who vowed to claim a portion of Allah’s servants.
=117=

“They invoke none besides Him but female-named deities, and in reality they invoke none but a rebellious Satan—”

Many Arabian idols had grammatically feminine names, including al-Lat, al-‘Uzza, and Manat, and were imagined as daughters of Allah. The Quran exposes the contradiction: people attributed daughters to Allah while many of them disliked daughters for themselves (16:57-59). Their theology combined falsehood with social hypocrisy.

The wording is not a condemnation of women or femininity. Maryam is honoured above the women of the worlds, mothers receive immense rights, and believing women share full moral reward. The target is lifeless idols given invented divine identities, not the sex Allah created.

Calling upon the idols is described as calling upon a rebellious Satan because Satan invited and decorated the worship. The stone itself neither hears nor plans; the spiritual direction behind turning worship from Allah serves his rebellion. Not every idolater consciously says “I worship Satan,” but following his invitation produces that reality.

Du‘a in the sense of worship belongs to Allah. Created people can be asked for ordinary help within their capacity—such as assistance, prayer, or advice—but beings absent or dead are not granted independent control over provision, forgiveness, or the unseen. The precise boundaries of disputed practices should be learned from qualified scholarship rather than accusations made in anger.

Satan is marid, defiantly rebellious and stripped of humble obedience. His strategy often presents itself not as open worship of evil but as beauty, tradition, ancestral loyalty, or a supposedly helpful intermediary. Revelation names the direction beneath the decoration.

Verse 118 now mentions Allah’s curse upon Satan and his vow to claim an appointed share of the servants. Verse 119 then exposes the methods by which he pursues that vow.

=118=

“Allah rejected him, and he said, ‘I will surely take from among Your servants an appointed share.’”

Allah’s la‘nah upon Satan means expulsion from divine mercy after his proud refusal to obey the command concerning Adam. He knew his Creator and spoke to Him, yet knowledge without humility did not become saving faith. Pride transformed knowledge into rebellion.

Satan then announced a campaign against humanity. He does not claim every servant, but a nasiban mafruda—an appointed or determined share. The expression reveals both his ambition and his limit. He seeks followers, while Allah’s sincere servants remain outside his coercive authority (15:40-42).

His plan depends on invitation rather than creative power. Satan cannot independently decree events, know the unseen, forgive sin, or force a heart that refuses him. On the Day of Judgment he will tell his followers that he merely called and they answered (14:22). Human responsibility therefore remains intact.

The statement is also an enemy’s open declaration. People guard themselves more carefully when they know someone intends their loss. Remembering Satan as an actual adversary makes temptation easier to identify beneath friendly names such as freedom, harmless delay, ancestral custom, or private pleasure.

Being from “Your servants” makes the attempted theft especially ugly. Human beings belong to Allah by creation, provision, and rightful worship. Satan tries to divert a portion of servants from their true Master, yet even his rebellion unfolds within limits Allah permits for the test of life.

A believer should not become obsessed with Satan or attribute every personal failure to him. The lower self, habits, social pressure, and deliberate choice also matter. The correct response is refuge in Allah, knowledge, remembrance, repentance, and practical distance from temptation.

Verse 119 now gives Satan’s own description of his methods: misguidance, false hopes, superstitious mutilation, and corrupt alteration of Allah’s creation. Whoever takes him as protecting ally instead of Allah makes an unmistakably losing trade.

=119=

“‘I will surely mislead them and fill them with false hopes. I will command them, and they will slit the ears of livestock; I will command them, and they will alter Allah’s creation.’ Whoever takes Satan as a protecting ally instead of Allah has certainly suffered a clear loss.”

Satan’s strategy moves from perception to desire and then action. He first misdirects, then nourishes fantasies: endless time to repent, pleasure without consequence, status without obedience, and security through what Allah forbade. Once the heart accepts the promise, the body is recruited.

Slitting livestock ears referred to pagan practices that marked animals for idols and surrounded them with invented taboos. The physical alteration expressed false worship and false claims about what Allah made lawful. Similar superstitions are rejected in 5:103.

“Alter Allah’s creation” includes corrupting the fitrah of tawhid, changing religious truth, and bodily changes pursued for forbidden worship, mutilation, deception, or forms of vanity specifically condemned by revelation. The Prophet, peace be upon him, prohibited tattooing and filing teeth for artificial beauty in the practices he addressed.

The phrase does not make every physical change satanic. Circumcision, medical treatment, repairing injury, permitted grooming, and technologies that restore function are judged through their own evidence, purpose, benefit, and harm. Cultivating land and treating disease are uses of creation, not rebellion against it. Complex medical questions require qualified religious and clinical guidance rather than a slogan.

Taking Satan as wali means accepting his direction and promised protection over Allah’s. He advertises freedom but produces servitude to appetite, fashion, fear, and false gods. The loss is clear because the servant exchanges the care of the Creator for an enemy who announced his intention to mislead.

The cure is to challenge the false hope before obeying the command. What right will this choice consume? What must be hidden? Where does the road end? Verse 120 summarises the deception: Satan promises and arouses wishes, but everything he promises is delusion.

=120=

“He makes them promises and arouses false hopes in them, but Satan promises them nothing except delusion.”

Satan rarely says, “Choose ruin.” He promises an attractive result: forbidden money will provide security, betrayal will remain hidden, revenge will heal pain, and repentance can always happen later. Ghurur is deception that makes danger appear safe and what is temporary appear lasting.

False hope pushes repentance into an imaginary future—after one more profit, relationship, promotion, or year. Death remains abstract while appetite feels immediate. Verse 18 warned that repentance announced only when death arrives is not the willing return Allah accepts.

He also sells spiritual exemptions: ancestry guarantees salvation, a public good deed cancels unreturned rights, or Allah’s mercy makes boundaries unimportant. True hope in Allah produces repentance and obedience; delusion produces delay while borrowing the language of mercy.

Human systems can carry satanic promises through manipulative advertising, propaganda, predatory finance, or religious speech that sells salvation through a personality. Their human agents remain responsible. Satan’s invitation does not remove anyone’s choice.

The believer tests promises against revelation and their likely end. Consultation, prayer, verification, and delaying an impulsive decision can break the spell of immediacy. If an offer requires concealment, injustice, or confidence that judgment will never arrive, its promised benefit deserves suspicion.

Allah’s promises are the opposite: true, generous, and never broken. He calls toward forgiveness and Paradise and gives more than a servant’s deeds could earn. Satan promises much at the beginning, takes from the follower along the road, and abandons him at the end.

Verse 121, opening the next file, states that end: his followers find Hell as their home and no route of escape. The warning points toward escape now, while repentance is still possible.
