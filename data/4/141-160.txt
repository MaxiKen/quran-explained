=141=

“They are waiting to see what happens to you. If Allah grants you victory, they say, ‘Were we not with you?’ But if the disbelievers gain a share of success, they say, ‘Did we not gain influence over you and protect you from the believers?’ Allah will judge between you all on the Day of Resurrection, and Allah will never grant the disbelievers a final way over the believers.”

The hypocrites watch rather than belong. Yatarabbasun suggests waiting for events so they can attach themselves to whichever side profits. When believers win, they claim membership and a share. When hostile disbelievers gain an advantage, they remind them of secret assistance and expect protection in return.

Their language exposes a marketplace of loyalty. “Were we not with you?” does not mean they shared faith and sacrifice; it means they want payment for proximity. To the other side they claim influence, intelligence, or restraint exercised against believers. Each audience receives a tailored history.

A “share” for the disbelievers indicates limited worldly success. Victory and defeat alternate as tests; temporary advantage is not proof that Allah approves a cause. The hypocrite mistakes every visible result for the final judgment because he has no stable measure beyond gain.

Allah will judge between all parties on Resurrection Day. There, edited claims collapse. He knows who sincerely belonged, who was compelled, who served both sides, and who suffered from betrayal. The delayed judgment allows repentance now but never forgets the case.

“Allah will never grant the disbelievers a sabil over the believers” has been understood as denying them a final decisive proof or ultimate victory over faith, especially on the Day just mentioned. It does not promise that Muslims will never lose a battle, territory, case, or worldly position; this surah itself followed Uhud’s losses. Nor may the phrase be used to deny lawful protections or rights to non-Muslims.

The verse warns modern opportunism without making every diplomatic relationship hypocrisy. Lawful treaties and cooperation have clear principles. The condemned person trades the community’s safety for status and then claims whichever identity pays.

Verse 142 moves from political performance to worship performance. Hypocrites imagine they deceive Allah, stand for prayer reluctantly to be seen by people, and remember Him only a little.

=142=

“Surely the hypocrites seek to deceive Allah, but He causes their deception to recoil upon them. When they stand for prayer, they stand lazily, only to be seen by people, and they remember Allah only a little.”

No creature can deceive Allah. He knows the hidden disbelief before the outward prayer begins. “He deceives them” means He answers their scheme in kind: He allows them temporary appearance and advantage, then exposes and judges them when they expect safety. The consequence fits the strategy without attributing human ignorance or dishonesty to Allah.

Prayer reveals the divided self. They stand because people are watching, not because they long to meet Allah. The body performs while the heart resents the act. Laziness here is not ordinary tiredness experienced by sincere believers; it is reluctance rooted in lack of faith and dependence on an audience.

A believer may struggle with fatigue, distraction, depression, or inconsistent energy without becoming a hypocrite in the creedal sense. The warning should move such a person toward help, structure, and sincerity, not despair. The key pattern is deliberate display without inward belief.

Riya turns worship into reputation. The Prophet, peace be upon him, called showing off a form of minor shirk because the gaze of people is placed beside Allah’s pleasure. A person can begin sincerely and notice pride later; the cure is to renew intention, not necessarily abandon the deed and let Satan win twice.

“They remember Allah only a little” explains why prayer feels empty. Dhikr is not limited to repeated phrases; it includes awareness, gratitude, recitation, and obedience. Someone whose heart remembers people’s reactions throughout prayer has little space left for the One addressed.

Private worship helps train sincerity: prayer no one praises, hidden charity, and du‘a in the night. Public worship remains necessary and can be sincere; concealment is a means, not the definition of ikhlas.

The verse asks a searching question: would the deed continue if no person knew? Allah already knows the honest answer. Verse 143 describes the broader result of living for competing audiences: the hypocrites waver between camps, belonging fully to neither.

=143=

“They waver in between, belonging neither to these believers nor to those disbelievers. Whoever Allah allows to stray, you will never find for them a way.”

Mudhabdhabin evokes something shaken back and forth, never settling. The hypocrites want the security of the believers and the approval of their enemies. Because they refuse the cost of either honest position, they possess neither genuine fellowship.

This is not the same as a sincere person working through uncertainty. Questions, fear, and gradual learning can accompany real faith. The wavering described here is strategic: prayer before one audience, mockery before another; claims of loyalty after victory, secret assistance when opponents gain strength.

A divided public life eventually divides the heart. Every performance demands another explanation until a person no longer knows which words are true. Integrity brings the inner and outer selves toward one direction, even when prudence limits what can safely be disclosed.

“Whoever Allah allows to stray” refers to judgment corresponding to persistent choice. Verse 141 showed repeated opportunism and verse 142 insincere worship. Allah does not wrong a seeker asking for guidance; He leaves to wandering those who insist on treating truth as a tool. As 61:5 says, when they deviated, Allah caused their hearts to deviate.

“You will never find a way” reminds callers that guidance cannot be engineered. Advice, evidence, prayer, and good example are required, but no person controls another heart. The absence of control should prevent both despair and coercion.

The verse also warns against building identity only from reaction to groups. A believer’s centre is Allah and revelation, not permanent anxiety over which camp is fashionable. This stable centre permits just cooperation with many people without selling principle to any.

Verse 144 now addresses believers directly: do not reproduce the hypocrites’ search for protection by taking hostile disbelievers as awliya in preference to believers. Doing so establishes clear evidence against oneself before Allah.

=144=

“O believers, do not take disbelievers as protecting allies instead of believers. Do you wish to give Allah clear proof against yourselves?”

The address “O believers” warns that a trait of hypocrisy can tempt people who possess faith. They must not make the strategic choice described in verse 139—seeking protection and honour through hostile forces at the expense of the believing community.

Awliya does not mean every friend, colleague, neighbour, treaty partner, or public institution outside Islam. The Quran permits kindness and justice toward peaceful non-Muslims (60:8), commands treaty faithfulness, and records the Prophet’s lawful dealings across religious lines. The key phrase is “instead of believers” in a context of betrayal and hostile alignment.

The prohibition concerns loyalty that empowers aggression against believers, exposes their entrusted safety, or treats rejection of faith as the source of honour. It does not require support for a Muslim wrongdoer against an innocent non-Muslim. Verse 135 ordered justice even against oneself and family; justice remains above faction.

A minority Muslim may fulfil citizenship, work for public welfare, and cooperate in lawful causes without violating this verse. Covenants are trusts. Reckless claims that all civic participation is forbidden can themselves betray neighbours and damage the community.

“Sultanan mubina”—clear authority or proof—means deliberate betrayal creates evidence deserving judgment. Allah needs no new information, but the deed becomes an open case against the actor. The rhetorical question asks why anyone would voluntarily build that evidence.

Security decisions require knowledge. Emotional suspicion of whole populations is not loyalty, and secret collaboration with actual aggression is not tolerance. Qualified authority, verified conduct, and the complete Quranic framework distinguish them.

The next verse states the destination of people whose outward Islam concealed persistent rejection and betrayal: the lowest depth of the Fire, with no helper able to rescue them. Its severity matches the damage of an enemy operating from inside trust.

=145=

“Surely the hypocrites will be in the lowest depths of the Fire, and you will never find for them any helper—”

Al-dark al-asfal is the deepest descending level. Major hypocrisy receives a more severe place than open disbelief because it adds deception, betrayal of trust, corruption from within, and worship performed as mockery. The hypocrite seeks the benefits of both camps and ends beneath both.

This is not a verdict on every Muslim who lies, misses prayer, or struggles with a hypocritical trait. The Sunnah names lying, broken promises, and betrayed trusts as characteristics of hypocrisy so believers will remove them. Major hypocrisy means concealing disbelief beneath a profession of Islam. Only Allah fully knows hearts, though revelation exposed particular people to the Prophet.

“No helper” answers their entire strategy. They cultivated protectors on both sides, but none descends into the lowest Fire to retrieve them. The honour and security sought outside Allah prove temporary.

The verse should create fear for sincerity, not satisfaction at imagining others punished. A believer asks Allah for a sound heart, examines private conduct, and avoids casually condemning named people. Final destinations are not material for factional entertainment.

The dash in meaning points toward the exception in verse 146. Even after the lowest depth is announced, repentance remains possible before death. The Quran does not merely frighten hypocrites; it gives a four-part route back: repent, reform, hold fast to Allah, and make religion sincerely His.

That opening also protects believers from despair over a divided past. Someone may have worshipped for display, assisted wrong, or concealed disbelief, yet genuine return changes the category. Allah’s warning is severe and His door remains open.

=146=

“—except those who repent, reform themselves, hold firmly to Allah, and make their devotion sincere for Allah. They will be with the believers, and Allah will grant the believers an immense reward.”

The exception supplies a complete treatment for hypocrisy. Tawbah turns away from the former path with regret. Islah repairs what corruption damaged. Holding firmly to Allah replaces reliance on shifting allies. Ikhlas cleans worship of the audience that once controlled it.

Repentance without reform can be another performance. A person who leaked secrets, stole rights, or spread falsehood should stop the harm, restore what can be restored, correct the record, and accept appropriate accountability. Inner sincerity produces outward repair.

Wa‘tasamu billah means fastening oneself to Allah through revelation, prayer, du‘a, trust, and obedience. The hypocrite was shaken between camps because there was no fixed hold. Allah provides the stability no political calculation can supply.

Making the din sincere for Allah answers the lazy, visible prayer of verse 142. The repentant person no longer asks primarily how faith appears, but whether Allah accepts it. Hidden acts, truthful worship, and consistency across audiences help rebuild that sincerity.

“They will be with the believers” restores belonging. The person is not sentenced forever to social suspicion because of a genuinely corrected past. Human trust may return gradually where serious harm occurred, but the Quran names the repentant among the believing community.

The immense reward is attached to believers generally, allowing the former hypocrite to enter the same promise. Allah does not offer a permanently inferior spiritual class for those whose repentance is true. His grace is larger than their previous duplicity.

Communities should leave room for reform while remaining wise about safety and evidence. Forgiveness is not denial of consequences, and caution is not refusal to recognise tawbah. Both can coexist.

Verse 147 asks what Allah would gain by punishing people who become grateful and faithful. He is Appreciative and Knowing; punishment is not a divine need but the just consequence from which revelation seeks to save them.

=147=

“Why would Allah punish you if you are grateful and faithful? Allah is Ever-Appreciative, All-Knowing.”

The question overturns the image of a deity eager for suffering. Allah gains nothing from punishment. He is Self-Sufficient, and His warnings call people away from the consequences they choose. If faith and gratitude replace hypocrisy, what purpose would punishment serve?

Shukr is more than saying alhamdulillah. It recognises the Giver in the heart, praises Him with the tongue, and uses the gift in obedience. Faith recognises who Allah is; gratitude responds to what He gives. Together they transform belief into a lived relationship.

Allah is Shakir, Appreciative of even small sincere deeds, though every ability came from Him. Human beings often overlook modest service; Allah multiplies a particle of good. The name expresses generosity rather than dependence.

He is also ‘Alim. He knows whether gratitude is genuine, whether repentance repaired harm, and whether worship continues in private. No performance can purchase safety, but no quiet return is missed.

The verse balances the lowest-Fire warning of 145. Fear without this question could become despair; hope without the warning could become presumption. Revelation trains a heart that fears persistence and trusts return.

For someone carrying shame from hypocrisy or grave sin, the question is an invitation: believe, thank, and act. Allah is not waiting to reject a servant who sincerely comes back. For someone comfortable in sin, it removes the excuse that punishment is arbitrary.

The next verse turns from Allah’s response to human speech. Allah does not love evil being broadcast, but someone who has been wronged may speak. The permission protects victims while preserving a general culture that does not feed on public ugliness.

=148=

“Allah does not like evil speech to be voiced publicly, except by someone who has been wronged. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Knowing.”

The general rule discourages broadcasting insult, obscenity, backbiting, accusation, and people’s faults. Public speech can multiply evil beyond the original event, damage repentance, and train audiences to enjoy scandal. Not everything true needs public circulation.

The exception protects the mazlum, the person wronged. A victim may name the harm to seek judgment, warn someone at genuine risk, ask for help, or answer a false public account. Silence is not always piety. Earlier verses condemned defending traitors; this verse permits the harmed person to make the case heard.

Permission is not limitless revenge. Speech should remain truthful, relevant, and directed toward protection or justice. Fabricating additional wrongs, abusing uninvolved relatives, exposing unrelated private matters, or inciting unlawful harm exceeds the exception. Pain explains speech but does not make falsehood true.

Reporting to police, courts, safeguarding bodies, counsellors, scholars, journalists, or a trusted support network can fall within the need. The appropriate audience depends on safety and purpose. A victim should not be forced into public disclosure to be believed, nor forced into silence to preserve an institution’s image.

The verse also distinguishes backbiting from a legitimate complaint. The Prophet’s teachings allowed mentioning a person where necessary for redress, legal opinion, warning, or identification, under disciplined conditions. Hind bint Utbah openly described Abu Sufyan’s stinginess when asking the Prophet for a ruling about maintenance; her need made the description relevant.

Allah hears the words and knows the wrong behind them. He knows whether the speaker is victim, avenger, fabricator, or reluctant witness. Outsiders should verify without treating either fashionable disbelief or automatic belief as a substitute for fair process.

Verse 149 presents a higher voluntary path where appropriate: disclose good, conceal good, or pardon an evil. Allah Himself is Pardoning despite complete power. Pardon is praised, but the victim’s permission to seek justice remains intact.

=149=

“Whether you reveal a good deed or conceal it, or pardon an evil, surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Powerful.”

Good may be done publicly when it encourages others, establishes transparency, or meets an open need. It may be concealed to protect sincerity and the recipient’s dignity. Both can be righteous; intention and consequence determine which is better in a situation.

Pardoning evil is placed beside doing good. Afw means letting go and erasing a claim one has the right to pursue. It can free hearts from endless retaliation and open a wrongdoer’s path to reform. The Quran praises those who restrain anger and pardon people (3:134).

The divine names establish the model: Allah is ‘Afuww and Qadir. His pardon does not come from inability to punish. Forgiveness is most noble when chosen from strength, not extracted by powerlessness. A person who cannot safely confront an abuser should not be told that forced silence is the same as pardon.

The previous verse’s exception remains valid. Victims may speak and seek justice. Pardon is an encouraged option where safe and wise, not an obligation imposed by offenders, relatives, or institutions. Criminal accountability may also protect others even if a victim personally forgives.

Publicising one’s own good can attract riya, while secrecy can attract self-admiration about being secretly righteous. The heart needs Allah in both. A public act should not humiliate its recipient; a secret act should not become imagined superiority.

Pardoning does not require restoring trust immediately. Forgiveness, reconciliation, and renewed access are different decisions. Boundaries may remain while a person releases personal vengeance.

After discussing truthful and harmful speech, the surah returns to the greatest truth: Allah and His messengers cannot be separated. Those who claim belief in some messengers while rejecting others seek an invented middle road that revelation calls disbelief.

=150=

“Surely those who disbelieve in Allah and His messengers, seek to separate Allah from His messengers, say, ‘We believe in some and disbelieve in others,’ and wish to take a path in between—”

Allah chose messengers as bearers of His revelation. Claiming to believe in Him while rejecting a messenger whose truth is established separates the Sender from His authorised message. Loyalty to one prophet cannot justify denial of another sent by the same Lord.

Some accepted Musa but rejected Isa and Muhammad; others accepted earlier prophets and elevated Isa while rejecting Muhammad, peace be upon them all. The Quran addresses theological choices, not ancestry. Individuals among every scriptural community responded differently, and many accepted the final Messenger.

“We believe in some” may appear respectful and moderate, but prophethood is not a menu selected by tribal preference. Genuine differences between prophetic laws do not imply rivalry between prophets. Their central religion is submission to one God, with later revelation confirming and completing what came before.

The “path in between” is not honest uncertainty under investigation. It is a settled compromise that wants the religious benefit of honoured names without submission to Allah’s full testimony. A person may need time and evidence; the condemnation concerns deliberate partition after truth.

Muslims must also honour the principle internally. They cannot praise Muhammad while mocking Musa, Isa, Maryam, or any true prophet. Quranic belief includes all messengers, known and unknown, without making any of them divine.

This verse does not forbid respectful interfaith cooperation. Clear disagreement about prophethood can coexist with justice, covenant, and neighbourliness. Concealing Islamic belief to create a false impression of identical creeds is not the only route to peace.

Verse 151 completes the sentence: those who institutionalise this selective rejection are truly disbelievers, and a humiliating punishment is prepared for persistent disbelief.

=151=

“—those are the true disbelievers. And We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment.”

“True” removes the imagined middle category from verse 150. Selective recognition does not preserve saving faith when it knowingly rejects Allah’s messengers. The issue is not whether a person uses the word God, but whether he submits to God’s actual revelation.

The humiliating punishment corresponds to pride that placed human preference above divine choice. People imagined themselves qualified to accept one messenger and dismiss another because of lineage, status, or desired doctrine. What was used to claim superiority ends in humiliation.

The verse speaks about the category of belief and its final consequence without giving ordinary Muslims permission to mistreat individuals. Earthly dealings remain governed by justice, covenants, evidence, and law. Final punishment belongs to Allah.

Nor should believers speculate carelessly about specific people’s complete access to proof. Allah knows what message reached each person and whether it was recognisable or distorted. Muslims convey clearly and judge outward legal matters according to knowledge, while leaving hidden accountability to Him.

Prepared punishment is a warning while there is time to avoid it. Many who first rejected prophets later believed and became righteous. The door closes at death, not at the first difficult conversation.

Humiliation is contrasted immediately with full reward. Verse 152 promises those who believe in Allah and all His messengers without divisive rejection that He will give their rewards. Forgiveness and mercy close that promise.

=152=

“As for those who believe in Allah and His messengers and make no rejecting distinction between any of them, He will give them their rewards. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

Making no distinction does not mean every prophet has the same rank or identical mission. The Quran says Allah favoured some messengers over others (2:253). It means believers do not accept some as true while rejecting others. Every authentic messenger receives faith, respect, and love.

Muslims therefore believe in Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Isa, Muhammad, and all prophets Allah sent, peace be upon them. The Quran names some and leaves many unnamed. No race or region can claim Allah never sent guidance beyond the names known to Arabs.

Their “rewards” are plural, reflecting the many acts flowing from integrated faith: worship of Allah, following the final Messenger, defending prophetic honour, and learning from their stories. Allah grants these rewards fully and multiplies them by grace.

Al-Ghafur and al-Rahim are fitting because believers still make mistakes in knowledge and conduct. Complete creed does not remove need for forgiveness. Mercy takes limited deeds toward an unlimited reward.

This belief creates a wide sacred history. Musa is not “their prophet” to Muslims, nor Isa a foreign religious figure. Both belong to the believer’s faith, while neither is worshipped. Muhammad confirms their original message and is the final messenger to be followed after his coming.

Respect should shape speech. Mockery of biblical prophetic names, antisemitism, anti-Christian hatred, and contempt for sincere questioners conflict with the duty to convey the Quranic position justly. Theological clarity needs prophetic character.

The next verse returns to some People of the Book who demanded that Muhammad bring a physical book down from heaven. Their demand is compared with a faction among Musa’s people who asked for something even greater—to see Allah openly—then took the calf despite clear signs.

=153=

“The People of the Scripture ask you to bring down for them a book from heaven. They demanded something even greater from Musa when they said, ‘Show us Allah openly.’ So a thunderbolt struck them for their wrongdoing. Then they took the calf for worship after clear signs had come to them, yet We pardoned that and gave Musa compelling authority.”

The demand was not a sincere request for any evidence. The Quran was already descending, recited openly, coherent in message, and supported by signs. They demanded a visible document from the sky on their terms, treating submission as conditional upon spectacle.

The verse recalls a faction among the Children of Israel who asked to see Allah openly (2:55). The thunderbolt was linked to their zulm—the arrogant demand after sufficient proof—not to honest longing for closeness. Musa himself later asked to see Allah and received a teaching without being treated as a rebellious mocker (7:143); intention and context matter.

Even dramatic signs do not force a willing heart. After deliverance, sea-splitting, provision, and revelation, some worshipped the calf. The problem was therefore not lack of visible evidence but readiness to obey. A person can demand miracle after miracle while protecting refusal.

Yet Allah says, “We pardoned that.” Calf worship was grave shirk, but sincere repentance before death remained possible. The passage combines historical criticism with divine mercy and gives no basis for inherited guilt against later Jews. No soul bears another’s burden.

Musa received sultanan mubina—clear authority, proof, and strength to establish truth. The demanders’ inconsistency did not weaken his prophethood. Likewise, refusal to accept the Quran does not diminish its evidence.

Muslims should apply the lesson inwardly. A person may postpone obedience until receiving a dream, perfect emotional certainty, or a sign designed to personal specification. Revelation already present may be the evidence he is avoiding.

Verse 154 recalls further covenant signs: the Mount raised above them, entry through the gate in humility, the Sabbath boundary, and a solemn covenant. Evidence was accompanied by explicit obligations, not spectacle alone.

=154=

“We raised the Mount above them because of their covenant, and We said to them, ‘Enter the gate humbly,’ and We said, ‘Do not transgress the Sabbath.’ And We took from them a solemn covenant.”

The raising of al-Tur recalls Sinai and the covenant described in 2:63 and 7:171. The mountain stood over them as a physical sign of the gravity of what they accepted: take revelation firmly and remember it. Covenant with Allah is not ceremonial identity.

They were commanded to enter the gate sujjadan—with bowing humility—and seek forgiveness, as detailed in 2:58. Entry into a place of victory should produce gratitude rather than triumphal arrogance. Some altered the word and posture, turning repentance into mockery.

The Sabbath was a clear boundary for the Israelite community. They were not to pursue prohibited work on that day. A group later used devices to capture the benefit while pretending technical obedience. Revelation judges the purpose beneath a loophole.

Mithaqan ghaliza, a solemn and weighty covenant, is the same powerful expression used for marriage in 4:21 and for the prophetic covenant in 33:7. Shared wording does not make the covenants identical; it shows none may be treated casually.

The verse lists commands without saying every Israelite broke them. The next verse criticises those who did, while verse 162 will praise those firmly grounded in knowledge among them. Quranic precision must not be replaced by collective hostility.

Muslims have no Sabbath law in the same form, but they have Friday prayer, contracts, and revealed boundaries of their own. Cleverness used to secure forbidden benefit remains spiritually dangerous. The lesson is not to feel superior to an earlier community but to recognise the same human temptation.

The next verse begins a long sentence listing grave violations: breaking the covenant, rejecting signs, killing prophets, and claiming their hearts were wrapped. Allah says their disbelief, not lack of access, led to a seal and little faith among the persistent offenders.

=155=

“Then, for their breaking of the covenant, rejecting Allah’s signs, killing prophets unjustly, and saying, ‘Our hearts are wrapped’—rather, Allah sealed their hearts because of their disbelief, so they do not believe except a few—”

The sentence accumulates moral causes. Covenant was not broken through one mistake but joined to rejection of signs, violence against prophets, and pride about closed hearts. The consequence described in following verses rests on repeated grave choices.

Killing prophets is “without right,” a phrase that also exposes the absurdity of imagining prophetic murder could ever be justified. Earlier communities killed some messengers and righteous people who commanded justice (3:21). Later members who approved and repeated the rebellion could share moral alignment without every individual committing the historical act.

“Our hearts are ghulf” could mean covered and unable to receive Muhammad’s message, or containers already full of knowledge and needing nothing more. Either reading turns refusal into a boast or excuse. Allah answers that the seal followed their disbelief.

A sealed heart is not an arbitrary starting condition. They rejected, distorted, and attacked until the consequence settled over the faculty of recognition. This pattern should frighten anyone who repeatedly dismisses truth for convenience. Each refusal trains the next.

“Except a few” may mean they possessed only slight belief or that few among them truly believed. In either case, the wording refuses totalisation. People such as Abdullah ibn Salam accepted the Prophet, and verse 162 explicitly honours the knowledgeable believers among the People of the Book.

Modern antisemitism cannot be justified from this passage. The Quran condemns defined violations, records diversity, and commands justice. Assigning guilt by ancestry would violate its own rule that no bearer carries another’s burden.

The sentence continues with another grave wrong in verse 156: disbelief and a tremendous slander against Maryam. A woman chosen and purified by Allah was attacked at the point of chastity, demonstrating how rejection of a prophet can recruit abuse of his mother.

=156=

“—and for their disbelief and their uttering against Maryam a monstrous slander—”

Maryam returned to her people carrying Isa and faced an accusation against her chastity. The Quran had already answered it through the miraculous speech of the infant, her chosen status, and her devotion (3:42-47; 19:27-33). Persisting in the accusation after these signs was buhtanan ‘azima, a stunning and enormous slander.

Buhtan is a lie that leaves the innocent person bewildered by its audacity. The surah used the term for accusations designed to recover a wife’s dowry and for throwing one’s own sin onto an innocent person. Here it attacks one of the purest women in revelation.

Slander against women often uses sexuality because honour-based societies make the wound socially devastating. The verse protects Maryam and, by principle, warns against casual accusations of chastity. Surah al-Nur later establishes strict evidentiary rules and punishment for unsupported charges.

Honouring Maryam is part of Muslim faith. She is not divine and is not the mother of God; she is a truthful, purified servant whom Allah chose for a miracle. Rejecting both slander and deification preserves her real dignity.

The phrase again criticises perpetrators and those committed to the false claim, not an ethnicity. Christians and Muslims honour Maryam, and individuals from Jewish backgrounds have varied beliefs and conduct. Justice in commentary is itself obedience to the Quran.

Modern rumours can repeat ancient cruelty at digital speed. A believer should verify, refuse salacious forwarding, and remember that a reputation may never fully recover in public even after facts emerge. Repentance from slander requires active correction.

Verse 157 adds their boast that they killed the Messiah, Isa son of Maryam, the Messenger of Allah. The Quran denies both killing and crucifixion as the actual end, says the matter was made to appear to them, and rejects conjecture as knowledge.

=157=

“—and for their claim, ‘We killed the Messiah, Isa son of Maryam, the Messenger of Allah.’ But they neither killed him nor crucified him; rather, it was made to appear so to them. Those who differ about it are certainly in doubt, having no knowledge except following conjecture. They certainly did not kill him.”

Calling Isa “the Messenger of Allah” within the boast exposes its enormity. The speakers may have used the title sarcastically, while the Quran states his true rank. They claimed triumph over a messenger; Allah denies that they achieved it.

Islamic belief is explicit: Isa was not killed and was not crucified as his enemies intended. Shubbiha lahum means the event or person was made to appear so to them. The Quran does not identify a substitute, and authentic prophetic teaching does not require the elaborate names and stories later commentators transmitted. Certainty should stop where revelation stops.

“Those who differed” includes conflicting claims around what happened. The verse characterises them as following zann rather than revealed knowledge. Historical testimony and Christian theology commonly affirm crucifixion, but Muslims take the Quran as the final divine criterion and state the disagreement respectfully rather than caricaturing another faith.

The denial also rejects the theology that humanity’s sins required Allah’s prophet to be killed as an atoning sacrifice. In the Quran, each soul bears its own burden, repentance is direct, and Allah forgives by mercy without injustice to an innocent substitute.

“They certainly did not kill him” closes the verse with emphasis. Political power, arrest, and visible planning did not determine the final reality. Allah’s plan overruled theirs, as 3:54 says: they planned and Allah planned, and Allah is the best of planners.

Muslims should not use this doctrine to insult Christians or attack churches. Clear theological disagreement coexists with the Quran’s commands for dialogue in the best manner, justice, and protection of peaceful people.

What happened to Isa is stated positively in verse 158: Allah raised him to Himself. The closing names—Almighty and Wise—show the power and purpose behind his rescue.

=158=

“Rather, Allah raised him to Himself. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”

Bal—“rather”—replaces the enemies’ claim with divine action. They did not kill Isa; Allah raised him. The passive uncertainty of human reports gives way to a subject with complete power: Allah.

The mainstream Islamic understanding is that Isa was raised alive, body and soul, and will return before the Hour, as established by numerous authentic hadiths. His return does not make him divine. He returns as Allah’s servant and messenger, follows the final revealed law, defeats the great deceiver, and later dies as a human being.

“To Himself” is language of honour and elevation. It does not imply Allah is contained within creation or that Isa became part of Him. The Quran distinguishes Creator and servant absolutely. Isa’s spirit and body are created; his raising is a miracle performed by the Creator.

Al-‘Aziz is unconquerable in might. Authorities may control a cross, court, and crowd, but they cannot overcome His decree. Al-Hakim raises with wisdom: preserving the messenger, exposing human uncertainty, and assigning Isa a role near the end of history.

The verse brings comfort beyond its unique miracle. Apparent control by oppressors is never total. Allah may rescue in an unforeseen way, grant martyrdom and eternal life of another kind, or reserve public justice for the Hereafter. Believers trust Him without demanding Isa’s exact miracle for every victim.

Speculation about the mechanics of ascent adds little. Revelation directs attention to who acted and what it proves. Faith honours the limit of available knowledge.

Verse 159 then speaks of People of the Book believing in Isa before death and of Isa serving as witness against them on Resurrection Day. Its pronouns have received more than one early interpretation, while his return supplies the widely held reading.

=159=

“There is none from the People of the Scripture but will believe in him before his death, and on the Day of Resurrection he will be a witness against them.”

The pronoun in “before his death” has been understood in two main ways. One interpretation says each person from the People of the Book recognises the truth about Isa at the approach of personal death, when recognition no longer carries the same voluntary value. The widely held interpretation connects it to Isa’s death after his future return, when surviving scriptural communities will recognise him correctly.

Authentic hadiths describe Isa descending before the Hour, breaking the cross, killing the false messiah, and ruling justly. He will correct both extremes: the slander that denied his honour and the deification that raised him beyond servanthood. His message remains worship of Allah alone.

On Resurrection Day, Isa will testify about what he taught and how people responded. In 5:116-117 he denies ever telling people to worship him and his mother, saying he taught only what Allah commanded: worship Allah, my Lord and your Lord.

Witness does not mean Isa is hostile to every person called Christian or Jew in the same undifferentiated way. He testifies truthfully to message and response, while Allah knows individual proof, intention, and circumstance. The Quran itself records believing and upright people among scriptural communities.

The verse calls Muslims to hold Isa’s identity in balance: Messiah, miraculous Word given to Maryam, honoured messenger, raised servant, future just ruler, and never a partner with Allah. Reducing him to an ordinary teacher or elevating him to deity both miss the Quranic portrait.

For every reader, the coming testimony asks whether prophets were followed or used as symbols for inherited claims. Love is proved by receiving what they actually taught.

The next verse returns to legal consequences in the history of some Jews. Because of wrongdoing and repeatedly blocking people from Allah’s way, certain wholesome foods formerly lawful were forbidden to them.

“For wrongdoing committed by some Jews, We forbade them certain wholesome foods that had previously been lawful to them, and because they frequently hindered others from Allah’s way.”

The verse explains that some dietary restrictions were punitive, not evidence that the foods were inherently impure for every nation. Surah al-An‘am 6:146 names certain animals and fats forbidden to Jews as recompense for rebellion, while 3:93 says food had generally been lawful before Israel prohibited something for himself.

The Quran says “for wrongdoing from those who were Jews,” tying consequence to conduct. It does not say every Jewish dietary practice is meaningless or that every Jew personally committed the listed sins. Earlier revelation contained genuine law, and individuals differ. The passage repeatedly distinguishes persistent offenders from those firmly grounded in knowledge.

Their zulm included covenant violation and other wrongs surrounding this section. The second cause named here is sadd—turning themselves and many others away from Allah’s path through rejection, concealment, obstruction, or hostile influence. Misguiding others multiplies a private sin into social harm.

A restriction can therefore function as discipline. The final Messenger is described elsewhere as removing burdens and shackles that had weighed upon earlier people (7:157), returning the community to a broad law of lawful good things while preserving necessary boundaries.

Muslims should not infer that every hardship or legal detail proves punishment. Only revelation can identify a specific divine cause with certainty. General moral reflection is valid; confident claims that a disaster struck a group for a named sin often exceed knowledge.

Nor may this verse feed antisemitism or mock kosher observance. Muslims themselves follow dietary law and should understand conscientious obedience. The theological explanation can be stated without contempt for people.
