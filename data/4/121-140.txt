=121=

“Those people will have Hell as their home, and they will find no escape from it.”

Ma’wa is a place to which someone returns for shelter, but here the supposed shelter is Hell. Satan offered protection, pleasure, and advantage; his followers arrive at an abode that protects from nothing. The promise is inverted.

Mahis is a place of escape or a route away. In worldly life, people move between excuses, identities, jurisdictions, and hidden rooms. At final judgment, no technicality, advocate, or second destination lies outside Allah’s dominion.

The verse describes those who persist in satanic allegiance until death. While life remains, escape exists through tawbah. Shirk can be abandoned, false worship renounced, rights restored, and the covenant with Allah renewed. The warning of no escape in the Hereafter is itself guidance toward escape now.

Hell is not entered because Satan overpowered innocent people against their will. The previous verses described taking him as ally and answering his commands. Human responsibility and satanic temptation operate together; invitation does not cancel choice.

This destination should produce fear without fascination or cruelty. Believers do not rejoice at people’s ruin, imitate divine punishment, or declare particular living individuals doomed. The Prophet, peace be upon him, invited enemies toward rescue and grieved over rejection.

The passage closes a strong contrast. Allah’s immense favour gives Book, wisdom, correction, forgiveness, and truthful guidance. Satan gives false counsel, deceptive hopes, and abandonment. Each calls for allegiance, but only Allah is a real protecting Guardian.

Verse 122 now presents the opposite home and the opposite promise: believers who do righteous deeds enter Gardens forever. Allah calls His promise true and asks whose word could possibly be more truthful than His.

=122=

“But those who believe and do righteous deeds, We will admit them into Gardens beneath which rivers flow, remaining there forever. This is Allah’s true promise. And whose word could be more truthful than Allah’s?”

The previous verse ended with Hell as the home of those who accept Satan’s promises. This verse gives the opposite destination and the opposite promise. Satan promises advantage and leaves his followers trapped; Allah promises Gardens and fulfils every word.

Faith and righteous action appear together. Iman gives deeds their foundation and direction, while good deeds show faith taking root in life. Neither means sinless perfection. Repentance, restitution, patience after failure, and renewed obedience are themselves among the actions of believers.

Gardens with flowing rivers present life, beauty, provision, and peace against the confinement of Hell. “Forever” removes the anxiety hidden inside every worldly pleasure. Here, health changes, water dries, relationships part, and ownership ends. There, the gift does not expire and its people are not threatened with removal.

Wa‘d Allahi haqqan—Allah’s promise is true in itself and certain to occur. Human promises can fail through dishonesty, forgetfulness, changing desire, or lack of power. None applies to Allah. He knows every servant, never forgets, never breaks His word, and possesses complete power to resurrect and reward.

“Whose word is more truthful?” repeats the challenge of verse 87, which guaranteed the gathering. The same truthful Speaker promises both judgment and Paradise. A believer should not accept the warning as real while treating the promise as poetic comfort, nor celebrate the promise while ignoring the warning.

Hope in this verse should produce action. A true promise does not make the path optional; it makes patient travel rational. Each prayer, honest judgment, protected trust, and resisted temptation moves toward an abode more lasting than the sacrifice.

The verse also frees believers from Satan’s false hopes. The cure for counterfeit promise is not hopelessness but trust in the real Promiser. Verse 123 now rejects another fantasy: salvation is not determined by the wishes of Muslims or the People of the Book. Claims must meet faith, action, justice, and Allah’s mercy.

=123=

“It will not be according to your wishful thinking, nor the wishful thinking of the People of the Scripture. Whoever does evil will be recompensed for it and will find neither protector nor helper besides Allah.”

Religious identity can become a false guarantee. Some among the People of the Book claimed exclusive salvation or only a few days of punishment; Muslims could fall into the same confidence simply because they belong to Muhammad’s community. The verse rejects both sets of amani—wishes detached from truth and conduct.

This does not deny the immense value of correct faith or the Prophet’s intercession by Allah’s permission. It denies entitlement that turns belief into a slogan and mercy into permission to persist. Allah’s promises are received through the conditions He names, not through stories communities tell about themselves.

“Whoever does evil will be recompensed” greatly concerned the companions. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that hardship touching a believer—even the prick of a thorn—can expiate sins, and directed them toward steadiness and hope rather than despair. Recompense can occur through worldly consequence, purification, repentance, or final judgment according to Allah’s wisdom.

The verse does not mean every pain proves a particular sin. Prophets and righteous people were tested most severely. Nor does it erase forgiveness, because the surrounding passage repeatedly announces mercy. It means evil is not made harmless by group membership and cannot simply be wished out of the account.

No wali or nasir besides Allah can protect a person against His judgment. Saints, ancestors, institutions, and leaders cannot independently override Him. The safest response is not to seek a stronger identity shield but to seek His forgiveness and change the deed.

Wishful thinking appears today when someone says, “My heart is good,” while consuming rights, or assumes one charitable act cancels ongoing injustice. True hope moves the person to prayer, restitution, and repentance; false hope leaves conduct untouched.

The next verse balances this warning exactly. Whoever performs righteous deeds, male or female, while possessing faith will enter Paradise and will not be wronged even by the tiny mark on a date stone.

=124=

“Whoever does righteous deeds, whether male or female, while being a believer—those will enter Paradise and will not be wronged even by the speck on a date stone.”

Men and women are addressed with equal moral agency and the same promised destination. A woman’s worship and service do not pass through a husband’s account, and a man cannot inherit righteousness from a mother or wife. Each stands before Allah as a believing servant.

The condition “while being a believer” joins action to iman. The Quran does not present Paradise as wages produced by outward usefulness alone. Faith in Allah gives deeds their ultimate orientation, while righteous conduct verifies that faith is more than a claim.

This equality of reward does not require every worldly duty or legal share to be identical. The surah has assigned different responsibilities in some family matters while repeatedly affirming one human origin, property rights, access to worship, and full recompense. Difference of role is not a scale of spiritual worth.

A naqir is the tiny indentation on a date stone. Earlier, verse 53 described stingy people who would not give others even a naqir if they controlled dominion. Allah’s judgment is the opposite: not even that amount is withheld. An unnoticed act by someone society overlooks remains fully present.

Righteous deeds include the visible and private: prayer, honesty, raising children, seeking knowledge, earning lawfully, caring for parents, protecting a victim, and restraining a harmful word. The promise allows no culture to tell women that only male public activity matters, or tell men that household service has no spiritual weight.

“Will enter” is a promise held alongside prophetic teaching that entry is ultimately by Allah’s mercy. Deeds are the commanded path and evidence of faith; mercy accepts and multiplies them beyond what they independently merit.

The verse answers wishful thinking with accessible action rather than elitism. Any believing man or woman can begin. Verse 125 then describes the finest religious orientation: submit the whole face to Allah, practise excellence, and follow the upright way of Ibrahim, whom Allah took as an intimate friend.

=125=

“Who is better in religion than one who submits their whole self to Allah, does good, and follows the upright way of Ibrahim? And Allah took Ibrahim as an intimate friend.”

Submitting the face means directing the whole person to Allah. The face represents identity, intention, and direction. Islam is not merely inherited membership; it is willing surrender of worship, judgment, hope, and conduct to the Lord.

Submission is joined to ihsan. A person may claim surrender while treating people harshly, or perform attractive social good without surrendering to Allah. The verse brings inner devotion and outward excellence together. The best din creates both a sound relationship with the Creator and beautiful dealings with creation.

Ibrahim’s millah was hanif: inclining away from every false god toward pure monotheism. He challenged idols, left homeland for Allah, welcomed guests, raised a family upon prayer, and submitted when tested. Jews, Christians, and Arabs all claimed connection to him; the Quran identifies his true inheritance as uncompromised submission.

Allah took Ibrahim as khalil, an intimate friend whose love penetrates deeply. Khullah is among the highest forms of love. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, said Allah took him as an intimate friend just as He took Ibrahim. This friendship never makes a prophet divine; it displays the highest honour of servanthood.

One does not become Allah’s friend through slogans, lineage, or extraordinary claims. Ibrahim’s closeness grew through tawhid, truthfulness, sacrifice, and obedience. The verse therefore answers the wishful thinking rejected earlier with a lived model.

Following Ibrahim today means worshipping Allah alone, honouring the prophets who came through his line, and following the final Messenger who was commanded to follow his upright creed. It does not mean selecting only the parts of Abrahamic heritage that modern preference finds comfortable.

The rhetorical question has only one answer: no religion is better. Verse 126 grounds that conclusion in ownership. Everything in the heavens and earth belongs to Allah, and He encompasses all things; submission is simply creation returning consciously to its true Owner.

=126=

“To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and Allah fully encompasses all things.”

Divine ownership explains why submission is right. Bodies, abilities, property, time, families, angels, and worlds all exist by Allah and remain under His command. Human ownership is real enough to create legal rights, but it is temporary stewardship inside His absolute dominion.

Muhit means He encompasses everything in knowledge, power, and authority. No secret counsel, hidden orphan property, private marriage injustice, or forgotten good deed falls outside. The word has appeared around conspiracies in this surah; here it expands to the entire cosmos.

Allah’s encompassing does not mean creation contains Him or that He is identical with the world. The Creator is above and distinct from creation while nothing escapes His knowledge and power. Tawhid rejects both idols outside and confusion between Creator and created.

The verse also places Ibrahim’s honour correctly. He is Allah’s intimate friend, yet still part of what Allah owns. Love for prophets never becomes worship of them. The most honoured servants remain servants.

For anxious people, ownership can be comfort. The affair feared and the means needed both belong to the same Lord. For arrogant people, it is warning: wealth, office, and influence are borrowed and can be reclaimed.

The verse bridges universal theology back to vulnerable family members. Because everything belongs to Allah, guardians do not own orphan girls and husbands do not own wives. Verse 127 answers questions about women by returning to rulings already recited concerning female orphans, weak children, and the obligation to stand firmly for justice.

=127=

“They ask you for a ruling concerning women. Say, ‘Allah gives you a ruling about them, along with what has been recited to you in the Book concerning orphan women whom you deny the shares prescribed for them while desiring to marry them, and concerning helpless children. You are commanded to uphold justice for orphans. Whatever good you do, Allah knows it fully.’”

Seeking a fatwa means asking for a clear ruling. Allah answers and directs listeners back to what was already recited at the surah’s beginning. New questions do not erase established principles: orphan wealth, fair dowry, marriage justice, and inheritance remain the foundation.

Aishah, may Allah be pleased with her, explained that a guardian might desire a beautiful orphan in his care and marry her without a dowry equal to her peers. If he did not desire her, he might still prevent her from marrying someone else so her wealth remained under his control. The wording can carry both desire to marry and desire away from marrying; either way, exploitation is forbidden.

Weak children were also deprived in a society that gave inheritance chiefly to fighting men. The Quran assigned shares to young people and women precisely where power had excluded them. A guardian cannot call control “protection” while withholding an owner’s right.

“To uphold justice” is broader than avoiding theft. It includes accurate records, fair valuation, appropriate care, freedom from coercion, access to education, and release of property when maturity and sound judgment appear. Justice must be sustained, not performed once.

The final sentence knows every good deed even when no court sees it. Managing an orphan’s property honestly, advocating for a girl against family pressure, or stepping aside when conflict of interest appears are known to Allah. That knowledge rewards quiet integrity and exposes hidden exploitation.

The verse also teaches a method for religious questions about women: ask revelation, include women’s actual rights and circumstances, and refuse answers built only around male convenience. The surah named “Women” repeatedly makes powerful people answerable.

Verse 128 now addresses a different imbalance: a wife fears nushuz or abandonment from her husband. Just as verse 34 discussed serious breach by a wife, the Quran recognises wrongdoing and withdrawal can come from the husband and offers negotiated reconciliation without pretending coercion is consent.

=128=

“If a woman fears mistreatment or abandonment from her husband, there is no blame if they reach a fair settlement between themselves, for reconciliation is best. Human souls are prone to selfishness. But if you act with excellence and remain mindful of Allah, surely Allah is fully aware of what you do.”

Nushuz here describes a husband’s serious ill-treatment, elevation of himself against marital duties, or withdrawal, while i‘rad is turning away emotionally or practically. The Quran does not frame marital failure as female only. A wife can recognise signs and seek resolution before complete breakdown.

Sulh may involve mutually agreed adjustments to time, maintenance, living arrangements, or other rights. Reports mention Sawdah, may Allah be pleased with her, voluntarily giving her day with the Prophet to Aishah when she feared divorce and wished to remain his wife. Her choice illustrates permission, not a rule requiring every wife to surrender rights.

“There is no blame” means spouses may make a lawful compromise that ordinary allocation would not require. Consent is essential. A husband may not neglect, threaten divorce, or create misery until a wife “agrees” to less. Such pressure is the very selfishness the verse warns about.

“Reconciliation is best” praises a just settlement over needless rupture, but does not declare every continuing marriage better than safe separation. Abuse, danger, or irreparable harm may make divorce necessary. Verse 130 explicitly promises Allah’s abundance if spouses separate.

“Souls are prone to shuhh”—a grasping reluctance to yield. A husband may cling to freedom from duties while wanting marriage’s benefits; a wife may find any concession painful; relatives may protect pride. Naming this tendency helps each person examine what blocks a fair outcome.

Ihsan and taqwa call for more than bargaining to the legal minimum. The stronger spouse should remember that Allah knows whether generosity or pressure shaped the settlement. Written clarity, balanced counsel, and independent support can protect consent.

Allah is Khabir, aware of details outsiders miss. A publicly peaceful agreement may hide coercion, while a quiet compromise may reflect sincere care. Verse 129 turns to polygynous justice: equal love is beyond human control, but complete inclination that leaves one wife suspended is forbidden.

=129=

“You will never be able to maintain complete equality between wives, no matter how earnestly you try. So do not incline entirely toward one, leaving another suspended. If you make amends and remain mindful of Allah, then surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

Verse 3 required justice and told a man who fears failing it to marry only one. This verse does not cancel that condition. It distinguishes what cannot be controlled perfectly—love and inward inclination—from what can: time, maintenance, housing, nights, respect, and outward treatment.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, divided his time among his wives and prayed that Allah not blame him for what He controlled of the heart beyond human control. Emotional equality cannot be manufactured, but ethical conduct can restrain emotion from becoming oppression.

Complete inclination leaves a wife mu‘allaqah, hanging: neither enjoying the rights and companionship of marriage nor released to build another life. A husband may not keep her for appearance, convenience, or retaliation while giving nearly everything to another. The Quran names suspension as harm.

Justice is not merely identical spending without regard to genuine needs, but differences must not become favouritism disguised as need. Honest records, schedules, communication, and qualified counsel may be necessary. Anyone considering plural marriage should evaluate capacity before creating obligations he cannot fulfil.

The verse speaks to wives’ rights without demanding that affection feel equal. A woman is entitled to fair conduct and may seek mediation or legal remedy; she cannot require a particular involuntary emotion. This distinction protects both realistic humanity and enforceable justice.

“If you make amends” opens repair after imbalance. Time can be restored, unpaid maintenance settled, apology made, and conduct changed. Taqwa keeps reform alive when no observer is present.

Allah’s forgiveness and mercy acknowledge that hearts are imperfect while refusing deliberate neglect. A person is forgiven for what lies beyond control, not authorised to ignore what lies within it.

If fairness and reconciliation cannot be sustained, the Quran does not command endless suspension. Verse 130 allows separation and promises that Allah can enrich each spouse from His vast abundance.

=130=

“If the spouses separate, Allah will enrich each of them from His abundance. And Allah is All-Encompassing in bounty, All-Wise.”

Reconciliation is good, but separation is not always failure. The Quran moves without contradiction from “reconciliation is best” to a promise for those who part. When marriage no longer provides tranquillity, justice, and safety, a lawful divorce may be the cleaner path.

Allah can enrich “each”—wife and husband. Enrichment may be financial, emotional, spiritual, relational, or the peace of release from continuing conflict. Neither spouse’s future is owned by the other. The Lord who provided before marriage remains after it.

Fear of poverty traps people in harmful arrangements. A wife may depend economically on a husband; a husband may fear financial obligations or loss of family structure. The promise encourages trust without abolishing planning. Maintenance, dowry debts, child support, housing, and custody rights must be settled justly.

Wasi‘ means vast in provision, mercy, and capacity. Human beings see one closed household and imagine no other good exists. Allah’s bounty is wider than the marriage. Hakim means the opening may arrive in a form and time governed by wisdom, not necessarily immediate comfort.

The verse should not be used to recommend divorce at every difficulty. Previous verses encouraged counsel, adjustment, taqwa, and repair. It prevents those tools from becoming chains when separation is truly needed.

Children should not be turned into weapons after divorce. Enrichment from Allah is sought through continuing parental responsibility, truthful speech, and protection of the child’s bond with both safe parents. Revenge can carry the old conflict into the next generation.

Communities can embody divine abundance through fair legal help, work opportunities, housing, emotional care, and rejection of stigma toward divorced people. Trust in Allah often arrives through duties performed by His servants.

The next verses broaden the foundation: everything in heaven and earth belongs to Allah, and taqwa was commanded to earlier scriptural communities and to Muslims. Marriage rules are part of a universal covenant of accountability before the Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy Lord.

=131=

“To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth. We have instructed those who received Scripture before you—and you yourselves—to be mindful of Allah. If you disbelieve, then surely everything in the heavens and earth belongs to Allah. Allah is Self-Sufficient, Praiseworthy.”

Taqwa is not a new ethnic rule. Allah charged earlier recipients of revelation and the final community with the same central duty: remain conscious of Him and guard His boundaries. Prophetic laws differ in details, but worship, accountability, and moral restraint run through them.

The ownership statement appears before and after the warning. Obedience does not increase Allah’s kingdom, and disbelief does not reduce it. Every rebel still lives on His earth, breathes His air, and remains under His power. The benefit or damage returns to the servant.

Al-Ghani is completely free of need. He did not legislate marriage justice because He requires human households; He legislated it because people need guidance. Worship offered with pride misunderstands its direction—Allah grants the honour of worship rather than receiving a necessity from creatures.

Al-Hamid is worthy of praise in Himself and praised for wisdom, justice, and gifts. Even if creation refused, His perfection would remain. The two names free faith from the idea of a needy deity demanding obedience for ego.

Mentioning previous scriptures also speaks to the debates in this surah. Muslims must not become self-congratulatory while criticising earlier communities. The same taqwa is demanded from them, and the same Owner can hold them accountable for distortion, partial obedience, and injustice.

In family conflict, taqwa reaches where contracts cannot. A spouse can comply on paper while humiliating privately; a guardian can hide value from an inventory. Remembering the Owner transforms minimum compliance into conscience.

Verse 132 repeats divine ownership and adds that Allah is sufficient as Wakil, the Trustee and Manager of affairs. The servant obeys and uses means, then entrusts outcomes—including provision after separation—to Him.

=132=

“To Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and whatever is on the earth, and Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of affairs.”

Repetition is not redundancy. Verse 131 used ownership to show Allah’s independence from human faith; this verse uses it to establish His sufficiency as Wakil. The One who manages all creation can be trusted with one family, livelihood, or uncertain future.

A wakil acts on behalf of another, but Allah’s name is higher than human agency. Creatures entrust matters to Him because He already owns the means, knows every path, and possesses power over outcomes. He is not a hired representative dependent on instructions.

Tawakkul does not mean neglecting duties. The surah commanded witnesses, records, arbitration, precautions, migration, and justice. A person ties the means to obedience and the heart to Allah. Refusing to plan is not superior trust, and believing the plan guarantees success is not trust at all.

For spouses considering separation, the verse answers fear: resources and future possibilities belong to Him. For guardians, it warns that entrusted wealth belongs ultimately to Him. For communities, it prevents reliance on powerful allies from replacing reliance on the Lord.

Allah’s trusteeship also relieves compulsive control. After honest effort, people cannot manage every heart, court, market, or consequence. Anxiety often tries to perform a divine role. Dhikr of al-Wakil restores the servant to responsible limits.

The next verse warns humanity not to confuse divine care with dependence upon them. If Allah wills, He can remove one people and bring others. He is fully capable of replacement.

=133=

“If He wills, He can remove you, O humanity, and bring others in your place. Allah is fully capable of doing so.”

The address expands to all people. Humanity depends on Allah; He does not depend on any generation. The Creator who brought the first human from no human parents can end a population and create or raise another.

Communities sometimes treat chosenness as indispensability. A religious group, nation, institution, or family imagines truth cannot continue without its leadership. The verse breaks that pride. Service is an opportunity, not leverage over Allah.

Replacement is a recurring Quranic warning. If people turn away, Allah can bring a people He loves and who love Him (5:54), and 47:38 warns that replacements need not resemble those who failed. History shows civilisations rising, declining, and yielding their place.

The verse does not encourage contempt for human life. The same surah has fiercely protected life, orphans, and the oppressed. Human dignity comes from Allah’s choice and command, not from His need. Precisely because life is His gift, people may not destroy it arrogantly.

For an individual, awareness of replaceability can be healthy. A role at work, in a mosque, or in a movement is a trust to fulfil, not an identity that permits abuse. If one servant refuses, Allah’s cause does not collapse.

This warning should produce urgency without despair. The ability to replace also proves the ability to revive and reform. A generation can choose taqwa and remain an instrument of good rather than wait for removal.

Allah’s capability extends to giving both worldly and otherworldly reward. Verse 134 redirects anyone seeking only immediate gain: both rewards are with Him, so narrowing desire to this life is unnecessary and shortsighted.

=134=

“Whoever desires the reward of this world should know that with Allah is the reward of this world and the Hereafter. And Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.”

The verse does not condemn asking for worldly good. Provision, health, family, safety, and lawful success are blessings. It corrects the person who makes worldly return the whole purpose of worship or justice when Allah possesses both worlds.

A believer can ask, “Our Lord, give us good in this world and good in the Hereafter” (2:201). The prayer refuses a false choice. Worldly good becomes a means to gratitude and lasting reward rather than a competitor to it.

Someone may give charity only for reputation, testify only for payment, or join a cause only when victory appears likely. Such people receive according to the narrow intention Allah wills, but they have reduced an eternal opportunity to a temporary transaction. Actions are judged by intentions.

“With Allah” means He controls lawful means and final outcomes. Seeking the world through disobedience is irrational because the desired provision is already in the hand of the One being disobeyed. Halal effort, du‘a, patience, and trust align both forms of reward.

Al-Sami‘ hears the stated request and private negotiation of the heart. Al-Basir sees how the desired reward shapes conduct. A public claim of serving the Hereafter does not conceal a worldly centre.

The verse prepares for the demanding command in 135. Justice may cost family loyalty, wealth, or social approval. One who knows both rewards are with Allah can testify truthfully even against self, parents, and close relatives.

=135=

“O believers, stand firmly for justice as witnesses for Allah, even if it is against yourselves, your parents, or close relatives. Whether one is rich or poor, Allah is more worthy of both. Do not follow desire, lest you deviate from justice. If you distort testimony or turn away, surely Allah is fully aware of what you do.”

Qawwamin means standing repeatedly and firmly, not supporting justice only when convenient. Testimony is “for Allah,” so truth outranks tribe, class, affection, fear, and personal advantage.

Against oneself may mean admitting liability, confessing a debt, or giving evidence that brings loss. Parents and relatives possess immense rights, but those rights never include false testimony. Real loyalty does not help loved ones carry injustice into the Hereafter.

Rich and poor can each attract bias. Wealth may intimidate a witness or purchase sympathy; poverty may tempt someone to alter facts out of compassion. Allah is awla bihima—more knowing of and caring for both. Justice does not need falsehood to help the poor, and wealth does not create a higher class of truth.

This does not forbid mercy, legal aid, or policies addressing disadvantage. It forbids changing evidence and rights because of identity. Compassion works within truth through charity, mitigation where lawful, and reform—not by declaring the innocent guilty or the guilty innocent.

Hawa is desire that bends the scale. It can be hatred as well as love, ideological loyalty as well as greed. The witness should identify conflicts, disclose them, and withdraw where impartiality cannot be maintained.

Talwu can mean twisting the tongue or distorting testimony; i‘rad is refusing to testify or turning away. Justice can be defeated by altered speech or strategic silence. Digital editing, selective data, and omission of context are modern forms of twisting.

Allah is Khabir, fully aware of the fact, motive, pressure, and omission. Courts may miss distortion, but the witness for Allah knows a second hearing remains.

Having commanded believers to act like believers under pressure, verse 136 calls them to renew faith itself—in Allah, His Messenger, the Quran, and every scripture originally revealed before it.

=136=

“O believers, believe firmly in Allah, His Messenger, the Book He has revealed to His Messenger, and the Scriptures He revealed before. Whoever disbelieves in Allah, His angels, His Books, His messengers, or the Last Day has certainly strayed far away.”

“O believers, believe” is not pointless repetition. Iman must be renewed, deepened, and completed. A person may possess the name of faith while needing greater certainty, consistency, and acceptance of everything faith entails.

The Quran is described with a form suggesting its progressive revelation, while earlier scripture is mentioned as sent before. Muslims believe in the original Torah, Psalms, Gospel, and all genuine revelation, while taking the Quran as the final preserved criterion over what remains and what people later changed.

Faith forms one connected worldview: Allah sends angels with Books to messengers who warn of the Last Day. Removing one link damages the whole. One cannot claim loyalty to Allah while rejecting every messenger, or honour one prophet by denying another whom Allah truly sent.

Belief in angels protects revelation from being reduced to human intuition. Belief in Books makes guidance objective. Belief in messengers shows it lived. Belief in the Last Day gives judgment its final horizon.

“Strayed far” does not describe an honest question someone is trying to resolve. Doubts should be brought to knowledge, prayer, and sincere reflection. It describes rejection that leaves the integrated road of revelation.

The verse also prevents Muslims from insulting earlier prophets or treating sacred history as tribal competition. Musa and Isa, peace be upon them, belong to Muslim faith. Following Muhammad confirms rather than erases their original message.

Verse 137 now warns about people who repeatedly move between belief and disbelief and then increase in rejection. Religion has become a tactic rather than a covenant, and persistence closes the heart to forgiveness and guidance.

=137=

“Indeed, those who believe, then disbelieve, then believe again, then disbelieve again, and thereafter increase in disbelief—Allah will neither forgive them nor guide them to a way.”

The sequence depicts repeated, manipulative reversal ending in increased kufr. It may include people who enter and leave faith to create confusion, like those described in 3:72 who proposed believing at the start of the day and rejecting at its end so others might turn back.

The verse does not teach that a person who apostatises once, or repeatedly sins and doubts, can never repent. Its final phrase is “then increase in disbelief”: they persist, deepen opposition, and meet Allah on that trajectory. Elsewhere sincere return remains open before death.

Repeated choice changes the heart. Each cynical use of faith makes the next reversal easier until guidance is no longer desired as guidance. Allah’s withholding corresponds to what they insist upon, not arbitrary refusal of a penitent person.

Religion should not become a tool for marriage convenience, intelligence operations, political access, or public branding. Outward entry while planning rejection resembles the night plots criticised earlier. Allah knows when a declaration is search and when it is strategy.

Communities must still judge outwardly and avoid interrogating hearts without evidence. New Muslims and returning Muslims need support, not suspicion based on this verse. Final knowledge of motive belongs to Allah.

The warning also speaks to less dramatic inconsistency. A believer should not treat faith as a switch controlled by social setting—devout among one group, mocking among another. That is not necessarily legal disbelief, but it trains the divided character described in hypocrisy.

Guidance is a gift to protect through du‘a: “Our Lord, do not let our hearts deviate after You have guided us” (3:8). The next verses turn directly to hypocrites, giving them painful tidings and exposing their search for honour through hostile allies instead of believers.

=138=

“Give the hypocrites news that they will have a painful punishment.”

Bushra normally means good news. Using it for painful punishment is severe irony: people who built life on favourable appearances will receive an announcement whose truth cannot be spun. Their private reality finally becomes public.

Hypocrisy here is the major hypocrisy of professing faith while concealing disbelief and working against the believers. Ordinary laziness, sin, or a single hypocritical trait does not automatically place a Muslim in that final category. The Prophet warned believers about traits of hypocrisy so they would reform, not so communities would recklessly declare hidden unbelief.

Painful punishment fits the pain hypocrisy creates. An open opponent can be recognised; an internal pretender receives trust, learns vulnerabilities, and undermines from inside. Earlier verses described public obedience followed by night plotting, opportunism between camps, and lazy prayer for display.

The verse’s brevity creates suspense before the next description: they take disbelievers as protecting allies instead of believers, seeking honour with them. Their alliance is not ordinary kindness or cooperation but transfer of strategic loyalty for status against the community whose faith they claim.

Warning remains a form of mercy while heard in life. Verse 146 will later exempt hypocrites who repent, repair, hold firmly to Allah, and make religion sincere. Painful news is meant to produce that turn, not satisfy believers’ desire for enemies to suffer.

A reader should ask which actions are performed for Allah and which merely protect a religious image. Hypocrisy is cured through sincerity, truthful prayer, repentance, and willingness to accept loss rather than betray faith.

=139=

“They are those who take disbelievers as protecting allies instead of believers. Do they seek honour through them? Surely all honour belongs to Allah.”

Awliya here means deep protective and strategic allegiance, not every friendship, business relationship, treaty, or act of kindness with non-Muslims. The Quran permits justice and goodness toward peaceful people (60:8), and the Prophet traded, made treaties, accepted assistance under lawful conditions, and maintained neighbourly relations.

The condemned pattern displaces believers and strengthens hostile powers against them because the hypocrite expects status and protection. “Instead of” is central. He treats the believing community as a liability and opposition to it as the path to prestige.

‘Izzah is honour joined to strength and dignity. All of it belongs to Allah in origin. A ruler, institution, or social class possesses only what He temporarily grants. Seeking honour through disobedience places dignity in the hand of something unable to preserve its own power.

This does not require political isolation or uncritical loyalty to Muslims who act unjustly. Justice is for everyone, as verse 135 commanded. A Muslim may oppose a Muslim oppressor and cooperate with non-Muslims for a lawful good without transferring ultimate allegiance to hostility against faith.

The verse challenges cultural shame. Some people hide Islamic practice to gain acceptance, laugh when revelation is mocked, or repeat prejudice against believers to appear sophisticated. Respect purchased by self-contempt is not ‘izzah.

Nor should the verse be used to spread suspicion against minority Muslims fulfilling citizenship and public covenants. Honouring a lawful contract is itself Islamic. Strategic rulings depend on conduct, threat, competence, and qualified judgment, not slogans about identity.

The next verse gives a concrete boundary for gatherings: when Allah’s signs are denied and ridiculed, believers should not remain as approving participants until the conversation changes. Sitting comfortably in mockery is one way false alliances erode honour.

=140=

“He has already revealed to you in the Book that when you hear Allah’s signs being denied and ridiculed, do not sit with those people until they engage in a different conversation, or else you would become like them. Surely Allah will gather the hypocrites and disbelievers all together in Hell.”

The earlier revelation is commonly connected to 6:68, which told the Prophet to leave those who mock Allah’s signs until they enter another discussion. Repetition in Madinah turns the instruction toward gatherings where hypocrisy made people remain for social advantage.

The verse does not say debate every mocker indefinitely. Sometimes the faithful response is to object wisely; sometimes it is to leave. Remaining silently as an approving companion normalises contempt and may encourage more. Departure withdraws the audience mockery seeks.

“Until they engage in a different conversation” shows the separation is tied to the conduct, not necessarily permanent hatred of the people. When the mockery stops, ordinary lawful interaction may resume. The boundary protects faith without turning every relationship into total social exclusion.

“You would become like them” means sharing in the sin through willing participation and approval, not automatically becoming identical in every legal sense. Context, ability, and intention matter. A person compelled to be present, a journalist documenting responsibly, a teacher correcting misinformation, or someone unable to leave is not the same as an entertained supporter, though each should guard the heart and minimise exposure.

Online spaces are gatherings too. A group chat, stream, or comment thread devoted to ridiculing revelation can shape the heart even when one never types. Leaving, muting, reporting threats, or offering one useful correction may fulfil the principle better than endless angry engagement that amplifies the content.

Muslims must not misuse the verse to threaten or attack critics. It commands believers to regulate their own seating, not to punish every offensive speaker. Lawful responses remain governed by authority, covenant, wisdom, and justice.

The final gathering in Hell explains the danger of chosen companionship. Hypocrites sought honour by sitting with rejection and mockery; on the Last Day, the gathering becomes permanent.
