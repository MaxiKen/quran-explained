=41=

“So how will it be when We bring a witness from every community and bring you, O Prophet, as a witness over these people?”

Verse 40 assured that Allah overlooks not even the weight of a tiny particle and multiplies every good deed. Now the private record opens into a public courtroom. Each community will have a witness—its messenger, who conveyed Allah’s guidance and can testify how the message was received. Muhammad, peace be upon him, will be brought as a witness concerning his community and, according to a wider reading, as a confirming witness in the assembly of nations.

A messenger’s testimony establishes that people were not judged without warning. Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Isa, and the other prophets, peace be upon them, did not control hearts; they conveyed, demonstrated, warned, and gave good news. Their people remain responsible for whether they followed, distorted, ignored, or opposed. Muhammad’s mission likewise makes receiving the final revelation a trust, not a cultural possession.

The Prophet once asked Abdullah ibn Mas‘ud to recite the Quran to him. Ibn Mas‘ud wondered why he should recite to the one upon whom it was revealed, but the Prophet said he loved to hear it from another. When the recitation reached this verse, he said, “Enough for now.” Ibn Mas‘ud looked up and saw tears flowing from his eyes. The witness over the community heard the future scene and wept; a believer should not read it with a dry sense of entitlement.

Elsewhere Allah says that He made the Muslim community balanced so that it may be a witness over humanity while the Messenger is a witness over it (2:143). This honour carries responsibility. A community cannot credibly witness to justice while hiding revelation, mistreating neighbours, or presenting Islam through cruelty. Conveying accurately, living honestly, and refusing to attach cultural oppression to Allah’s law are part of preparing that testimony.

“How will it be?” leaves the scene open so the heart supplies its weight. There will be no public relations strategy before the prophets, no deleted record, and no power able to silence the witness. Yet the one who believed and followed need not hear only terror: a truthful messenger will also testify to faith, patience, and sincere struggle.

The next verse shows what those who rejected that messenger will wish when testimony begins. Rather than defend their worldly pride, they will long to be levelled into the earth and discover that no statement can be hidden from Allah.

=42=

“On that Day, those who disbelieved and disobeyed the Messenger will wish the earth were levelled over them, and they will not be able to hide any statement from Allah.”

The same people who once wanted prominence will wish to disappear into the ground. Law tusawwa bihim al-ard evokes being flattened with the earth until no trace remains. Surah al-Naba describes the disbeliever seeing what his hands sent ahead and saying, “If only I were dust” (78:40). An existence lived without concern for resurrection ends in longing for non-existence.

Disbelief and disobedience to the Messenger are joined because the Messenger delivered the path by which belief becomes lived obedience. Rejecting him is not a minor disagreement with a historical personality; it is refusing the authorised bearer of revelation. At the same time, Muslims should not apply the final judgment casually to every individual they meet. Allah alone knows what proof reached a person, in what form, and what the heart chose.

“They will not hide any statement” means concealment will fail. Other verses describe some initially denying their shirk or making excuses (6:22-24), while this verse says nothing remains hidden. The scenes can be understood as stages of the Day: attempts to lie are exposed, mouths may be sealed, limbs testify, and the complete truth becomes undeniable (36:65). There is no contradiction between a failed lie and final inability to conceal.

In this life, facts can be buried under influence, edited documents, intimidation, or repeated denial. Before Allah, skin, hands, feet, earth, angels, and messengers can all bear witness. The verse comforts those whose truth was suppressed and warns anyone relying on secrecy. As 3:5 says, nothing on earth or in heaven is hidden from Him.

The wise response is to bring hidden matters into repentance before they are brought into testimony. A private sin between servant and Allah need not be advertised; it should be abandoned and confessed to Him. A concealed wrong against another person requires restoration. Voluntary honesty now is lighter than involuntary exposure then.

Having shown the Day when speech cannot be hidden, the surah turns to speech before Allah in prayer: believers must know what they are saying. It then explains purification when water is unavailable, joining inward awareness to outward readiness for worship.

=43=

“O believers, do not approach prayer while intoxicated until you know what you are saying, nor in a state of major ritual impurity—except while passing through—until you have bathed. But if you are ill, travelling, have relieved yourselves, or been intimate with women and cannot find water, then seek clean earth and wipe your faces and hands with it. Surely Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving.”

This verse came during the gradual prohibition of alcohol. First, 2:219 taught that wine and gambling contain some benefit but far greater sin. Here intoxication was excluded from the times of prayer, making sustained drinking increasingly impossible. Finally, 5:90-91 ordered believers to avoid intoxicants altogether. The sequence trained a community away from an embedded habit, but the final ruling is complete prohibition, not permission outside prayer times.

The immediate reason is striking: prayer requires awareness of one’s words. Salah is not meaningless movement. A worshipper recites revelation, praises Allah, asks for guidance, and renews servanthood. Anything that removes reason—including intoxicating drugs—opposes that consciousness. Even without intoxication, the verse invites the believer to learn the meanings of recitation and resist praying on mental autopilot.

Janabah, the state following intercourse or sexual discharge, requires ghusl before prayer. “Except passing through” was understood by many commentators as passing through a mosque without remaining, while others connected it to a traveller unable to bathe. The Sunnah details the complete ritual bath. The state is ritual, not moral dirtiness; lawful intimacy is not sinful, but worship has an appointed preparation.

When illness makes water harmful, travel creates difficulty, or water cannot be found after using the toilet or sexual contact, tayammum replaces wudu or ghusl. “Touching women” was interpreted by some jurists as intercourse and by others as physical touch under specified conditions, producing familiar differences among legal schools about what breaks wudu. Believers can follow reliable scholarship without turning a valid disagreement into conflict.

Tayammum uses clean earth: strike or touch it and wipe face and hands as taught by the Prophet, peace be upon him. It preserves prayer when ordinary means are absent. A hospital patient, stranded traveller, or person in genuine water shortage is not cut off from worship.

The names al-‘Afuww and al-Ghafur frame concession as divine pardon and forgiveness. Allah does not demand harmful purification to prove devotion. He preserves both prayer and human capacity. The passage now returns to people who received part of scripture but traded guidance away, contrasting clean submission with the deliberate purchase of error.

=44=

“Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scripture? They purchase misguidance and wish that you too would lose the way.”

Receiving scripture is an honour only when guidance is followed. A “portion” may indicate their share of revealed knowledge or the incomplete use they made of it. Knowledge that should have led toward the confirming Messenger was exchanged for error through envy, status, tribal interest, or deliberate distortion.

The Quran often describes moral choice as commerce. In 2:16, hypocrites buy misguidance with guidance, making a trade that brings no profit. Here the price is especially tragic because the sellers were not entirely uninformed. They surrendered something already placed within reach. The language asks every reader what truth is being spent to preserve comfort or influence.

Their error did not remain private. They wished the believers would stray from al-sabil, the clear path. People often seek company in a choice that troubles the conscience; if others can be persuaded to join, deviation feels normal. Religious misinformation, selective quotation, and mockery of obedience can all become attempts to make another person lose the road.

The verse refers to particular hostile figures among the People of the Book in the Prophet’s setting, not to every Jew or Christian. The Quran itself praises upright people among earlier scriptural communities who recite Allah’s signs, believe in the Last Day, and hasten to good (3:113-115). Precision is part of justice: condemnation follows the described conduct, not ethnicity.

A Muslim can repeat the same pattern in another form. Possessing the Quran, memorising passages, or carrying a scholarly title does not prevent someone from selling guidance for money, politics, or popularity. Worse, a corrupt teacher may want students to follow so his own deviation remains unchallenged. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned about callers at the gates of Hell who speak the community’s language.

The believer should not become consumed by plots or assume human opponents know everything. The next verse redirects fear: Allah knows enemies fully, and His guardianship and help are sufficient. Awareness should produce reliance and wise caution, not paranoia or injustice toward whole populations.

=45=

“Allah knows your enemies best. Allah is sufficient as a Guardian, and Allah is sufficient as a Helper.”

The previous verse described people who wanted believers to lose the way. Human beings see words and actions; Allah sees motives, networks, hidden plans, and future outcomes. He knows who is truly hostile, who is merely ignorant, who may later become a friend, and who pretends friendship while designing harm. His knowledge prevents both naivety and reckless suspicion.

Calling Allah sufficient as Wali means He is enough as the protecting Guardian who manages the believer’s affairs. Calling Him sufficient as Nasir means His help determines the final outcome. The repetition of “Allah is sufficient” steadies a community that might feel intellectually, politically, or numerically vulnerable. Similar reassurance came after Uhud: “Allah is your Protector, and He is the best of helpers” (3:150).

Reliance does not eliminate practical measures. The Prophet, peace be upon him, used guards, intelligence, treaties, migration plans, armour, and careful consultation while relying completely on Allah. Tawakkul is not refusal to act; it is refusal to treat the means as independent gods. A locked door and a verified source can coexist with a heart that knows protection comes only by Allah’s permission.

The verse also limits retaliation. Because Allah alone knows enemies “best,” believers should not turn disagreement into accusations of treachery without evidence. Someone from a criticised group may be righteous, and someone wearing the right label may betray. Justice depends on demonstrated conduct, while secret hearts belong to Allah.

For an individual, the enemy may be an abuser, dishonest colleague, manipulative voice, or coordinated campaign. One should document, seek lawful help, set boundaries, and then place the result with the Guardian. Fear becomes unhealthy when it grants the enemy more presence in the heart than Allah. Repeating the two phrases restores proportion: sufficient as Guardian; sufficient as Helper.

The next verse identifies a specific form of hostility in Madinah: some among the Jewish community twisted words and used double expressions to insult the Prophet and stab at the religion. The Quran again describes “some,” judging deliberate speech without making ancestry itself the crime.

=46=

“Among those who are Jews are some who distort words from their proper places and say, ‘We hear and disobey,’ and, ‘Hear—may you not hear,’ and, ‘Ra‘ina,’ twisting their tongues and insulting the faith. If they had said, ‘We hear and obey,’ and, ‘Listen to us,’ and, ‘Unzurna,’ it would have been better and more upright for them. But Allah has rejected them for their disbelief, so they do not believe except a few.”

The verse begins with min—“among”—and criticises a faction defined by actions. Some altered revealed words or pulled them away from their intended meaning. Distortion can occur by changing text, mispronouncing expressions, removing context, or giving a true sentence a false interpretation. Sacred language becomes a weapon when the tongue is clever but the heart refuses guidance.

“We hear and disobey” is the opposite of the believers’ response in 2:285: “We hear and obey.” “Hear—may you not hear” could sound like a request while carrying an insult or curse. Ra‘ina in ordinary Arabic could mean “attend to us,” but hostile speakers exploited a similar sound or sarcastic inflection in their own language. Earlier, 2:104 taught believers to avoid the compromised expression and use unzurna, “look toward us” or “give us time,” instead.

The Quran notices not only dictionary meaning but tone and intent: “twisting their tongues and insulting the faith.” Plausible deniability does not hide mockery from Allah. The lesson reaches coded slurs, edited clips, malicious wordplay, and statements designed to wound while allowing the speaker to claim innocence.

Yet the verse offers them better words rather than treating reform as impossible: hear and obey; listen; give us consideration. Better speech would reflect a straighter inner posture. Islamic manners similarly encourage abandoning an ambiguous expression when it predictably causes harm, even if one can argue for an innocent meaning.

The closing consequence follows disbelief and persistent distortion, not Jewish identity. “Except a few” acknowledges that individuals differed, and elsewhere the Quran identifies Jews who were just. Muslims betray the verse if they turn a specific critique into antisemitism, inherited hatred, or false accusation. No one carries another’s sin (6:164).

The passage now addresses the People of the Book directly, inviting belief in the Quran as confirmation of original revelation before a severe judgment arrives. Warning itself is mercy while there is still time to respond.

=47=

“O People of the Scripture, believe in what We have revealed, confirming what is with you, before We efface faces and turn them backward, or reject them as We rejected the Sabbath-breakers. Allah’s command is always carried out.”

The call begins with belief, not punishment. The Quran confirms the original tawhid, prophethood, moral truth, and promised final messenger present in earlier revelation. People given scripture should recognise continuity rather than reject the final Book from rivalry. “What is with you” does not endorse every later alteration; it points to the divine truth still serving as witness.

The warning of effaced faces is deliberately alarming. Tams can mean obliterating distinguishing features, and turning faces backward presents a complete reversal of direction. Classical commentators allowed a literal punishment and also reflected on spiritual reversal—people who refuse to face guidance are turned back from the path. The literal possibility should not be dismissed, while the moral image remains powerful: knowledge without obedience can leave a person moving backward while imagining progress.

The Sabbath-breakers were members of an earlier Israelite community who knowingly violated a command by devising tricks around the prohibited day. Their story appears in 2:65 and 7:163-166. Invoking them warns that legal cleverness does not defeat the Lord of law. A technical scheme designed to preserve the forbidden benefit while pretending obedience may deepen guilt rather than remove it.

“Allah’s command is carried out” ends the illusion that judgment depends on human consent. When He decrees a consequence, no political position, ancestry, or scriptural title can prevent it. But before that decree, the imperative remains “believe.” Severe language can itself be a rescue alarm.

Muslims should hear the warning too. The Quran may be carried, recited, and used as identity while its plain demands are evaded. A believer who invents loopholes for interest, inheritance theft, or oppression should not read the Sabbath story only about others. Confirmation of scripture requires submission to it.

The next verse identifies the ultimate danger beneath every refusal: associating partners with Allah. If someone dies upon unrepented shirk, it is not forgiven, while every lesser sin remains subject to Allah’s will. This places hope and fear with theological precision.

=48=

“Surely Allah does not forgive associating partners with Him, but He forgives anything less than that for whomever He wills. Whoever associates partners with Allah has indeed committed a tremendous sin.”

This verse concerns the person who meets Allah upon shirk without repentance. It does not mean shirk can never be forgiven during life. Some of the greatest companions once worshipped idols; sincere Islam erased what came before. Allah tells even those who called upon other gods and committed grave sins that whoever repents, believes, and does good can have evil deeds changed into good (25:68-70).

If death closes upon unrepented shirk, however, the foundation of salvation is absent. Shirk gives the Creator’s exclusive right of worship to a creature—an idol, prophet, angel, spirit, ruler, or desire treated as ultimate lord. It is “tremendous” because it lies about reality, degrades worshippers before created things, and corrupts the purpose for which human beings were made.

Everything below shirk is “for whomever He wills.” A believer with major sins must therefore stand between hope and fear. Allah may forgive by mercy, through repentance, trials, intercession He permits, or good deeds; He may punish according to justice and ultimately bring a person with genuine tawhid from the Fire. No sinner should presume upon forgiveness, and no Muslim should declare that Allah cannot forgive a sin He placed beneath shirk.

Rights owed to people remain serious. Hoping for divine pardon does not authorise keeping stolen wealth or ignoring a victim. Restitution and reconciliation belong to repentance. The verse protects hope in Allah without turning hope into escape from responsibility.

It also guards tawhid from vague spirituality. Saying “all paths are the same” may sound generous but erases the central testimony of every prophet: worship Allah alone. Respect and justice toward people of other beliefs are commanded; agreement with shirk is not. The Prophet, peace be upon him, combined clear monotheism with covenants, neighbourly dealings, and protection of non-Muslim lives.

The next verses expose a subtler religious danger: claiming purity for oneself. Avoiding shirk and possessing scripture do not make self-congratulation safe. Allah alone knows whom He has truly purified, and His judgment will not wrong even the tiny thread on a date stone.

=49=

“Have you not seen those who declare themselves pure? Rather, Allah purifies whom He wills, and they will not be wronged even by the thread of a date stone.”

Tazkiyah can mean purification and a declaration of purity. The problem here is not working to cleanse the soul; that is a Quranic aim. It is claiming guaranteed righteousness, superiority, or salvation for oneself and one’s group. Earlier scriptural communities said Paradise belonged only to them or that punishment would touch them for only a few days (2:80, 111). A religious label became a certificate they issued to themselves.

Allah alone purifies in the decisive sense. He knows whether worship is sincere, repentance accepted, and hidden conduct consistent with public claims. A believer asks for purification, uses the means Allah taught, and hopes for acceptance without treating personal confidence as revelation. Surah al-Najm says, “Do not claim purity for yourselves; He knows best who has taqwa” (53:32).

This does not prohibit acknowledging a blessing or giving an honest account when needed. Yusuf said he was a knowledgeable guardian when offering to manage Egypt’s storehouses (12:55), and a witness may describe someone’s reliability. The distinction lies between relevant truth and self-exaltation, especially claims about inner rank with Allah.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, discouraged names that implied guaranteed purity and changed the name Barrah, “pious woman,” because Allah knows best who is righteous. He also warned against excessive praise to a person’s face. Communities should appreciate good without building celebrities who begin to believe they are beyond correction.

The fatil is the tiny fibre in the groove of a date stone. Allah will not reduce anyone’s due even by that. Those denied the exclusive spiritual status they claimed are not being wronged; they receive exact justice. Likewise, a humble believer outside an admired circle loses nothing because people failed to praise him.

The cure for self-certification is honest worship, repeated repentance, and trust in Allah’s judgment. The next verse names the claim for what it becomes when attributed to God: fabrication. Saying Allah has guaranteed a group or approved a practice without revelation is not harmless confidence but a clear sin.

=50=

“See how they fabricate lies against Allah. That alone is sufficient as a blatant sin.”

The command “see” asks the reader to examine the mechanism of religious falsehood. A human preference becomes a claim about God; inherited status becomes divine guarantee; a scholar’s invention becomes sacred law. Once Allah’s name is attached, people may surrender conscience to something He never commanded.

Fabrication is especially grave because it corrupts the source by which every other wrong should be corrected. If someone steals, revelation can call him back. If he declares that Allah authorised the theft, he has built a wall against repentance and may mislead others. The Quran therefore repeatedly asks, “Who is more unjust than one who invents a lie against Allah?”

Examples include declaring lawful what Allah forbade, forbidding His lawful gifts without proof, assigning salvation to a lineage, or speaking confidently about the unseen. Muslims must be careful with phrases such as “Islam says” and “Allah will never forgive you.” A personal view, cultural habit, dream, or viral quotation does not become revelation through confident delivery.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that whoever intentionally lies about him should take a seat in the Fire. Verifying hadith, quoting Quran in context, and distinguishing consensus from personal interpretation are acts of worship. A teacher can say “I do not know” without losing honour; pretending certainty is the greater failure.

“That alone is sufficient” means no additional scandal is needed to establish seriousness. People may excuse the speaker because he is charitable, eloquent, or useful to their faction. Lying about Allah remains a manifest sin even when wrapped in religious service.

The verse does not condemn sincere scholarly error reached through qualified effort. The Prophet taught that a judge who strives and is correct receives two rewards and one who strives and errs receives one. Fabrication involves speaking without right, manipulating evidence, or knowingly attributing falsehood—not every good-faith difference.

The following verse presents a startling example: people who received a share of scripture gave credibility to jibt and taghut and even declared pagan opponents more guided than believers. Rivalry with the Prophet pushed them into praising what their own revelation opposed.

=51=

“Have you not seen those who were given a portion of the Scripture? They believe in false objects of worship and oppressive powers, and say of the disbelievers, ‘These are better guided than the believers.’”

Jibt and taghut gather several forms of falsehood. Early commentators applied jibt to idols, sorcery, superstition, or anything empty worship is directed toward. Taghut is whatever crosses proper limits and is obeyed, worshipped, or followed in rebellion against Allah—such as a false god, satanic authority, or tyrant demanding ultimate submission.

Reports connect the verse to scriptural figures who sought alliance with pagan Quraysh against the Prophet and, to please them, claimed the idolaters’ way was better guided. If so, political hostility had pushed people with knowledge of monotheism to praise idolatry. The Quran exposes the moral price of an alliance purchased by denying known truth.

The criticism again concerns “those” who acted this way, not everyone given earlier scripture. Some recognised the Prophet and believed. Quranic fairness does not allow one faction’s betrayal to become permanent suspicion of an ethnicity or religion. At the same time, respect for individuals must not soften the judgment on endorsing false worship.

A similar temptation can affect Muslims when partisan rivalry becomes stronger than principle. Someone may praise corruption, racism, disbelief, or oppression in an ally merely because the ally attacks a disliked Muslim group. Guidance is then ranked by political usefulness rather than truth. The verse asks whether enmity has made the tongue certify what the heart once knew was false.

Belief in jibt also warns against superstition inside religious communities: fortune-telling, magic, charms involving shirk, and claims that created objects independently control destiny. The Prophet, peace be upon him, closed paths to divination and taught reliance upon Allah with lawful means.

Taghut should not become a slogan for reckless excommunication or violence. Identifying a person or institution with its most severe legal meaning requires knowledge, proof, and scholarly judgment. The concept first commands the believer to reject worship and ultimate obedience to falsehood, beginning with the tyranny of desire in one’s own heart.

The next verse states the consequence of knowingly inverting guidance: distance from Allah’s mercy leaves no true helper. The alliances for which truth was sold cannot rescue anyone whom Allah has rejected.

=52=

“They are the ones rejected by Allah. And whoever Allah rejects, you will never find for them any helper.”

The Quranic word la‘nah means being cast away from Allah’s mercy because of disbelief and persistent rebellion. It is not casual profanity. The people described received knowledge, supported false worship, and called paganism more guided in order to oppose truth. Their chosen separation from guidance led to separation from mercy.

No nasir can reverse Allah’s judgment. Political partners, religious titles, ancestors, wealth, and public praise may provide temporary shelter, but none can enter the divine court as an independent rescuer. Intercession itself belongs to Allah and occurs only by His permission for those He accepts.

The warning does not authorise believers to curse particular living individuals freely. A person may repent, and the hidden end is unknown. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was sent as mercy and often prayed for guidance even after mistreatment. General curses found in revelation apply to described categories; applying final damnation to a named person requires proof believers usually do not possess.

This distinction also prevents the verse from feeding prejudice. Allah’s rejection follows choices, not bloodline. A descendant of a righteous family can rebel, and a person raised amid idolatry can become a beloved servant through faith. The Quran’s own first audience included both realities.

For the believer, the practical question is where help is being sought at the cost of obedience. A person may not worship an ally, yet fear of losing that ally can make him conceal truth or support injustice. Verse 45 already supplied the cure: Allah is sufficient as Guardian and Helper. Depending on falsehood for rescue can eventually cut a person off from the only help that matters.

The passage next asks whether these critics possess a share of dominion. Their envy and stinginess show that, if authority belonged to them, they would deny others even the smallest speck. Divine generosity is contrasted with human attempts to control who deserves revelation and honour.

=53=

“Or do they have a share in the dominion? If they did, they would not give people even the speck on a date stone.”

The question is rhetorical. Revelation, prophethood, honour, and ultimate authority belong to Allah. Those objecting to His choice of Muhammad, peace be upon him, do not own a portion of the divine kingdom from which they can decide who receives guidance.

A naqir is the tiny mark or indentation on a date stone. The image exposes severe stinginess: if these envious critics controlled the treasury, people would not receive even that negligible amount. Their protest is therefore not principled concern for fair distribution but a wish to keep bounty away from others.

This continues the date-stone imagery of verse 49, where Allah wrongs no one even by a fatil, the fine thread. Human miserliness denies a naqir; divine justice preserves a fatil. The contrast teaches whom to trust with judgment. Allah gives without His stores diminishing, while people can treat honour as if another person’s gift steals from them.

Mulk includes political rule but is larger than politics: ownership, command, and the right to allocate. In 3:26 believers pray, “Owner of all dominion, You give dominion to whom You will and remove it from whom You will.” Authority in human hands is a temporary trust, not evidence that its holder can distribute spiritual worth.

The verse challenges gatekeeping within Muslim communities too. A family, ethnicity, class, or scholarly circle may behave as though knowledge and service belong to it, withholding opportunity from anyone outside. Protecting standards is necessary, but denying qualified people because their excellence threatens status is the stinginess condemned here.

A person should also notice small refusals in ordinary life: withholding useful information, a deserved recommendation, acknowledgment, or access that costs almost nothing. The naqir may be tiny, but it reveals the heart’s relationship to bounty.

The next verse names the disease directly as envy. Why resent the Prophet for Allah’s favour when the family of Ibrahim had already received scripture, wisdom, and magnificent authority? The history they claimed to honour proves that Allah may gather revelation and leadership in a chosen household.

=54=

“Or do they envy people for what Allah has given them of His bounty? Indeed, We gave the family of Ibrahim the Scripture and wisdom, and We gave them a mighty kingdom.”

Envy objects not merely to the recipient but to the Giver’s choice. “The people” here centrally includes Muhammad, peace be upon him, and the believers favoured with revelation. Some opponents recognised signs yet resented prophethood appearing outside the lineage or faction in which they expected to control it.

The answer points to the family of Ibrahim. Musa and Isa came through Ishaq’s line; Muhammad came through Isma‘il’s. Scripture and hikmah had long been granted across this blessed family. Dawud and Sulayman, peace be upon them, also received immense kingdoms. If combining revelation, wisdom, and authority was acceptable in earlier prophets, it is inconsistent to reject it now only when favour reaches another branch.

Hikmah is wisdom that places knowledge correctly in belief, judgment, and conduct. A book without wisdom can be quoted harshly or manipulated, while wisdom without revelation can lose its measure. Allah joined both in prophetic teaching.

The Quran does not deny that envy is emotionally real; it diagnoses and redirects it. Another person’s blessing does not reduce Allah’s treasury. The Prophet taught believers to ask blessing for others and seek from Allah’s bounty. Admiration can become healthy competition in good, while hasad wishes the blessing removed and may drive lies or sabotage.

Communities should examine envy when responding to new teachers, leaders, converts, younger workers, or people from overlooked backgrounds. Criticism may be necessary, but standards applied only when an outsider succeeds often reveal a territorial heart. One can verify qualifications without imagining divine favour is privately owned.

The family of Ibrahim also proves that ancestry alone does not save. Some descendants believed and others turned away, as the next verse says. Honour lies in following the revelation given to the family, not merely claiming connection to its names.

Thus the passage moves from the grant to the response: among them were believers, and among them were those who obstructed. For persistent rejection, Hell is sufficient as a blazing fire.

=55=

“Some of them believed in him, while others turned away from him. And Hell is sufficient as a blazing Fire.”

The pronoun “him” has been understood as referring to Muhammad, peace be upon him, or to Ibrahim and the revelation and favour just mentioned. The larger meaning remains: receiving evidence divides people according to response. Some believe; others sadda—turn away themselves and may obstruct others.

This distinction prevents collective judgment. Among People of the Book were Abdullah ibn Salam and others who recognised the Prophet and accepted Islam; others opposed him. Earlier communities likewise differed over their own messengers. The Quran repeatedly evaluates faith and action rather than assuming every member of a group has one heart.

Turning away can be quiet or active. A person may simply refuse to listen, or may block access, distort the message, threaten believers, and use influence to keep others from considering it. The second meaning is especially serious because one person’s pride becomes a barrier in another’s search for truth.

“Hell is sufficient” means no worldly punishment is required to complete divine justice. Sa‘ir is a fiercely kindled blaze. The brevity after the complex discussion of envy, politics, and scripture strips away excuses: if a person knowingly persists in rejection, the final consequence is not an academic abstraction.

Believers should convey this warning without delight in anyone’s suffering. The Prophet wept over people’s refusal and nearly exhausted himself in concern for them (18:6). Fear of Hell should produce sincere invitation, prayer for guidance, and fear for one’s own end—not superiority.

The verse also asks Muslims whether they merely inherited access to Muhammad’s message or truly believed in him. Faith includes trusting his authentic teaching, following his moral example, and not obstructing others through hypocrisy or repellent conduct. A Muslim label alongside systematic disobedience is not the settled safety this passage criticises in others.

The next verse expands the image of the Blaze with severe physical detail: skins burned through will be replaced so punishment remains perceptible. The description is meant to break denial and drive repentance while escape is still possible.

=56=

“Surely those who reject Our signs, We will cast them into a Fire. Whenever their skins are completely burned, We will replace them with new skins so they may continue to taste the punishment. Surely Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”

The Quran speaks plainly about bodily resurrection and bodily consequence. Hell is not reduced to regret or metaphor, though it includes spiritual anguish. Skin, sensation, replacement, and taste make the warning concrete. The One who created nerves and perception the first time can restore them after they are destroyed.

“Whenever” conveys continuity. In worldly burning, destroyed tissue may lose sensation; in the Hereafter, replacement prevents punishment from fading through bodily exhaustion. This is terrifying because the people described persistently rejected Allah’s signs, including the signs warning them of this very meeting.

Al-‘Aziz means the Almighty whom no one can overpower or escape. Al-Hakim means punishment is placed with perfect wisdom, never sadism or arbitrary rage. Human beings may punish excessively, target the innocent, or enjoy vengeance. Allah knows proof, intention, capacity, hidden repentance, and every surrounding fact. His might is governed by flawless wisdom.

The verse should never be used to imitate Hell through torture. Human criminal law has limits, due process, and prohibitions against cruelty; individuals have no authority to stage divine vengeance. Description of Allah’s punishment is a call to flee to Him, not permission to brutalise His creation.

Nor should graphic warning produce despair in the living. The same Quran says Allah forgives all sins for those who turn sincerely (39:53). A threat functions as mercy when it wakes someone before judgment. Disbelief can be abandoned, distorted teaching corrected, and rights restored today.

Modern knowledge about pain receptors may help a reader appreciate why skin is mentioned, but the verse did not depend on a scientific discovery for its truth and should not be forced into exaggerated scientific claims. Its primary purpose is moral and eschatological: rejection has a real end, and recreated bodies will experience it.

The Quran immediately balances warning with promise. Those who believe and do righteous deeds enter gardens, purified companionship, and deep shade forever. The reader is shown two destinations and invited to choose while action still has weight.

=57=

“But those who believe and do righteous deeds, We will admit them into Gardens beneath which rivers flow, remaining there forever. There they will have purified spouses, and We will admit them into deep and pleasant shade.”

Faith and righteous action stand together. Iman gives deeds their direction, while action demonstrates that faith is alive. The promise is not for flawless people who never needed forgiveness; it is for believers whose lives, including repeated repentance, were oriented toward Allah.

Gardens and flowing rivers answer the fire of the previous verse. Instead of skin repeatedly burned, there is abiding life; instead of blazing heat, zillan zalila—full, gentle, extending shade. In the climate of the first hearers, deep shade conveyed safety, rest, and relief, but Paradise exceeds every earthly comparison.

“Forever” removes the fear attached to every worldly pleasure. Health declines, gardens dry, loved ones part, and shade moves. In Paradise no death, eviction, boredom, or final goodbye threatens the gift. The Prophet, peace be upon him, described an announcement to its people: they will remain healthy and never become ill, live and never die, stay young and never grow old, and enjoy without misery.

Purified spouses are cleansed of bodily impurity, harmful character, jealousy, betrayal, and every defect that wounds companionship. The promise includes believing women and men; no inhabitant of Paradise is made miserable for another’s pleasure. Allah removes resentment from hearts (7:43), so relationships there carry intimacy without insecurity.

The verse does not encourage neglect of the earth while dreaming of the next world. “Righteous deeds” include trusts, justice, worship, family care, and generosity discussed throughout the surah. Every such act plants toward the Garden. Hope should energise responsibility rather than replace it.

The movement from final destinations to public duty is immediate. Verse 58 commands that trusts be returned to their owners and judgments made with justice. The path to gardens runs through keys, property, offices, testimony, and everyday decisions in which someone else depends on one’s honesty.

=58=

“Indeed, Allah commands you to return trusts to their rightful owners, and when you judge between people, judge with justice. Excellent indeed is what Allah instructs you. Surely Allah is All-Hearing, All-Seeing.”

Amanat is plural and broad: money held for another, an orphan’s estate, confidential information, a borrowed item, a vote, public office, professional knowledge, a child in one’s care, and the religious message itself. Every trust has an ahl, a rightful recipient or person qualified to carry it. It must be delivered rather than used as private property.

Reports connect the verse to the conquest of Makkah, when the key of the Ka‘bah was returned to its keeper, Uthman ibn Talhah, despite the new power Muslims held. Whether considered as the immediate setting or an example, the lesson is striking: victory does not cancel another person’s trust. Authority is proved through restitution.

The second command addresses judgment bayna al-nas—between people, without restricting justice to Muslims, friends, or one’s own group. A judge, parent, manager, teacher, mediator, and online administrator all make decisions that distribute rights or consequences. Justice requires hearing relevant sides, using sound standards consistently, refusing bribery and favouritism, and admitting conflicts of interest.

The order is significant. Leadership itself is an amanah, so public roles should be given to people of competence and integrity, not merely relatives or loyal supporters. The Prophet, peace be upon him, told Abu Dharr that authority is a trust and can become disgrace and regret on the Day of Resurrection except for the one who takes it rightfully and fulfils its duties. A person should neither seek office from vanity nor treat it as private reward.

“Excellent is what Allah instructs you” presents law as beautiful counsel. Returning a deposit and issuing an impartial ruling may cost immediate advantage, but they build a society in which life, property, and dignity can stand. Corruption begins when officeholders treat public resources as spoils.

Allah hears the testimony, whispered pressure, and private promise; He sees the document, gesture, and hidden exchange. Human oversight is necessary, but no closed meeting escapes al-Sami‘ al-Basir. The next verse explains how authority should function beneath revelation and how disputes—including disputes with authority—must return to Allah and the Messenger.

=59=

“O believers, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those entrusted with authority among you. If you disagree over anything, refer it to Allah and the Messenger, if you truly believe in Allah and the Last Day. That is better and best in outcome.”

The command “obey” is repeated for Allah and the Messenger, whose authentic instruction is binding, while it is not repeated independently before those in authority. Muslim rulers, judges, scholars, commanders, parents, and responsible leaders are obeyed within their proper sphere and within obedience to Allah. Their authority is real but delegated and limited.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said obedience is required in what is right and that there is no obedience when commanded to disobey Allah. A leader cannot make oppression, fraud, or neglect of prayer lawful. Yet disagreement with a policy is not automatically proof of sin, and personal preference does not remove the duty to obey lawful order. Stable society requires both accountable authority and citizens disciplined by more than impulse.

“Among you” ties leadership to the believing community and its trust. Verse 58 has already demanded competence and justice from those who hold office. Obedience is therefore not a personality cult; it operates inside an ethical structure of consultation, counsel, law, and answerability.

When disagreement arises “over anything,” return it to Allah and the Messenger—to the Quran and the Prophet’s authentic Sunnah. After his death, returning to him means returning to reliably transmitted teaching, understood with sound methods. Isolated quotation without language, context, or the full body of evidence can pretend to refer to revelation while actually referring to desire. Qualified scholarship and sincere consultation help communities make the return honestly.

Differences will still occur because evidence can admit more than one valid interpretation. Returning to revelation does not require declaring every juristic disagreement rebellion. Scholars may reach different conclusions while sharing sources and method. The verse condemns replacing the criterion, not careful reasoning within it.

Faith in the Last Day makes submission meaningful when a ruling costs the self. “Better and best in outcome” includes worldly coherence and the final consequence before Allah. The next verse contrasts people who claim faith yet seek a corrupt rival authority precisely because it may give the verdict they desire.

=60=

“Have you not seen those who claim to believe in what was revealed to you and what was revealed before you? They seek judgment from false authorities, although they were commanded to reject them. Satan desires to lead them far astray.”

The key word is “claim.” Their speech announces faith in the Quran and earlier revelation, but when a real dispute threatens their interest, they prefer taghut—a false, corrupt, or rebellious authority expected to judge against Allah’s truth. The courtroom reveals what slogans conceal.

Reports mention a hypocrite who wanted to avoid the Prophet’s judgment and take a dispute to an authority more open to manipulation. Whatever the specific occasion, the principle is clear: selecting a judge because he accepts bribery, tribal pressure, fabricated evidence, or a knowingly false rule is not neutral forum shopping. It is using power to escape justice.

Believers were commanded to reject taghut in 2:256 before grasping the firmest handhold of faith. Rejection begins with refusing to worship false gods and refusing ultimate moral authority to rebellion against Allah. It also includes refusing fortune-tellers, tyrannical demands, and corrupt adjudication designed to legalise wrong.

The verse should not be flattened into the claim that every non-Muslim institution or use of a civil court is worship of taghut. Muslims may enter contracts, use courts to recover rights, and live under public regulations where these do not require sin, especially where no Islamic judiciary exists. Yusuf administered within Egypt, and the Prophet honoured lawful agreements. The condemned choice is a deliberate preference for falsehood because it defeats a known right, or a belief that human rebellion is better than Allah’s judgment.

Nor may individuals weaponise the label taghut to excommunicate people or justify violence. Such rulings involve definitions, evidence, impediments, authority, and consequences beyond slogans. The verse first demands self-examination: do I want revelation when it supports me but search for another standard when it restrains me?

Satan’s destination is dalalan ba‘ida, far-reaching error. The drift begins with one convenient verdict and can end with aversion to the Messenger himself. Verse 61 will show that next stage: when invited to what Allah revealed and to the Messenger, hypocrites turn away forcefully. Thus verse 60 closes this file at the point where claimed belief is tested by willingness to accept judgment rather than merely praise revelation.
