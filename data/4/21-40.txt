=21=

“And how could you take it back after you have been intimate with one another and they have taken from you a solemn covenant?”

The question continues the prohibition in verse 20. A husband may not recover a wife’s dowry merely because he wants to end the marriage and replace her. Allah now gives two reasons that go deeper than ownership: the spouses shared the closest intimacy, and marriage placed a weighty covenant upon them.

Afda ba‘dukum ila ba‘d describes one spouse reaching the other without a barrier. It includes sexual intimacy, but its wording also evokes the privacy, vulnerabilities, secrets, and ordinary life shared in a marriage. A person who was once trusted with another’s body and inner world should not turn that access into leverage when affection changes. Revealing private details, fabricating accusations, or using knowledge gained in marriage to force repayment betrays the intimacy Allah invokes here.

The “solemn covenant”—mithaqan ghaliza—is exceptionally strong Quranic language. The same expression is used for the covenant Allah took from the prophets (33:7) and for His covenant with the Children of Israel (4:154). Marriage is not identical to prophethood, but the shared wording shows that it is far more than a casual arrangement. Through offer, acceptance, witnesses, and agreed rights, a woman entrusts herself to a bond formed in Allah’s name. The Prophet, peace be upon him, reminded men in his Farewell Sermon that they took women as wives through Allah’s trust and that mutual rights follow.

This covenant requires good treatment while marriage lasts and justice if it ends. The Quran repeatedly commands either retaining a wife honourably or releasing her with kindness (2:229-231). Divorce may be lawful, and sometimes necessary, but lawful separation does not erase debts, permit humiliation, or make the previous life together worthless. A believer’s character is tested when he no longer expects companionship from the person whose rights he must settle.

The verse also speaks to both spouses through the phrase “one another.” Intimacy is mutual, even though the financial prohibition here addresses the husband. Neither side should publish bedroom details or weaponise confidential knowledge. The Prophet compared those who disclose a spouse’s intimate secrets to the people in the worst position before Allah on the Day of Resurrection.

After protecting the covenant from financial betrayal, the surah identifies relationships in which marriage itself is forbidden. It begins with a custom directly connected to inheritance: marrying a woman who had been married to one’s father.

=22=

“Do not marry women whom your fathers married, except what has already occurred. It was indeed a shameful, hateful, and evil practice.”

Some pre-Islamic heirs treated a deceased father’s widow as part of the estate. A son could claim a stepmother, prevent her from remarrying, or marry her himself. Verse 19 abolished inheriting women by force; this verse permanently forbids the marriage itself. Once a woman has entered a valid marriage with a man’s father or grandfather, she is forbidden to his sons and grandsons, whether or not they are related to her by blood.

The prohibition is expressed as “women whom your fathers married,” not merely “your fathers’ wives,” drawing attention to the father’s marital bond. The family home must remain safe from sexual competition across generations. A stepmother occupies a protected place linked to the father, and turning that relationship into a potential marriage confuses loyalties, violates modesty, and wounds the dignity of the whole household.

Allah gives the former custom three descriptions. Fahishah is a gross indecency that crosses recognised moral limits. Maqt is intense hatred or abhorrence; Arabs even called a child born from such a union al-maqti. “An evil way” judges not only an isolated act but the road it creates. Sexual boundaries shape generations, so a corrupt path in one household can produce lasting confusion and resentment.

“Except what has already occurred” does not approve the practice. It means that acts before the prohibition are not retroactively punished when people enter Islam and stop. The Quran often combines firm reform with mercy toward the past: 2:275 similarly says that whoever stops taking interest after the warning may keep what previously passed, while future conduct belongs to Allah’s judgment. Repentance does not require a new believer to carry endless blame for something done before guidance reached him.

This single case now opens into a comprehensive list. Verse 23 maps the women permanently forbidden through blood, breastfeeding, and marriage, then forbids combining two sisters at one time. The aim is not arbitrary restriction but a stable family in which close relationships can exist without being treated as a field of sexual possibility.

=23=

“Forbidden to you are your mothers, your daughters, your sisters, your paternal and maternal aunts, your brother’s daughters, your sister’s daughters, your milk-mothers who nursed you, your milk-sisters, your wives’ mothers, your stepdaughters under your care—born of wives with whom you have consummated marriage; but if you have not consummated the marriage, there is no blame upon you—nor the wives of your own sons, nor two sisters together, except what has already passed. Surely Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The verse gathers three sources of permanent marital prohibition. Blood relationship includes every mother and grandmother upward, every daughter and granddaughter downward, full or half-sisters, paternal and maternal aunts, and nieces through brothers or sisters. These are not merely people one happens not to marry; they are maharim around whom family life, care, travel rules, and modest interaction are organised.

Breastfeeding creates another family bond. A woman who nursed a child under the legally recognised conditions becomes a milk-mother, and her children become milk-siblings. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that breastfeeding makes unlawful what birth makes unlawful. The Sunnah and jurists explain questions of age and number of feedings, so uncertain cases should be checked carefully rather than decided from memory. Milk kinship does not transfer inheritance or lineage, but it does establish marital boundaries.

Marriage itself creates prohibitions. A wife’s mother becomes permanently forbidden as soon as the valid marriage contract is made. A wife’s daughter becomes forbidden if the marriage with her mother was consummated; if the couple separated before consummation, the daughter is not prohibited by that unconsummated bond. “Under your care” describes the usual tenderness and proximity of stepdaughters, but the ruling does not disappear merely because one was raised elsewhere. The wife of one’s biological son or grandson is also permanently forbidden. The phrase “sons from your loins” excluded adopted sons from being treated as biological for this rule, while Islam still commands care and honour for adopted children without changing lineage (33:4-5, 37).

Two sisters may not be wives of the same man at the same time. The Sunnah also forbids combining a woman with her paternal or maternal aunt, because the rivalry likely to accompany co-wifedom would tear a close kinship Allah commands people to preserve. Once a marriage and its waiting period have fully ended, marrying the sister is not permanently forbidden, but combining them is.

“Except what has passed” again closes the pre-revelation account for those who stopped. Forgiveness and mercy at the end do not weaken the boundaries; they show why guidance arrives—to cleanse lives rather than trap people forever in an ignorant past. Having named permanent prohibitions, the next verse adds married women as temporarily unavailable and then explains the lawful path: a genuine union sought with wealth, chastity, and an obligatory dowry.

=24=

“Also forbidden are married women, except those in your possession. This is Allah’s decree upon you. Lawful to you are all beyond these, provided that you seek them with your wealth in lawful marriage, desiring chastity, not fornication. So for the benefit you receive through marriage, give them their dowries as an obligation. There is no blame if you mutually agree to something after the obligation. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

A woman already joined in a valid marriage cannot be married by another man. This simple boundary protects lineage, trust, and the rights of both spouses. Attraction and mutual willingness do not dissolve an existing covenant; it must end lawfully, and the prescribed waiting period must pass, before another marriage can begin.

The historical exception concerned female captives in a lawful war under the old international system of slavery. Classical law held that captivity could end a prior marriage to an enemy combatant’s society, subject to a waiting period that established an empty womb. It never authorised kidnapping, trafficking, rape, or private groups seizing women. The Prophet, peace be upon him, commanded good treatment of those under authority, and the Quran repeatedly opened routes to emancipation. With lawful slavery no longer existing, this phrase cannot be used to create it anew or to excuse modern abuse.

Beyond the prohibited relationships, marriage is lawful when sought bi-amwalikum—with the financial commitment of mahr and maintenance—and with ihsan, the intention to guard chastity. Musafahah describes sex poured out without covenant or responsibility. The contrast is not between affection and religion; spouses may desire and enjoy one another. It is between desire protected by rights and desire that takes pleasure while avoiding duties.

The “benefit” received refers in the mainstream Sunni understanding to marital intimacy within a valid nikah, after which the agreed mahr is due in full. The wording was also discussed in relation to temporary marriage because it had been permitted briefly under exceptional early circumstances, but authentic Sunnah records its final prohibition. The enduring Quranic framework joins intimacy to an actual marriage, lineage, waiting periods, divorce rules, and continuing obligations—not a timed exchange that ends automatically.

After the mahr has been fixed, husband and wife may mutually agree to adjust its timing, increase it, or have the wife willingly remit part, as verse 4 explained. “Mutually” excludes pressure. If consummation occurs, the financial right cannot be treated as payment optional at the husband’s convenience.

Allah knows the intention behind the contract and wisely places pleasure inside responsibility. For someone unable to afford the ordinary expectations of marriage in the verse’s original society, the next verse addressed marriage to a believing enslaved woman—again insisting on permission, mahr, chastity, and human equality before Allah.

=25=

“Whoever among you cannot afford to marry free believing women may marry believing women from among those in your possession. Allah knows best your faith; you are from one another. Marry them with the permission of their people and give them their dowries fairly, provided they are chaste—neither openly promiscuous nor taking secret lovers. Once married, if they commit sexual immorality, they receive half the punishment of free women. This is for those of you who fear falling into sin, though patience is better for you. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The verse speaks into a society where slavery already existed and where status strongly controlled marriage. A man without the means to marry a free believing woman was permitted to marry an enslaved believing woman rather than fall into zina. She was not to be used without commitment: a real marriage, the permission required under that legal system, and a fair dowry paid to her were all commanded.

“Allah knows best your faith; you are from one another” interrupts the language of social rank with spiritual equality. Free and enslaved believers come from the same human family described in verse 1 and belong to one community of faith. An enslaved woman’s hidden iman might exceed that of people who looked down on her. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that Allah looks not at bodies and wealth but at hearts and deeds.

Her chastity is described in two directions: she is neither openly sexually available nor maintaining a secret lover. The same moral standard applies to men. Marriage is not a cover placed over an ongoing affair but a covenant requiring exclusivity. The verse’s concern for her mahr and reputation gave legal personhood in a world that often treated enslaved women only as assets.

If a married enslaved woman committed proven zina, her prescribed punishment was half that of a free woman—fifty lashes rather than one hundred. Because stoning cannot be halved, the verse is among the evidence that the referenced “free women” here are those whose Quranic punishment is divisible. Her reduced punishment recognises the constraints and vulnerability surrounding enslaved life. Such penalties require lawful authority, legal process, and strict proof; this is no licence for angry spouses or crowds.

Patience is called better because such a marriage carried difficult legal consequences in that social structure, including questions about the status of children according to ownership rules. The preference is not contempt for the believing woman; the verse has just affirmed shared faith and origin. It counsels restraint where a person can remain chaste without entering a complicated arrangement.

Today no one may use this passage to buy a bride, revive ownership, or bypass free consent. Its living lessons include chastity under financial pressure, refusal to rank faith by class, and the duty to protect vulnerable women through enforceable rights. The closing names promise forgiveness for human weakness and mercy through lawful alternatives. The next verses explicitly explain that these rules aim at clarity, guidance, repentance, and relief—not needless burden.

=26=

“Allah intends to make things clear to you, guide you to the ways of those before you, and turn to you in mercy. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

After detailed family rules, Allah states their purpose. He intends bayan—clarity. Desire can make boundaries seem arbitrary precisely when a person wants to cross them. Revelation names who may marry, how property is protected, and what obligations accompany intimacy so that people do not have to build family life on impulse, inherited abuse, or social experimentation without moral direction.

He also guides to the sunan, the sound paths and established ways, of those before. The essential ethics of marriage and chastity did not begin with the Madinan community. Earlier prophets called people to worship Allah, preserve lineage, honour covenants, and avoid sexual indecency. The Quran restores that continuous road while correcting distortions that entered communities over time. Guidance is therefore both ancient in principle and freshly clear in its final legislation.

“And turn to you” uses the language of tawbah. Divine law is not only a list by which people are condemned; it is a path by which damaged people return. Some first hear these verses after unlawful relationships, exploitative marriages, broken kinship, or financial betrayal. Allah’s intention toward those who respond is to accept their return. They must stop the wrong and restore human rights, but they are not ordered to despair.

Al-‘Alim knows human bodies, emotions, family pressures, and hidden motives. Al-Hakim places each command with wisdom. The pairing answers the suspicion that modern people understand relationships while revelation does not. Human experience can help apply a command wisely, but it does not exceed the Creator’s knowledge of the people involved or the consequences that unfold across generations.

Three divine intentions flow through verses 26-28: to clarify and guide, to accept repentance, and to lighten burdens. Against them stands a different intention in the next verse—the wish of those who follow uncontrolled desires that believers should lean far away from the path. The conflict is not between freedom and a God who dislikes people; it is between two destinations offered to a human being whose weakness Allah already knows.

=27=

“Allah intends to turn to you in mercy, but those who follow their desires want you to deviate with a tremendous deviation.”

The repetition of Allah’s intention to accept repentance deserves attention. He does not merely permit return reluctantly. He wants servants to turn from confused and harmful paths toward Him. The restrictions just given are doors into that return: marriage instead of fornication, mahr instead of exploitation, clear kinship instead of family disorder, and chastity instead of secret betrayal.

Opposing this mercy are “those who follow desires.” The verse does not condemn having desire. Attraction was created by Allah and receives a lawful home in marriage. The problem is ittiba‘ al-shahawat—placing appetites in front and following wherever they lead. Once desire becomes guide rather than passenger, it resents every boundary and recruits arguments, entertainment, pressure, and ridicule to make restraint appear abnormal.

Their wish is not described as a small adjustment but maylan ‘azima, a great leaning away. Major moral change often occurs through accumulated tilts: modesty is mocked, private temptation is commercialised, betrayal is renamed self-discovery, and those who remain chaste are made to feel ashamed. A society may still use the words love and consent while powerful people exploit bodies, abandon children, and sell desire as an industry. The Quran asks readers to judge the whole road, not only the appealing first step.

The contrast also lives within each person. Allah calls the heart toward tawbah while the lower self calls it toward delay and justification. A screen, friendship, private message, or workplace attachment can become the site of that contest. The response is to leave the path early, before a slight leaning becomes a great one, and to seek help without exposing sins unnecessarily.

The next verse prevents the law from being misunderstood as hostility toward human needs. Allah knows that people are weak and explicitly intends relief. Boundaries are not denial of human nature; they channel a vulnerable nature away from consequences it is often too short-sighted to see.

=28=

“Allah intends to lighten your burdens, for the human being was created weak.”

The third statement of divine intent is relief. The One who set the boundaries knows the weakness of those asked to observe them. Human beings tire, desire companionship, fear loneliness, respond strongly to attraction, struggle with money, and make poor judgments under pressure. The law does not assume angels; it guides embodied people.

Relief appears in the surrounding verses. Marriage makes intimacy lawful. A person unable to meet one social route to marriage was given another in the historical setting. Repentance repairs a past instead of making sin an unchangeable identity. Penalties took differing levels of social vulnerability into account. Elsewhere travellers shorten prayer, the sick may postpone fasting, and necessity has carefully defined concessions. As 2:185 says regarding Ramadan, Allah intends ease, not hardship.

Ease does not mean that every command must match immediate comfort. Discipline can feel difficult while saving a person from a heavier result. A guardrail restricts movement at the edge because falling is worse. The person who refuses a forbidden relationship may feel pain today while being spared betrayal, confused lineage, disease, guilt, or a shattered household tomorrow. Allah’s lightening is measured by perfect knowledge of the whole journey, not by the mood of one moment.

“Created weak” should produce humility rather than an excuse. Acknowledging weakness leads a believer to avoid isolation with temptation, lower the gaze, fast when unable to marry as the Prophet advised, choose supportive companions, and ask Allah for steadiness. Claiming to be beyond temptation often removes the very precautions that preserved a person.

Weakness includes financial temptation as well as sexual desire. The passage therefore moves from marriage to property and life: do not consume one another’s wealth falsely, and do not kill one another or yourselves. Allah’s mercy protects the human being from appetites for money, aggression, and despair just as it protects from uncontrolled sexual desire.

=29=

“O believers, do not consume one another’s wealth unjustly, but only through trade conducted by mutual consent. And do not kill one another or yourselves. Surely Allah is ever Merciful to you.”

The surah has already protected orphan property, dowries, and inheritance. This verse states the general economic rule: another person’s wealth is unlawful unless a valid basis transfers it. Batil includes theft, fraud, bribery, gambling, interest, embezzlement, false claims, defective contracts, and any method that takes value without a right recognised by Allah.

Trade is singled out as a lawful route, but even trade requires taradin—real mutual satisfaction. Consent obtained through threat, material deception, hidden defects, exploitation of incapacity, or terms a party was never allowed to understand is not the healthy agreement the verse praises. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said buyer and seller retain a choice while they have not separated and that if they are truthful and disclose defects their transaction is blessed, but concealment and lies erase its blessing.

Modern contracts can be technically dense while remaining morally false. Manipulative digital subscriptions, forged land sales, insider dealing, wage theft, counterfeit products, and the misuse of personal data all belong under the demand for valid right and informed agreement. Business skill does not turn batil into trade. The verse calls the property “your wealth” collectively because taking from another corrodes the security on which everyone’s economic life stands.

“Do not kill anfusakum” can mean do not kill one another, since believers are like one body, and it also plainly includes killing oneself. Economic injustice and violence stand together because greed frequently destroys lives: inheritance murders, robbery, exploitative labour, and despair under debt all expose their connection. No person owns his life absolutely; it is a trust from Allah.

The prohibition of suicide requires compassion toward someone in crisis, not condemnation that drives them further into isolation. Immediate safety, professional care, supportive presence, and removal of means can be religious duties. Mental illness can greatly affect responsibility, which Allah judges with complete knowledge. The verse’s closing—Allah is Merciful to you—frames preservation of life as mercy.

Financial integrity and personal safety are therefore acts of receiving divine compassion. The next verse warns that deliberate aggression and injustice in these matters lead to Fire. Mercy sets the boundary before punishment comes; it does not make the boundary optional.

=30=

“Whoever does that through aggression and injustice, We will cast them into the Fire. And that is easy for Allah.”

“That” points back to consuming wealth falsely and killing persons, though its warning naturally fits the pattern of transgression throughout the passage. Two words expose the moral character of the act: ‘udwan is aggressive crossing of a boundary, and zulm is placing a thing where it does not belong and violating a right. The offender did not merely stumble in an ambiguous transaction; he advanced against someone through wrong.

The threat of Fire recalls verse 10, where consuming an orphan’s wealth is described as eating flames into the stomach. Here unjust gain and unjust blood meet the same final justice. A profit may be celebrated and a killing hidden, but Allah can turn what looked like success into punishment.

A person guilty of financial or physical wrong must not turn the severity of the warning into despair. Before death, repentance remains open, but rights owed to people require action. Stolen money must be restored, fraudulent benefit disgorged, and physical harm addressed through the victim’s lawful rights. Worship does not substitute for restitution. The Prophet, peace be upon him, described the truly bankrupt person as one who arrives with prayer, fasting, and charity but has insulted, slandered, taken wealth, shed blood, and struck others; his good deeds are distributed among those he wronged.

After this concentrated warning, the next verse opens a wide promise. If believers guard themselves from the major sins, Allah will erase lesser wrongs and bring them into an honourable entrance. Fear is not intended to paralyse; it makes the road to forgiveness visible.

=31=

“If you avoid the major sins forbidden to you, We will erase your lesser misdeeds and admit you through an honourable entrance.”

The Quran distinguishes kaba’ir, major sins, from lesser misdeeds. Scholars identified a major sin through signs such as a specific threat of Fire or punishment, a legal penalty, a curse, or an explicit declaration of Allah’s anger. Shirk, unlawful killing, zina, consuming interest or orphan wealth, false accusation of chastity, serious disobedience to parents, and bearing false witness are among well-established examples. Lists vary because the texts mention many grave acts and because a sin’s seriousness can grow with circumstance.

Avoidance means more than not completing the final act. Ijtinab is to keep to one side, away from the approaches. A person avoids zina by guarding looks, private meetings, messages, and fantasies that cultivate it. One avoids unjust wealth by learning a transaction before signing it and refusing income built on deception. The Prophet, peace be upon him, described halal and haram as clear with doubtful matters between them; protecting oneself from the doubtful protects religion and honour.

The promise is immense: Allah covers and erases lesser sins for those who sincerely preserve the major boundaries and fulfil the means of forgiveness. The five daily prayers, Friday to Friday, and Ramadan to Ramadan were described by the Prophet as expiations for what lies between them when major sins are avoided. This does not make minor sins harmless. Persistence, contempt, public boasting, or harm to others can magnify what a person keeps calling small.

Nor does the verse mean that someone who committed a major sin is lost. Major sins require specific repentance, and Allah says in 39:53 that those who have transgressed against themselves must not despair of His mercy. If another person’s right is involved, repentance includes restoring it. Avoidance protects; tawbah repairs after a fall.

The “honourable entrance” is Paradise and the dignified welcome into it. The contrast with the humiliating punishment of transgressors is deliberate. Leaving a major sin may cost status, money, or pleasure in the short term, but Allah calls the final entry karim—noble, generous, and honoured.

One path into sin is resentment of what someone else has been given. The next verse treats envy and unproductive comparison, directing both men and women away from coveting another’s allocation and toward their own effort and prayer for Allah’s bounty.

=32=

“Do not covet what Allah has given some of you over others. Men will have a share of what they earn, and women will have a share of what they earn. Ask Allah for His bounty. Surely Allah has perfect knowledge of all things.”

The verse forbids tamanni that resentfully longs for another person’s distinctive allotment or wishes it removed. People differ in wealth, health, beauty, ability, family roles, opportunities, and legal responsibilities. Constant comparison can turn difference into objection against Allah and hostility toward the person who received the gift.

This does not prohibit ambition, learning from others, seeking equal justice, or working to remove oppression. A woman claiming the inheritance Allah assigned her is not envying a man; a worker seeking fair pay is not rejecting divine decree. The forbidden attitude covets another’s gift in a way that neglects one’s own duties or resents the Giver’s wisdom. Constructive aspiration says, “May Allah bless them and grant me from His bounty,” rather than, “Why should they have it?”

“Earn” includes moral acquisition as well as lawful worldly effort. A person cannot inherit someone else’s patience, prayer, or courage. The Quran says elsewhere that no bearer carries another’s burden and that a person has only what he strives for (53:38-39). Differences in role do not prevent equal access to Allah’s pleasure.

The remedy for envy is du‘a: ask Allah from His fadl, bounty beyond what anyone can demand as a wage. Prayer turns the eyes from another servant’s hand to the limitless treasury of the Lord. It should be joined to effort, gratitude, and prayer for blessing in what others possess. The Quran teaches believers to seek refuge from the envier’s harm (113:5) and shows how envy can corrupt judgment and relationships.

Allah knows what each gift would do to each heart and what each hidden effort deserves. The next verse returns specifically to property: every estate has heirs appointed by Allah, and pledged relationships also carry rights. One need not covet an heir’s share, because the Witness has assigned obligations and sees whether they are fulfilled.

=33=

“For everyone We have appointed heirs to what parents and close relatives leave. And as for those with whom you have made a binding pledge, give them their due. Surely Allah is Witness over all things.”

Mawali here includes the heirs and relatives entitled to succeed to property. Their rights do not arise because the executor likes them or because they were strongest at the funeral. Allah “appointed” them. This returns to the fixed shares of verses 7-12 and corrects the envy addressed in verse 32: another heir’s portion is not available for someone else to covet.

The second sentence refers to binding alliances made by oath. Early in Madinah, bonds of brotherhood between emigrants and helpers included inheritance arrangements at a time when many emigrants had left family and property behind. Later revelation gave blood relatives priority in inheritance (8:75). Many commentators therefore understood the remaining “due” of pledged allies as support, counsel, loyalty, and any valid bequest rather than a fixed inheritance that overrides Quranic heirs. Other juristic explanations preserve particular contractual rights without allowing them to cancel the established shares.

The principle of honouring covenants remains broad. Business partnerships, guarantees, marriage settlements, employment agreements, and promises of lawful support should not be discarded once benefit has been received. “Give them their due” places action after words. A pledge that never produces the promised right is evidence against its maker.

Allah is Shahid, present as Witness over every arrangement. He saw the handshake no one recorded, the clause hidden from an inexperienced party, the service faithfully performed, and the promise quietly abandoned. Human documentation remains wise, but lack of documentation never produces lack of divine evidence.

The passage now moves from inheritance and contractual responsibilities to the ongoing organisation of a household. Verse 34 describes qiwamah, marital responsibility, and a staged response to serious marital breach. It has often been isolated either to justify abuse or to reject revelation; read with the surrounding commands of maintenance, justice, covenant, and arbitration, it places authority under demanding accountability.

=34=

“Men are caretakers of women because Allah has given some a responsibility over others and because they spend from their wealth. So righteous women are devoutly faithful and guard in their husbands’ absence what Allah has entrusted them to guard. As for those from whom you fear serious marital rebellion, advise them, then leave them alone in their beds, then strike them lightly. But if they return to harmony, do not seek a way against them. Surely Allah is Most High, All-Great.”

Qawwamun describes sustained responsibility: standing over the needs of a household through provision, protection, and care. The verse ties it not to unlimited male privilege but to burdens—capacities distributed by Allah and the obligation to spend. A husband owes mahr and maintenance even when his wife owns more. Leadership severed from service and accountability is a distortion of qiwamah.

Righteous wives are qanitat, devoted in obedience to Allah, and trustworthy when a husband is absent. They guard chastity, private information, children, and household property through Allah’s protection. The phrase does not erase the husband’s duties of fidelity and trust. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said each person is a shepherd responsible for those under their care, naming both the man in his family and the woman in her household.

Nushuz is not a minor disagreement, a different opinion, a late meal, or failure to satisfy a husband’s ego. It is a serious pattern of marital breach, defiance of core obligations, or destructive conduct threatening the union. The Quran later addresses nushuz from a husband as well (4:128), so moral failure is not imagined as female only.

The response is graduated and directed toward restoring the marriage: sincere admonition, then separation in beds that communicates the seriousness without public disgrace, and finally daraba. Classical scholars understood the last as a restricted, non-injurious physical gesture of last resort: never the face, never causing injury, leaving a mark, or expressing rage. The Prophet, peace be upon him, never struck a wife or servant, condemned men who beat women, and said the best of Muslims are best to their wives. Many jurists held it disliked, authorities may restrict it to prevent harm, and choosing nonviolent means follows the Prophet’s own example. Domestic violence, coercive control, injury, and terror are forbidden; the verse offers no shelter to an abuser.

“If they return to harmony” ends every pretext for retaliation. A husband may not keep punishing, investigate obsessively, or use qiwamah to seek superiority. The closing names place him beneath the One truly High and Great. If conflict remains, he is not authorised to escalate. Verse 35 takes the matter beyond the couple to balanced arbitration from both families.

=35=

“If you fear a breach between the two, appoint an arbitrator from his family and an arbitrator from hers. If they both sincerely desire reconciliation, Allah will bring harmony between them. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Aware.”

When a dispute grows into shiqaq—a split with each spouse on a different side—the matter becomes a community concern. The verse addresses those able to intervene responsibly, whether family elders, community leaders, or lawful authorities. One representative comes from each side so that neither spouse enters mediation surrounded only by the other’s supporters.

Family members may understand history, temperament, finances, and cultural details that an outsider misses. Yet kinship alone does not qualify an arbitrator. Each hakam should be mature, trustworthy, discreet, knowledgeable enough to distinguish right from pressure, and genuinely committed to justice. A relative who excuses abuse, spreads secrets, or cares only about family reputation cannot fulfil the role. Skilled counsellors and legal professionals may be needed alongside or instead of unsuitable relatives while preserving the verse’s balance.

The arbitrators should hear both spouses, verify claims, identify rights, and seek islah—real repair, not a photograph of reconciliation. That may involve restitution, changed living arrangements, treatment for addiction, financial transparency, enforceable safety plans, or recognition that separation is the least harmful lawful outcome. Classical jurists differed about how much authority arbitrators possess to order separation without fresh consent, but agreed that their task is serious and not ceremonial.

“If they both desire reconciliation” can refer to the arbitrators, the spouses, or all involved. Sincere intention invites Allah’s tawfiq, the alignment of means and hearts that people cannot manufacture alone. Outward mediation fails when a party attends only to gather information, delay rights, or protect an image. Allah is ‘Alim and Khabir: He knows the stated position and is fully aware of what lies beneath it.

Reconciliation is praised, but it is not used to send someone back into danger. The Quran’s rules against harm, aggression, and unlawful consumption remain in force. Where violence or coercion exists, safety and lawful accountability come before preserving appearances. A marriage is a solemn covenant, not a prison.

The movement from private advice to two-sided arbitration prevents impulsive divorce while also limiting unilateral power. Having ordered justice in the closest human relationship, the next verse widens the circle dramatically: worship Allah alone, then do good to parents, relatives, vulnerable people, neighbours, companions, travellers, and those under one’s authority.

=36=

“Worship Allah and associate nothing with Him. Show excellence to parents, relatives, orphans, the poor, the near neighbour, the distant neighbour, the companion at your side, the stranded traveller, and those in your possession. Surely Allah does not love whoever is arrogant and boastful.”

The social map begins with tawhid. Every human right that follows is anchored in the right of the Creator: worship Him without partner. A person cannot compensate for shirk by public kindness, nor claim sincere worship while habitually crushing the people Allah commands him to serve. The Quran repeatedly joins worship with treatment of parents and creation because devotion must reshape conduct.

After Allah’s right come parents, whose care, sacrifice, and age demand ihsan—more than bare justice. Relatives follow, continuing the protection of womb ties from verse 1. Orphans and the poor are named because households and markets can overlook those without power. Financial help matters, but ihsan also includes time, advocacy, education, and speech that preserves dignity.

The near and distant neighbour include those close by relationship or location and those farther away, Muslim and non-Muslim. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said Jibril kept instructing him about the neighbour until he thought the neighbour would be assigned inheritance. He also said a person whose neighbour is not safe from his harm does not have complete faith. Noise, waste, parking, shared security, emergencies, and online neighbourhood groups are present-day fields for this command.

“The companion at your side” has been understood to include a spouse, friend, colleague, fellow traveller, classmate, or anyone temporarily beside you. The stranded traveller may possess wealth at home yet need help where he is. Moral attention follows proximity: whoever Allah places beside you acquires a claim to decent treatment.

“Those in your possession” addressed enslaved people in that society, placing them within the circle of ihsan rather than outside humanity. The Prophet commanded feeding and clothing them like oneself, forbade overburdening them without help, and made emancipation a recurring virtue. Today the phrase’s ethical force reaches every dependent worker and person under authority; it cannot be used to recreate ownership.

Arrogance and boasting oppose this entire list. The arrogant person sees service as beneath him and gifts as proof of superiority. Allah does not love that posture. The next verses uncover two ways pride corrupts giving: stinginess that hides Allah’s bounty and charity performed only to be seen.

=37=

“Those who are stingy, urge others to be stingy, and conceal what Allah has given them of His bounty—We have prepared for the disbelievers a humiliating punishment.”

Arrogance often produces bukhl. A person imagines his wealth came entirely from his own brilliance, so the needs of parents, relatives, neighbours, and the poor feel like intrusions. The previous verse listed those entitled to ihsan; this verse describes the person who closes his hand toward them.

The wrong expands when the miser tells others not to give. He may mock generosity as weakness, discourage relatives from supporting a widow, design institutions that reward hoarding, or use financial advice to dress greed as wisdom. Sin becomes a culture when people recruit one another into it. The Quran condemns not only private refusal but the social preaching of refusal.

They also conceal what Allah gave from His bounty. This can mean hiding wealth to escape zakah and human obligations, concealing knowledge or signs given by Allah, or refusing to acknowledge favour with gratitude and beneficial use. Islam does not require displaying every possession; privacy can protect sincerity and safety. Blameworthy concealment denies a right or hides the Giver behind self-congratulation.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned against stinginess because it destroyed earlier peoples, driving them to shed blood and make unlawful things lawful. He also taught that charity does not decrease wealth and that each morning angels pray for replacement for the spender and destruction for the withholder. These teachings challenge the fear that only a tightly closed hand is secure.

The humiliating punishment fits arrogance. Wealth was used to feel elevated above needy people, but it cannot purchase honour before Allah. The verse says punishment is prepared for disbelievers because the passage describes a pattern rooted in rejection and ingratitude. A Muslim can still commit serious stinginess and must fear sharing a trait of those condemned, without every miser being automatically declared outside Islam.

Generosity, however, is not measured by movement of money alone. A person may escape the label of stingy in public while seeking only applause. The next verse places ostentatious spending beside lack of true faith and describes Satan as the companion who turns an apparent good deed into spiritual loss.

=38=

“And those who spend their wealth only to be seen by people and do not believe in Allah or the Last Day—whoever takes Satan as a companion has indeed taken an evil companion.”

The passage exposes two opposite failures. Some refuse to give; others give publicly for reputation. Both keep Allah out of the centre. Riya is performing an act so that people will see, praise, trust, or elevate the performer. Charity remains in the recipient’s hand, but its reward can disappear from the giver’s record.

The description of not believing in Allah and the Last Day refers directly to hypocrites and disbelievers whose spending has no foundation of faith. Believers can also fall into degrees of showing off, and the Prophet feared “minor shirk”—riya—for his community. This does not mean that every public donation is false. Public giving can encourage others, provide transparency, and support a campaign, as long as Allah’s pleasure remains the ruling intention. A heart may contain mixed motives and must keep correcting them.

Al-Baqarah 2:264 compares charity followed by injury or performed to be seen to soil on a smooth rock: a downpour leaves it bare. The image is sobering because the act looked fertile until testing exposed its lack of root. Anonymous giving, hidden worship, refusing unnecessary publicity, and remembering one’s own need for Allah’s acceptance help cultivate sincerity.

Satan is called a qarin, a close companion. He can ruin charity from either side: before giving, he threatens poverty; during giving, he inflates the desire to be noticed; afterward, he encourages reminders, humiliation, and self-admiration. An evil companion does not always tell a person to stop doing good. Sometimes he teaches how to use good deeds to feed the ego.

The cure is not to stop giving whenever one notices pride. That may be another satanic victory. One should renew intention, conceal what need not be public, continue the beneficial act, and ask Allah for acceptance. The Prophet taught that actions are judged by intentions, so inner work accompanies outward generosity throughout.

The next verse asks what loss sincere faith and spending could possibly bring. Instead of Satan as companion and people as audience, it places the giver before Allah, who provided the wealth and knows the heart completely.

=39=

“What harm would it have caused them to believe in Allah and the Last Day and spend from what Allah provided for them? And Allah knows them perfectly.”

The question exposes the irrationality of disbelief, stinginess, and display. What genuine loss would faith have brought? What injury would they suffer by spending sincerely from provision that was Allah’s gift in the first place? They traded lasting reward for the unstable opinions of people without gaining anything that could survive death.

“From what Allah provided” corrects ownership at its root. A donor gives from health, opportunity, skill, land, money, and time that Allah first supplied. This does not erase human labour; it places labour inside gifts the worker did not create. Gratitude becomes easier when spending is understood as returning part of a trust rather than losing an absolute possession.

Faith in the Last Day changes financial arithmetic. A hidden gift may bring no public status, but it has not vanished. The Quran compares sincere spending to a grain producing seven hundred grains and more (2:261). The Prophet, peace be upon him, said Allah accepts a lawful charity, even as little as a date, and nurtures its reward until it becomes like a mountain. What looks like subtraction here becomes multiplication there.

“And Allah knows them” is both assurance and warning. He knows the wealthy person who gives little with great sacrifice, the poor person whose smile is charity, the public donor who sincerely hopes others will join, and the hidden vanity behind an impressive pledge. Human beings should avoid claiming certainty about another’s intention while auditing their own.

No sincere amount is too small for that knowledge. The next verse makes the scale explicit: Allah does not wrong even the weight of a tiny particle. A good deed is not merely preserved at its original size; He multiplies it and grants an immense reward from Himself.

=40=

“Indeed, Allah does not wrong even the weight of a tiny particle. If there is a good deed, He multiplies it and grants from Himself a tremendous reward.”

A dharrah was used for something extremely small—a tiny ant or a speck visible in light. Whatever measure people use for the smallest quantity, Allah’s justice misses nothing of it. No unseen service, restrained word, returned coin, or private moment of patience is lost. Likewise, no apparently minor wrong is absent from His knowledge, though His mercy may forgive it.

Divine justice is exact, but divine generosity goes beyond exact exchange. Allah does not merely credit a good deed once; He multiplies it. Other verses describe tenfold reward for a good deed (6:160), up to seven hundred and more for spending (2:261), and reward without measure for the patient (39:10). The multiplication is not owed to the servant. It is fadl—an immense gift “from Himself.”

The Prophet, peace be upon him, explained that Allah records an intended good deed even when it is not completed, multiplies it when performed, and records a bad deed as one; if it is abandoned for Allah, it may be written as good. This asymmetry shows mercy surrounding the scale without any injustice. Allah can forgive His own right, but rights taken from people still demand settlement under His perfect judgment.

For someone ignored or exploited, the verse is consolation. A caregiver whose labour receives no thanks, an employee denied recognition, or a believer who quietly resists temptation is not working outside the record. For someone powerful, it is warning: systems may round vulnerable people down to zero, but Allah never does.

The promise also cures obsession with dramatic deeds. A sincere greeting, a small recurring gift, removal of harm from a path, or two units of prayer in private may carry multiplied weight. The believer should not despise any good, as the Prophet instructed, while still fulfilling major obligations before optional displays.

This precise and generous accounting prepares the scene in verse 41. Every community will arrive with a witness, and Muhammad, peace be upon him, will be brought as a witness over his people. The tiny deed known perfectly in verse 40 will stand within a public Day of testimony; justice that was invisible in the world will become impossible to deny.
