=61=

“When it is said to them, ‘Come to what Allah has revealed and to the Messenger,’ you see the hypocrites turn away from you completely.”

Verse 60 exposed people who claimed belief yet sought a corrupt authority to decide in their favour. Now their response to the honest alternative is shown. They are invited not to a personality cult or tribal custom but to revelation and the Messenger entrusted to explain and apply it. Instead of coming, they pull away with deliberate aversion.

Sudud can mean both turning oneself away and obstructing others. Hypocrisy rarely remains an entirely private doubt. Someone who resents a ruling may discourage family members from consulting knowledge, discredit every fair mediator, or create an alternative forum designed to guarantee the desired answer. Refusal becomes a system.

The verse does not condemn asking questions, seeking evidence, or struggling emotionally with a difficult command. The companions asked for clarification, and sincere believers can need time to understand. The mark described here is not honest confusion but strategic evasion: claiming faith while refusing the very source one professes to believe whenever it threatens self-interest.

“Come” also shows that the door remains open. The Messenger did not summon them merely to expose them; judgment by revelation could correct the wrong and restore relationships. A believer inviting someone back to Quran and Sunnah should likewise aim at guidance and justice, not at winning a public humiliation.

Today, aversion may hide beneath endless verdict-shopping. A person asks one teacher after another, not to discover the truth but to collect the first answer that serves desire. Another quotes revelation eagerly against opponents but becomes hostile when the same standard addresses his own money, marriage, or speech. The test of submission is often the ruling that costs us something.

The phrase “you see” indicates outward conduct while Allah alone knows the heart with certainty. Muslims should not casually label every sinner or disputant a hypocrite. The passage gives traits for self-examination and warns against a pattern; final judgment of hidden faith belongs to Allah.

The next verse shows what happens when consequences reach such people. After rejecting the Messenger’s judgment, they return swearing that their detour was intended only for goodness and reconciliation. Calamity removes confidence, but excuses do not change what their hands sent ahead.

=62=

“How will it be when disaster strikes them because of what their own hands have sent ahead, then they come to you swearing by Allah, ‘We intended nothing but goodwill and reconciliation’?”

“How will it be?” forces the reader to imagine the moment excuses collide with consequence. The calamity is linked to what their hands advanced—the corrupt judgment they sought, the right they tried to defeat, and the aversion they showed toward revelation. The Quran repeatedly uses the hands as an image of personal responsibility because hands carry decisions into action.

When the result becomes painful, they approach the Prophet, peace be upon him, with an oath. They claim ihsan and tawfiq: we only wanted to do good, keep relationships smooth, or reach a workable compromise. These are excellent words when truthful. Verse 35 promised Allah’s help to arbitrators sincerely desiring reconciliation. Here the same language is used as cover after revelation was deliberately avoided.

Good intention cannot make a knowingly unjust means lawful. A bribe is not permitted because it was meant to preserve a job; an inheritance share cannot be erased for “family unity”; false testimony does not become righteous because it helps a friend. In Islamic ethics, the goal, the means, and the rights affected all matter.

Swearing by Allah adds another danger. The hypocrite recruits the divine name to protect a false story. Frequent oaths are not proof of sincerity. Allah knows the decision before it is narrated and the motive before it is polished for an audience. The Prophet warned against false oaths used to take a Muslim’s property.

The calamity may be worldly exposure, loss, conflict, or the consequence awaiting in the Hereafter. Not every hardship proves a particular sin; prophets suffered greatly. The verse concerns a consequence Allah identifies, so believers should not tell every afflicted person that disaster is punishment. Its first use is inward: when my choices bear bitter fruit, do I repent honestly or rewrite my motive?

Real reconciliation begins by admitting the wrong, returning the right, and accepting sound judgment. Public language about unity without restitution only delays the breach. Allah knows what is inside these hearts, as the next verse says, yet the Prophet is instructed to turn from their excuses, admonish them, and speak words that reach deeply into their very selves.

=63=

“Those are the ones whose hearts Allah knows. So turn away from their excuses, admonish them, and speak to them about themselves with penetrating words.”

Human observers hear oaths; Allah knows hearts. He knows whether “we intended good” is regret, confusion, or calculated deception. The Prophet is not told to pretend the excuse is convincing, nor to expose every secret with uncontrolled anger. He receives a measured strategy: disengage from the false performance, offer moral warning, and speak effectively to the person beneath it.

“Turn away from them” here does not mean abandon every effort to guide them, because admonition follows immediately. It means do not be trapped by their evasions or grant their story the trust they want. Leaders and families sometimes need boundaries before advice can be heard. Endless debate with a manipulator can reward the manipulation.

Wa‘izhum is admonition that softens through reminder of Allah, consequence, and the better path. It differs from insult. The aim is to awaken a conscience, not prove the speaker’s dominance. The Prophet, peace be upon him, used warnings, questions, silence, private counsel, and carefully chosen moments according to the person before him.

Qawlan baligha is speech that reaches its destination. It is truthful, clear, suited to the listener, and difficult to dismiss. The Quran has already commanded kind words to dependants, straight words around vulnerable heirs, and honourable words to the needy. Here a hardened heart needs language with force, but force does not require cruelty or public shaming.

Effective counsel today may include naming the contradiction between professed values and actual conduct, explaining who was harmed, and describing the action needed to repair it. A generic lecture may leave every defence intact, while a private, specific conversation can reach “into themselves.” Confidentiality often makes honesty possible.

The verse also humbles the adviser. Only Allah knows the heart completely. A penetrating word is an effort, not control over the result. Psychological insight, eloquence, and pastoral skill remain means whose success depends on Him.

What should these people have done after wronging themselves? Verse 64 answers: come to the Messenger, seek Allah’s forgiveness, and receive the Messenger’s prayer. Messengers were sent to be obeyed, yet disobedience did not close the door of repentance while they were alive and willing to return.

=64=

“We sent no messenger except to be obeyed by Allah’s permission. If only, when they wronged themselves, they had come to you, sought Allah’s forgiveness, and the Messenger had sought forgiveness for them, they would certainly have found Allah Ever-Accepting of repentance, Most Merciful.”

A messenger is not sent merely to deliver inspiring information. Revelation carries authority, and the authorised messenger demonstrates its meaning. “By Allah’s permission” makes clear that obedience is not independent worship of a human being; it is obedience Allah Himself commanded. Following Muhammad, peace be upon him, is therefore part of following the Lord who sent him.

Their wrong against others was also a wrong against their own souls. Sin cannot reduce Allah’s kingdom, but it damages the sinner’s heart, record, relationships, and final destination. The wording encourages personal responsibility without suggesting the offender is beyond repair.

During the Prophet’s lifetime, the path described was direct: approach him rather than avoid his judgment, ask Allah for forgiveness, and request the Prophet’s supplication. He prayed for believers and for repentant wrongdoers by Allah’s permission. His prayer did not replace their own tawbah; the verse mentions their seeking forgiveness first.

After his death, Muslims continue to come to the guidance he brought by following the Quran and authentic Sunnah, and they ask Allah directly for forgiveness. Scholars have discussed forms of tawassul and conduct when visiting his mosque and grave, but all agree that Allah alone forgives sins and that authentic repentance requires turning to Him. The verse must not be used to make the Messenger a rival to the Lord whose mercy it announces.

Al-Tawwab repeatedly accepts those who repeatedly return, and al-Rahim surrounds them with mercy. The hypocrites’ past aversion could have ended in accepted repentance if they had come sincerely. No political embarrassment or long record of evasion makes the return impossible before death.

Where another person was wronged, seeking Allah’s forgiveness includes correcting the judgment and restoring the right. The Prophet’s prayer was never a device for keeping stolen property while receiving spiritual comfort.

The next verse intensifies the place of prophetic judgment. Allah swears by Himself that full faith is not realised until believers make the Messenger judge in their disputes, find no resistant objection within themselves, and submit completely.

=65=

“But no—by your Lord! They will not truly believe until they make you judge in their disputes, then find no resistance within themselves against your decision and submit wholeheartedly.”

Allah swears by “your Lord,” honouring the Prophet while binding his authority to the Lord who nurtured and sent him. The verse describes three layers of submission: taking the dispute to the Messenger, removing inward objection to his true judgment, and carrying it out fully.

The first layer rejects selective courts chosen for favourable results. During his lifetime, the Prophet’s decision was the living application of revelation. Afterward, believers return disputes to the Quran and authentic Sunnah through sound scholarship and lawful adjudication. A fabricated report or careless personal reading cannot be attributed to him and then used to demand submission.

The second layer concerns haraj, a constricting resistance in the chest. A natural feeling of disappointment when losing a case is not necessarily rejection of faith. The danger is believing the Prophet’s ruling unjust, resenting its authority, or searching for a way to defeat it after its truth is established. Hearts may need education and spiritual work to align emotion with obedience.

Reports connect the verse to a water dispute between al-Zubayr and an Ansari. The Prophet first proposed a generous arrangement, but when his fairness was challenged, he issued al-Zubayr’s full legal right. The incident shows that prophetic gentleness did not erase justice and that angry suspicion can make a person reject a ruling that initially favoured compromise.

Wholehearted submission does not mean human judges are infallible. A Muslim may appeal an error, present overlooked evidence, or challenge corruption. The unconditional standard belongs to Allah and His Messenger; everyone else is measured by it. Verse 59 itself requires rulers and scholars to refer disagreement back to revelation.

This verse should first discipline the self, not become a weapon for authoritarian control. An abusive person cannot declare his preference “the Prophet’s judgment” and demand silence. Evidence, knowledge, and fair process are essential.

Submission appears demanding, but Allah has not imposed the severe tests given to some earlier communities. Verse 66 imagines commands to sacrifice life or leave home and notes that few would carry them out. The actual exhortations are a mercy, and obeying them would strengthen faith.

=66=

“If We had commanded them, ‘Sacrifice your lives,’ or, ‘Leave your homes,’ only a few of them would have done it. Yet if they had done what they were instructed, it would have been better for them and would have made them firmer in faith.”

The verse exposes the weakness behind proud claims. People may speak grandly about devotion while resisting a fair judgment. Had they faced commands as severe as giving up life or homeland, only a few would have obeyed. Allah knows the true capacity beneath religious performance.

“Kill yourselves” has been understood literally in comparison with the repentance commanded of the calf-worshippers in 2:54, and more generally as accepting a command of ultimate sacrifice. “Leave your homes” recalls migration for faith, a real test endured by the Prophet and the emigrants from Makkah. Life and home are among the strongest human attachments.

The verse is hypothetical for those addressed; it does not authorise suicide or private violence. Verse 29 expressly forbids killing oneself. Commands involving life belong only to revelation and lawful authority, not to charismatic leaders demanding destructive loyalty. No group may recruit followers by borrowing this language.

What they were actually exhorted to do was far lighter: accept the Messenger’s judgment, return trusts, establish worship, and stop wrongdoing. Human beings sometimes evade an ordinary duty while imagining they would pass a dramatic test. The truthful measure of faith is today’s clear command, not a fantasy of heroism.

Obedience would be “better for them” because Allah gains nothing from it; the benefit returns to the servant. It would also produce stronger tathbit—inner firmness. Action reinforces conviction. Each prayer performed on time, temptation refused, and right restored trains the soul for the next demand. Repeated evasion trains it in the opposite direction.

The “few” remind believers that sincere people can rise to extraordinary sacrifice by Allah’s help. The emigrants did leave homes, and martyrs did give lives without seeking death for its own sake. Their courage grew through ordinary obedience before the great test arrived.

The next two verses describe what Allah would grant to those who obey: an immense reward from His presence and guidance along a straight path. Sacrifice is never lost with the One who multiplies even a particle of good.

=67=

“Then We would certainly have granted them from Us an immense reward.”

The reward follows obedience described in verse 66. Had they accepted what Allah instructed, He would give ajran ‘azima—not a bare wage calculated at human rates, but a tremendous reward “from Us.” The source makes the gift greater than any description.

Allah does not need obedience and is not enriched by sacrifice. Reward is pure favour from the Self-Sufficient. A servant performs limited deeds with borrowed strength during a short life, yet Allah can answer with eternal good. This disproportion is mercy, not an exchange between equals.

“From Us” also gives closeness and honour. The believer seeks not only created pleasures but acceptance from the Creator. In 3:15, the gifts of Paradise culminate in Allah’s pleasure; in 9:72, His approval is called greater. Material and spiritual reward meet rather than compete.

The verse’s brevity allows every promised form of good to enter: forgiveness, multiplied deeds, safety at judgment, gardens, honour, and companionship with the righteous soon described in verse 69. What people feared losing through obedience cannot compare with what Allah stores.

This promise helps when doing right appears costly. Returning money may shrink an account, leaving a forbidden relationship may bring loneliness, and speaking truth may reduce status. The immediate loss is visible while the immense reward is unseen. Faith gives the unseen enough reality to govern the decision.

A person should not demand a specific worldly outcome as proof that obedience worked. Reward may include worldly relief, but its guaranteed fullness belongs to the Hereafter. Prophets obeyed and still experienced poverty, exile, wounds, and grief. Their reward was not disproved by difficulty.

The wording also guards against self-admiration. If the reward comes “from Us,” successful obedience and its return both rest on Allah’s grace. Gratitude is more fitting than entitlement.

Reward alone is not the entire gift. A person can desire a destination and still need a road. Verse 68 adds that Allah would guide the obedient to a straight path—steadiness in truth that is itself both means and reward.

=68=

“And We would surely have guided them to a straight path.”

Guidance is not only information received before obedience. It is also light granted after obedience. When people act upon what they know, Allah opens firmer understanding, clearer judgment, and strength for the next step. The path becomes more visible to the feet that walk it.

The straight path is the road believers ask for in every unit of prayer: “Guide us to the straight path” (1:6). A person already inside Islam continues asking because hidayah includes entering, remaining, increasing, correcting after error, and arriving safely. No one graduates from need for guidance.

This verse follows “made them firmer” in verse 66. Obedience, firmness, reward, and guidance form a gracious cycle. By contrast, repeated rejection can darken perception until a person calls corrupt judgment reconciliation and truth extremism. Choices train the heart’s sight.

Guidance to a straight path does not mean life becomes free from uncertainty or scholarly disagreement. It gives a reliable criterion, sincerity in seeking, and protection from major deviation. A believer still consults, studies evidence, and admits error. Humility is one of the path’s signs.

Modern life offers endless routes through algorithms, influencers, institutions, and desires, each claiming to be natural or inevitable. The straight path is not necessarily the loudest or easiest. It is measured by revelation and the Messenger, whose judgment the passage has just commanded believers to accept.

The promise should be turned into practice: obey the clear duty already known, then ask Allah for clarity about what remains. Some people postpone prayer, restitution, or repentance until every intellectual question is settled. Often the disobeyed certainty is itself blocking guidance about the uncertainty.

The path is not lonely. Verse 69 reveals its travellers and final company: prophets, the utterly truthful, martyrs or witnesses, and the righteous. Obedience to Allah and the Messenger joins an ordinary believer to the finest fellowship ever created.

=69=

“Whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with those Allah has blessed: the prophets, the people of complete truth, the martyrs, and the righteous. What excellent companions they are!”

This verse identifies the people of the straight path named in al-Fatihah: those whom Allah has blessed. At their head are the prophets, recipients of revelation and models of worship. Then come the siddiqun, whose truthfulness is complete in belief, speech, and action; the shuhada, understood as martyrs who gave life for Allah and witnesses to truth; and the broad company of salihin whose lives were made sound through faith and good works.

The promise is to be “with” them, not to acquire their identical rank. Prophethood cannot be earned, and degrees in Paradise differ. Yet companionship itself is an astonishing gift. An unknown believer who obeyed amid ordinary struggles may enter a society containing Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Isa, Muhammad, Maryam, the truthful companions, and righteous people of every generation, peace be upon the prophets.

A companion worried that he loved the Prophet but would not share his high station in Paradise. Reports connect that concern with this promise. The Prophet also said, “A person will be with those he loves,” words that made the companions rejoice greatly. True love is not a substitute for obedience; it is shown by wanting the beloved’s road.

The four groups give believers reachable models. Not everyone dies as a martyr or becomes famous for scholarship, but everyone can practise truthfulness and righteousness. A truthful invoice, guarded prayer, patient care for a parent, and private refusal of sin belong to the path of this company.

“Excellent companions” answers the influence of bad company described earlier, where Satan was called an evil companion (4:38). Companionship shapes direction in this world and reflects destination in the next. Choosing friends who remind one of Allah is preparation for the gathering one hopes to enter.

The verse also comforts believers separated from righteous loved ones by death. If both die upon faith, earthly separation need not be final. The response to grief is not only memory but continued obedience toward reunion.

Such companionship cannot be purchased through lineage, fame, or personal achievement alone. Verse 70 immediately attributes it to Allah’s bounty and says His knowledge is sufficient. He knows who sincerely followed and where each servant belongs.

=70=

“That is the bounty from Allah, and Allah is sufficient as the One who knows.”

Being guided, rewarded, and gathered with the finest believers is fadl—bounty beyond what deeds could independently earn. The servant obeys, but the ability to obey, acceptance of the deed, multiplication of reward, and noble companionship all come from Allah.

This balance protects against two errors. One person abandons effort and says salvation is only grace; another trusts deeds as if Paradise were a salary Allah owes. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said no one enters Paradise by deeds alone—not even him—unless Allah covers him with mercy. Yet he was the most constant in worship. Grace energises action and removes pride from it.

“Allah is sufficient as Knower” reassures the unnoticed believer. Human beings may not recognise a siddiq living quietly, a martyr whose intention was hidden, or a righteous person with no platform. Allah knows truth beneath reputation and can place each soul with its proper companions.

It is also warning against manufacturing spiritual rank. Titles such as saint, martyr, scholar, or reformer do not bind the divine judgment. People judge outwardly with care; Allah knows motives, endings, and acceptance. Even those killed in battle are not declared pure solely by appearance, because intention distinguishes sacrifice for Allah from fighting for fame or spoils.

The verse’s brevity closes the section about prophetic judgment on grace rather than severity. Submission may initially feel like losing control, but its destination is bounty and companionship.

The surah now turns to collective danger. Reliance on Allah and desire for the Hereafter do not mean carelessness. Believers are commanded to take precautions and mobilise in organised groups or together. Spiritual obedience must produce disciplined protection of the community.

=71=

“O believers, take your precautions, then advance in separate groups or advance all together.”

Hidhr means vigilance, readiness, and measures that protect against danger. The command comes before mobilisation. Courage without planning can waste lives; trust in Allah without using available means misunderstands trust. The Prophet, peace be upon him, gathered intelligence, selected positions, wore armour, arranged guards, and consulted companions while knowing victory came only from Allah.

Believers may advance thubat—units or detachments—or jami‘an, as one body, according to need. The verse leaves tactical organisation flexible while demanding unity of purpose and legitimate command. Different threats require different formations; revelation does not freeze military planning into one historical method.

The setting concerns armed defence of the early Muslim community, but the principle of precaution extends responsibly: disaster preparation, cybersecurity, public health, safeguarding children, and verifying risk are not signs of weak faith. The same Lord who decrees outcomes commands means.

Precaution must not become paranoia. Verse 45 said Allah knows enemies best, so suspicion cannot replace evidence or justify harming innocent people. Ethical readiness distinguishes combatant from non-combatant, real warning from rumour, and necessary secrecy from lies against one’s own community.

Nor is this a call for private militias or self-appointed warfare. Decisions involving force, public safety, treaties, and collective risk belong to legitimate authority and lawful process. Individuals acting on online rhetoric can produce the very chaos Islamic rules of command seek to prevent.

The plural commands train communal discipline. A person who leaks plans, ignores instructions for glory, or abandons a post can endanger everyone. Later in this passage, uncontrolled broadcasting of news about security and fear will be condemned (4:83).

After ordering readiness, the Quran reveals an internal weakness more dangerous than limited equipment: someone within the community will deliberately lag, and perhaps slow others. If believers suffer, he congratulates himself for staying away; if they receive bounty, he wishes he had joined only for the gain.

=72=

“Indeed, among you is one who deliberately lags behind. If a calamity strikes you, he says, ‘Allah has favoured me by keeping me from being present with them.’”

Yubatti’anna carries the sense of moving slowly and causing delay. The person does not merely have a valid excuse; he deliberately holds back and may discourage others. His relationship to the community appears when hardship arrives: instead of grieving or helping, he interprets absence as special favour.

There are legitimate reasons not to join dangerous collective duty—illness, disability, lack of means, dependent care, or instruction to remain. The Quran elsewhere excuses those genuinely unable who are sincere to Allah and His Messenger (9:91). The blame here rests on hypocrisy, self-preservation at others’ expense, and the story the laggard tells afterward.

Calling his safety “Allah’s favour” misuses religious language. Safety is normally a blessing, but not when obtained through betrayal of an obligation while others carry the burden. Worldly ease is not automatic evidence of divine approval, just as hardship is not automatic evidence of anger. Prophets suffered most while remaining most beloved.

“Not being a witness with them” reveals emotional distance. He watches the believers as “them,” not “us.” Shared faith becomes a convenience rather than belonging. Communities discover such loyalties during crisis, when sacrifice cannot be imitated by slogans.

The verse is about mobilisation, yet the trait appears in ordinary responsibilities. Someone avoids every difficult task, then praises his own wisdom when workers face setbacks. In a family, organisation, or neighbourhood, he wants membership without cost. Valid boundaries and protection from exploitation are necessary, but habitual abandonment of others should not be renamed divine favour.

Believers should avoid judging a person’s excuse without knowledge; Allah may know a hidden incapacity. The verse is best used to examine one’s own delight at escaping a duty and one’s willingness to share collective pain.

The laggard’s opposite reaction appears in verse 73. If the believers receive bounty, he speaks as though no affection had existed and wishes he had gone with them—not to serve Allah, but to obtain what he calls a great success.

=73=

“But if bounty from Allah reaches you, he will surely say—as though there had never been any affection between you and him—‘If only I had been with them, I would have achieved a great triumph.’”

The same person who called absence a blessing during calamity regrets it when believers succeed. His standard is not obedience but outcome. Risk makes him distant; spoils and recognition revive his desire to belong.

The parenthetical phrase exposes the wound: “as though no affection existed between you.” Genuine mawaddah shares another’s joy even without personal profit and shares grief even when personally safe. The hypocritical laggard views the community as an opportunity, so affection disappears whenever no advantage is available.

He calls worldly gain fawzan ‘azima, “a great triumph.” The Quran uses the same expression for salvation and Paradise (4:13). His vocabulary is religious, but his scale has changed. Temporary benefit occupies the place of eternal success. This is a recurring mark of hypocrisy: correct words carrying a worldly centre.

The verse does not condemn lawful enjoyment of victory, compensation, or public good. Those who serve may receive material benefit gratefully. The corruption lies in making benefit the reason for participation and wishing only for a share after refusing the burden.

Modern forms appear when people join a cause after it becomes fashionable, claim closeness to successful work they once obstructed, or treat suffering communities as routes to grants and status. Late participation can be sincere, so motives belong finally to Allah; the warning is against opportunism within ourselves.

A believer tries to define success before events define it. If obedience leads through loss, Allah still records it. If disobedience produces profit, the profit is not triumph. This stable measure prevents both cowardice during danger and arrogance after success.

The next verse calls those willing to exchange worldly life for the Hereafter to fight in Allah’s path. Whether they are killed or prevail, their reward is great. Unlike the laggard, they do not wait for the outcome before deciding whether obedience was worthwhile.

=74=

“Let those who trade the life of this world for the Hereafter fight in the cause of Allah. Whoever fights in Allah’s cause and is killed or is victorious, We will grant them an immense reward.”

The verse uses commercial language: the temporary life is exchanged for the lasting one. This does not require hating life or seeking death. Life is valuable property entrusted by Allah; the believer is willing to risk it only for a cause He recognises and under the restraints He established.

Two worldly outcomes are named—death or victory—but one spiritual outcome follows: immense reward. The sincere person is not a failure if killed and not automatically righteous merely because victorious. Intention, cause, and conduct determine whether fighting is fi sabil Allah.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, was asked about people who fight from courage, tribal zeal, or to be seen. He answered that the one who fights so Allah’s word is highest is in Allah’s cause. This excludes aggression for race, land, revenge, loot, fame, or personal power even when religious slogans are used.

Islamic teaching also regulates conduct in war. The Prophet forbade killing women and children who were not fighting, treachery, mutilation, and other abuses. Treaties and protection must be honoured. A lawful end does not permit every means, just as good intention did not excuse the hypocrites in verse 62.

No individual may declare a war or target others from private interpretation. Armed force involves legitimate authority, verified conditions, capacity, proportionality, and protection of non-combatants. Misusing verses of collective defence for terrorism is a betrayal of the path, not martyrdom.

The promise gave courage to a community facing persecution and real armed enemies. Its wider personal lesson is willingness to spend a finite life for eternal priorities: truth, worship, family duties, and service to the vulnerable. Not every “sacrifice” is combat, though the verse itself is.

Verse 75 names one just cause present in their struggle: oppressed men, women, and children trapped in Makkah cried for rescue from a city whose powerful people persecuted them. Fighting in Allah’s path is joined to defending those unable to defend themselves.

=75=

“And why should you not fight in the cause of Allah and for the oppressed men, women, and children who cry, ‘Our Lord, deliver us from this town of oppressive people, and appoint for us from Yourself a protector and a helper’?”

The mustad‘afin are not inherently weak; they have been made powerless by others. Men, women, and children are named so military language cannot hide the human beings trapped beneath oppression. Reports identify believers unable to migrate from Makkah, suffering under persecutors and asking Allah for release.

Their prayer has three movements: remove us from the oppressive environment, appoint a protecting guardian, and appoint a helper. Rescue requires more than extraction. Displaced people need safety, leadership, shelter, livelihood, and support after escape. The Wali and Nasir they request ultimately come from Allah, even when human hands become the means.

The verse challenges indifference: “What is wrong with you?” Worship that ignores preventable persecution has missed a communal duty. Advocacy, asylum, food, legal assistance, ransom of captives under lawful arrangements, and diplomatic pressure can all serve the oppressed according to capacity. Armed protection was necessary in the verse’s setting, but relief is broader than combat.

This passage does not grant every faction permission to invade wherever it alleges injustice. Claims must be verified, consequences weighed, authority legitimate, treaties and law considered, and innocent life protected. Reckless intervention can multiply the suffering it claims to end. Classical law placed warfare under public leadership, not private enthusiasm.

The oppressed are defined by their condition, not by usefulness to one’s tribe. The Quran includes children, who cannot offer political benefit, and it commands justice even beyond the Muslim community elsewhere. A believer should resist selective compassion that mourns only victims sharing his identity.

The verse also gives language to someone trapped: it is lawful to ask Allah for a way out, a protector, and practical help. Patience does not mean accepting abuse as sacred fate. Leaving an oppressive home, workplace, or region may be part of reliance when safe means exist.

The next verse clarifies the moral divide in the battle then underway: believers fight for Allah’s cause, while disbelieving aggressors fight for taghut. Satan’s strategy may look imposing, but against Allah it is fundamentally weak.

=76=

“Believers fight in the cause of Allah, while disbelievers fight in the cause of false powers. So fight the allies of Satan. Surely Satan’s strategy is ever weak.”

The distinction is not simply between people carrying Muslim and non-Muslim labels. Fighting “in Allah’s cause” requires a just purpose, lawful authority, sincere intention, and moral limits. A Muslim who fights for fame, ethnic supremacy, plunder, or tyranny does not make that motive sacred by reciting a slogan.

Taghut is false authority that exceeds limits and demands allegiance in rebellion against Allah. In this context, its allies use force to preserve persecution and block people from faith. They serve a system of falsehood even if each fighter understands his role differently.

The command was directed to a recognised community under the Prophet’s leadership facing armed opponents. It cannot be lifted out as an instruction for individuals to attack those they personally call satanic. The Sunnah’s rules of command, covenant, distinction, and restraint remain binding. Declaring people allies of Satan is not a shortcut around evidence or law.

“Satan’s strategy is weak” does not mean his harm is imaginary. He deceived many, and battles caused real death. His weakness is ultimate: he creates nothing, knows no unseen except what he learns, compels no sincere heart, and cannot defeat Allah’s plan. On Judgment Day he will admit that he only invited people and they responded (14:22).

This knowledge gives courage without carelessness. Verse 71 still ordered precautions. A weak enemy can injure someone who ignores means, while no enemy can overturn what Allah finally decrees. Tawakkul joins preparedness to confidence in the true balance of power.

Satan’s strategy also operates away from battle through fear, delay, rivalry, and false attribution of motives. The laggard of verses 72-73 was already serving it by discouraging and calculating only worldly outcomes. Resisting that strategy begins with obedience in ordinary decisions.

The next verse remembers an earlier stage when believers were ordered to restrain their hands, establish prayer, and give zakah. Some who had wanted action then feared people once fighting was actually prescribed. Timing and authority, not raw eagerness, determine obedient courage.

=77=

“Have you not seen those who were told, ‘Restrain your hands, establish prayer, and give zakah’? But when fighting was prescribed for them, a group suddenly feared people as they should fear Allah—or even more—and said, ‘Our Lord, why have You prescribed fighting for us? If only You had delayed it for a little while!’ Say, ‘The enjoyment of this world is brief, and the Hereafter is better for those mindful of Allah. You will not be wronged even by the thread of a date stone.’”

In Makkah, persecuted Muslims were commanded to hold back from armed response. They trained through prayer, charity, patience, and community building. Later in Madinah, when authority and conditions changed, fighting was prescribed. The contrast teaches that zeal is not obedience unless it follows Allah’s timing.

Some who had longed for permission then recoiled when duty became real. Imagined courage costs little; actual mobilisation threatens comfort, family, and life. Their fear of people grew equal to or greater than fear of Allah—not ordinary caution, which is natural, but fear allowed to overrule a clear obligation.

The passage does not shame every bodily fear. Prophets and believers experienced fear and took precautions. Moral courage means acting rightly despite fear, not feeling nothing. Nor was fighting required from every person regardless of capacity; revelation elsewhere names exemptions.

Their request for brief delay exposes attachment to the small remaining portion of worldly life. The answer does not say the world has no good. It says its enjoyment is qalil—little in duration and scale beside the Hereafter. The mindful person makes temporary safety serve eternal safety.

No one who obeys will be wronged even by a fatil, the fine thread on a date stone. A lost day, wound, expense, or fear is recorded precisely. Allah’s justice removes the suspicion that sacrifice disappears, while His multiplication makes the final return greater.

The history also restrains modern militancy. There are times to hold hands back, build worship, serve financially, negotiate, and endure without retaliation. Private anger cannot announce that the stage has changed. Lawful authority and actual conditions matter.

Fearful delay will not ultimately preserve life. Verse 78 answers the request for more time: death reaches people wherever they are, even inside fortified towers. It then addresses another hypocritical habit—crediting Allah for pleasant events but blaming the Messenger for hardship.

=78=

“Wherever you may be, death will overtake you, even if you are inside fortified towers. If good comes to them, they say, ‘This is from Allah,’ but if hardship strikes, they say, ‘This is because of you.’ Say, ‘All is from Allah.’ What is wrong with these people that they can hardly understand any message?”

Fortified towers represent the strongest security people can build. Walls can reduce ordinary risk, and taking protection is commanded, but no structure excludes the appointed death. A person who abandons duty solely to avoid mortality is trying to escape something that travels inside every fortress.

This does not justify recklessness. Medicine, safe buildings, defensive measures, and flight from danger are lawful means. The Prophet, peace be upon him, instructed people not to enter or leave a plague-stricken land in a way that spreads disease. Means manage risk; they do not cancel the decree.

The hypocrites used a double standard of attribution. Prosperity was comfortably called Allah’s gift, while hardship was blamed on Muhammad, as if his presence caused misfortune. Earlier nations similarly treated prophets as bad omens (7:131, 36:18). Scapegoating the messenger allowed people to avoid examining their own choices.

“All is from Allah” affirms universal decree. Nothing exists outside His creation, knowledge, permission, and wisdom. Ease and trial both occur within His qadar. This does not make Allah pleased with every human sin or remove human agency; He may decree the existence of an act He forbids morally.

The closing question diagnoses shallow understanding. They hear words about decree but cannot hold divine sovereignty together with personal responsibility. The next verse supplies the complementary truth: every good that reaches a person is from Allah, while evil is from the self. The two statements address different aspects, not competing gods.

In adversity, a believer avoids both superstition and self-exoneration. He knows the event did not escape Allah, asks whether his conduct contributed, repents where needed, and seeks wisdom without claiming certainty about every hidden reason. In ease, he thanks Allah rather than treating success as proof of personal greatness.

Death, blessing, and hardship all unfold under the Lord who sent Muhammad to humanity. His task is faithful conveyance, and Allah is sufficient as witness to it, as verse 79 continues.

=79=

“Whatever good reaches you is from Allah, and whatever evil befalls you is from yourself. We have sent you, O Prophet, as a messenger to humanity, and Allah is sufficient as a Witness.”

This verse completes the previous one. “All is from Allah” speaks about creation and decree; “evil is from yourself” speaks about moral cause, deserving, and human responsibility. Allah creates the world in which choices have effects, while the servant earns blame through disobedience. There is no contradiction.

Every good is from Allah because guidance, ability, opportunity, and result are His gifts. Even a good deed performed by choice depends on life, knowledge, and strength He supplied. Gratitude therefore accompanies achievement. “Whatever blessing you have is from Allah” (16:53).

Evil can mean sin itself and hardships that come through what people earn, as 42:30 says. Human greed produces poverty, reckless choices cause injury, and disobedience wounds the heart. Yet not every hardship proves personal guilt. Prophets, children, and righteous people are tested without the trial being punishment for a particular sin. The verse must not be used to blame victims of illness, abuse, or disaster.

The believer holds several possibilities humbly: a hardship may be consequence, test, purification, elevation, warning, or a mixture whose wisdom is not fully known. He examines himself and repents without pretending to read Allah’s secret decree about others.

“We sent you to humanity” answers those blaming the Messenger. His mission is universal and his role is delivery of truth, not production of their misfortunes. Rejecting him because obedience feels costly does not change who sent him.

Allah’s witness is sufficient. He knows that the Messenger conveyed faithfully and knows how each listener responded. The Prophet does not need every hostile person to validate his mission. This comfort also guides callers today: convey accurately, correct mistakes, and leave hearts to Allah rather than manipulating people for visible success.

The next verse makes the relationship explicit: obedience to the Messenger is obedience to Allah because he conveys by divine authority. Whoever turns away remains responsible; the Prophet was not sent as a controlling keeper over every heart.

=80=

“Whoever obeys the Messenger has truly obeyed Allah. But whoever turns away—We have not sent you as a keeper over them.”

The Messenger’s authority is neither rivalry with Allah nor independent divinity. It comes entirely from being Allah’s messenger. When he commands as Messenger, obedience reaches the One who authorised him. The Quran therefore cannot be accepted while the authentic Sunnah is dismissed in principle; the Quran itself commands this obedience.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said, “Whoever obeys me has obeyed Allah, and whoever disobeys me has disobeyed Allah.” His Sunnah explains prayer, zakah, pilgrimage, marriage, commerce, and character. Without it, many Quranic commands would be praised in general while left unapplied.

Care is needed in claiming prophetic authority. Not every cultural habit, weak narration, personal opinion, or ruler’s preference is “the Messenger.” Hadith verification and sound interpretation protect people from obeying a human invention in his name. Scholars can be respected without becoming infallible intermediaries.

“Whoever turns away” establishes accountability but also limits the Prophet’s burden. He was not sent as hafiz—a keeper who forces faith into hearts and guarantees every response. His duties included conveying, teaching, judging, warning, and leading; guidance of the inner heart belongs to Allah. As 28:56 says, “You do not guide whom you love, but Allah guides whom He wills.”

This limitation rejects coercive control in religious work. Parents, teachers, and callers should use wisdom, patience, boundaries, and lawful authority, but they cannot manufacture belief. After sincere effort, another adult’s refusal is not proof that the caller must manipulate, spy, or destroy himself with grief.

It also removes excuses from the listener. The fact that the Messenger cannot force a heart does not make turning away harmless. Freedom from coercion is responsibility before judgment.

Verse 80 closes this file by reconnecting the whole section to verse 61. Hypocrites turned away when invited to revelation and the Messenger; true obedience to that Messenger is obedience to Allah. The following verse will show their split behaviour more closely: they say “obedience” in his presence, then groups spend the night planning something else after they leave.
