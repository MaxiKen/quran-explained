=81=

“They say, ‘We obey.’ But when they leave your presence, a group of them spend the night plotting something contrary to what you said. Allah records whatever they plot by night. So turn away from them, put your trust in Allah, and Allah is sufficient as a Trustee of affairs.”

The words “we obey” are easy in the Prophet’s gathering. The test comes after people leave, when no believer appears to be listening. Bayyata describes arranging something at night: private planning, calculated revision, or conspiracy. Their public agreement and hidden agenda point in opposite directions.

Allah “records” what they plan. Every message, whispered agreement, concealed instruction, and intention that becomes settled design enters a record no deleted chat can erase. The hypocrites treated darkness as protection, but night and day are equally open to the One who knows hearts.

The verse does not condemn all confidential planning. Families, governments, and communities need private deliberation, especially in danger. The wrong is planning “other than what you said”—professing loyalty while secretly undermining a lawful command, betraying people who trusted the public word, or organising harm.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, is told to turn away from them rather than let every plot consume his attention. This does not mean ignoring credible threats; the surah has commanded precautions. It means refusing to be emotionally ruled by people whose secrecy Allah already surrounds. Appropriate action is taken, then the matter is entrusted to Him.

Tawakkul rests on Allah as Wakil, the One sufficient to manage affairs. A human trustee can lack information or power; Allah lacks neither. Reliance therefore combines truthful effort with release of the outcome. Endless suspicion is not vigilance, and anxiety is not an extra layer of security.

For believers, the verse is a private integrity test. Do words of agreement survive after leaving a meeting? Does a person praise justice before a leader and sabotage it among friends? The cure is not merely avoiding discovery but remembering the divine record.

How can hidden contradiction be healed? The next verse points to tadabbur—deep reflection on the Quran. If the plotters considered revelation carefully, they would recognise its coherent divine origin instead of treating it as words to approve publicly and evade privately.

=82=

“Do they not reflect deeply on the Quran? Had it been from anyone other than Allah, they would certainly have found in it many contradictions.”

Tadabbur is more than sounding out words. It means following a passage toward its implications, connecting its parts, asking what it reveals about Allah and the self, and allowing its guidance to change judgment. The question “Do they not?” makes failure to reflect a moral problem when the Book is available.

The Quran was revealed across roughly twenty-three years: in Makkah and Madinah, during persecution and authority, addressing worship, family, economics, war, peace, history, and the unseen. Yet its central testimony, moral direction, and account of reality remain coherent. Later passages clarify or legislate stages without exposing a competing author or changing deity.

“Many contradictions” does not mean every reader will immediately understand every verse. Some passages are concise, others detailed; some rules were revealed gradually; words carry several valid shades; and verses address different circumstances. Apparent tension calls for language, context, authentic Sunnah, and the method stated in 3:7 of returning what is less clear to what is clear. Honest questions differ from collecting fragments to manufacture a contradiction.

Human speech produced over decades usually reflects forgotten claims, shifting interests, and limited knowledge. The Quran openly challenges scrutiny while attributing its unity to divine origin. It also corrects the Prophet in places, which is difficult to explain as a text designed merely to protect his personal image.

Reflection must be joined to humility. A clever person can use study to display himself rather than receive guidance. The hypocrites heard enough to say “obedience” but did not let the Book unify their public and private selves. Tadabbur reaches conduct.

Today, reflection includes reading reliable translation, learning reasons of revelation, comparing related passages, consulting scholarship, and giving a verse time beyond a short clip. It does not require everyone to become a specialist, but it forbids confident shallowness.

The next verse gives an example of judgment without reflection: people broadcast every report of safety or fear. Instead of understanding consequences or referring news to those able to analyse it, they spread it immediately and risk serving Satan’s design.

=83=

“Whenever news of security or fear comes to them, they broadcast it. Had they referred it to the Messenger and those in authority among them, those able to draw sound conclusions would have understood it. Were it not for Allah’s favour and mercy upon you, you would have followed Satan—except for a few.”

Information about security and fear can change behaviour instantly. A report of victory may create reckless confidence; a report of danger may cause panic, expose plans, or damage morale. The verse condemns idha‘ah—spreading sensitive news before its truth, meaning, and consequences are understood.

The proper response was to refer it to the Messenger and responsible authorities. Among them were people who could yastanbitunahu, draw out its meaning as water is drawn from a hidden source. Raw information is not the same as sound conclusion. Verification, context, expertise, and awareness of public impact are necessary.

The verse supports responsible authority without making officials automatically truthful. Verse 59 already placed authority beneath Allah and the Messenger, and verse 58 required justice and trust. Reliable analysts are those qualified and accountable to derive a conclusion, not simply those holding a title. Where authorities are themselves corrupt, evidence and principled counsel remain essential.

The application to modern communication is immediate. Forwarded voice notes, edited videos, casualty claims, financial rumours, health scares, and alleged security alerts can travel to thousands before one person checks a source. Adding “unconfirmed” does not erase harm caused by sharing. A believer pauses, verifies, and asks whether the public needs the information.

Not every true fact should be broadcast at once. A troop location, identity of a vulnerable victim, private medical detail, or negotiation may be true but entrusted. Truthfulness includes placing truth where it belongs. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said it is enough falsehood for a person to repeat everything he hears.

Allah’s favour and mercy include revelation, prophetic guidance, trustworthy scholarship, and protection from impulsive satanic influence. Without that help, few would escape fear, excitement, and the social reward of being first with news.

After controlling information, the Quran commands the Prophet to stand firm even if others hesitate. He is responsible for his own obedience and must encourage believers, while Allah alone can restrain the enemy’s force.

=84=

“So fight in the cause of Allah; you are accountable only for yourself. Encourage the believers. Perhaps Allah will restrain the might of the disbelievers. Allah is far greater in might and more severe in punishment.”

The Prophet, peace be upon him, is commanded to fulfil his duty even when hypocrites lag and rumours weaken others. “You are accountable only for yourself” releases him from dependence on their courage, not from leadership. He must still encourage believers, organise lawful defence, and take the precautions already commanded.

This verse addressed an actual Messenger and head of a community confronting armed enemies. It is not permission for an isolated person to begin violence while claiming responsibility only for himself. War affects lives, treaties, and public order and therefore requires legitimate authority, a just cause, capacity, and the ethical limits taught by revelation.

Encouragement differs from coercion and manipulation. The Prophet reminded believers of Allah’s promise, modelled courage, consulted them, and cared for their conditions. He did not invent reports or hide the cost. Faithful leadership helps people act from knowledge and sincere intention.

“Perhaps Allah will restrain” teaches that one obedient stand can become a means of wider protection, while the outcome remains Allah’s. At times, readiness deters attack without battle. Strength can serve peace when disciplined by justice.

Allah is stronger in ba’s—force and might—than every army. Human power should therefore neither terrify believers into abandoning duty nor intoxicate them when they possess it. The One stronger than the enemy is also stronger than the Muslim force and will judge its conduct.

“More severe in punishment” warns persistent aggressors, but no individual may appoint himself executor of divine punishment beyond lawful limits. Worldly fighting has rules; Allah’s final requital belongs to Him.

The verse also gives a wider pattern for moral courage. A person cannot make his own clear duty conditional on everyone joining. He speaks truth, returns the trust, or protects the vulnerable while encouraging others without pretending to control them.

Encouragement itself carries moral weight. Verse 85 now generalises the principle: whoever supports a good cause receives a share in its result, while whoever uses influence for evil bears a share of that burden.

=85=

“Whoever intercedes for a good cause will have a share in it, and whoever intercedes for an evil cause will bear a share of it. And Allah is ever Watchful over all things.”

Shafa‘ah is using one’s position, words, or relationship to support another person’s matter. It can be a recommendation, mediation, appeal, introduction, public endorsement, or encouragement. Influence is not morally empty; it joins the influencer to the outcome.

A good intercession helps a qualified person obtain work, reconciles a conflict, protects someone from injustice, encourages lawful service, or brings a need to a person able to meet it. The Prophet, peace be upon him, told companions to intercede in good causes so they would be rewarded, while Allah would decree through his tongue what He willed.

An evil intercession uses access to defeat justice: pressuring a judge, concealing incompetence in a recommendation, arranging a bribe, excusing abuse, or mobilising people for aggression. Even if the intermediary does not receive the stolen money or commit the final act, he carries a kifl, a share of its burden.

This principle reaches digital influence. Sharing a fraudulent campaign, endorsing a harmful product without care, or directing followers toward slander can multiply wrongdoing far beyond the original speaker. Conversely, pointing people toward sound knowledge or urgent relief can continue producing reward.

Not every request should receive advocacy. The Quran later forbids cooperation in sin and aggression (5:2). Loyalty to family or friends does not make their cause good. An honest recommendation states what the speaker actually knows and discloses relevant limitations rather than lending a respected name to uncertainty.

Allah is Muqit, the One who maintains, witnesses, and has full power over every thing. He can trace influence through chains no human analytics recover. A sentence that shaped one decision and the many effects following it are not lost.

The passage moves from large public influence to the smallest social exchange: a greeting. Verse 86 teaches that when someone offers a greeting of peace, it should be answered with something better or at least equal. Great communal ethics are built through repeated acts of recognition.

=86=

“When you are greeted with a greeting, respond with one better than it, or at least return it equally. Surely Allah takes account of all things.”

Tahiyyah is a prayer of life, peace, and honour. The Islamic greeting, al-salamu ‘alaykum, is not a casual sound; it tells the other person that safety is intended and asks Allah’s peace upon them. Returning it protects the social bond the first speaker opened.

A better response adds sincere blessing: “Wa ‘alaykum al-salam wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuh.” At minimum, return what was offered. The verse establishes reciprocity while encouraging generosity. Goodness need not merely mirror; it can increase.

The Sunnah teaches initiating salam, spreading it among people, greeting those one knows and does not know, and allowing the rider, walker, smaller group, or younger person to begin according to manners. These guidelines organise courtesy, but anyone may take the reward of starting. Pride that waits to be greeted contradicts the purpose.

Context and wording matter when a greeting is hostile or deliberately distorted. The Prophet instructed an appropriate equal response when some People of the Book twisted al-salam into a wish of death. The principle of justice remains: kindness is not gullibility, and restraint does not require returning abuse with excess.

The greeting reaches homes, messages, calls, workplaces, and gatherings. It should not become an empty automated prefix followed by cruelty. A person who says peace and then spreads harm has contradicted his own prayer. Likewise, failing to answer from arrogance can wound more than the speaker realises.

Allah is Hasib, taking account of all things. Even a returned greeting is counted. This comforts those who imagine only dramatic sacrifices matter and warns against dismissing “small” manners. Societies become safe partly through thousands of such acknowledgements.

From a single greeting, the Quran returns to the ultimate gathering. The One who accounts for it all is Allah, alone worthy of worship, who will certainly gather humanity on the Day of Resurrection. No statement is truer than His.

=87=

“Allah—there is no deity except Him. He will surely gather you all on the Day of Resurrection, about which there is no doubt. And whose word is more truthful than Allah’s?”

Tawhid and resurrection are joined. The only One worthy of worship is the One able to recreate and gather every generation. No rival shares His court, and no soul remains outside the summons.

The emphatic wording removes uncertainty: He will surely gather you. Humanity spread from one soul at the beginning of this surah and returns to one assembly before its Lord. People separated by centuries, borders, status, and hidden graves will stand in the same reality.

The Day is called qiyamah, the rising. Resurrection is bodily and moral: bodies rise and truth stands. Accounts, trusts, greetings, influence, private plots, and public courage all meet their proper weight. The promise of gathering gives small actions seriousness without making worldly recognition necessary.

“Whose word is more truthful?” invites comparison only to close it. Human beings speak from limited knowledge, forget, exaggerate, or break promises. Allah’s knowledge is complete and His power ensures what He promises occurs. Belief in the Hereafter is therefore not guesswork about an unseen future but trust in the most truthful Speaker.

Doubt can still enter a believer’s emotions. The Quran treats it through signs of first creation, revived earth, coherent revelation, and Allah’s truthfulness. Seeking understanding is different from proudly suspending every duty until the unseen becomes visible; by then, faith’s test has ended.

The gathering also restrains conflict. People who disagree will not escape into separate realities forever. Oppressor and victim, misleading advocate and follower, soldier and civilian will meet before one Judge. This knowledge should make earthly judgments more careful.

The next verse addresses a disagreement among believers about a group of hypocritical deserters. The certain gathering means decisions about loyalty and danger must follow Allah’s revealed assessment rather than sentiment, while subsequent verses carefully distinguish active enemies from treaty partners and peaceful neutrals.

=88=

“Why are you divided into two groups concerning the hypocrites, when Allah has cast them back because of what they earned? Do you wish to guide those Allah has allowed to stray? Whoever Allah allows to stray, you will never find for them a way.”

Believers had split over how to assess a group whose outward affiliation conflicted with hostile conduct. Reports connect the passage to people who withdrew, deserted, or returned to an enemy environment, though commentators differed on the exact group. Some Muslims defended them while others recognised the threat.

Allah “cast them back” because of what they earned. Misguidance is not presented as arbitrary. They repeatedly chose hypocrisy, betrayal, or rejection until the consequence matched the direction they purchased. As 61:5 says, when people deviated, Allah let their hearts deviate.

The question about guidance does not forbid inviting wrongdoers to repent. While alive, people can return by Allah’s permission. It denies believers the power to manufacture guidance for those persisting under divine judgment. Sympathy cannot rename betrayal faithfulness, and personal affection cannot overrule clear evidence.

At the same time, the verse is not a licence to call every uncertain Muslim a hypocrite. The Prophet knew this group through revelation and demonstrated acts. Hidden hypocrisy is Allah’s knowledge; ordinary believers judge outward conduct through evidence and lawful process. Reckless accusations divide communities and can themselves serve enemies.

The “two groups” warning urges unity around a sound criterion. Security decisions made through factional loyalty—one side excusing every ally, the other condemning every suspect—lose justice. Revelation will now distinguish categories carefully.

Those actively wishing believers would abandon faith and joining hostile forces could not be treated as trusted protectors until their allegiance became real. Yet verses 90 and 91 immediately protect treaty members, neutral people who withdraw, and those offering peace. Reading only the harsh line while hiding its qualifications repeats the selective approach the Quran condemns.

Guidance and security both belong finally to Allah, but human responsibility remains evidence, distinction, and restraint. Verse 89 now describes the hostile category: people who want believers to become disbelievers like them and who turn back into belligerent opposition.

=89=

“They wish that you would disbelieve as they have disbelieved, so that you would all become alike. Therefore do not take them as protecting allies until they emigrate in Allah’s cause. But if they turn back into hostility, then seize them and fight them wherever you encounter them, and do not take any of them as a protecting ally or helper—”

The verse concerns a group combining professed connection to Muslims with active alignment to an enemy in wartime. Their wish was not peaceful difference; they wanted believers to abandon faith and become equal in rejection. Wilayah—strategic loyalty and protection—could not be entrusted to them while that hostility remained.

Migration was then a visible test of allegiance. Leaving the enemy camp for Madinah demonstrated willingness to join the believing community and bear its risks. The condition was tied to that historical order; after the conquest of Makkah, the Prophet said there was no longer that specific migration, though migration from sin and from places where faith cannot be practised retains broader meanings.

“If they turn back” leads to force because these were not merely private apostates or peaceful non-Muslims. The next verses show they were belligerents from whom Muslims needed military separation. Seizing and fighting belong to an armed conflict under the Prophet’s authority, not permission for individuals to kill someone who changes belief, criticises Islam, or lives among non-Muslims.

“Wherever you encounter them” concerns the theatre and condition of that conflict; it does not erase treaties, sanctuary, surrender, or non-combatant protection. Verse 90 begins with “except” and removes protected and peaceful groups from the command. Quranic interpretation must carry the qualification with the rule.

The verse is severe because divided loyalty during war can expose lives and destroy a vulnerable community. Trust cannot be built on slogans while conduct materially assists attack. Modern law similarly distinguishes private opinion from espionage or armed treason, though Islamic judgment requires due authority and proof.

Muslims must not weaponise this passage against minorities or political rivals. The Prophet, not an online faction, held lawful command; collective force has conditions, and intentional killing is gravely warned against in verse 93.

The sentence continues into verse 90, where treaty protection and genuine neutrality are made explicit. Anyone who joins a people with whom Muslims have a covenant, or who withdraws from fighting and offers peace, is not a lawful target.

=90=

“—except those who join a people bound to you by treaty, or those who come to you with hearts reluctant to fight either you or their own people. Had Allah willed, He could have empowered them to fight you. So if they withdraw from you, do not fight you, and offer you peace, then Allah gives you no way against them.”

This exception is essential to the previous verse. Association with a hostile population does not make every individual targetable. Treaty protection, genuine neutrality, withdrawal, and an offer of peace change the legal situation.

Some people joined tribes with whom Muslims had a covenant, thereby entering their protection. Honouring that covenant was more important than suspicion. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned severely against killing a person under treaty, saying the killer would not smell the fragrance of Paradise. Agreements remain binding even when dealing with disliked parties.

Others felt unable to fight Muslims or their own people. Their constricted chests describe moral or tribal conflict, not a secret licence to attack. If they withdrew, refrained from fighting, and offered salam—peaceful surrender or non-hostility—Allah allowed “no way” against them. Force ends where the stated threat ends.

The phrase “had Allah willed” reminds believers that neutral restraint is itself a mercy. Allah could have added these people to the enemy’s strength. Gratitude for reduced conflict should replace the urge to expand it.

Three practical conditions appear together: disengagement, no fighting, and peace. Judgment rests on observable conduct. If those conditions hold, speculation about what they might privately believe cannot justify violence. This is a major Quranic restraint on wartime excess.

The verse also corrects claims that Islamic warfare requires combat against everyone outside the faith. Here non-belligerence is protected explicitly, and elsewhere Allah permits kindness and justice toward those who do not fight believers or expel them (60:8). Faith can be proclaimed clearly without coercive aggression.

Treaties and neutrality today are governed through states, law, and recognised authorities. Private groups cannot decide that public covenants do not count or invent a personal state of war. Breaking security granted by one’s society is betrayal.

Verse 91 distinguishes another group who spoke peace opportunistically but repeatedly returned to persecution and attack. Unlike genuine neutrals, they neither withdrew reliably nor restrained their hands. Conduct again determines the category.

=91=

“You will find others who wish to be safe from you and safe from their own people. Whenever they are returned to persecution and hostility, they plunge back into it. If they neither withdraw from you, nor offer you peace, nor restrain their hands, then seize them and fight them wherever you encounter them. Against such people We have given you clear authority.”

This group sought security from both sides without the principled neutrality of verse 90. When among Muslims they presented peace; whenever hostile people called them back to fitnah, they plunged in. Fitnah here includes persecution, armed hostility, and pressure to abandon faith. Their repeated conduct exposed the deception.

The verse still offers them clear exits: withdraw, offer peace, and restrain their hands. Only if they refuse these conditions does fighting follow. Even with proven double-dealing, the Quran looks for observable cessation before authorising force.

“Clear authority” means the uncertainty surrounding peaceful neutrals no longer applies when active aggression is established. It does not mean unrestricted cruelty. The general rules against treachery, mutilation, killing non-combatants, and breaking covenants remain. Authority to fight a combatant is not authority to wrong everyone connected to him.

The early Muslim community faced fluid tribal allegiances in which a person could claim safety in Madinah and assist an attack elsewhere. Revelation supplied distinctions needed for survival without allowing fear to erase justice. Modern security problems also require distinguishing protected dissent, neutrality, criminal support, and direct participation through evidence rather than labels.

The language must not be detached from public authority. Individuals cannot investigate, convict, and execute people they suspect of double loyalty. Such vigilantism destroys due process and often kills the innocent—the very danger the next verses address.

The sequence from verses 88-91 is a lesson in complete reading. It moves from internal disagreement, to a hostile category, to treaty and neutral exceptions, and then to active double agents. Quoting one command while omitting its neighbouring restraints falsifies the passage’s moral structure.

Having regulated force against hostile groups, the Quran now turns to the sanctity of a believer’s life. Even an accidental killing requires emancipation, compensation, and atonement. War does not reduce human blood to a minor administrative error.

=92=

“It is not for a believer to kill another believer except by mistake. Whoever kills a believer accidentally must free a believing enslaved person and pay compensation to the victim’s family, unless they waive it as charity. If the victim was a believer from a people at war with you, then a believing enslaved person must be freed. If the victim belonged to a people bound to you by treaty, then compensation must be paid to the family and a believing enslaved person freed. Whoever cannot find one must fast two consecutive months as repentance from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

The opening makes intentional killing incompatible with faithful conduct. “Except by mistake” is not permission but recognition that fatal errors occur, especially amid conflict. Even without murderous intent, a life has been lost, so both the victim’s family and the killer’s relationship with Allah require response.

The kaffarah is freeing a believing enslaved person, turning a tragic loss of life into liberation of another life. The Quran repeatedly used expiations to open doors out of slavery. Where that institution no longer lawfully exists, the stated alternative applies: fasting two consecutive lunar months for one who cannot find a person to free.

Diyah, financial compensation, belongs to the victim’s family; it is not a price suggesting life can be replaced. It acknowledges their right and material loss. They may freely waive it as sadaqah, but social pressure must not turn forgiveness into another injustice. Legal systems and qualified scholarship determine liability, amount, and categories of accident rather than private bargaining alone.

If the believer belonged to an enemy people with no treaty, compensation was not delivered into the hostile group, but emancipation still remained as atonement. If the victim’s people had a treaty, their family received compensation and the emancipation was also required. Covenant protects rights across communal boundaries.

Fasting must be consecutive, showing the seriousness of negligent bloodshed. Illness and genuine interruption require juristic guidance. Repentance also includes regret, safer conduct, and any additional legal responsibility for recklessness.

Allah is Knowing of the difference between accident, negligence, and concealed intent, and Wise in joining spiritual expiation to family rights. Human investigators may be deceived; He is not.

The next verse removes every appearance of leniency toward deliberate murder. Intentional killing of a believer is threatened with Hell, anger, curse, and tremendous punishment—one of the Quran’s most concentrated warnings.

=93=

“Whoever kills a believer intentionally, their reward is Hell, remaining therein; Allah is angry with them, has rejected them, and has prepared for them a tremendous punishment.”

Five layers make the warning overwhelming: Hell as recompense, lasting residence, divine anger, exclusion from mercy, and an immense prepared punishment. Human life is sacred, and deliberate murder tears through a person, a family, and the security of the believing community.

The verse must be read with the Quran’s wider teaching on repentance. Scholars differed over how to express hope for an intentional murderer because of this severe wording, but the majority held that sincere repentance remains possible before death, supported by 25:68-70 and 39:53. Repentance does not cancel the victim’s rights: lawful retribution, compensation where accepted, and judgment remain for the family and authorities, while the Hereafter’s final decision belongs to Allah.

“Khalidan” carries a terrifying threat of abiding. If a person treated murder as lawful in defiance of Allah, disbelief may make it eternal in its fullest sense. A sinful believer who did not deny the prohibition remains under Allah’s judgment according to the complete body of texts. The verse should not be weakened into a temporary inconvenience or used to declare a named person’s final fate without authority.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said the destruction of the whole world is lighter before Allah than the unlawful killing of a believer, and taught that bloodshed is among the first matters judged between people. Religious slogans, factional loyalty, and anger do not reduce its gravity.

This warning condemns terrorism, sectarian killing, lynching, honour murder, death squads, and private execution. No individual may decide another Muslim is outside protection through suspicion or an online accusation. Courts can make grave mistakes; mobs are even less qualified.

The order after wartime verses is deliberate. Once force is discussed, revelation surrounds it with treaty exceptions, peace offers, accidental-killing liability, and the strongest warning against intention. The aim is disciplined justice, not release of violence.

Verse 94 adds another safeguard: when travelling or operating in danger, verify before treating a person as an enemy. Do not deny the faith or peace offered by someone merely to obtain worldly spoils.

=94=

“O believers, when you go forth in Allah’s cause, investigate carefully. Do not say to one who offers you peace, ‘You are not a believer,’ seeking the fleeting gains of worldly life, for with Allah are abundant gains. You yourselves were once in a similar condition, then Allah favoured you, so investigate carefully. Surely Allah is fully aware of what you do.”

The command fa-tabayyanu—verify carefully—is repeated. In a tense encounter, fear and desire can turn assumptions into fatal judgments. Repetition places a pause between suspicion and irreversible action.

Reports describe a man who offered salam or declared faith when encountered by a Muslim expedition, yet was killed and his property taken because the declaration was dismissed as self-protection. The verse rejects that dismissal when worldly gain motivates it. Usamah ibn Zayd was similarly devastated when the Prophet repeatedly asked how he could know the heart of a man he killed after the man pronounced the testimony of faith.

Outward signs receive legal protection; hidden sincerity belongs to Allah. A soldier cannot say, “He only said it from fear,” and then kill for livestock or possessions. If immediate conduct presents a real threat, it is assessed through evidence and rules of engagement, but imagined access to the heart is not evidence.

“You were once like that” reminds believers of vulnerability. They too may once have hidden faith, been unknown to the community, or possessed only an outward declaration that others had to accept. Allah favoured them with safety and clearer belonging. Memory of one’s own fragile beginning should produce restraint toward another.

The “fleeting gain” may be spoils, promotion, revenge, or the removal of a rival. Allah has abundant gains gained lawfully and without blood guilt. Greed narrows vision until a small possession appears worth a life; faith widens it toward His treasury.

The principle reaches beyond battle. Verify before declaring someone an unbeliever, destroying a reputation, forwarding an accusation, or imposing an irreversible penalty. The greater the harm, the stronger the duty of verification. Surah al-Hujurat repeats the command regarding news from an unreliable source (49:6).

Allah is Khabir, aware of action and motive. A false security explanation cannot hide desire for gain. After protecting those wrongly excluded from faith, the passage compares believers who remain seated without valid excuse with those who strive through wealth and life.

=95=

“Believers who remain behind—except those with a disabling condition—are not equal to those who strive in Allah’s cause with their wealth and their lives. Allah has raised those who strive with wealth and life one degree above those who remain behind. To each Allah has promised the finest reward, but He has favoured those who strive over those who remain with an immense reward—”

The exception “those with a disabling condition” is central. Ibn Umm Maktum, a blind companion, asked about his inability, and the qualifying words were revealed according to authentic reports. Revelation distinguishes unwillingness from incapacity. Disability does not make faith inferior or require a person to prove worth through harmful participation.

The verse concerns lawful striving in the military setting, supported by both wealth and personal service. Those who accepted danger and expense were not equal in rank to able believers who stayed without excuse. Equal human dignity does not mean every choice earns an equal degree.

Yet “to each Allah has promised the finest reward,” commonly understood as Paradise. The non-participating people addressed are still believers, not automatically hypocrites. Differences of rank exist within salvation. This protects motivation from becoming excommunication.

Jihad is broader linguistically than combat and includes striving against self and through truth, but the immediate contrast of sitting and risking life should not be dissolved into metaphor. At the same time, this verse cannot legitimate an unlawful war; reward attaches to “Allah’s cause,” with the conditions already explained.

Wealth is named before lives, showing that collective defence requires provision, equipment, care for dependants, and support from those not at the front. A person unable to serve physically may contribute through other lawful means according to need and authority.

Today, genuine disability includes more than what is visible, and qualified assessment may be needed. Mocking illness, trauma, or incapacity contradicts the revealed exception. Conversely, inventing an excuse while able repeats the lagging spirit criticised earlier.

The promise of greater reward should create service, not veteran arrogance. Sacrifice accepted by Allah produces humility because ability and survival were gifts. Verse 96 completes the sentence by describing degrees, forgiveness, and mercy from Him.

=96=

“—degrees of rank from Him, as well as forgiveness and mercy. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

The single degree mentioned in verse 95 now opens into darajat—many degrees from Allah. Differences in intention, sacrifice, endurance, benefit, and conduct are known fully to Him. Two people can stand in the same place while their inner ranks are far apart.

The rewards are not only status. Maghfirah covers sins, and rahmah brings the servant into divine care and Paradise. Strivers still need forgiveness; danger and sacrifice do not make anyone morally self-sufficient. A person could serve bravely while carrying faults only Allah’s mercy repairs.

“From Him” makes the degrees immune to human propaganda. Medals, titles, stories, and public memory may honour the wrong people or overlook the sincere. Allah knows the person who gave quietly, guarded ethics under pressure, or intended service but was prevented by a true excuse. The Prophet said that people left behind in Madinah shared the reward of an expedition because illness held them back while sincere intention accompanied the travellers.

This does not erase the practical distinction made in verse 95. Sincere intention rewards genuine incapacity; it is not an easy substitute for action when the ability and duty are present. Allah knows the difference between prevented and unwilling.

Forgiving and Merciful also comfort those who failed a past duty and repent. The passage has exposed lagging, opportunism, and careless force, but return remains open before death. Repentance should produce different conduct when the next responsibility arrives.

Degrees in the Hereafter place worldly rank in proportion. A person with little public authority may be elevated through sincerity, while a celebrated figure can lose through injustice. Compete for what Allah ranks, not merely for what institutions display.

The next verses address people who claimed weakness while remaining in a place where they could not practise faith and even supported hostility, though they had the ability to migrate. Angels challenge their excuse at death: was Allah’s earth not spacious enough to leave?

=97=

“When the angels take the souls of those who wronged themselves, they ask, ‘What condition were you in?’ They reply, ‘We were oppressed in the land.’ The angels say, ‘Was Allah’s earth not spacious enough for you to emigrate?’ Their home will be Hell—what an evil destination!”

The verse concerns people who remained with the hostile Makkan order despite having the ability to leave, and whose presence or participation compromised faith. They describe themselves as mustad‘afin, made weak, but the angels expose an available choice: migration.

“What condition were you in?” is not a request for information the angels lack; it makes the person state how life was spent. The answer appeals to environment, pressure, and helplessness. Those forces can be real, but they are not the whole account when an exit was reasonably possible.

They “wronged themselves.” Remaining under oppression can become self-wrong when a person can move but chooses property, status, comfort, or fear over the ability to worship. The earth belongs to Allah, not to the tyrant controlling one region. Migration is therefore an act of worship when undertaken to preserve faith.

This verse must not be used to shame every person trapped in war, poverty, abusive control, immigration restrictions, disability, or family dependence. Verses 98-99 immediately exempt those truly unable to devise a plan or find a route. Ability includes safe access, means, information, and realistic capacity—not merely a map showing another country.

Nor does Islam require leaving every non-Muslim society. Muslims may live where they can practise faith, fulfil covenants, raise families, and contribute justly. The issue is a situation that fundamentally prevents obedience or compels participation in persecution, combined with genuine capacity to relocate.

Migration also has internal forms. The Prophet defined the muhajir as one who abandons what Allah forbids. Physical relocation without leaving sin may change scenery but not allegiance. Still, the verse’s immediate meaning is real movement from a place of disabling oppression.

The severe destination warns against converting attachment into an excuse until death. Yet the Quran does not leave the category vague. The next verse identifies the genuinely oppressed—men, women, and children who possess neither a workable plan nor guidance to a way out.

=98=

“Except the truly oppressed among men, women, and children who have no means to escape and cannot find a way.”

The exception prevents a powerful command from crushing the powerless. Some people cannot migrate: they lack money, physical capacity, documents, safe transport, knowledge, or someone trustworthy to guide them. Children and dependants may not control any decision at all. Allah knows constraint in its full reality.

The Quran distinguishes mustad‘af from someone merely claiming the word. In verse 97, the excuse failed because the earth was open to those speakers. Here inability is described through two dimensions: no hilah, a workable means or plan, and no sabil, a route they can locate and follow. Desire without capacity is recognised.

This precision should shape human judgment. From safety, it is easy to tell refugees, abuse victims, or persecuted minorities simply to leave. A route may exist geographically but be closed by cost, checkpoints, legal custody, threats to relatives, or severe illness. Advice should be based on listening and practical support rather than accusation.

The verse also creates duties for those with power. If people have no means, others can help create one through sponsorship, safe passage, legal services, shelter, accurate information, and protection. Verse 75 already asked why believers would not defend oppressed men, women, and children crying for a protector and helper.

An exemption from migration does not mean approval of oppression. The unable person preserves faith as much as possible, avoids supporting wrong, asks Allah for release, and takes a safe opportunity if it later appears. Responsibility changes when capacity changes.

Men are included with women and children, rejecting the idea that every male can automatically escape or that vulnerability is shameful. Physical strength may not defeat imprisonment, surveillance, poverty, or legal barriers.

Allah’s judgment of these people is stated in verse 99 with hopeful tenderness: He may pardon them, and He is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving. The One who knows inability does not measure it by the impatience of outsiders.

=99=

“It is expected that Allah will pardon them. And Allah is Ever-Pardoning, All-Forgiving.”

‘Asa, “it is expected,” when used of Allah carries firm hope while preserving the servant’s humility. The truly incapable are not treated like those who used weakness as a cover. Divine judgment accounts for capacity, access, knowledge, coercion, and intention more accurately than any human tribunal.

Al-‘Afuww does more than conceal; He effaces and removes the trace of sin. Al-Ghafur covers and forgives repeatedly. These names are especially fitting for people whose constrained situation may leave unavoidable shortcomings. They do what they can and rest in a mercy larger than their options.

The verse demonstrates a general principle: Allah does not burden a soul beyond capacity (2:286). Obligations remain real, but their application includes illness, compulsion, inability, and necessity. Exceptions are not loopholes invented against the law; they are part of the law revealed by the Merciful.

Humans should be cautious where Allah expresses pardon. A community can become harsher than revelation by demanding identical performance from unequal capacities. Accessible mosques, disability accommodations, financial support, and trauma-aware counsel can embody belief in these names.

At the same time, a person should not rush to award himself the exemption while refusing available means. Self-deception was the issue in verse 97. Honest consultation can help distinguish genuine inability from fear that must be worked through. Allah knows when capacity returns.

Pardon also gives hope to anyone who recognises too late that he remained in a harmful environment longer than he should have. If life remains, repentance includes taking the feasible next step. Mercy does not require waiting passively.

The passage now encourages those able to migrate. Leaving for Allah does not lead only into emptiness: the earth contains many places of refuge and spacious provision. Even if death overtakes the traveller before arrival, the reward has already become due with Allah.

=100=

“Whoever emigrates in Allah’s cause will find many places of refuge and abundant room throughout the earth. Whoever leaves home emigrating to Allah and His Messenger and is then overtaken by death—their reward has already become due from Allah. And Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful.”

Muragham carries the sense of a place of escape where the oppressor is frustrated: the person thought trapped finds another ground on which to live and worship. Sa‘ah is spaciousness in provision, community, and possibility. Oppression tries to make its territory feel like the whole world; Allah reminds believers that His earth is wide.

Migration remains costly. The verse does not promise every traveller immediate comfort or wealth. The emigrants to Madinah experienced disease, homesickness, and poverty before stability. “Abundant room” may appear through new relationships, lawful worship, inner freedom, or provision unfolded over time.

The intention is “to Allah and His Messenger.” The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that deeds are by intentions and mentioned the emigrant whose migration is for Allah and His Messenger, as distinct from one travelling only for worldly gain or marriage. The same outward journey can carry different eternal value.

Death before arrival does not cancel the deed. Once the person sincerely leaves home and takes the path, reward “falls due” with Allah—a divine promise, not a debt creatures imposed upon Him. This comforts everyone whose faithful work ends unfinished. Results are not the only measure; truthful intention joined to real action has weight.

The verse can guide flight from persecution, relocation to preserve children’s faith, or leaving an environment of coercive sin when conditions truly require it. Such decisions need consultation, legal planning, family responsibility, and realistic assessment. Reckless travel that exposes dependants to greater avoidable harm should not be romanticised.

It also speaks to refugees and migrants already displaced: needing safety is not spiritual failure. Communities receiving them can become part of the “many refuges” Allah promised through housing, work, language support, and brotherhood.

The names Forgiving and Merciful close the journey. Migrants carry imperfect plans and past sins, but move toward a Lord who forgives. Verse 101 will address the traveller’s next practical need: prayer is shortened during travel, showing that even on the road worship remains central while divine law makes room for hardship.
