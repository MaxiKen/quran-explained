=161=

“And for taking interest although it had been forbidden to them, and for consuming people’s wealth unjustly. We have prepared for the disbelievers among them a painful punishment.”

The verse continues the reasons certain lawful foods were restricted for some among the Children of Israel. Two economic wrongs are added: taking riba despite prohibition and consuming wealth through batil—false and invalid means. Worship and finance cannot be separated when money becomes a tool of oppression.

Riba enriches a creditor through a guaranteed increase tied to another person’s need or delay. It had been forbidden in earlier revelation, just as the Quran forbids it decisively to Muslims (2:275-279). The criticism is therefore covenant-breaking after knowledge, not an ethnic trait. Muslim involvement in interest would repeat the sin rather than stand above it.

“Consuming people’s wealth unjustly” is broader: theft, bribery, fraud, embezzlement, manipulated judgment, false fees, and exploitation of vulnerable people. This surah has already protected orphan property, dowries, inheritance, trade by mutual consent, and trusts. The same moral law applies across communities.

The metaphor of eating makes unlawful wealth personal. Money becomes food, housing, education, and inheritance for one’s children, but the method by which it entered remains in the account. A polished contract cannot make batil lawful if deception or a forbidden return is its foundation.

“We prepared for the disbelievers among them” is deliberately specific. It does not condemn every Jewish person, nor say every sinner has left faith. The painful punishment concerns those among them who joined persistent disbelief to these wrongs. Verse 162 immediately honours those firmly grounded in knowledge who believed.

Modern finance is complex, and not every price difference, investment return, or instalment sale is riba. Muslims should learn contracts from qualified scholars and competent financial professionals rather than declaring products lawful or forbidden from labels alone. Complexity, however, cannot be used to hide an interest-bearing loan behind cosmetic wording.

Repentance requires leaving the prohibited arrangement as lawfully as possible, returning unjust property, and resolving claims without creating further harm. Allah’s warning protects the financially weak and the soul of the person tempted by gain.

Verse 162 now contrasts corrupt use of scripture with knowledge that leads to humility, prayer, zakah, and complete faith in the Quran and earlier revelation.

=162=

“But those among them firmly grounded in knowledge, as well as the believers, believe in what has been revealed to you and what was revealed before you. Those who establish prayer, give zakah, and believe in Allah and the Last Day—We will grant them an immense reward.”

The word lakin—“but”—refuses collective condemnation. After listing serious violations by some, Allah praises others from the same scriptural communities. Quranic justice distinguishes people according to knowledge, faith, and conduct rather than ancestry.

Al-rasikhuna fi al-‘ilm are rooted in knowledge. Their learning produces stability and humility, like those in 3:7 who say of revelation, “We believe in it; all is from our Lord.” Knowledge that feeds envy or concealment is not rooted; it is information detached from surrender.

They believe in what came to Muhammad, peace be upon him, and in genuine revelation before him. Accepting the Quran does not require contempt for the original Torah, Psalms, or Gospel. The final Book confirms their divine origin, corrects later claims, and becomes the preserved criterion.

Prayer and zakah show belief entering body and property. Salah orders the servant’s time around Allah; zakah prevents religious learning from ignoring the poor. Belief in the Last Day gives both practices accountability beyond appearance.

The unusual grammatical form in “those who establish prayer” was understood by Arabic scholars as a construction of praise, drawing special attention to prayer, not as a textual error. The Quran’s transmission and early recitation preserve the wording, while Arabic grammar recognises such shifts for emphasis.

Abdullah ibn Salam is often named among learned Jewish figures who recognised the Prophet and believed. The verse leaves the category broader than one person: anyone who responds sincerely enters the promise.

An “immense reward” replaces the painful punishment of verse 161. A past community label does not lock an individual into either outcome. Truthful knowledge, faith, worship, and generosity open the same reward to all.

The next verses place Muhammad’s revelation in the long prophetic chain. What came to him is the same kind of divine communication given to Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Isa, and many other messengers.

=163=

“Indeed, We have revealed to you as We revealed to Nuh and the prophets after him. We revealed to Ibrahim, Isma‘il, Ishaq, Ya‘qub and the Tribes, and to Isa, Ayyub, Yunus, Harun, and Sulayman. And to Dawud We gave the Psalms.”

Muhammad’s revelation is not an isolated spiritual claim. Allah places it in a recognisable history beginning here with Nuh, commonly described as the first messenger sent to a people sunk in widespread idolatry. The source, essential creed, and moral purpose are one.

Ibrahim’s family follows: Isma‘il, ancestor of Muhammad’s Arab line; Ishaq and Ya‘qub; and al-Asbat, the tribal descendants among whom prophets arose. Mentioning both branches defeats the idea that divine choice belongs permanently to one ethnicity. Prophethood is Allah’s gift.

Isa appears in the same list as the other prophets. This preserves his immense honour without separating him into divinity. Ayyub models patience through suffering; Yunus repentance and renewed mission; Harun supportive leadership beside Musa; Sulayman gratitude amid kingdom. Their lives differ while their worship points to one Lord.

Dawud receives the Zabur, the Psalms, a revealed book associated with praise, wisdom, and remembrance. Muslims honour its original revelation without claiming that every later textual form is identical to what Allah first gave.

The list is not chronological at every point and does not aim to contain every prophet. It teaches continuity and representative variety: family patriarchs, kings, sufferers, callers, and the Messiah all received guidance from the same God.

The verb of revelation protects two truths. Prophets are not authors of independent religions, and they are not divine. They are chosen servants receiving communication. Muhammad belongs to their brotherhood and seals it rather than competing with it.

For Muslims, belief in these names should become informed love, not a memorised list. Their Quranic stories train patience, tawhid, leadership, family care, and repentance. Insulting any true prophet conflicts with faith in the One who sent them.

Verse 164 expands beyond named prophets. Some messengers were already recounted and others were not, and Musa is singled out for the distinction that Allah spoke to him directly.

=164=

“There are messengers whose stories We have already told you and others We have not told you. And Allah spoke directly to Musa.”

The Quran’s named prophets are not the complete history of revelation. Allah says every nation received a messenger (16:36), while many names and stories remain unknown. Guidance was not restricted to one small region merely because the final Book focuses on histories most relevant to its first audience and message.

This creates humility when Muslims encounter ancient cultures. A revered historical teacher cannot be confidently declared a prophet without evidence, nor can it be claimed that Allah left distant peoples entirely without warning. The safe position honours the general truth and stops where revelation stops.

The untold messengers also comfort unnoticed callers. A prophet can be real and honoured before Allah without his name appearing in the final scripture. Human fame is not the measure of acceptance.

Musa is then given a distinctive honour: kallama Allahu Musa taklima. The emphatic wording affirms real divine speech to him, in a manner befitting Allah, without reducing it to Musa’s imagination. For this reason he is called Kalim Allah, the one to whom Allah spoke.

Affirming speech does not require imagining Allah through created vocal organs or physical limitations. “Nothing is like Him” (42:11). Believers affirm what revelation affirms and avoid speculation about its modality.

Prophets have differing virtues. Musa was spoken to, Ibrahim taken as intimate friend, Isa created by a word and raised, and Muhammad given the final universal revelation. These distinctions do not permit rejecting one in favour of another.

The breadth of prophetic history removes humanity’s excuse. Verse 165 states that messengers brought good news and warning so people would have no argument against Allah after the message reached them. Perfect might and wisdom govern that system.

=165=

“They were messengers bringing good news and warning, so that humanity would have no argument against Allah after the messengers. And Allah is Almighty, All-Wise.”

Prophetic teaching holds hope and fear together. Good news announces forgiveness, divine pleasure, guidance, and Paradise for those who believe and return. Warning names sin, judgment, and Hell so people do not mistake delay for safety. Removing either side distorts the mission.

Messengers establish hujjah, the decisive case. No community is judged for rejecting a message never conveyed in a recognisable way. Allah says elsewhere that He does not punish until He sends a messenger (17:15). His justice is never based on inaccessible information.

How this principle applies to individuals who received only a distorted image of faith belongs to Allah’s complete knowledge and judgment. Scholars have discussed people unreached by an authentic message, but believers should avoid confident verdicts about named souls. The One who knows access, capacity, and intention will not wrong anyone.

Once truth is made clear, “I did not know” cannot remain a shield for chosen refusal. The messenger teaches, demonstrates, answers, and warns; responsibility then rests on the listener. The presence of disagreement does not mean evidence was absent.

Callers after the prophets do not establish proof with prophetic authority of their own. They must convey accurately, admit limits, and avoid making Islam unrecognisable through cruelty or falsehood. A bad presentation can increase the caller’s burden rather than the listener’s clarity.

Al-‘Aziz has power to send, protect, and judge His messengers. Al-Hakim chooses the right message, time, and method. Might ensures the case cannot be defeated; wisdom ensures it is not unjust.

The Prophet faced denial despite this prophetic continuity. Verse 166 comforts him that Allah Himself bears witness to the Quran, sent with His knowledge, and the angels also testify. Allah alone is sufficient as Witness.

=166=

“But Allah bears witness to what He has revealed to you. He sent it down with His knowledge, and the angels bear witness as well. Yet Allah is sufficient as a Witness.”

Human acceptance does not create revelation’s truth. Even if hostile scholars deny Muhammad, peace be upon him, Allah testifies to what He sent. The highest witness makes the refusal of every lesser witness unable to invalidate the message.

“Sent it with His knowledge” means the Quran comes from complete knowledge and contains what He knowingly chose to reveal. Its law understands human nature; its history is not guesswork; its account of the unseen comes from the Knower of the unseen. It also descended under His supervision and protection.

This phrase does not mean the Quran contains the totality of Allah’s infinite knowledge. Creation could never encompass that. It contains knowledge sufficient for the purpose of guidance, in wording He selected.

The angels witnessed the transmission of revelation, especially Jibril who brought it to the Prophet’s heart. Their testimony joins the testimony in 3:18, where Allah, the angels, and people of knowledge bear witness to tawhid and justice.

Allah’s sufficiency as Shahid strengthened the Prophet when recognition from the People of the Book was mixed. Callers today can also avoid measuring truth solely by polls, applause, or ridicule. This does not permit arrogance or refusal to examine evidence; it prevents popularity from becoming the criterion.

The verse also warns anyone who claims Allah’s testimony for a personal idea. Only revelation carries this guarantee. Dreams, political programmes, and scholarly opinions remain human and must be measured rather than equated with the Quran.

Witness leads to accountability. Verse 167 describes those who disbelieve and actively hinder others from Allah’s way as having wandered far. Their harm extends beyond personal refusal into obstruction.

=167=

“Indeed, those who disbelieve and hinder others from Allah’s way have certainly strayed far away.”

Personal disbelief becomes socially more destructive when it turns into sadd—blocking the path. This may include persecution, distortion, threats, mockery designed to intimidate seekers, concealment of evidence, or using power to prevent worship.

The verse does not treat every disagreement or criticism as “hindering Allah’s way.” People may ask difficult questions, argue from another belief, or expose genuine Muslim misconduct. Hindrance is active obstruction of truth and access, not the existence of an opposing opinion.

A Muslim can also become an obstacle through hypocrisy, abuse, ignorance spoken confidently, or needless harshness. Although the verse directly addresses disbelieving opponents, believers should fear presenting the religion in a way that drives people from what Allah actually taught. Correcting misconduct is part of opening the path.

“Far straying” describes both distance and direction. Someone who refuses privately may still encounter reminders; the person investing influence in keeping others away repeatedly reinforces his own rejection. Each new follower of the obstruction makes retreat feel more costly to pride.

Those misled retain their own responsibility, but the leader receives a share of the harm he encouraged. The Prophet taught that whoever calls to misguidance bears sin like the sins of those who follow without reducing their burdens.

The door of repentance remains open while alive. A former opponent can stop obstructing, correct misinformation, and use the same influence to open access. Some companions once fought Islam and later defended it sincerely.

Verse 168 intensifies the warning for those who combine disbelief with wrongdoing and persist to death: Allah will not forgive them or guide them to a saving path. Verse 169 names the only path left as Hell forever.

=168=

“Indeed, those who disbelieve and commit wrongdoing—Allah will neither forgive them nor guide them to any path—”

The pairing of kufr and zulm describes persistent rejection expressed through wrong. Every disbelief wrongs the soul, and in this context it also includes obstructing others. The warning concerns people who remain on that course until the opportunity for repentance closes.

Allah’s refusal to forgive here must be read with verses promising forgiveness to anyone who repents before death. Former disbelievers who enter faith sincerely are not excluded. The final state, not an irreversible label placed at the first refusal, is the issue.

Guidance is withheld after people repeatedly reject its means. Allah does not turn away a sincere seeker arbitrarily. Their chosen path becomes the judgment upon them, as earlier verses said He leaves people to what they deliberately turn toward.

Zulm also warns that creed and conduct interact. A person may defend disbelief through oppression, and oppression can make truth threatening because accepting it would require restitution. Returning to guidance may therefore demand returning property, admitting slander, or releasing power.

The verse should not be used to claim knowledge of who has died upon fully established rejection. Allah knows what reached each person and what remained hidden. Muslims state the general warning, convey truth, and leave the complete individual account to Him.

The unfinished sentence draws the reader into verse 169. No path to pardon or Paradise remains for those who die persisting in that state; the path is Hell, lasting forever, and maintaining that judgment is easy for Allah.

=169=

“—except the path of Hell, where they will remain forever. And that is easy for Allah.”

The wording uses “guide” with a terrible irony. They refused the straight path until the only destination left is the road to Hell. It is not guidance of love and approval but being delivered to the consequence their choices earned.

“Khalidina fiha abada” removes expectation that final disbelief will simply fade into non-existence or guaranteed release. The Quran’s warning is eternal. It should create urgency now, when faith and repentance remain possible.

“That is easy for Allah” affirms power, not pleasure in suffering. Resurrecting generations, judging every detail, and sustaining the final order do not tire Him. The same effortless power creates Paradise and grants mercy to believers.

Human intuition may struggle with eternity. The Quran places final judgment in the knowledge of the One who fully knows the reality of rebellion, every opportunity, and the nature of the Hereafter. Believers affirm His justice and avoid pretending their partial information exceeds His wisdom.

Fear of this path should not become despair over a living person. The verse describes persistence ending in death. A breath used for sincere shahadah and repentance can change the destination by Allah’s mercy. The warning’s purpose is movement toward that rescue.

Nor does belief in Hell permit cruelty on earth. Humans are bound by justice, due process, treaties, and mercy. Final punishment belongs to Allah, who alone possesses the knowledge and authority it requires.

After this severe conclusion, verse 170 addresses all humanity with an open invitation. The Messenger has come with truth from their own Lord; belief is better for them, while rejection cannot diminish the Owner of the heavens and earth.

=170=

“O humanity, the Messenger has come to you with the truth from your Lord, so believe—it is better for you. But if you disbelieve, then surely to Allah belongs whatever is in the heavens and the earth. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

The address is universal. Muhammad, peace be upon him, was not sent only to Arabs, one class, or one century. The Quran calls every people while respecting that actual accountability depends on how the message reaches each individual.

He came bil-haqq: with a message corresponding to reality, establishing justice, and sent purposefully by the Lord who created and nurtures humanity. Faith is not surrender to a foreign tribal claim but response to one’s own Rabb.

“Believe; it is better for you” is remarkable in gentleness after the warning of Hell. Allah does not need to exaggerate the invitation. Faith benefits the believer through meaning, purification, guidance, and eternal rescue. Rejection injures the rejecter.

If humanity disbelieves, Allah’s ownership remains complete. Stars, angels, earth, bodies, and every resource are already His. Worship does not fill a divine lack, and denial does not shrink His kingdom. This removes the fantasy that belief is a favour people grant God.

Al-‘Alim knows who has received truth, what barriers exist, and what the heart chooses. Al-Hakim sends a universal Messenger and judges every response wisely. The paired names prevent both careless universal condemnation and the idea that truth is merely relative.

Believers should convey the verse in its own spirit: clear that Muhammad is the Messenger, confident in truth, and sincerely concerned for the listener’s good. Coercion, mockery, and cultural superiority contradict the invitation.

The next verse addresses the People of the Book, especially Christians, correcting exaggeration about Isa. It honours him as Messiah, son of Maryam, Allah’s messenger, His word cast to Maryam, and a spirit from Him—while forbidding the claim of three and affirming the one God above having a child.

=171=

“O People of the Scripture, do not go to extremes in your religion or say anything about Allah except the truth. The Messiah, Isa son of Maryam, was only a messenger of Allah, His word conveyed to Maryam, and a spirit from Him. So believe in Allah and His messengers, and do not say, ‘Three.’ Stop—it is better for you. Allah is only One God. Glory be to Him—He is far above having a son! To Him belongs whatever is in the heavens and earth, and Allah is sufficient as Trustee of affairs.”

Ghuluw is crossing proper limits through exaggeration. Love of a prophet becomes misguidance when honour is raised into worship. The correction does not insult Isa; it protects the identity he taught: a servant and messenger of the One God.

“Son of Maryam” anchors him in miraculous human birth and honours his mother. “Allah’s word” means he was created by the command “Be” without a human father, as 3:59 compares him to Adam. The word created Isa; Isa is not the eternal divine speech or a second deity.

“A spirit from Him” is an attribution of origin and honour: a created spirit brought into being by Allah and sent by His command. “From Him” does not mean a piece of the divine essence, just as provisions “from Allah” are not parts of Him. The Creator is indivisible and unlike creation.

“Do not say Three” rejects every formulation that divides divinity or places Isa and the Holy Spirit within a triune godhead. The Quran’s alternative is direct: Allah is one ilah. His knowledge, speech, and power are perfect attributes, not separate persons sharing deity.

A child answers needs of created life—continuity, likeness, companionship, or inheritance. Allah is eternal, self-sufficient, and owner of everything; sonship in a divine sense is unworthy of His transcendence. Subhanahu declares Him far above the claim.

Muslims should explain this disagreement respectfully. Christians use careful language for the Trinity and do not describe themselves as worshipping three independent gods, but Islam still rejects the doctrine as incompatible with uncompromised tawhid. Accuracy about another belief strengthens honest dialogue.

The verse also warns Muslims against excess concerning Muhammad, saints, or scholars. The Prophet forbade exaggerating him as Christians exaggerated Isa and said he should be called Allah’s servant and messenger. Love stays within the truth.

Verse 172 continues through Isa’s own posture: the Messiah would never disdain being Allah’s servant, nor would the nearest angels. Servanthood is his honour, not a status from which admirers should try to rescue him.

=172=

“The Messiah would never disdain to be a servant of Allah, nor would the angels nearest to Him. Whoever disdains His worship and acts arrogantly—He will gather them all before Him.”

Yastankif means regarding something as beneath one’s dignity. Isa does not consider ‘ubudiyyah beneath him. In the cradle he declared, “Indeed, I am the servant of Allah” (19:30). Calling him servant is therefore agreement with his own honourable identity.

The nearest angels likewise worship without exhaustion or pride. Their closeness to Allah is a closeness of rank and honour, not shared divinity. If Messiah and mighty angels bow, no human achievement can make worship too lowly.

Servanthood to Allah frees a person from servitude to ego, fashion, rulers, and created approval. What appears like surrender is the highest dignity because the Master is perfectly just and merciful. Refusing Him does not produce independence; it usually creates many lesser masters.

The verse distinguishes difficulty from disdain. A believer may struggle to rise for prayer or overcome a sin while still accepting Allah’s right. Arrogance says worship itself is beneath me or the command has no claim. The treatment is humility, knowledge, and repentance rather than pretending the two states are identical.

Allah will gather all—the humble and arrogant, humans and angels—before Him. The most honoured servants arrive as willing worshippers; the proud arrive unable to avoid the Lord they refused. Gathering exposes the irrationality of temporary pride.

The verse also denies angel worship. Nearness does not make an angel a god or independent intercessor. Every created being stands within Allah’s ownership.

Verse 173 explains the two outcomes at that gathering. Believing servants receive their wages in full and an increase from divine bounty; those who disdained worship receive painful punishment without protector or helper.

=173=

“As for those who believe and do righteous deeds, He will pay them their rewards in full and increase them from His bounty. But those who disdain worship and act arrogantly, He will punish with a painful punishment, and they will find neither protector nor helper besides Allah.”

Full payment establishes justice; increase establishes grace. No prayer, sacrifice, kindness, or restrained sin is deducted from the accepted record. Then Allah gives beyond equivalence—eternal life for brief obedience, multiplied deeds, and pleasures no servant could earn by calculation.

Faith and righteous action again belong together. Claim without obedience is empty, while deeds without the foundation Allah requires do not establish the same covenant. Repentance remains part of righteous action for imperfect believers.

Arrogance receives pain because it rejected the very relationship that brings mercy. The disdainful person treated worship as humiliation; on the Last Day, imagined independence collapses into real humiliation.

No wali can protect and no nasir can rescue independently. Isa, angels, saints, wealth, and status cannot overrule Allah. Intercession belongs entirely to His permission and never supports a creed He rejects.

The verse does not say believers enter by merit without mercy. The “increase from His bounty” surrounds their payment, and the Prophet taught that no one enters Paradise by deeds alone unless Allah covers him with mercy. Deeds are the path He accepts and grace is the reason the reward exceeds them.

A believer should seek the increase, not merely minimum survival. Sincerity, excellence, hidden service, and love of Allah turn duty into a path toward fadl. Yet no one can demand a particular rank from Him.

The surah’s long theological argument now concludes with another universal call. Verse 174 names revelation a conclusive proof and a clear light from the Lord. Proof addresses the mind; light enables the whole person to see the road.

=174=

“O humanity, there has come to you a conclusive proof from your Lord, and We have sent down to you a clear light.”

Burhan is evidence that establishes a case. Commentators applied it to the Prophet, the Quran, or the combined proof of messenger and revelation. Muhammad’s character, message, signs, and the Book he brought form a coherent testimony from the Lord.

The Quran is nuran mubina—a light clear in itself and clarifying what it touches. It illuminates Allah’s identity, human purpose, lawful and unlawful paths, history, death, and final return. Without revelation, intelligence can discover much but remains unable to see the unseen destination by itself.

Light requires opened eyes. Possessing a Quran, displaying calligraphy, or admiring recitation does not guarantee guidance if its demands are kept closed. Tadabbur, humility, and action allow light to reach the road.

Proof and light correct two imbalances. Religion is not blind emotion without evidence, and it is not argument without spiritual transformation. The mind receives burhan; the heart and life walk by nur.

“From your Lord” makes the address personal and universal. The One who formed and sustains people did not leave them without a map. Revelation is an act of care before it is a basis for judgment.

Clear light does not imply every legal question is simple to an untrained reader. Language, Sunnah, context, and scholarship remain necessary. Light enables sound distinction; it does not reward careless extraction.

The verse should make Muslims ask whether their conduct transmits or blocks that light. Injustice can make people mistake a Muslim shadow for Islam itself. Accurate teaching and beautiful character hold the lamp without claiming to create it.

Verse 175 describes the response: believe in Allah and hold firmly to Him. Such people are admitted into mercy and bounty and guided toward Him on a straight path.

=175=

“As for those who believe in Allah and hold firmly to Him, He will admit them into mercy and bounty from Himself and guide them to Himself along a straight path.”

I‘tisam is gripping something that protects from falling. Belief is not merely recognising that Allah exists; it is fastening trust, worship, and obedience to Him when desires and social pressures pull in other directions.

Holding to Allah occurs through the Quran, authentic Sunnah, prayer, du‘a, remembrance, repentance, and the believing community. These are not alternatives to Him but means He provided. A person who claims private attachment while rejecting His guidance is holding an idea of God rather than the revealed Lord.

Mercy and fadl are distinct gifts. Mercy shelters from deserved harm and admits to Paradise; bounty adds honour and blessing beyond what deeds merit. Both are “from Him,” so the servant enters with gratitude rather than self-congratulation.

The straight path leads “to Him”—to His pleasure and honoured nearness, not physical merging with the Creator. Allah remains distinct from creation. The destination is acceptance, vision in the Hereafter as authentic texts promise, and life in the home He prepared.

Guidance continues after belief. The believer needs direction through death, resurrection, the bridge, and entry into Paradise, just as guidance is needed through daily choices now. Asking for the straight path in every prayer never becomes redundant.

Holding firmly does not mean never slipping. It means returning the grip after a fall rather than choosing another protector. Allah’s mercy is especially visible in allowing repeated sincere return.

This verse could serve as a majestic ending, but the surah closes with practical law. Verse 176 returns from proof, light, and divine nearness to a disputed estate. Spirituality is tested in sibling shares, and Allah clarifies kalalah so families do not go astray.

=176=

“They ask you for a ruling. Say, ‘Allah gives you a ruling concerning one who dies without direct ascendants or descendants. If a man dies childless and has a sister, she receives one-half of what he leaves. And he inherits from her if she dies childless. If there are two sisters, they receive two-thirds of what he leaves. If there are brothers and sisters, the male receives the share of two females. Allah makes this clear to you so that you do not go astray. And Allah has full knowledge of all things.’”

Kalalah is an estate where the deceased leaves no direct descendant and, in the established legal understanding, no surviving father or direct male ascendant who would block these siblings. The verse answers a question reportedly asked by Jabir ibn Abdullah during illness and completes the sibling rules introduced in verse 12.

The siblings here are full siblings or paternal siblings under their respective conditions. Maternal siblings have the different shares stated in 4:12: one-sixth for one and a shared one-third for several, with male and female equal in that category.

One sister receives one-half when the stated conditions hold. Two or more sisters share two-thirds according to the prophetic and juristic application. Where brothers and sisters inherit together as residuaries, each brother takes the portion of two sisters, within the wider system of financial obligations placed upon men.

“He inherits from her” does not mean a brother always takes every asset regardless of other heirs. Spouses, mothers, grandmothers, debts, funeral expenses, and valid bequests may affect the distributable estate. It means he is her residuary heir in the childless kalalah situation after any fixed shares and applicable blockers are considered.

Inheritance calculations can change with one living relative. Families should not divide a kalalah estate from a short online chart or this commentary alone. A qualified inheritance specialist must identify every heir, debt, bequest, prior transfer, and jurisdictional requirement.

“Allah makes this clear so you do not go astray” shows that financial error can be moral deviation, not merely bad arithmetic. Clarity prevents stronger relatives from inventing shares and protects family ties from disputes after grief.

The surah ends with knowledge. Allah knows which relative lives, who benefited the deceased, what each law will preserve, and every hidden asset. Al-Nisa began with the Lord watching a single human family and ends with His perfect knowledge governing its final division. Between those points, worship becomes justice toward women, orphans, spouses, neighbours, travellers, minorities, witnesses, and the vulnerable. The next folder begins Surah al-Ma’idah with a command that gathers this entire ethic into two words: fulfil your covenants.
