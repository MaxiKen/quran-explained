=1=

“O humanity, be mindful of your Lord, who created you from a single soul, and from it created its mate, and from the two spread countless men and women. And be mindful of Allah, in whose name you appeal to one another, and honour family ties. Surely Allah is ever Watchful over you.”

Al-Nisa begins by addressing all people, not believers alone. Every law that follows—about orphans, marriage, property, and inheritance—is placed beneath one command: be conscious of the Lord who created you. Human rights are therefore not gifts granted by the strong. They are duties set by the Creator, who watches how the strong treat those placed in their care.

The “single soul” is Adam, and its mate is Hawwa. The Quran presents humanity as one extended family from one original pair. Differences of nation, colour, language, wealth, and sex come later; none creates a separate class of human worth. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said in his Farewell Sermon that no Arab is superior to a non-Arab, nor a non-Arab to an Arab, and no red person to a black person or black person to a red person, except through God-consciousness. The same truth appears in 49:13: peoples and tribes exist so that they may know one another, while honour before Allah rests on taqwa.

“From it created its mate” joins man and woman in one human origin and makes the family a place of companionship rather than ownership. The Sunnah also teaches that woman was created from a rib and urges men to treat women well; the lesson the Prophet drew was patience and gentleness, not contempt. This is especially important in a surah that corrects customs by which women and orphan girls were exploited.

People used Allah’s name when asking one another for help—“I ask you by Allah”—and they also appealed to bonds of kinship. The verse commands reverence for both. Al-arham, literally “the wombs,” turns kinship into something sacred: parents, children, siblings, spouses, and wider relatives must not be cut off over pride or money. The Prophet warned that the one who severs family ties will not enter Paradise, and he taught that maintaining them brings blessing in provision and life.

The final name, al-Raqib, means the One constantly observing and guarding. A guardian may hide an orphan’s missing money from a court, and a relative may disguise cruelty as family custom, but neither escapes His sight. That closing prepares the next verse: the orphan whose father is absent is not unprotected, because Allah is watching the guardian.

=2=

“Give orphans their wealth, and do not exchange the bad for the good, nor consume their wealth by mixing it with your own. Surely this is a great sin.”

After commanding care for family ties, Allah begins with those whose family protection has been broken by death. In Islamic law, an orphan is a child whose father died before the child reached puberty. The command to “give” them their wealth means to preserve it faithfully and hand it over when the conditions explained in verse 6 are met. Until then, the guardian is a trustee, never an owner.

Some guardians in pre-Islamic society would take a valuable animal, fertile field, or sound piece of property belonging to the orphan and replace it with one of inferior quality. Others mixed accounts so thoroughly that theft could be made to look like household expense. The Quran names both tricks. Changing labels does not change bad property into good property, and placing stolen wealth inside one’s own account does not make it lawful. The warning recalls 2:188: do not consume one another’s wealth falsely or use authorities to consume knowingly what belongs to others.

The command does not forbid every practical mixing. Verse 2:220 allowed guardians to share a household with orphans, saying, “They are your brothers,” while reminding them that Allah knows the corrupter from the reformer. The issue is dishonest mixing that benefits the guardian at the child’s expense. Accurate records, separate accounts where possible, fair valuation, and transparent expenses express the trust today. The same principle governs trustees of a minor’s inheritance, managers of orphan charities, foster carers, lawyers, and anyone holding money for a person too young or vulnerable to defend it.

Calling the act “a great sin” removes the excuse that the amount was small or the victim unlikely to notice. Its gravity comes partly from combining betrayal with exploitation of weakness. The Prophet, peace be upon him, counted consuming an orphan’s property among the seven destructive sins. Later, verse 10 will make the unseen reality vivid: those who devour such wealth are filling their stomachs with fire.

The verse also continues the watchfulness announced in verse 1. A child may lack an earthly advocate, but the Raqib sees each exchange and every entry in the ledger. Before discussing how many wives a man may marry or what heirs may receive, the surah teaches a more basic discipline: return property to its rightful owner. That discipline now leads directly to orphan girls whose beauty or inheritance could tempt their guardians into an unfair marriage.

=3=

“If you fear that you will not deal justly with orphan girls, then marry other women of your choice—two, three, or four. But if you fear that you will not maintain justice, then marry only one, or those in your possession. That is more likely to keep you from injustice.”

Aishah, may Allah be pleased with her, explained the setting of this verse. An orphan girl might grow up under a male guardian who admired her beauty or wanted to keep her property. He would marry her without offering the dowry that a woman of her standing would normally receive. The verse tells such a guardian: if you fear failing her rights, leave her alone and marry women outside your guardianship. The opening is therefore not an invitation to collect wives; it is a protection against exploiting an orphan through marriage.

The Quran limits an existing practice that had previously been unrestricted. Two, three, or four are permitted, never more at one time, and the permission is bound to justice. Justice includes equitable maintenance, housing, time, and outward treatment. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that a man with two wives who inclines unfairly to one will come on the Day of Resurrection with one side leaning. Feelings of the heart cannot be divided with mathematical equality, as 4:129 acknowledges, but a husband must not allow unequal affection to become neglect: “Do not incline completely toward one and leave another suspended.”

“If you fear” asks for honest judgment before desire takes control. A person who cannot support more than one household, protect the dignity of each wife, divide his presence fairly, and manage the likely effects on children should remain with one. The Quran does not command every man to marry several women. It gives a lawful permission that can meet real family and social needs while making justice a serious condition. Neither treating polygyny as a limitless male entitlement nor declaring unlawful what Allah permitted respects the verse.

“Those in your possession” refers to the historical institution of slavery and war captivity into which the Quran spoke. Islam did not create that worldwide system; it regulated it, closed many sources of enslavement, repeatedly encouraged emancipation, and made freeing people an act of worship and an expiation for sins. This phrase cannot be used to revive private enslavement, trafficking, coercion, or sexual abuse today. The Quran’s wider commands of consent, dignity, and manumission, and the absence of any lawful personal ownership system, leave no cover for such crimes.

The last phrase, alla ta‘ulu, carries the sense of not deviating into unfairness and not becoming overburdened in a way that produces neglect. Verse 2 protected an orphan’s assets; this verse protects her person and marital rights. The next verse makes one of those rights unmistakable: her dowry belongs to her.

=4=

“Give women their dowries graciously. But if they willingly remit any of it to you, then enjoy it with satisfaction and ease.”

The dowry, or mahr, is a wife’s direct financial right. Allah tells husbands to give it to the women themselves, not to their fathers, guardians, or tribes. Saduqatihinna is linked to sincerity, while nihlah conveys a freely presented gift and an obligation given with a good spirit. The mahr honours the marriage covenant; it is not a purchase price for the woman, and it does not transfer ownership of her person to her husband.

This instruction is particularly significant after the orphan girls of verse 3. A guardian who married his ward might reduce or withhold her mahr because she had no powerful family member to object. The universal wording now closes that path. Whether the bride is wealthy or poor, previously married or young, from a prominent family or an orphan in someone’s care, what was agreed as her dowry is hers. She retains her separate legal property after marriage and is not obliged to use the mahr to fund the household.

The second sentence is equally careful. A wife may choose to return or share some of it, but the Quran locates that choice in the willingness of her nafs—her own self. Pressure, embarrassment, threats of divorce, or a family custom that leaves her unable to say no do not amount to cheerful consent. Only when she gives genuinely may the husband take it as something wholesome and pleasant, without guilt. The Prophet, peace be upon him, established the general rule that a Muslim’s property is not lawful to another without willing consent.

Mahr need not be extravagant—the Prophet permitted a very simple dowry for a man of little means—but simplicity must be agreed, not imposed by belittling the bride. Once a valid mahr is due, its size does not make it less binding. The flow is deliberate: do not take an orphan’s property, marry an orphan girl unfairly, or withhold a woman’s dowry. In each case, power is restrained by ownership and consent.

=5=

“Do not hand over to the incapable the wealth which Allah has made a means of support for you, but provide them with food and clothing from it, and speak to them kindly.”

The word al-sufaha refers here to people unable to manage property responsibly—especially immature wards, though the principle can include someone whose severe lack of judgment would quickly destroy essential assets. The verse does not call them worthless. It distinguishes dignity from financial capacity. A person may need protection from a large transfer while still deserving maintenance, decent clothing, and respectful speech.

Wealth is described as qiyam: something by which life stands. Food, shelter, education, health, family stability, and the ability to give all depend upon it. Islam therefore rejects both worship of money and careless waste. Property ultimately comes from Allah and must be managed as a trust. Handing a minor an inheritance merely because he demands it, while knowing he will lose it through manipulation or impulse, is not generosity; it is abandonment.

Although Allah calls it “your wealth,” verse 6 confirms the ward’s ownership and orders its return upon sound judgment. Meanwhile, “provide them from it” permits reasonable, recorded spending for the beneficiary’s food, education, and welfare. Guardianship must not preserve an impressive balance while the child goes hungry, nor risk the inheritance through self-serving speculation.

Then comes an easily neglected command: speak honourable words. Financial restriction can humiliate. A guardian must not insult a child as foolish, use dependence to control him, or answer every request with contempt. He can explain, promise the property at maturity, teach budgeting, and involve the young person gradually. Kind speech appears beside material support because emotional care is part of the trust. As 2:263 says, a kind word and forgiveness are better than charity followed by injury.

The next verse turns restriction into a process rather than a permanent label. Test the orphans, look for rushd—sound judgment—and release their property promptly when it appears. Protection must never become an excuse for endless control.

=6=

“Test the orphans until they reach marriageable age. Then, if you perceive sound judgment in them, hand over their wealth to them. Do not consume it wastefully and hastily before they grow up. If the guardian is rich, let him abstain; if he is poor, let him take according to what is fair. When you hand over their property, call witnesses. And Allah is sufficient as a Reckoner.”

The Quran joins two measures of readiness. Reaching marriageable age indicates physical adulthood, while rushd means demonstrated sound judgment in financial affairs. Neither age alone nor a vague impression is enough. Before full transfer, guardians should test with suitable responsibilities: a small budget, purchases, saving, work, or supervised trade. The aim is education and evidence, not setting impossible tests so the guardian can retain control.

Once sound judgment is perceived, “hand over their wealth” is immediate. Guardianship has an end. A trustee cannot keep an adult’s inheritance because he thinks his own plans are wiser, dislikes the person’s lawful choices, or benefits from controlling the assets. If serious incapacity remains, protective legal measures should be proportionate and reviewable rather than an informal lifetime seizure.

Allah exposes a particularly ugly temptation: spending rapidly because the orphan will soon grow and ask for an account. Israf is waste beyond proper need; bidaran is haste driven by the approaching deadline. A guardian who renovates his own home, inflates charges, or arranges dubious fees just before transfer repeats this wrongdoing in modern form. Transparent accounts and independent oversight help turn the verse into practice.

A wealthy guardian should take nothing for himself. A poor guardian who devotes real time to the estate may take a reasonable amount bil-ma‘ruf—according to recognised fairness, not whatever he can conceal. Scholars discussed whether this is compensation, a loan, or an allowance according to need, but all readings exclude enrichment and excess. The Prophet, peace be upon him, told the guardian of an orphan to eat from the property without extravagance, without haste, and without accumulating it for himself.

Witnesses at handover protect both sides. They confirm what was transferred and prevent later accusation or denial. Today, signed inventories, receipts, valuations, bank statements, and legal acknowledgment serve the same wise purpose. Still, documents are not the final judge. “Allah is sufficient as a Reckoner” returns to the Raqib of verse 1: He calculates the unrecorded benefit, the disguised fee, and the sincere service perfectly.

The child whose wealth is protected may have received it through inheritance. The surah now moves from individual guardianship to the estate itself, first establishing a revolutionary rule for its society: women as well as men possess obligatory shares.

=7=

“For men there is a share of what parents and close relatives leave, and for women there is a share of what parents and close relatives leave, whether the property is little or much—a mandatory share.”

Before listing fractions, Allah establishes the principle twice: men have a share, and women have a share. In pre-Islamic Arabia, inheritance was often reserved for males who could fight, while women and young children—those most likely to need protection—were excluded. The Quran did not present their inclusion as optional kindness. Nasiban mafruda means an assigned, binding share.

The repetition gives women independent legal standing. A daughter, wife, mother, and sister do not receive merely through the goodwill of male relatives. Their entitlements come from Allah and become their own property. A family cannot replace a daughter’s inheritance with wedding expenses, claim that supporting her earlier cancelled her share, or pressure her to “gift” it back for family harmony. A gift extracted through shame is not free consent. Cultural practice cannot overrule a mandatory portion.

“Whether little or much” blocks another excuse. A modest estate still deserves lawful division. After funeral expenses, debts, and valid bequests are settled, even a small balance belongs to the heirs in their assigned proportions. Families sometimes leave land undivided for decades because subdivision is inconvenient, allowing one person to enjoy it while others hold only names on paper. Fair valuation, rent accounting, a buyout, or sale may be needed so that each heir actually receives the right rather than an empty promise.

The later rules do not give every male twice every female: parents can receive equal sixths, and maternal siblings share equally. Debts, bequests, surviving relatives, and degrees of kinship also change results. The system requires careful learning rather than slogans, and prevents personal affection or prejudice from deciding everything.

Once legal entitlement has been secured, the next verse adds generosity. A division may be mathematically correct and still wound needy relatives or orphans who are present but do not inherit. Law gives each owner a boundary; mercy teaches owners what to do around that boundary.

=8=

“If other relatives, orphans, and the poor are present at the time of distribution, give them something from the estate, and speak to them kindly.”

Inheritance draws people into one room at a time of grief, expectation, and possible conflict. Some relatives are too distant to receive a fixed share. Orphans and poor people may watch property pass to others while feeling their own need sharply. The Quran does not permit the legal heirs to use technical correctness as an excuse for coldness. Give those present something and address them in an honourable way.

Scholars differed over whether this command is obligatory or strongly recommended and whether the gift is made from the estate before division with the consent of adult heirs or from the heirs’ shares afterward. They agreed on the moral direction: those without an assigned portion should not be treated with contempt. If some heirs are minors, their fixed property cannot be donated without lawful authority, but adult heirs can give from their own portions. Where nothing can properly be given, a gracious explanation still fulfils the command to speak well.

This kindness must not be confused with altering Allah’s shares. Compassion for a needy cousin cannot justify depriving a daughter, and a public gift cannot be taken from an absent heir without permission. The Quran combines exact justice with voluntary generosity: first ownership is identified, then owners are invited to care for those outside the formal allocation. The same pattern appeared in 2:177, where righteousness includes both fulfilment of commitments and giving beloved wealth to relatives, orphans, and the needy.

Speech matters because a small gift can be ruined by humiliation. Announcing someone’s poverty, reminding him that he had no legal claim, or turning the occasion into a display of the giver’s superiority injures the very person the verse seeks to comfort. A warm word, prayer for the deceased, clear explanation of the shares, and discreet assistance preserve dignity. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that every good word is charity.

An orphan may not know how to ask, and a poor relative may remain silent from self-respect. Responsible distribution notices them. The next verse deepens this concern through empathy: imagine leaving your own vulnerable children behind, and treat another person’s children as you would want yours treated.

=9=

“Let those who would fear for their own vulnerable children if they left them behind be mindful of Allah and speak words that are right.”

The verse asks adults to imagine their own death. If they left young, disabled, inexperienced, or otherwise vulnerable children, they would worry about dishonest guardians, careless relatives, and decisions made over the children’s heads. That fear should become mercy toward every weak child now in their reach. The Quran turns imagination into accountability: treat another person’s dependants as you would pray that people treat yours.

This addresses guardians and those present around an estate, and it also guides anyone advising a dying person. A person should not be urged to give away so much voluntarily that dependent children are left destitute. When Sa‘d ibn Abi Waqqas wanted to bequeath most of his wealth, the Prophet, peace be upon him, eventually allowed one third and said even one third is much: leaving one’s heirs well provided is better than leaving them poor and asking people. Care for heirs is not selfish when joined to lawful charity and trust in Allah.

At the same time, fear for one’s family cannot justify unlawful hoarding, evading zakah, taking another orphan’s property, or earning through interest and fraud. Provision belongs to Allah. The command is first “be mindful of Allah,” so planning remains inside His limits. A will, documented debts, nominated trustworthy carers, financial education, and fair arrangements for a child with special needs can all express responsible concern without pretending that paperwork controls the future.

“Speak words that are right”—qawlan sadida—means speech that is straight, accurate, and aimed at setting matters right. In an inheritance setting this excludes false testimony, manipulative advice, promises that cannot be kept, and language designed to frighten a vulnerable heir into surrendering a share. Kind words were commanded in verses 5 and 8; here kindness is joined to truth. Soft speech that conceals theft is not righteous, and blunt truth used to crush a child is not the Quranic balance.

The verse reaches far beyond estates. A teacher deciding for a struggling pupil, an official drafting policy for foster children, and a relative speaking to a bereaved family can ask the same question: what would I hope for if this were my child? Taqwa keeps that empathy from becoming mere emotion and makes it a duty before Allah.

Verse 10 now gives the opposite picture. The one who ignores this empathy and consumes orphan property may think he has swallowed money. Revelation shows what he has actually placed inside himself.

=10=

“Indeed, those who consume the property of orphans unjustly are only consuming fire into their bellies, and they will burn in a blazing Fire.”

The warning turns an invisible moral reality into a physical image. The meal, house, investment, or luxury purchased with stolen orphan wealth may look lawful to observers, but in Allah’s account it is fire. “Consume” includes more than eating: it is every form of taking, using, transferring, or destroying the property for oneself without right.

The qualification “unjustly” matters. Earlier verses allowed proper spending on the child and, for a poor guardian, a reasonable amount according to recognised fairness. A sincere trustee should not become paralysed by fear when paying school fees or necessary expenses. The threat is for zulm—for crossing the owner’s right through theft, excess, deception, or self-dealing.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, named devouring orphan wealth among the seven destructive sins. The crime harms a child’s future and can destroy the guardian in the next life. Repentance therefore requires returning the property, repairing losses as far as possible, and seeking forgiveness; if the orphan has died, the right passes to the heirs rather than disappearing.

Modern systems can multiply the distance between thief and victim without changing the ruling. False administrative fees in an orphan charity, misuse of foster payments, an executor transferring a minor’s land to his own company, or relatives concealing a child’s name from probate all fall under the verse’s moral vision. A digital transfer can carry fire just as surely as stolen coins.

After this severe warning, Allah details the inheritance shares from which much orphan property originates. Exact fractions are mercy: they reduce the space in which powerful relatives can claim that custom, age, or usefulness gives them the whole estate.

=11=

“Allah instructs you concerning your children: the share of a male is equal to that of two females. If there are only daughters, two or more, they receive two-thirds of the estate; if there is only one, she receives one-half. Each parent receives one-sixth if the deceased leaves children. But if there are no children and the parents are the only heirs, the mother receives one-third. If the deceased has siblings, the mother receives one-sixth. This is after fulfilling any bequest and paying debts. You do not know whether your parents or your children are closer to you in benefit. This is an obligation from Allah. Surely Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

Allah says “instructs you,” using language of caring counsel even while setting binding law. A son receives the portion of two daughters when sons and daughters inherit together. This rule exists within a financial structure: a man is required to maintain his wife and children and provide mahr, while a woman is not required to spend her inheritance on a husband or household. The fractions are legal allocations, not measurements of intelligence, holiness, or human worth.

If there is one daughter and no son, she receives one-half. Two or more daughters share two-thirds; the Sunnah clarified the share of two when the Prophet awarded two-thirds to the two daughters of Sa‘d ibn al-Rabi‘ after their uncle had taken the estate. Other eligible relatives receive what remains according to the full inheritance rules.

Parents are never casually forgotten. Where the deceased leaves a child, mother and father each begin with one-sixth, though the father may in some cases also take a residue. If there is no child and only the parents inherit, the mother has one-third and the father the remainder. The presence of siblings reduces the mother to one-sixth under the stated conditions, even where those siblings may be blocked from ultimately inheriting by the father. These details show why an estate should not be divided from a simplified chart without identifying every living relative.

Debts and valid bequests are settled before heirs take distributable shares. The verse mentions bequest before debt in expression, though the Sunnah and scholarly consensus give debt priority in payment after funeral costs. A bequest is generally limited to one-third and is not made for an existing fixed heir unless the other adult heirs freely approve after death. The Prophet said, “Allah has given everyone entitled his right, so there is no bequest for an heir.”

People cannot reliably rank which parent or child will bring greater benefit in life, prayer, care, or the Hereafter. Personal guesses therefore do not replace divine allocation. Al-‘Alim knows every relationship; al-Hakim places each boundary wisely. Human responsibility is to disclose the estate, settle claims, calculate carefully, and obey.

=12=

“You receive one-half of what your wives leave if they have no child. If they have a child, you receive one-fourth, after any bequest and debt. Your wives receive one-fourth of what you leave if you have no child. If you have a child, they receive one-eighth, after any bequest and debt. If a man or woman leaves neither parent nor child but has a brother or sister through the mother, each receives one-sixth. If there are two or more, they share one-third, after any bequest and debt, without causing harm. This is a command from Allah. And Allah is All-Knowing, Most Forbearing.”

The verse adds spouses and one category of siblings to the framework. A husband receives half if his deceased wife leaves no descendant, and one-quarter if she leaves a child or qualifying descendant. A wife receives one-quarter if her husband leaves no descendant and one-eighth if he does. If there are several wives, they share that one-quarter or one-eighth; each does not receive the full fraction.

Marriage therefore creates a real inheritance bond. A husband cannot say that his wife owned nothing because he supported the household, and a deceased wife’s salary, investments, mahr, and personal possessions do not automatically become his. Nor can in-laws exclude a widow because she will remarry. The share belongs by Allah’s command once a valid marriage existed at death, subject to the detailed rules concerning divorce and waiting periods.

The kalalah case mentioned here is a person who leaves neither direct descendant nor ascendant. The brother or sister in this verse is understood from prophetic and companion explanation to be a maternal sibling. One receives one-sixth; two or more share one-third, male and female equally. Full and paternal siblings are addressed under different rules, including the final verse of this surah. The variation again proves that “a man always gets twice a woman” is not an accurate summary of the whole system.

“Without causing harm” guards the stage before distribution. A dying person must not invent a debt, make a bequest designed to punish an heir, transfer assets secretly to defeat Allah’s shares, or acknowledge one child’s false claim. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said there should be neither harming nor reciprocating harm. Estate planning that organises lawful ownership is one thing; planning whose purpose is to disinherit a Quranic heir is another.

Allah closes with knowledge and forbearance. He knows the hidden intention behind a last-minute transfer and the genuine debt no family member witnessed. His forbearance gives time to correct wrongs, not permission to treat boundaries lightly. Verses 11 and 12 are not detached arithmetic: they protect widows, parents, daughters, sons, and siblings from the moods of whoever controls the documents. The next two verses name these fractions “the limits of Allah” and attach eternal consequences to how they are received.

=13=

“These are the limits set by Allah. Whoever obeys Allah and His Messenger, He will admit them into Gardens beneath which rivers flow, remaining there forever. That is the great triumph.”

Tilka hudud Allah—these are Allah’s boundaries. The immediate reference is the family and inheritance law just given, but the phrase teaches a wider attitude to revelation. A boundary marks where lawful choice ends and transgression begins. It is not moved because an heir is unpopular, a custom is old, or compliance is financially inconvenient.

The promise of Gardens after detailed fractions gives inheritance unusual spiritual weight. Dividing an estate can look like clerical work, yet obedience there may be worship leading to Paradise. The executor who discloses an overlooked account, the brother who ensures his sister receives her full portion, the widow who does not take what belongs to the children, and the heirs who honour a real debt are obeying Allah and His Messenger in an area where greed often speaks loudly.

Rivers flowing beneath Gardens present the opposite of worldly hoarding. Property here changes hands and eventually leaves every owner; the reward there is secure and lasting. “Remaining forever” removes the fear of another death or another division. That is al-fawz al-‘azim, the great success—not merely obtaining the largest share now, but reaching what never runs out.

The Sunnah explains these boundaries: the Prophet clarified daughters’ shares, priority of fixed portions, residues, and bequests. A lawful share should not excuse cruelty or severing kinship, while pressure for peace must not erase that share. Qualified mediation can preserve both justice and relationship. Deliberately crossing the limits, however, turns a private dispute into disobedience toward Allah and His Messenger.

=14=

“But whoever disobeys Allah and His Messenger and transgresses His limits, He will admit them into a Fire, remaining there, and they will suffer a humiliating punishment.”

The verse mirrors the one before it: obedience and Gardens; disobedience, crossed boundaries, and Fire. Family law is not beneath spirituality. A person may appear devout in public while secretly depriving sisters, hiding assets from a widow, or consuming a minor’s share. The Quran places those acts beside direct disobedience to Allah and His Messenger.

“To transgress” means to pass beyond a known limit. Mistakes in complicated calculation are not the same as deliberate defiance; errors should be corrected and rights restored when discovered. The threat focuses on the person who knows the boundary and steps over it through greed, pride, or rejection. Consulting someone qualified, preserving records, and pausing distribution when facts are unclear are practical forms of fearing such a mistake.

The humiliating punishment fits the sin. Much inheritance oppression is driven by social pride: one relative believes a woman should have to ask him, that a younger heir has no voice, or that family status allows him to rewrite Allah’s allocation. What was done to humiliate the weak returns as humiliation before the One whose boundary was mocked.

The wording about remaining in the Fire is a severe warning. Sunni scholars read such threats together with the Quran’s full teaching: one who rejects Allah’s law as false or treats it with contempt may fall into disbelief, while a Muslim who accepts the ruling but violates it through sin remains under Allah’s will, as stated in 4:48 and 4:116 regarding sins below shirk. The threat must neither be emptied of force nor used recklessly to declare particular sinners unbelievers.

Repentance remains open before death. It requires ending the transgression, regretting it, resolving not to return, and restoring the financial right. Saying “Allah forgives” while keeping someone else’s land is not complete tawbah. The Prophet, peace be upon him, urged anyone who had wronged another in honour or anything else to seek settlement before a Day when there will be no dinar or dirham, only transfer of good and bad deeds.

Having protected the family’s property, the surah turns to sexual wrongdoing and the evidence required before punishment. The transition continues the protection of households, but it also prevents accusation from becoming another weapon of oppression.

=15=

“Those of your women who commit sexual immorality—call four witnesses from among you against them. If they testify, confine the women to their homes until death takes them or Allah appoints another way for them.”

This was an early Madinan ruling for proven zina, revealed while the Muslim community’s legal order was developing. It required four witnesses and prescribed confinement, explicitly leaving room for a later ruling: “or Allah appoints another way.” That way came through subsequent revelation and the Sunnah. Surah al-Nur 24:2 prescribed one hundred lashes for unmarried offenders, while authentic hadith established the ruling for a previously married adulterer under a lawful judiciary and an exceptionally demanding standard of proof.

The historical sequence matters. The verse is not a licence for families to imprison women indefinitely today, nor for private individuals to administer criminal penalties. Fixed punishments belong to legitimate judicial authority, with rules of evidence, competence, absence of coercion, and the possibility of doubt carefully examined. Vigilantism, so-called honour violence, forced confession, and domestic detention are themselves wrongs.

Four witnesses are an immense safeguard. They must testify to the act itself, not to rumours, suspicious messages, pregnancy alone in disputed circumstances, or a person entering a house. Surah al-Nur later punishes those who accuse chaste people without four witnesses (24:4). The law protects chastity in two directions: it condemns zina and also protects people—especially women—from reputations destroyed by gossip.

The verse’s wording addresses women because this first provision specifies their confinement. The next verse addresses the two who commit the act and commands a response followed by release upon repentance and reform. Classical commentators discussed the exact referents and stages, but the passage as a whole shows gradual legislation rather than unequal moral responsibility. Elsewhere the Quran plainly addresses male and female offenders together.

A believer should therefore resist both extremes: normalising sexual betrayal as harmless private freedom, and hunting for scandal in order to punish or shame. Allah says in 24:19 that those who love to see immorality spread among believers face painful punishment. Privacy, lowering the gaze, lawful marriage, and sincere repentance close the doors to sin; strict evidence closes the door to public slander.

The phrase “Allah appoints another way” also demonstrates that the Prophet’s community expected revelation to clarify developing law. The next verse keeps repentance visible even in this grave subject: where offenders truly turn back and reform, people are not to continue persecuting them.

=16=

“And the two among you who commit it, discipline them both. But if they repent and mend their ways, leave them alone. Surely Allah is Ever-Accepting of repentance, Most Merciful.”

The dual form holds both participants accountable. Early commentators differed over whether it refers to the man and woman guilty of zina, to two men committing a sexual act, or to another early stage of the zina ruling. On the common reading, this verse prescribed a form of censure or disciplinary hurt before the later fixed penalties clarified the “way” promised in verse 15. The exact early measure is no basis for individuals to invent punishments now.

What remains unmistakable is the moral pair: repentance and reform. Tawbah is not merely saying sorry after exposure. It means turning away from the act, regretting it, seeking Allah’s forgiveness, and repairing conduct. Islah—making things right—shows itself through changed boundaries, ending an unlawful relationship, protecting a spouse from further betrayal, and avoiding the paths that led back to the sin.

When those signs appear, “leave them alone.” People may not make a repentant person’s past into permanent entertainment, repeatedly shame them, circulate old evidence, or block every route to a decent future. Allah conceals faults, accepts return, and loves purification. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that all children of Adam sin and the best sinners are those who repent. He also discouraged searching for private faults and taught that whoever conceals a Muslim’s fault, Allah will conceal him in this world and the Hereafter.

This does not erase another person’s rights. If betrayal caused a spouse harm, if coercion or assault occurred, or if parentage and financial duties are involved, repentance includes justice and lawful resolution. Sexual assault is not treated as consensual zina by the victim; responsibility belongs to the attacker, and the harmed person must not be blamed. “Leave them alone” follows genuine repentance from their own wrongdoing, not suppression of a victim’s claim.

The divine names carry hope. Al-Tawwab does more than accept one return; He repeatedly turns toward servants who repeatedly return sincerely. Al-Rahim meets moral ruin with a path back to dignity. Yet the next two verses distinguish timely repentance from a last-second statement made only when death makes further sin impossible. Mercy is wide, but postponing return is dangerous.

=17=

“Allah only accepts the repentance of those who commit evil in ignorance and then repent soon afterward. It is they to whom Allah turns in mercy. And Allah is All-Knowing, All-Wise.”

“Ignorance” here does not mean that only a person unaware of a rule can repent. The early Muslims explained that every act of disobedience is an act of jahalah while it is being committed, because the sinner behaves foolishly toward Allah even if he knows the legal ruling. Desire, anger, pride, or heedlessness pushes knowledge out of command. The door of tawbah therefore remains open for deliberate sins when the servant truly returns.

“To repent soon” urges a person not to settle comfortably into wrongdoing. Some early commentators understood “soon” as any time before the death struggle, because all worldly life is short beside the Hereafter. That does not make delay safe. Each repetition hardens habit, injuries may multiply, and no one knows when the final illness or sudden accident will arrive. The wise response to a fall is immediate: stop, ask forgiveness, restore rights, and rebuild the practice that sin damaged.

The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that Allah accepts a servant’s repentance so long as the soul has not reached the throat. He also reported Allah’s joy at a servant’s return as greater than the joy of a traveller who unexpectedly recovers his lost mount and supplies in a deadly desert. That image replaces the idea of a reluctant Lord with the Quranic name al-Tawwab, while still warning against waiting for the final breath.

Repentance has recognised elements: sincerity for Allah, regret, immediate abandonment, and firm resolve not to return. If the sin concerns another person, their property or right must be restored or forgiveness sought. A later relapse does not necessarily make an earlier, sincere repentance false; the servant must return again rather than use shame as an excuse. Allah knows regret from performance, and no hidden tear escapes Him. The flow is compassionate: even serious wrongdoing does not make a person irredeemable.

But mercy should move the sinner now. The next verse describes those who keep choosing evil until death is visibly present and only then announce repentance, when their words no longer represent a free turn away from sin.

=18=

“Repentance is not for those who persist in evil deeds until death approaches one of them and then say, ‘Now I repent,’ nor for those who die while they are disbelievers. For them We have prepared a painful punishment.”

The contrast is not between a person with many sins and one with few. It is between returning while choice remains and speaking only when the unseen has begun to become visible. A person may carry a long, dark history and still be forgiven through sincere repentance before the final threshold. Another may delay until death removes every opportunity to continue and discover that “now” is too late.

Pharaoh is the Quran’s clearest example. While drowning, after a lifetime of tyranny and repeated signs, he declared belief, but was answered, “Now? You had disobeyed before” (10:90-91). His statement was recognition under unavoidable sight, not faith in the unseen or a willing surrender. The warning makes procrastination irrational. Youth, health, and an ordinary morning are not promises of another evening.

Those who die upon disbelief have allowed the time for accepting faith to close, though Allah alone knows each person’s end. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught frequent remembrance of death, the destroyer of pleasures, so decisions return to their true scale. An apology should be sent, a debt disclosed, stolen property returned, and prayer restored while action remains possible. Good intentions stored for old age may never reach it.

A painful punishment is “prepared,” while the previous verse described Allah actively turning toward those who return. Fear and hope remain together. Despair is not the lesson: as long as life has not reached its final limit, the command is to turn now. Presumption is equally dangerous: one must not plan decades of disobedience around a last-minute declaration.

The surah then returns to women’s rights. Some pre-Islamic heirs treated a widow as part of the deceased man’s estate. After explaining that women inherit property, the Quran now declares that women themselves cannot be inherited, confined, or pressured for financial gain.

=19=

“O believers, it is not lawful for you to inherit women against their will, nor should you mistreat them in order to take back part of what you have given them, unless they commit clear sexual immorality. Live with them honourably. If you dislike them, perhaps you dislike something in which Allah has placed much good.”

In a pre-Islamic custom, a deceased man’s male heir could claim the widow, marry her without a new mahr, marry her to someone else for payment, or prevent her remarriage until she surrendered property. The Quran addresses believers and abolishes the idea beneath the practice: a woman is not inherited. She is a moral and legal person whose consent, property, and future are protected.

The prohibition also covers ‘adl—constricting a wife’s life to recover what was given to her. A husband may not make the home unbearable, deny maintenance, threaten, or suspend a marriage so that she pays for release. The exception concerning manifest sexual immorality has been interpreted in several ways by jurists, but it never licenses unsupported accusation or private abuse. Other Quranic passages regulate divorce and khul‘, and all action remains subject to proof, proportion, and lawful process.

The positive command is broader than avoiding violence: “live with them bil-ma‘ruf,” according to recognised goodness. It includes fair maintenance, safe housing, respectful speech, intimacy with consideration, consultation, privacy, and patience with ordinary human faults. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said the best believers in faith are those best in character, and the best of you are those best to their wives. A person’s manners at home, where public praise is absent, reveal much of his character.

Dislike can occur in marriage; the Quran does not pretend every feeling is constant. It tells the believer not to turn one disliked quality or difficult season into blindness toward all good. The Prophet taught that a believing man should not hate a believing woman: if he dislikes one characteristic, he should be pleased with another. Patience may preserve children, uncover loyalty, allow growth, or teach virtues that immediate reaction would destroy.

This is not a command to remain in danger. Abuse should be stopped, help sought, and separation can be lawful when a marriage cannot fulfil its purposes. The verse restrains impulsive cruelty and financial coercion; it does not make suffering harm a measure of piety.

“Much good” is known fully by Allah, while human judgment often freezes around a present irritation. If separation is ultimately chosen, it must still be just. The next verse forbids manufacturing charges or reclaiming even a very large dowry simply because a husband wants another wife.

=20=

“If you desire to replace one wife with another and you have given the former even a great amount of gold, do not take any of it back. Would you take it through slander and blatant sin?”

The verse deals with a marriage ending at the husband’s initiative. He may wish to marry someone else, but that desire gives him no claim over the first wife’s mahr. Qintar indicates a very large amount—a heap of wealth. If even that cannot be seized back, a smaller dowry is certainly protected.

“Slander” points to a common route of financial abuse: accuse a wife of misconduct, exaggerate private faults, or create a hostile situation so that she will surrender property to escape. The Quran names such a strategy buhtan, a shocking falsehood that leaves the accused bewildered, and ithman mubina, manifest sin. A legal document signed under intimidation does not turn oppression into honesty before Allah.

This ruling fits the passage from its opening. Orphan wealth may not be exchanged or consumed; a woman’s dowry must be given to her willingly; inheritance shares may not be manipulated; widows may not be held for gain; and now divorce may not become a device for recovering gifts. Across every stage of family life, property rights survive changes in emotion and power.

Other verses complete the picture. In 2:229, husbands are generally forbidden to take back what they gave, except in a khul‘ arrangement where spouses fear they cannot maintain Allah’s limits and the wife freely offers compensation for release. That distinct process cannot be manufactured by deliberate mistreatment. Where a husband pronounces divorce, the Quran repeatedly commands release with kindness rather than harm (2:231).

The Sunnah encourages ease in dowry, but an amount freely agreed and validly owed remains a debt; advice to keep mahr moderate cannot become permission to confiscate it. The closing question awakens conscience before verse 21 gives a deeper reason: the spouses shared the closest intimacy, and the wife took a solemn covenant. Financial betrayal after such trust dishonours both their human closeness and that sacred promise, carrying the commentary naturally into the next verse.
