=1=

This surah opens with an address that is deeply intertwined with the most momentous event in human history—the beginning of the final revelation. “O you covered up in your clothes” refers to the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, in a state of being wrapped or enveloped in his cloak.

The Arabic word “muddaththir” comes from “daththara,” meaning to cover oneself up heavily, to bury oneself in garments. This is the twin surah of Al-Muzzammil, which began with “O you wrapped in your clothes. ” The authentic reports from Bukhari and Muslim narrate the context. After the first terrifying encounter with the angel Jibril in the cave of Hira, the Prophet rushed home to his wife Khadijah, trembling and profoundly shaken. He cried out, “Cover me, cover me!” They wrapped him in a cloak until his fear subsided. Later, after a period of pause in revelation, he saw the angel again, this time sitting on a chair between the heavens and the earth, and he was struck with the same overwhelming awe and ran home saying, “Cover me, cover me!” It was then that Allah revealed this surah. The address “O you covered up” is thus filled with tender understanding of the Prophet’s human vulnerability. The Divine voice does not rebuke him for his fear but meets him in his state of needing comfort.

However, the comfort of the covering is about to be transformed into a launching pad for action. The covering was a temporary shelter, but the mission cannot wait. The same Lord who allowed him to rest and recover is now calling him to arise. This balance between acknowledging human weakness and demanding prophetic action is a hallmark of the Quran’s approach.

The verse teaches that moments of fear, withdrawal, or exhaustion are not signs of failure; they are part of the human condition. But the call of duty eventually comes to pull the servant out of his cloak and into the arena. It is also a reminder that the weight of prophethood was not carried by a superhuman; it was carried by a man who felt fear, who needed a covering, and who then, by Allah’s strength, stood up and changed the world.

=2=

From the covered state comes the explosive command: “Arise and warn.” The Arabic “qum fa-andhir” is a powerful, terse imperative. “Arise” is not merely a physical standing up; it is a call to action, to shake off the comfort of rest and to step into the role of a warner.

The word “fa” indicates immediate sequence: as soon as you arise, warn. There is no delay, no period of further hiding. The covering phase is over. The command “warn” (andhir) defines the core of the prophetic mission at this stage. The Prophet is to warn humanity about the consequences of their disbelief and sins. The warning encompasses the punishment in this world and the far greater punishment in the Hereafter.

The Arabic word carries the sense of cautioning against an imminent danger. The Prophet is like a scout who sees an approaching enemy army and runs to his people to alert them.

This is the meaning of his title “Nadhir,” the warner. The command to “warn all” emphasizes the universality of his mission, even in this early Meccan phase. While the immediate audience was the Quraysh, the message is ultimately for all humanity and jinn.

This verse establishes that the comfort of faith cannot remain a private, hidden affair. Once the heart has been wrapped in the mercy of Allah, the believer must arise and share the warning with others. The Prophet could have chosen to remain in his home, wrapped and safe, but Allah commanded him to step into the public square.

This is a lesson for every Muslim: iman is not just personal solace; it carries a responsibility to convey the message. The command also implicitly addresses the psychological state of the Prophet. The natural response to trauma is to withdraw. The divine prescription for that trauma is to engage, to channel the overwhelming experience into purposeful action. Arising and warning became the therapy that transformed his fear into the driving force of his life.

=3=

After the command to warn, the next command establishes the content and the source of the warning’s authority: “Revere your Lord alone.” The Arabic “rabbaka fa-kabbir” literally means “your Lord, so magnify.

” The word “kabbir” comes from takbeer, declaring Allahu Akbar—Allah is the Greatest. This is the first and fundamental message of the warner: that Allah alone is great, and He must be declared and recognized as such.

The phrase “your Lord” is intensely personal. The command is not to magnify an abstract deity but the Lord who has a direct relationship with the Prophet, who sheltered him, who guided him. The “fa” again connects closely: because He is your Lord, magnify Him. This magnification is a comprehensive act. It includes declaring His greatness with the tongue, but it also means living a life that reflects His supreme importance over all else. The idols that the Quraysh worshipped are to be utterly dismissed, and Allah’s oneness and supreme majesty are to be proclaimed. This verse is also a direct answer to the arrogance of the disbelievers who magnified their idols, their wealth, and their ancestors. The warner is to counter their false takbeer with the true takbeer. The command to magnify the Lord also shapes the psychology of the warner. When one truly internalizes that Allah is the Greatest, all other fears—the fear of people, the fear of poverty, the fear of ridicule—shrink into insignificance. The Prophet, emerging from his cloak, needed this foundation of takbeer to face a society that would call him a madman and a liar.

This is why the call to prayer begins with “Allahu Akbar,” and why the Muslim repeats it throughout the day. It is the spiritual engine that fuels courage and conviction. The verse is a timeless instruction: the da‘wah must always start with and center on the magnification of the one true God.

=4=

The sequence of commands continues with a directive that operates on multiple levels: “Purify your garments.” On the literal level, this means to keep one’s clothes physically clean and free from impurities. Physical cleanliness is a fundamental aspect of Islamic practice, especially in prayer. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was known for his meticulous cleanliness and pleasant appearance. The pagans of Mecca might have worn unclean clothes, especially during their rituals, but the Muslim is commanded to be pure outwardly as a reflection of inward purity. On a deeper level, the “garments” are a metaphor for the soul, character, and reputation.

The Arabic idiom “thiyab” can refer to one’s conduct and moral state. When a person is praised for purity of garments, it means his character is spotless. The command is therefore to purify one’s deeds, intentions, and ethics from every stain of sin, hypocrisy, and falsehood. A third interpretation, mentioned by some commentators, is that the command refers specifically to the garment of prophethood; the Prophet is to keep his mission pure from any blemish. Another view is that it means to shorten the garments, avoiding the arrogance of trailing clothes, which was a sign of pride among the Arabs. The genius of the Quranic style is that all these meanings can coexist without contradiction. The warner must be pure in body, pure in character, and pure in mission. He cannot call others to purity while he himself is soiled. This verse pairs beautifully with the previous one: after magnifying the Lord in the heart, the believer purifies his external and internal self. The combination of takbeer and tatheer (purification) creates the balanced personality of the da‘i. For the early Muslims, this was a radical departure from the moral laxity of pre-Islamic society. Today, it remains a vital reminder that the message of Islam is carried not just by words but by the visible purity of the messenger’s life.

=5=

The surah now addresses the gravest spiritual impurity: “Continue to shun idols.” The Arabic word “rujz” here means idols, or more broadly, filth, abomination, and the punishment associated with idolatry. The command “fahjur” means to abandon completely, to emigrate from, to cut off all ties. The Prophet is instructed to stay away from the idols that the Quraysh worshipped—both the physical statues and the entire system of shirk that they represented.

This is not a passive avoidance but an active, declared separation. The command “continue to” is implied in the Arabic form, indicating that this is an ongoing, permanent state of boycott. The Prophet had always instinctively rejected idol worship even before his prophethood, as he was a follower of the pure monotheism of Abraham. Now he receives a formal divine command to maintain and intensify that rejection.

The placement of this command after the purification of garments is significant: the greatest impurity is shirk. You can clean your clothes a thousand times, but if your heart is attached to idols, you remain impure. The idols are described as “rujz” because they are a source of spiritual filth and a cause of divine punishment. In the earlier surahs like Al-Ma‘arij and Nuh, the destruction of idolatrous nations was recounted. The Prophet is to learn from those lessons and shun what brought destruction upon them.

This command also carries a lesson for all believers: it is not enough to worship Allah; one must also actively reject and distance oneself from all forms of shirk, whether it is the worship of statues, money, power, or ego. The early Muslims lived this by refusing to participate in the pagan rituals around the Kaaba. The verse is a call to a clear, uncompromising identity.

=6=

This verse touches on the inner purity of intention: “Do not do a favour expecting more in return.” The Arabic “la tamnun tastakthir” is a beautifully compact phrase with layers of meaning.

The word “tamnun” comes from “mann,” which can mean to bestow a favour or to remind someone of a favour. The phrase “tastakthir” means to consider it much or to seek increase. One interpretation is that the Prophet should not give gifts or perform acts of kindness while expecting a greater return from the recipients. His da‘wah and his charity must be solely for Allah, not for worldly reciprocation. The pagans often engaged in such calculated generosity. Another interpretation, deeply relevant to the Prophet’s psychology, is that he should not consider his immense efforts in conveying the message—his years of struggle, his night prayers, his patience—as a huge favour to his Lord that entitles him to feel burdened or to demand immediate results. His service is for Allah, and he should not “count” it as a big thing for which he expects a reward; rather, he serves out of pure devotion, leaving the reward entirely to Allah. A third interpretation is that he should not give up conveying the message thinking he has already done a great deal; instead, he should continue without tallying his efforts. The overarching lesson is the purification of motives. Every believer must guard against the subtle disease of performing good deeds while expecting praise, gratitude, or a quid pro quo. The da‘i works for Allah’s pleasure alone. This verse connects to the earlier command to purify garments; purity of the soul requires cleansing the intention of any expectation from creation. It is a profound spiritual training for the Prophet and for all who follow in his footsteps: give, but do not count; serve, but do not invoice. The reward is with Allah, and it will be far greater than any worldly return.

=7=

The sequence of commands culminates in this powerful directive: “And persevere for the sake of your Lord.” The Arabic “wa li-rabbika fasbir” places “for your Lord” first for emphasis. This perseverance (sabr) is not a general endurance; it is specifically dedicated to Allah, done for His sake, and sustained by His support. The command to persevere is the capstone of all the previous instructions. Arising to warn will bring mockery, rejection, and hostility. Magnifying the Lord will provoke the anger of the idolaters. Purifying the garments and shunning idols will isolate the believer from his society. Giving without expecting return will be met with ingratitude. All of this requires an immense reservoir of patience. The Prophet is told to dig deep and endure, not for personal pride, but “for your Lord.” The preposition “li” (for) indicates that the patience is a dedicated offering to Allah, an act of worship in itself. The early Meccan period was a crucible of persecution. The companions were tortured, the Prophet was ridiculed, and the message was rejected by the majority. This verse was a lifeline. It reminded them that their suffering was not meaningless; it was for the Lord of the worlds. This transforms the psychology of endurance. When patience is for Allah, it becomes sweet, because the lover gladly bears hardship for the sake of the Beloved.

The verse also implies that the source of patience is the Lord Himself; one perseveres by relying on His help. This echoes the command of beautiful patience in Al-Ma‘arij and the patience enjoined upon Noah. The Prophet’s entire life would be a testament to this verse. He persevered in the face of personal loss, the death of his wife and uncle, the boycott, the expulsion, and the battles—all for his Lord. For the believers, this verse is a constant reminder that the struggles of faith are not endured in vain; they are an offering to the One who sees and will reward without measure.

=8=

The surah now pivots from commands to the Prophet to a powerful cosmic scene that underpins the warning he is to deliver. “For when the Trumpet will be sounded.

” The Arabic “naqara fee an-naqoor” literally means when the horn is blown. The “naqoor” is the Trumpet, the horn into which the angel Israfil will blow.

This is the second blast, the blast of resurrection, when all the dead will rise from their graves to face judgment. The word “naqara” suggests a piercing, penetrating sound that cannot be ignored. The “fa” particle connects this verse as the reason or the context for the perseverance commanded previously. Endure now, for the Day is coming when the Trumpet will sound and everything will change.

This is the ultimate motivation for the warner. The sound of the Trumpet is the dividing line between the temporary world of injustice and the eternal world of justice. The pagans who mock the warning will hear this Trumpet, and their mockery will turn to terror. The Prophet, who suffers their abuse, will hear it and be vindicated. The verse is brief but its impact is thunderous. It suddenly expands the listener’s perspective from the narrow streets of Mecca to the vast arena of the Hereafter. The Trumpet is a reality that every prophet, including Noah and the messengers mentioned in the previous surahs, has warned about. It is the Day that the jinn in Al-Jinn admitted they had no knowledge of its timing. The believer is meant to live with the sound of the Trumpet echoing in the soul, keeping priorities straight and making the hardships of the world seem light and temporary. The verse serves as a hinge, swinging the surah from the preparation of the warner to the description of the Day that is the core of the warning.

=9=

The surah now characterizes the Day of the Trumpet: “that will truly be a difficult Day.” The Arabic “‘asir” means hard, severe, grim, and full of distress. The Day will be difficult not because Allah is unjust but because the weight of judgment, the uncovering of deeds, the terror of the scene, and the strict accounting will press down on all souls.

The word “truly” (dhalika yawma’idhin) emphasizes that this is a definite, certain reality, not a myth or a distant possibility. The difficulty of the Day is described across the Quran: the pregnant mother will drop her load, the children will turn grey-haired, the sky will split, the mountains will crumble. It is a Day when every relationship will be severed except those built on faith. The verse prepares the listener for the specific difficulty mentioned next for the disbelievers. But even for the believers, the Day will be a moment of immense awe. The Prophet, peace be upon him, will be on his knees, and every prophet will be concerned with his own salvation until Allah grants them permission to intercede. The “difficult Day” is the great filter that separates the eras of worldly test from the eternal consequences. No one will pass through it casually. The reminder of this difficulty is a mercy, because it prompts the living to prepare. The warner’s task is precisely to alert people to this difficulty so that they can make it easy for themselves through faith and good deeds. The verse makes the heart tremble and asks: what am I doing today to face that difficult Day?

=10=

The description narrows its focus to the specific plight of those who rejected the warning: “far from easy for the disbelievers.” The Arabic “‘ala al-kafireena ghayru yaseer” means it will not be easy upon them at all; it will be a harsh, crushing difficulty. For the believers, the difficulty will be alleviated. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that the Day will be made light for the believer, like the time between the afternoon and sunset prayers. But for the kafireen, the difficulty will be unrelieved and eternal in its consequence.

The word “kafireen” refers to those who actively covered up the truth after it was made clear. They disbelieved in the message, mocked the warner, and persisted in their shirk. The verse is a direct threat to the Quraysh who heard the Prophet’s warning and turned away. It answers the very question they might have had: if that Day is so difficult, who will bear the brunt?

The answer is them. This is the core of the warning that the Prophet was commanded to deliver. The difficulty is not a random cosmic event; it is a specifically targeted consequence of disbelief.

The verse also serves as a crucial clarification: the existence of a difficult Day is not a problem of divine mercy; it is a manifestation of divine justice. Those who rejected the clear signs and persecuted the believers will find the Day far from easy.

The phrase “far from easy” uses a litotes to emphasize the intensity; it is not just difficult, it is the opposite of easy, an unassailable mountain of hardship. This verse concludes the introductory section of the surah, having moved from the personal command to the Prophet to the universal reality of the Hereafter, setting the stage for the detailed example of a specific denier who will face that difficult Day.

=11=

The surah now transitions from the general warning to a terrifying case study. “And leave to Me O Prophet the one I created all by Myself.

” The Arabic “dharnee wa man khalaqtu waheedan” is an ominous command. Allah is telling the Prophet to step aside and hand this particular individual over to Him alone. The Prophet is not to worry about this man, not to seek revenge, not to be consumed by his insults. Allah will deal with him directly.

The phrase “the one I created all by Myself” emphasizes that this human being, despite his arrogance and power, is a solitary creation of Allah. He came into this world alone, without any helpers or partners assisting Allah in his creation. He was a weak, dependent embryo, and Allah alone formed him. His current status and wealth are all gifts from that same Creator. The description “waheedan” (alone) strips away all the pretensions of the man. He may have a huge tribe and many children, but in the divine act of his creation, he was utterly alone and helpless. Most scholars identify this person as al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah, a powerful chief of the Quraysh, a man of immense wealth and influence, known for his eloquence and intelligence. He was one of the most stubborn opponents of the Prophet. The command to “leave him to Me” is a profound comfort. It tells the Prophet that the battle is not his personal fight; the Lord will champion him. This echoes the similar command in Al-Muzzammil, “Leave to Me the deniers, the people of luxury. ” The verse teaches the believers to entrust their oppressors to Allah’s justice and not to be consumed by hatred or the desire for personal vengeance. The divine “I” will take over the case.

=12=

The surah now lists the worldly blessings that this denier was given, blessings that he misused and that became a cause of his arrogance. “And granted him abundant wealth.

” The Arabic “ja‘altu lahu maalan mamdoodan” literally means “I made for him extended, stretched-out wealth.” This wealth was vast, continuous, and seemingly endless. Al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah was one of the richest men in Mecca. His wealth included huge herds of camels, flocks of sheep, orchards, and trade goods that stretched as far as the eye could see. The description “mamdood” (extended) suggests that his riches were not just a lump sum but a flowing river of income. This was all a grant from Allah, a test and a trust. But he saw it as a proof of his own superiority and a sign that he had no need for the Prophet’s God. The verse highlights the severe trial of wealth. When Allah grants money to a person who then becomes arrogant, that wealth is not a blessing but a delayed punishment. The owner will be held accountable for every coin, and his wealth will witness against him on the Day of Judgment. The specific mention of this man’s wealth serves as a warning to all: do not be deceived by the prosperity of the disbelievers. The Quraysh had many wealthy chiefs who mocked the poor believers. But their wealth was actually a link in the chain of their destruction. It made them feel secure and independent, so they rejected the truth. The verse uses the first-person “I granted” to emphasize that the same God who gave the wealth can take it away and can punish its possessor. Every rich person should read this with fear: is my wealth leading me to gratitude or to arrogance?

=13=

The next blessing is “and children always by his side.” The Arabic “baneena shuhoodan” means sons who are present with him, always in his company. Unlike the poor who had to send their children away for work, al-Walid’s sons were constantly around him, attending to his needs, adding to his prestige, and serving as his entourage. He had several sons, including Khalid ibn al-Walid, who would later become the great Muslim general, though at this time he was a staunch polytheist.

The phrase “always by his side” paints a picture of a man surrounded by a loyal, visible entourage of male offspring. In Arab society, having many sons was a sign of immense honor and power. They were his manpower, his legacy, his defense against enemies. Al-Walid would walk through Mecca with his sons flanking him, a display of his might. But the verse subtly implies that these children are a part of the test. They are “shuhood,” witnesses, present with him in his worldly life but utterly unable to help him in the Hereafter.

In fact, they will be witnesses against him on the Day of Judgment if he led them astray. The Quran elsewhere warns that neither wealth nor children will avail anyone except the one who comes to Allah with a sound heart. The blessing of children, like wealth, can become a curse if it leads to arrogance and neglect of the Creator.

The verse reminds us that the presence of family should remind a person of his responsibility, not inflate his ego. Al-Walid used his sons as proof that he was favored by the gods, but the true God was listing them as evidence of how thoroughly He had tested him, a test he failed.

=14=

The list of favors culminates in a comprehensive statement: “and made life very easy for him.” The Arabic “mahhadtu lahu tamheedan” means I smoothed, leveled, and paved his path. Everything in his life was made comfortable, accessible, and obstacle-free. His business thrived, his health was good, his voice was respected in the tribal councils. He did not face poverty, illness, or social rejection. Allah had removed the hardships that many others struggled with daily. This “smoothing” was a divine respite (istidraj), a gradual leading toward destruction through uninterrupted blessings. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that when you see Allah giving a person the things he wants while he persists in disobedience, that is a form of divine plan to seize him unawares. Al-Walid’s easy life was not a sign of Allah’s pleasure; it was a preparation for a greater fall. The more ease he enjoyed, the more hardened his heart became, and the less he felt any need for the Hereafter. This verse is a powerful corrective to the prosperity gospel. It teaches that comfort is not always a reward, and hardship is not always a punishment. A person surrounded by ease may be in spiritual freefall. The believer should examine his own life: if things are easy, is it drawing him closer to Allah or making him negligent?

The word “tamheed” also implies that the path was made level so that he would have no excuse. He could not say, “I was too burdened by life to contemplate the truth. ” His life was uncluttered; he had the time, the resources, and the mental space to reflect, yet he chose disbelief. This makes his sin greater and his punishment more severe. The ease was a test, and he failed spectacularly.

=15=

Despite all these abundant blessings, the surah exposes the man’s true nature: “Yet he is hungry for more.” The Arabic “thumma yatma‘u an azeed” shows a greedy, insatiable desire. After the extended wealth, the sons by his side, and the perfectly smoothed life, instead of gratitude and humility, his response is to demand more. He wants an increase in riches, in status, in influence.

This is the psychology of the materialist: the more he gets, the more he wants. The appetite grows with feeding. Al-Walid was not content with what would have satisfied any reasonable person for multiple lifetimes. The verse diagnoses a profound spiritual disease: the feeling of self-sufficiency and the love of more. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that if the son of Adam had a valley of gold, he would desire a second one, and nothing fills the mouth of the son of Adam except the dust of the grave. Al-Walid was a living example of this. His hunger for more also extended to his attitude toward the Prophet’s message. He was not satisfied with his own disbelief; he wanted the Prophet to compromise, to give him a special place, or to see the message fail entirely.

The word “thumma” (yet) indicates a surprising, disappointing contrast. One would expect blessings to produce gratitude; instead, they produced more greed. This verse holds up a mirror to all who measure life by accumulation. The constant craving for more is a form of slavery that blinds a person to the blessings already in hand. It is the opposite of the contentment that faith brings. The denier, for all his wealth, was a poor man in his soul, always hungry, never at peace. His hunger would never be satisfied, and it would ultimately lead him to the Fire, where hunger is eternal.

=16=

The surah now reveals the root cause of his spiritual ruin: “But no! For he has been truly stubborn with Our revelations.

” The opening “Kalla” is a strong negation, repelling his greedy ambition. It means: absolutely not, he will not get what he wants. His demand for more will not be met with increased blessings; it will be met with punishment. The reason is then given: “he has been truly stubborn with Our revelations.

” The Arabic “kaana li-ayatina ‘aneedan” means he was persistently, deliberately obstinate against the signs of Allah. The word “‘aneed” describes someone who knows the truth but actively opposes it, who digs in his heels and refuses to yield out of pride and stubbornness. Al-Walid heard the Quran, recognized its unmatched eloquence and its profound wisdom, but his arrogance would not allow him to submit. He was not an ignorant denier; he was a knowing rejector. The “revelations” (ayat) refer to the verses of the Quran as well as the natural signs within creation and within himself. He had all the evidence, but his will was bent against it.

This verse teaches that rejection of the truth is often not an intellectual problem but a moral one. The stubbornness is a choice of the heart. Allah calls the revelations “Our revelations,” emphasizing their divine source. The man’s stubbornness is therefore a direct affront to Allah, not just a disagreement with the Prophet. The strong negation “Kalla” coupled with this diagnosis strips away all excuses. The denier is not a victim of insufficient proof; he is a perpetrator of willful rebellion. For the believers, this is a warning to check their own hearts for any trace of such stubbornness against any command of Allah.

The verse also prepares for the detailed account of al-Walid’s mental machinations against the Quran in the following verses.

=17=

The divine response to his stubbornness is swift and severe: “I will make his fate unbearable.” The Arabic “sa-urhiquhu sa‘ooda” literally means “I will drive him up an arduous, steep ascent.

” The word “sa‘ood” refers to a mountain pass that is extremely difficult to climb, a punishing upward path that exhausts the traveler and offers no relief. In the context of Hell, it is interpreted as a mountain of fire that the denier will be forced to climb, only to fall and climb again, in endless torment. Some commentators say it refers to a specific punishment where he will be made to ascend a scalding, slippery slope in Hell for seventy years, and when he reaches the top, he will be pushed down, and the cycle will repeat forever. The punishment perfectly fits the crime. He wanted more wealth and an easier life; Allah will give him a fate that is pure hardship, a relentless upward climb with no summit. The “sa‘ood” is the opposite of the “tamheed” (smoothed path) that he enjoyed in the world. Allah had made his worldly life easy; now his afterlife will be a ceaseless, grueling ascent.

The word “urhiquhu” means to burden him with something that overwhelms him. The punishment will cover him, crush him, and he will not be able to escape it. This verse is a direct answer to the Prophet’s persecution. The man who had everything easy will now face the ultimate difficulty. It is a manifestation of the “difficult Day” mentioned in verse nine. The warning is not just for al-Walid; it is for every arrogant rich person who uses his blessings as a reason to reject the truth. The easy life is temporary; the unbearable climb is eternal. The believer is comforted by the knowledge that the oppressor’s comfort is a fleeting illusion.

=18=

The surah now zooms in on the specific thought process that led al-Walid to his doom: “for he contemplated and determined a degrading label for the Quran.” The Arabic “innahu fakkara wa qaddar” shows a deliberate, calculated mental effort. When al-Walid heard the Quran, he was shaken. He recognized that it was something extraordinary. But instead of submitting, he sat down to think, to scheme, to devise a way to discredit it. He “contemplated” (fakkara) with his mind, weighing different options. Then he “determined” (qaddar), meaning he estimated, measured, and decided upon a specific label that would stick. He was crafting a propaganda strategy. The people of Quraysh had gathered during the Hajj season, concerned about the impact of the Prophet’s message on the pilgrims. They went to al-Walid to get a unified verdict. They suggested various labels: soothsayer, madman, poet. Al-Walid listened to each and rejected them as ineffective. He then asked to hear the Quran. After listening, he made his calculated decision: the most damaging and effective label was “magic” (sihr). He said, “Call him a magician, because his words separate a man from his father, his brother, his wife, and his tribe.” This was his “determination. ” The verse exposes that his rejection was not a matter of simple disbelief; it was a cold, premeditated plot to protect his power and prestige.

The word “qaddara” also means that he measured the Quran against human standards and arrogantly presumed to judge it. This is the epitome of intellectual arrogance. The verse warns of the danger of using one’s intellect not to seek truth but to construct barriers against it. Al-Walid’s intelligence became a weapon of his destruction.

=19=

The surah now pronounces a powerful curse upon this man. “May he be condemned!” translates “qutila,” which literally means “may he be killed” or “may he be destroyed.” It is an invocation of divine curse and rejection.

The word is used in the Quran for those who reach the peak of wretchedness. The curse is repeated for emphasis and intensity.

The phrase “How evil was what he determined!” (kayfa qaddar) expresses astonishment and condemnation at the wickedness of his decision. He had a choice between accepting the truth and crafting a lie, and he chose the lie with deliberate calculation.

The word “qaddar” is the same verb from the previous verse, now thrown back at him as a mark of his infamy. This curse is the direct answer from heaven to his plot. While he was sitting in his council, thinking he was so clever, the revelation was descending with a curse upon his head.

This is a profound lesson: the machinations of the human mind against the truth are transparent to Allah and are met with divine wrath. The repetition of the curse in the next verse emphasizes that his sin was so heinous that one curse is not enough.

The verse also serves as a warning to all who mock and plot against the message of Islam. Their plots are known, and they are cursed in the sight of Allah. For the believers, this verse is a reassurance that the intellectual attacks on the faith are not new; they were done by the best minds of Quraysh, and they were met with the strongest divine condemnation. The truth does not need to be defended by anger; Allah defends it with His curse upon the schemers.

=20=

The curse is repeated and intensified: “May he be condemned even more! How evil was what he determined!

” The Arabic “thumma qutila kayfa qaddar” uses “thumma” to indicate a second, further curse. It is as if the first curse was not sufficient; his crime is so monstrous that it deserves doubling. This repetition reflects the severity of his offense. Al-Walid’s determination was not a casual mistake; it was a calculated, malicious attempt to obstruct the path to Allah. By labeling the Quran as magic, he aimed to prevent thousands from accepting the truth. The sin of misleading others is immense. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that whoever calls to misguidance will bear the sin of all who follow it without their sins being reduced. Al-Walid, as a respected leader, set a precedent of rejection that others imitated. The repeated curse is a measure of the multiplied evil he unleashed.

The phrase “How evil was what he determined!” is also repeated, emphasizing that the focus of the curse is not just on his person but on that specific act of intellectual dishonesty. It is a warning against the sin of deliberate misrepresentation of the truth. The verses do not merely describe his fate; they actively curse him, making him an eternal exemplar of the doomed plotter. This repetition creates a powerful emotional and spiritual impact on the listener. It teaches that the Quran is not a neutral document; it takes a fierce stance against the enemies of truth.

For the believer, this repetition is a source of comfort; Allah’s anger against the plotters is fierce and certain. The curse upon al-Walid stands as a protective barrier around the Quran; those who attack it invite this very curse upon themselves.
