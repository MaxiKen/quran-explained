=41=

In the Gardens, the believers, who are the people of the right, are engaged in peaceful and reflective conversation. The surah now reveals the content of their mutual questioning: they ask one another “about the wicked.

” The Arabic “al-mujrimeen” refers to the criminals, those who committed grave sins and persisted in disbelief. The believers are not asking out of gloating or spite; their inquiry flows from a state of wonder, gratitude, and the desire to understand the full scope of divine justice. They witnessed the horrors of the Day and the mercy that saved them, and now they are curious about the fate of those who mocked the truth in the world. This scene demonstrates that the believers’ relationships and memories persist in Paradise, but in a purified form. They remember the people of the world, especially those who opposed the truth, and they ask about them.

The phrase “who will then be asked” is an explanatory insertion that prepares for the next verse. It means that the wicked themselves are being questioned, or the believers direct their question toward them. Some commentators explain that the believers ask the angels, or they ask one another, “What has landed them in Hell?” and the answer comes either through direct observation or through a divinely enabled dialogue. The believers’ asking is part of the completion of their understanding; they see the end of the two paths they once heard about, and it deepens their appreciation of Allah’s guidance. It also serves as a warning to the reader: one day, the saved will look at the damned and recognize them, and the damned will have to answer the question. The verse creates a powerful narrative tension that makes the listener want to know what the answer will be. It also reinforces the idea that the Hereafter is not a solitary experience but a social one, where the righteous witness the fate of the wicked as a confirmation of the truth they held onto. This asking is, in itself, a part of the reward, for the believers see the truth of the Quran’s warnings actualized.

=42=

The question that the believers, or the angels, put to the wicked is now given: “What has landed you in Hell?” The Arabic “ma salakakum fee saqar” is a direct and piercing inquiry.

The word “salakakum” comes from “salaka,” meaning to enter, to thread, to insert. It asks: what was the cause, the path, the deed that led you into this terrible place called Saqar?

The question is not a request for information, because the answer is already known to the divine court. It is a question of rebuke, of establishing the undeniable truth, and of making the wicked confess their own crimes. This confession is part of their humiliation. In the world, they were arrogant and offered a thousand justifications for their disbelief. Now, stripped of all pretense, they must speak the simple, humiliating truth.

The question is also a teaching moment for the believers who are listening. By hearing the confession of the damned, the believers’ faith in the justice of Allah is further solidified. They see that Hell is not an arbitrary punishment; it is the logical outcome of specific choices and behaviors.

The question also serves as a warning to the living: it forces every listener to ask himself the same question now, before it is too late. What are the deeds, the attitudes, the neglects that could lead a person to Saqar? The verse is a merciful opportunity for self-reflection. The believers in Paradise are not asking to torment the wicked; they are asking as part of the divine drama that manifests the truth.

The question is a mirror. The next verses contain the answer, which lists the crimes that led to Hell. This structure—question followed by a detailed answer—is a powerful rhetorical device that etches the causes of damnation into the memory. It is a call to examine one’s own life in light of that answer.

=43=

The wicked now begin their confession, and the first item on their list is startling in its simplicity and gravity: “We were not of those who prayed.” The Arabic “lam naku min al-musalleen” means we were not among the performers of salah, the ritual prayer.

This is the primary identifier of their failure. They did not pray. In the previous surah Al-Muzzammil, the immense importance of night prayer and regular prayer was established. Here, the neglect of prayer is given as the first reason for entering Hell. The prayer is the pillar of faith, the first deed for which a person is held accountable on the Day of Judgment. The one who abandons prayer has severed the direct link between himself and his Lord. The wicked confess that they belonged to the group that did not pray. This does not necessarily mean that every one of them never prostrated at all; some might have prayed occasionally or performed empty rituals. But they were not “of those who prayed” in the true sense—those who prayed consistently, with faith, devotion, and humility. They were strangers to the mosques, absent from the rows of worshippers. Their neglect of prayer was a symptom of a deeper disease: the lack of faith in the unseen and the lack of love and fear for Allah. The confession is devastating. All their worldly excuses—business, leisure, arrogance—evaporate. They now see that the few minutes of prayer they avoided have become the difference between eternal bliss and eternal agony. The verse elevates the status of prayer to its rightful place: it is the fundamental distinguishing mark between the believers and the disbelievers.

For the Muslim, this is a sobering call to guard the prayers strictly, to pray on time, and to cultivate the inner presence that makes the prayer a true connection. The wicked’s confession is a warning that no good deed can compensate for the loss of prayer if it is willfully abandoned out of disbelief or severe negligence.

=44=

The second item in the confession of the wicked is a sin of omission against other human beings: “nor did we feed the poor.” The Arabic “wa lam naku nut‘imu al-miskeen” means we did not provide food for the needy.

This is a direct violation of the social responsibility that faith demands. In the earlier surah Al-Ma‘arij, the faithful were described as those who give the rightful share of their wealth to the beggar and the poor. The wicked here are the opposite. They were not merely indifferent; they actively withheld sustenance from those who needed it. In a society where the poor were often neglected and despised, feeding them was a clear sign of a compassionate heart. The failure to feed the poor is linked to the failure to pray because both stem from the same root: a heart that does not believe in accountability. The one who does not pray has no connection with Allah, and the one who has no connection with Allah has no motive to sacrifice his wealth for the sake of the unseen reward.

The mention of “feeding the poor” specifically points to the basic human need of hunger. It is the most fundamental act of charity. The wicked did not do even this simple deed. They hoarded their wealth, as described of the deniers in Al-Ma‘arij, and they turned away from the hungry. The confession is stark: their wealth did not save them; their failure to share it is now a cause of their torment.

The verse reminds the believer that faith must translate into tangible acts of mercy. The prayer is the right of Allah, and feeding the poor is the right of His creatures. Both are intertwined. Neglecting the latter is a proof of the emptiness of the former. The wicked confess this freely, because on that Day, no one can lie. Their admission serves as a timeless lesson: a society that does not care for its poor is heading toward divine wrath. The believer must examine his own attitude toward the needy and ensure that his prayers are accompanied by open hands.

=45=

The confession continues with a sin of the tongue and social interaction: “We used to indulge in falsehood along with others.” The Arabic “kunna nakhudu ma‘a al-kha’ideen” means we used to wade into vain talk, to plunge into falsehood, along with those who did the same.

The word “nakhudu” implies an eager, enthusiastic immersion. It refers to engaging in idle speech, mockery, slander, and the denial of the truth.

In the context of the Quraysh, this specifically points to their gatherings where they would ridicule the Prophet, mock the Quran, and spread lies about the believers. They would laugh, jest, and entertain each other with falsehood. This was not a passive listening; it was active participation.

The phrase “along with others” highlights the collective nature of the sin. They fed off each other’s energy. Peer pressure and the desire to belong to the group led them deeper into sin.

The verse teaches the danger of bad company. A person might be weak in his own convictions, but when surrounded by those who indulge in falsehood, he joins in to avoid being an outsider. The wicked now admit that this was their lifestyle. They preferred the amusement of falsehood over the seriousness of truth. The verb “indulge” captures the heedless enjoyment they derived from it. But now, in Hell, that enjoyment is a distant memory, and its consequence is eternal. This confession is a powerful warning against wasting time in vain talk, especially when it involves belittling the truth or harming others. The believer is called to guard his tongue and to choose his company wisely. If a gathering is indulging in falsehood, the believer must either change the subject or leave, lest he be counted among the kha’ideen. The verse exposes the moral bankruptcy of a life centered on frivolity and mockery.

=46=

The fourth item in the confession of the damned is the root of all their misdeeds: “and deny the Day of Judgment.” The Arabic “kunna nukadhdhibu bi yawm id-deen” means we used to call the Day of Recompense a lie.

This is the foundational error. If a person truly believes in the Day of Judgment, he will pray, he will feed the poor, and he will guard his tongue. Denying the Day removes all accountability. It allows a person to live for the moment, to follow desires without fear of consequences. The wicked confess that they were among the deniers. They heard the warning, but they treated it as a fable. They said, as in Al-Ma‘arij, that the Day was impossible. This denial was not a result of intellectual inquiry; it was a choice to reject the evidence that came through the messengers.

The word “nukadhdhibu” is in the past continuous tense, indicating a persistent, habitual denial. It was their settled conviction. Now, they are in the midst of the very Day they denied. The irony is crushing. Their denial did not make it go away; it only ensured that they were unprepared. The verse demonstrates that the Day of Judgment is the central article of faith around which all actions revolve. A person who truly believes in it will live a life of purpose and caution. The one who denies it lives a life of heedlessness. The confession is also a proof of the Quran’s truthfulness. The very people who denied the Day are now testifying to its reality. Their testimony, though involuntary, is recorded for all eternity as a warning. The believer reads this and strengthens his own belief in the Day, asking Allah to make him among those who live with its constant awareness.

The verse concludes the list of the four major sins: abandoning prayer, neglecting the poor, indulging in falsehood, and denying the Judgment. These four encapsulate the complete ruin of the soul.

=47=

The confession ends with a chilling statement of finality: “until the inevitable came to us.” The Arabic “hatta atana al-yaqeen” means until the certainty came to us.

The word “yaqeen” here is a metaphor for death. Death is the certain reality that everyone knows is coming but most live as if it will never arrive. It is called “the certainty” because it removes all doubt. At the moment of death, the veil is lifted, and the truths of the unseen become as clear as day. The wicked admit that they persisted in their sins until that very moment. They did not repent, they did not change. They gambled that they would have more time, or that the warnings were false, until death slammed the door shut.

The use of “until” indicates that their whole life was a single, continuous block of denial that ended only with the crushing arrival of death. Now, after death, they are in Hell, and there is no return.

The phrase “al-yaqeen” also ironically echoes the language of faith. The believers are those who have certainty (yaqeen) in their hearts about the unseen. The disbelievers only achieve yaqeen when it is too late. The acceptance of truth at the point of death or after it is not accepted.

The verse teaches the urgency of repentance. It warns against the delusion of “later.” The inevitable is coming, and its timing is unknown. The wise person lives as if the inevitable may arrive today. The confession of the wicked is a gift to the living, because it comes from those who have no more chances. They tell us, in effect: we waited until it was too late; do not make our mistake. The verse completes the answer to the question “What has landed you in Hell?” The sum of their answer is a life of neglecting the rights of Allah and His creation, wrapped in a denial of accountability, sealed by a death that came before repentance. It is a comprehensive and terrifying answer that should shake every soul that hears it.

=48=

After the wicked have confessed their sins and their fate is sealed, the surah delivers the final verdict on their situation: “So the pleas of intercessors will be of no benefit to them.” The Arabic “fama tanfa‘uhum shafa‘at al-shafi‘een” means that no intercession from any intercessor will profit them.

The word “shafa‘ah” refers to the act of pleading on behalf of another. On the Day of Judgment, Allah will grant permission to certain chosen ones—the prophets, the angels, the martyrs, the righteous—to intercede for specific groups of people. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, will be granted the grand intercession.

However, this intercession is not a blanket amnesty. It is conditional upon Allah’s permission and is only for those who died upon faith, even if they had major sins. As for those who died upon disbelief, no intercession can help them. The verse explicitly cuts off any hope that the pagans might have harbored. The Quraysh believed their idols would intercede for them, that their ancestors or their powerful leaders would speak on their behalf. This verse shatters that illusion. Even the greatest intercessors, such as the angels or the prophets, will not be able to help the people of Hell who have been condemned for their disbelief.

The phrase “of no benefit” is absolute. The intercession exists, but it will not reach them. The door is closed.

This is the final death of hope for the damned. After the confession, they might have looked around desperately for any savior, but the verse declares that no one can save them. For the believers, this verse is both a warning and a comfort. It warns against relying on lineage, tribal affiliation, or the mere hope of intercession without living a life of faith. It comforts them because it confirms that the intercession they hope for, by the mercy of Allah and through the Prophet, is a real and exclusive privilege of the believers.

The verse emphasizes that salvation is based on one’s own faith, not on the borrowed status of others. The deniers who mocked the concept of intercession now find that their mockery has turned into a reality from which they are utterly excluded.

=49=

The surah shifts back to address the present state of the Meccan deniers with a tone of astonishment and rebuke. “Now, what is the matter with them that they are turning away from the reminder?

” The Arabic “fama lahum ‘an al-tadhkirati mu‘rideen” expresses bewilderment at their behavior. After all the clear signs, the powerful warnings, the examples of past nations, the vivid descriptions of Hell and the confessions of the damned—what could possibly be wrong with them that they persist in turning away? The “reminder” (tadhkirah) is the Quran itself, which is a reminder of the truth, a reminder of Allah, a reminder of the Hereafter.

The word “mu‘rideen” means they are actively turning their backs, showing aversion. This is the same turning away described in the case of al-Walid, but here it applies to the whole group of disbelievers.

The question is rhetorical; it implies that there is no reasonable justification. Their turning away is irrational and self-destructive. The verse echoes the similar rhetorical questions in Al-Ma‘arij and Al-Jinn, where Allah asked what was the matter with the disbelievers who rush toward the Prophet in mockery. It is a call to the listener to reflect on the sheer absurdity of rejecting a message that is entirely beneficial and backed by overwhelming evidence. The disbelievers are like a deer that runs away from a clear stream of water. The Prophet is comforted by this verse; their rejection is not a failure of the message but a symptom of their own diseased hearts.

The verse also serves as a mirror for all humanity: what is it that prevents people from embracing the truth? Is it pride, fear of losing status, attachment to desires?

The question is personal and demands an honest answer. The reminder is here, clear and accessible; the act of turning away is a conscious, willful decision.

=50=

The surah provides a vivid simile to illustrate the irrational flight of the disbelievers: “as if they were spooked zebras.” The Arabic “ka’annahum humurun mustanfirah” paints a comical yet tragic picture.

The word “humur” is the plural of “himar,” which can mean a wild donkey or a zebra. “Mustanfirah” means startled, fleeing in panic, scared off.

The image is of a herd of wild zebras on the African plains that have been suddenly startled by something. They bolt away with wild speed, their eyes wide with terror, their legs kicking up dust, scattering in all directions. There is no thought, no direction, just blind, panicked flight.

This is exactly the state of the Quraysh when they heard the Quran. Instead of calmly reflecting, they fled from it in a mental and emotional panic. The comparison to donkeys or zebras is deliberately humiliating. It reduces their grand posturing as proud chieftains to the level of frightened animals. They prided themselves on their intelligence and nobility, but the Quran says they are behaving like startled beasts.

The word “mustanfirah” can also be read as “mustanfirah,” meaning they are actively, vigorously fleeing. The speed and energy of their escape highlight the intensity of their aversion. The verse is a psychological diagnosis. Their flight is not based on reason; it is an instinctual, animal-like reaction to something they perceive as a threat. The truth threatens their falsehood, so they run. The simile also suggests that their leader, the one who should guide them, is absent or is also fleeing, which is exactly what al-Walid and others did. They could not refute the Quran, so they avoided it. The verse serves as a warning: do not let your response to the truth be that of a startled animal. Pause, reflect, and use the intellect that Allah has given you. The animal flees, but the human is supposed to reason.

=51=

The simile is completed with the source of the terror: “fleeing from a lion?” The Arabic “farrat min qaswarah” means they have fled from a lion, a predator, a hunter.

The word “qaswarah” is a powerful term for a lion, a beast that hunts and devours. Some commentators also interpret it as a hunter, a group of archers, or any powerful predator. The zebras flee not from a trivial thing but from a mortal threat. The disbelievers are treating the Quran and the Prophet as if they were a deadly lion seeking to devour them. In reality, the message is a mercy and a cure, not a predator. Their misperception is the root of their panic. They see the call to monotheism as an attack on their entire way of life, their status, their wealth, and their gods. So they run as if their very existence is threatened. The lion represents the truth that corners falsehood. The irony is that the very thing they are fleeing from is their only salvation. By running from the truth, they are actually running directly toward the true predator, which is Hell, as described earlier. The “lion” is a metaphor for the inevitable judgment. They flee the warner, but they cannot flee the warning. The verse exposes the tragic folly of their state. A person who sees a lion might flee rationally, but a person who sees a doctor and flees because he dislikes the diagnosis is irrational. The Quraysh were in the latter category. The comparison to a lion also suggests the intensity of the Quran’s impact. Even those who reject it feel its power; they sense that it is something formidable that demands a response. Their flight is a backhanded acknowledgment of its strength. The believer is called to face the truth with courage, not to flee from it. The verse completes the vivid picture of their spiritual state: startled animals running in panic from a perceived threat, when the real threat lies in the direction they are running.

=52=

The surah now uncovers another layer of their psychology, explaining their flight and rejection. “In fact, each one of them wishes to be given a personal letter from Allah for all to read.

” The Arabic “bal yureedu kullu imri’in minhum an yu’ta suhufan munashsharah” reveals a deep-seated arrogance. They are not satisfied with a single messenger bringing a universal message to all. Each of them wants his own private, exclusive revelation. They want a scroll, a written document, descending from heaven specifically addressed to him by name, something he can show off to others. This was one of the demands of the Quraysh chiefs. They said, “We will not believe until we are given something like what the messengers were given. ” They were jealous of the Prophet’s status. They considered themselves too important to follow another human being. They wanted God to validate their personal greatness with a custom-made scripture. The verse exposes the childishness and egotism of this demand. Allah does not run His affairs according to the whims of the arrogant. The Prophet is chosen by divine wisdom, not by human election. The demand for a “personal letter for all to read” is a way of saying, “I want to be a prophet myself, or I want a direct line to God that bypasses Muhammad.” It is the height of insolence. This wishful thinking is part of what makes them turn away. They are not looking for truth; they are looking for recognition.

The verse also implies that even if they were given such personal letters, they would still find an excuse. Their problem is not a lack of evidence but a rejection of the very concept of submission to a messenger. The “suhufan munashsharah” (pages spread open) suggests a public, undeniable miracle.

Yet the Quran itself, which is an open, recited book, is not enough for them. The lesson for believers is to accept the means of guidance that Allah has chosen, rather than demanding that Allah conforms to our preferences. The Prophet is sufficient, the Quran is sufficient. Wanting a special, exclusive sign is often a disguise for pride.

=53=

The surah delivers a sharp diagnosis that cuts through all their demands and justifications. “But no!

In fact, they do not fear the Hereafter.” The Arabic “kalla bal la yakhafoon al-akhirah” uses “kalla” to powerfully reject their wishes and excuses, and “bal” to turn to the real, hidden cause. The root of their turning away, their flight, and their demand for personal letters is not intellectual dissatisfaction. It is a spiritual and moral problem: they do not fear the Hereafter.

The word “yakhafoon” (they fear) is crucial. If they truly feared the Day of Judgment, they would not flee from the warning. They would not make arrogant demands. They would humble themselves and listen. The absence of fear of the Hereafter is the disease behind all their symptoms. Because they see this world as the only reality, they live for status, wealth, and pride. The idea of resurrection and accountability is a nuisance that they push away. This lack of fear is not a neutral state; it is a willful suppression of the natural instinct to seek the divine. The verse explains why the warnings, no matter how vivid, did not move them: the heart that has killed its fear of the Hereafter is like a dead soil that cannot absorb rain. The verse is a profound call to self-examination. The listener must ask: do I truly fear the Hereafter? Does that fear translate into action? The disbelievers’ problem was not that they were uneducated; they were experts in eloquence and poetry. Their problem was a heart empty of awe for the ultimate reality. This verse links back to the confessions of the wicked in Hell, who admitted they denied the Day of Judgment. The disbelievers in the world are on the same path. The warning is a mercy to awaken that fear. Without fear, knowledge is useless. With fear, even a little knowledge leads to salvation. The verse is the turning point that explains everything else.

The final verses of the surah will offer the reminder and the means to cultivate this fear.

=54=

The surah now brings the argument to a close with a powerful affirmation. “Enough! Surely this Quran is a reminder.

” The Arabic “kalla innahu tadhkirah” uses “kalla” to halt all objections and disputes. It means: enough of your arguments, enough of your demands, enough of your flight. The matter is settled.

The word “innahu” (surely it) refers to the Quran. It is a “tadhkirah,” a reminder, an admonition, a wake-up call. The Quran does not need to be supplemented by personal letters from heaven. It is complete, sufficient, and clear. Its purpose is to remind the human being of what he already knows deep in his fitrah: his Lord, his duties, his end. The reminder is a mercy, because humans forget. The hustle of the world clouds the soul. The Quran comes to polish that clouded mirror. The verse is a defense of the self-sufficiency of the Quranic message. It is not lacking anything; the problem lies in the heedlessness of the recipients.

The phrase “Enough!” is also a comfort to the Prophet. He does not need to bring new signs on demand. He has this reminder, and it is sufficient for those who want to be reminded.

The verse also calls the believers to treat the Quran as their primary source of reminder. When the heart grows hard, when the fear of the Hereafter dims, the Quran is the cure. Reciting it, pondering it, and hearing it rekindles the fear and the hope. The reminder is not a one-time thing; it is a continuous need.

The word “tadhkirah” implies that the truth is already embedded in the soul; the Quran merely activates it. The disbelievers who reject it are not rejecting an alien message; they are rejecting their own innate knowledge. The verse is a final, emphatic declaration that everything needed for guidance is right here. No excuses, no additional requirements. The door is open, and the reminder is on the table.

=55=

After affirming that the Quran is a sufficient reminder, the surah opens the gate of free choice wide. “So let whoever wills be mindful of it.

” The Arabic “faman shaa dhakarah” means whoever wishes, let him remember it, take heed of it, be mindful of it, accept it. The particle “fa” (so) links this directly to the previous statement. Since it is a clear reminder, the natural consequence is that the one who wants guidance will take it. The verb “dhakara” means not just to remember intellectually but to internalize, to act upon, to keep in constant awareness.

The phrase “whoever wills” (man shaa) emphasizes human agency. Allah has provided the reminder; now the choice belongs to the individual. No one is forced.

This is the dignity and the test of the human being. The verse also implies a gentle invitation. It does not say, “You must, or else. ” It says, “Whoever wishes.

” This is the Quranic method of persuasion, appealing to the heart’s desire for goodness. The mindfulness mentioned here is the cure for the lack of fear of the Hereafter. To be mindful of the Quran is to live with the awareness of its teachings, to let them shape one’s thoughts, words, and deeds. It is a continuous, conscious act.

The verse also carries a hidden warning: whoever does not wish to be mindful will be left to his own doom. The door is open, but the invitation is time-sensitive. This verse is a profound expression of divine mercy and justice. Mercy is in the provision of the reminder; justice is in the freedom to accept or reject. The believer responds to this verse by saying, “I will, O Lord. I choose to be mindful.” And then he strives to make the Quran his constant companion. The verse is a call to action, moving from passive hearing to active willing.

=56=

The final verse of the surah places the entire matter back into the hands of Allah, balancing human choice with divine decree. “But they cannot do so unless Allah wills.

” The Arabic “wama tasha’oona illa an yashaa Allah” means that a person’s will to be mindful is itself dependent on the will of Allah. This is a profound theological statement. Human beings have free will, but that free will operates within the overarching will of the Creator. No one can take the path of mindfulness unless Allah grants him the ability and the success to do so. This verse prevents arrogance in the believer, who must never think that his guidance is solely his own accomplishment. It is a gift from Allah. It also provides an explanation for why some, despite hearing the reminder, do not take heed: Allah has not willed it for them due to their stubbornness and rejection. This does not mean Allah is unfair; rather, His will follows His wisdom. Those who turn away are left to their own devices, while those who turn toward Him are aided.

The verse then closes the surah with two attributes of Allah that encapsulate the balance of the entire message: “He alone is worthy to be feared and entitled to forgive. ” The Arabic “huwa ahlu al-taqwa wa ahlu al-maghfirah” means He is the one who deserves to be feared, the one entitled to grant forgiveness. “Ahl al-taqwa” means He is the sole object of reverential awe. No idols, no humans, no jinn deserve to be feared. Only Allah is worthy of that profound, worshipful fear. “Ahl al-maghfirah” means He is the one who has the authority and the readiness to forgive. No one else can forgive sins. This beautiful pairing is the ultimate summary of the surah. The warnings of Hell cultivate fear; the reminder and the open invitation cultivate hope in forgiveness. The surah that began with the command to arise and warn, that thundered against the arrogance of al-Walid, that detailed the agonies of Saqar, ends with the gentle note that the same Lord is the Master of forgiveness. The believer stands between these two pillars: fear of His punishment and hope for His mercy. The surah does not leave the listener in despair; it opens the door to the very forgiveness that is needed.
