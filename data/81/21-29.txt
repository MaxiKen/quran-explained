=21=

Having described Jibrīl as full of power and held in honour by the Lord of the Throne, verse twenty-one completes this portrait with two further qualities: he is “obeyed there in heaven, and trustworthy.” The phrase “obeyed there” translates muṭā‘in thamma. Jibrīl is not an isolated figure; he is a leader among the angels, one whose commands are heeded by the heavenly host. When he conveys an order from Allah, the other angels obey him without hesitation.

The word “there” (thamma) refers to the celestial realm, the heavens, the locus of pure obedience where no rebellion exists. This emphasises that the revelation was not brought by a rogue or isolated spirit but by a commander surrounded by obedient angels, all of whom recognise his authority.

The second attribute, “trustworthy” (amīn), is of immense importance. Jibrīl is absolutely faithful in delivering the divine message. He does not add, subtract, alter, or betray the trust placed in him. He brought the Qur’ān exactly as he received it from the Preserved Tablet. The combination of being obeyed and being trustworthy refutes any possibility of distortion in the chain of revelation. The Meccans claimed the Prophet was taught by a devil or a mere human; the Qur’ān responds that the true carrier is a mighty, obeyed, trustworthy angel.

This description also subtly links to the Prophet Muḥammad (ﷺ), who was himself known among his people as al-Amīn, the trustworthy. Just as the angelic messenger is amīn, so too is the human messenger, and both are absolutely reliable in the transmission of Allah’s words. The verse builds a bridge from the heavenly reality to the earthly, preparing for the next verse which directly addresses the character of the Prophet.

For the believer, this is a reassurance that the scripture they hold is not a corrupted text but the pure word of Allah, carried by an angel of flawless integrity, establishing a foundation of trust that demands a response of faith and action.

=22=

With the identity and integrity of the angelic messenger firmly established, the sūrah now turns to defend the human messenger in verse twenty-two: “And your fellow man is not insane.” The phrase “your fellow man” translates ṣāḥibukum, which means your companion, your associate, the one who has lived among you and whom you know intimately.

This is a powerful appeal to the direct, personal knowledge of the Quraysh. They had known Muḥammad (ﷺ) for forty years before his prophethood. They called him al-Amīn, the trustworthy. He had never shown any sign of mental illness, deception, or delusion. He was known for his sound judgment, honesty, and balanced character. So the accusation of insanity (jinnah) was not only false but absurd given their long experience of him. The verse uses the third person “he” but the address “your fellow man” makes it direct and personal. It strips away the hostility and invites the disbelievers to rely on their own firsthand knowledge rather than the slander they spread. This defense of the Prophet also consoles him. The revelation reassures him that Allah Himself bears witness to his sanity and uprightness. The charge of madness was a common weapon used against prophets, levelled at Nūḥ, Mūsā, and others, because the truth they brought seemed so radical that those who rejected it could only explain it as madness. The verse dismisses that claim with the simplest, most unassailable argument: you know this man. The implication is that if they truly examined their own experience, they would admit that he is not insane, and therefore the message he brings deserves serious consideration.

This verse also serves as a timeless principle: the moral character of a messenger is a crucial testimony to the truth of his message, and the Prophet’s life was an open book of integrity.

=23=

Verse twenty-three provides a specific proof of the Prophet’s truthfulness and the authenticity of his experience: “And he did see that angel on the clear horizon.” The Arabic uses the particle laqad for emphasis, confirming that this vision is an established fact. The “angel” referred to is Jibrīl, and the “clear horizon” is the bright, visible horizon of the sky. The Prophet saw Jibrīl in his true, magnificent form on two notable occasions.

The first was at the very beginning of revelation in the cave of Ḥirā’, when Jibrīl appeared filling the horizon with his immense form, and the Prophet was overwhelmed. The second was during the Night Journey and Ascension (al-Isrā’ wa al-Mi‘rāj), where the Prophet saw Jibrīl in his original angelic shape near the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary.

The mention of the “clear horizon” evokes the visible, undeniable nature of this experience. It was not a dream, a hallucination, or a whisper in the soul; it was an external, physical vision that occurred in a specific place and time. The horizon being “clear” (mubīn) indicates that there was no ambiguity or hiddenness. This verse refutes the pagans’ claim that the Prophet was deluded or that his revelation came from obscure, hidden sources. The source was seen openly, on the bright edge of the sky. It also serves to confirm that the Prophet is an eyewitness to the heavenly realities he conveys; he is not narrating hearsay but reporting what he himself witnessed. For the believers, this strengthens faith in the unseen world and in the Prophet’s direct connection to it. It demonstrates that the gap between the earthly and the heavenly was bridged, and the Prophet saw the noble messenger with his own eyes. This vision is a gift of certainty that fortified the Prophet for his mission.

=24=

The defense of the Prophet’s character and integrity continues in verse twenty-four: “and he does not withhold what is revealed to him of the unseen.” The Arabic phrase is wa mā huwa ‘alā al-ghaybi bi-ḍanīn. The key word ḍanīn has been interpreted in two authentic ways. One reading is that he is not stingy or withholding; he does not keep back any part of the revelation.

The second reading is that he is not a suspect or a person of doubt concerning the unseen; he is not suspicious about what he receives. Both meanings are complementary and powerful.

The first meaning refutes the accusation that the Prophet might have concealed certain revelations out of fear or favouritism. He delivered the entire message, the warnings and the glad tidings, without holding anything back.

This is a vital point because the Prophet was entrusted with the unseen knowledge of Paradise, Hell, and the events of the Day of Judgment. He could have omitted the difficult verses that criticised the pagan ways to appease his tribe, but he did not.

The second meaning, that he is not suspicious or uncertain about the unseen, confirms his absolute faith in the revelation. He did not doubt the truth of what Jibrīl brought him; he received it with complete conviction. The “unseen” (al-ghayb) is all that is hidden from human senses—the angels, the Day of Judgment, the realities of the grave, the divine decree. The Prophet is not a reluctant or uncertain transmitter; he is a fully trusting and completely honest conveyor. This verse completes the picture of the faithful messenger. The angelic messenger was trustworthy, and the human messenger is equally trustworthy in receiving and delivering. For the believers, this is a source of confidence that the Qur’ān they have is complete and unaltered, a perfect trust from Allah.

=25=

Having established the nobility of Jibrīl and the integrity of the Prophet, verse twenty-five delivers a direct blow to one of the main accusations of the Meccans: “And this Quran is not the word of an outcast devil.” The pagans sometimes claimed that the Qur’ān was the speech of a shayṭān or that it was a form of soothsaying inspired by devils who eavesdropped on the heavens. This verse categorically denies that.

The word “outcast” translates rajīm, meaning one who is pelted with stones, driven away from divine mercy, and expelled from the heavenly realm. The devils are precisely the opposite of the honoured, powerful, obeyed, trustworthy angel Jibrīl. They are cursed, weak, and deceitful.

The verse establishes an unbridgeable contrast: the carrier of the Qur’ān is an angel of high rank and purity, so the message itself can only be pure and true. The devils, on the other hand, are barred from the heavens and cannot eavesdrop on the divine decrees, as other sūrahs explain.

Therefore, they have no access to such a sublime message. The Qur’ān is the Word of Allah, delivered by an honoured angel to an honest prophet; it has nothing to do with the whispers of the outcast.

This verse also has a deeper theological implication: the message of Islam leads to righteousness, worship of the One God, moral purity, and justice. The devils would never inspire a message that condemns them, calls to their enemy, and guides people away from their path. The very content of the Qur’ān is proof against its satanic origin.

For the believer, this verse is a call to appreciate the pure lineage of the revelation. It descended from the Lord of the Throne, through a noble angel, to a trustworthy prophet, and it remains a healing and guidance for humanity, free from any taint of darkness. This clear refutation should settle any doubt about the divine source of the scripture.

=26=

After the powerful refutation of all false accusations against the Prophet and the Qur’ān, verse twenty-six asks a searching rhetorical question: “So what other path would you take?” The Arabic fa-ayna tadhhabūn literally means “Where then are you going?” It is a question that confronts the disbelievers with the absurdity of their position. After the truth has been made so clear—the cosmic signs, the Day of Judgment, the integrity of the messenger, the nobility of the angel, the purity of the revelation—what alternative do they really have? What path can they possibly take that leads anywhere but to the truth?

The question implies that all other paths are dead ends, misguidance, and darkness. The clarity of the Qur’ān has left no room for a reasonable excuse. The verse uses the present tense, indicating the ongoing wandering of those who reject the message. It is as if the sūrah places the listener at a crossroads, where one path is illuminated by the divine word, and all other paths are shrouded in ignorance and destruction.

This verse also serves as a turning point, shifting from defense to invitation. It challenges the listener to make a conscious decision. The pagans who heard this were left with no logical escape; they could not discredit the Prophet, they could not discredit the angel, they could not discredit the Qur’ān. So where would they go? The same question reverberates through time. Every human being who hears the message and recognises its truth is faced with this inquiry: if you turn away from the Qur’ān, where will you go? To materialism, to empty philosophies, to man-made systems that lead to despair? The verse is both a warning and a compassionate plea. It reveals the futility of rejection and the clarity of the Straight Path. It calls the soul to stop wandering and to walk towards the undeniable light that has been set before them.

=27=

The surah now states the very nature and purpose of the Qur’ān in verse twenty-seven: “Surely this Quran is only a reminder to the whole world.” The word “surely” (in) adds emphasis, and “only” (illā) signifies exclusivity, meaning its essential function is to serve as a reminder.

The word “reminder” (dhikr) once again highlights that the Qur’ān is not bringing a strange or new message; it is awakening the primordial knowledge of the Creator that is already etched into the human soul. It is a reminder of the covenant, of the purpose of life, of the Day of meeting the Lord, of the moral law. The scope of this reminder is universal: “to the whole world” (li’l-‘ālamīn). This includes all of humanity and the jinn, across all times and places. The Meccan pagans were mistaken if they thought the message was only for them or that they could determine its worth. The Qur’ān is a global and timeless call.

This verse also counters the earlier arrogance of those who felt self-sufficient and indifferent. The reminder is not for the elite or the powerful alone; it is for all creation. The universality of the Qur’ān means that every person, regardless of race, status, or era, can access its guidance. This elevates the responsibility of the believers to convey the message and the responsibility of every individual to heed it.

The phrase “only a reminder” also corrects any misconception that the Prophet was a tyrant forcing people or a soothsayer predicting arbitrary events. He was simply a warner and a bearer of a reminder. The reminder is sufficient for those who have hearts. The verse thus connects back to the earlier sūrahs and within this sūrah, emphasising that the purpose of the cosmic signs and the revelation is to awaken. For the modern reader, the verse reaffirms that the Qur’ān remains as relevant today as it was at its revelation, a living reminder for a world that constantly forgets its Creator.

=28=

Verse twenty-eight narrows the focus of the universal reminder to individual responsibility: “to whoever of you wills to take the Straight Way.” The phrase “whoever of you” (li-man shā’a minkum) places the choice squarely in the hands of each person. The reminder is available, clear, and accessible; now it is up to the individual to will to take the Straight Way. The “Straight Way” (al-ṣirāṭ al-mustaqīm) is the path of Islam, the path of submission to Allah, the path that leads to His pleasure and to Paradise. It is straight because it is free of crookedness, confusion, and deviation. It is the direct route to the destination of eternal happiness. The verse affirms human free will and accountability. The Qur’ān does not compel belief; it presents the truth and invites the will.

The phrase “whoever wills” also contains a sense of dignity and honour. Allah has given human beings the capacity to choose, and He addresses them as beings capable of making a conscious, meaningful decision.

This is a profound affirmation of human agency. Yet the verse also implies that merely wanting is not enough; one must take active steps to walk the Straight Way. It is a combination of sincere intention and righteous action. This verse connects back to the opening of the sūrah, where the cosmic upheavals were described. Faced with that inevitable Day, the only sane response is to will to take the Straight Way now. The reminder is presented, the choice is offered, and the consequences are known. The believer’s heart responds to this verse with a renewed determination to stay on the path, while the seeker is encouraged that the door is open. The verse serves as a beautiful summary of the sūrah’s call: after all the terrifying warnings and the clear proofs, the mercy of Allah shines through in the simple, open invitation to walk the path of guidance.

=29=

The sūrah concludes with a profound theological statement that perfectly balances the affirmation of human choice in the previous verse. Verse twenty-nine declares: “But you cannot will to do so, except by the Will of Allah, the Lord of all worlds.

” The word “but” introduces a necessary condition. The human being may will to take the Straight Way, but even that volition is not an independent power; it is entirely dependent on the will of Allah.

This is the doctrine of divine decree in its most beautiful formulation. Human beings have real will and real choice, but that will is encompassed by and operates within the overarching will of the Creator. No one can will to believe, move, or do anything unless Allah has willed to grant them the ability and the permission. This verse safeguards against arrogance. A person cannot boast, “I chose the Straight Path by my own intelligence and power,” because their very choice was made possible by Allah. At the same time, the verse does not negate responsibility, because the previous verse commands to will and to walk. The two verses together present the balanced Islamic position: full reliance on Allah while exerting one’s utmost effort.

The phrase “the Lord of all worlds” (Rabbu’l-‘Ālamīn) reminds that Allah’s will governs not just human beings but all creation. The Lord who folds the sun, scatters the stars, and brings Paradise near is the same Lord who guides hearts and enables wills. This final verse is a source of immense comfort and humility for the believer. It teaches them to constantly seek Allah’s help in staying on the Straight Path, as they recite in Sūrah al-Fātiḥah, “Guide us to the Straight Path.” The human will is weak and prone to fluctuation, but the one who attaches their will to the will of the Lord of all worlds finds stability and success. The surah thus ends with the most essential truth: all power and will belong to Allah, and to Him is the ultimate return.
