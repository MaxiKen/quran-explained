=41=

The description of the righteous in the previous verse—those who stood in awe of their Lord and restrained themselves from evil desires—now reaches its beautiful culmination: “Paradise will certainly be their home.” The Arabic particle inna brings emphatic certainty, and the sentence structure “Paradise, it is the home” leaves no room for doubt.

The word al-Jannah means a garden, a place of lush, hidden beauty, and it is the same word used in Surah An-Naba’ for the dense orchards that serve as a glimpse of the eternal gardens to come. Here, it is the full, unblemished reality.

The term ma’wā, translated as “home,” carries the sense of a refuge, a dwelling place, a place of return and ultimate belonging. This is the same word that was used for the Hellfire being the home of the transgressors in verse 39, and that parallel is deliberate. The righteous and the wicked each have their ma’wā, their final, fitting abode. For the righteous, the home is not a place of torment but of perfect peace, joy and honour. In this world, the believers were often strangers, misunderstood and persecuted for their faith. The Prophet ﷺ said, “Islam began as something strange, and it will return to being strange, so glad tidings to the strangers.” Paradise is the true homeland for those strangers. It is the restoration of the garden that Adam and Hawwa’ were created for and then temporarily left. The righteous, through their awe of the standing before Allah and their restraint over their desires, have earned this return. The verse is a promise that the fear they felt at the thought of standing before their Lord is now replaced by eternal security. Their home is one where there is no idle talk, no lying, no fear, no death. The angels will greet them with peace, and they will dwell in what no eye has seen, no ear has heard, and no human heart has imagined. The certainty of “will certainly be” comforts every believer who struggles against the pulls of the lower self, reassuring them that the struggle is temporary and the reward is everlasting. The surah has moved from the violent oaths of the angels extracting souls, to the cosmic quaking, to the example of Pharaoh’s destruction, to the rational proof of creation, and now to the final, peaceful destination of those who answer the call. Paradise as their home is the fitting reward from the Most Compassionate, the generous gift mentioned in the earlier surah. It is the ultimate success, the mafāz, the salvation that every believing heart longs for.

=42=

After the grand dichotomy of the two destinies, the surah now addresses one of the persistent questions that the deniers used to pose to the Prophet ﷺ: “They ask you, O Prophet, regarding the Hour, ‘When will it be?’” The “they” refers to the pagan Quraysh, though the question has been asked by sceptics in every age. Their asking was not a sincere quest for guidance but a mixture of mockery, a challenge, and an attempt to trip up the Prophet. They thought that if he could not specify the exact timing of the Day of Judgement, his entire message was discredited.

The question “When will it be?” uses the Arabic word ayyāna, which seeks a precise point in time. It reveals their materialist mindset: they wanted the Hour to be scheduled, like a market day or a seasonal fair. The deep irony is that the very question they asked mockingly is the question that every human being should ask sincerely, with a heart full of concern and a desire to prepare. The Qur’an records their question to expose their arrogance and to provide the proper divine response. The Hour, as-sā‘ah, is one of the names of the Day of Resurrection, emphasising its suddenness and its inevitability. It is the moment when time as we know it ends and eternity begins. The Prophet ﷺ himself did not know when the Hour would occur. In the famous hadith of Jibrīl, when the angel asked, “When is the Hour?” the Prophet replied, “The one questioned knows no more than the questioner.” The knowledge of the Hour was kept hidden by divine wisdom, for if humans knew the exact date, they might either be paralysed by fear or emboldened to delay repentance until the last moment. The concealment serves to keep every generation in a state of readiness. The verse transitions the surah from the vivid depictions of the Supreme Disaster to the practical reality that its timing is a secret of the unseen. The deniers’ question is about to receive a firm and clarifying answer that reorients the Prophet’s mission and the believers’ focus from idle speculation to urgent action.

=43=

The divine response to their question is direct and absolving: “But it is not for you to tell its time.” The Arabic phrase fīma anta min dhikrāhā is a powerful construction that literally means “In what capacity are you to mention it?” It is a rhetorical question that negates any role for the Prophet in determining or disclosing the Hour’s timing. The Prophet is the messenger, the warner, the bearer of glad tidings, but he is not the keeper of the unseen keys. Allah is telling him that this matter is entirely outside his jurisdiction. This verse served to relieve the Prophet from the pressure the Quraysh tried to put on him. They wanted him to prove his prophethood by predicting the exact date, but his prophethood was never about fortune-telling; it was about conveying the message of monotheism, morality and accountability. The knowledge of the Hour is part of ‘ilm al-ghayb, the knowledge of the unseen, which belongs exclusively to Allah.

The verse also serves as an important doctrinal clarification. Some might mistakenly think that the Prophet ﷺ, due to his elevated status, had access to all unseen matters, but the Qur’an consistently teaches that even he did not know the Hour. In Surah Al-A‘raf, the Prophet is instructed to say, “I do not know whether what you are promised is near or whether my Lord will extend it for a period” (7:187). This protects the belief from exaggeration about the Prophet and keeps the focus on Allah alone as the Knower of the unseen.

The phrase “to tell its time” translates dhikrāhā, which can also mean to make mention of it, to discuss its timing with authority. The Prophet’s role is to warn about its reality, not to pinpoint its occurrence.

The answer, therefore, shifts the question from the “when” to the “what now.” The deniers are fixated on a detail that is none of their business; what should concern them is their state when it arrives. The verse is a masterclass in redirecting a futile question to a beneficial truth.

=44=

The negation of the Prophet’s knowledge is now completed with an affirmation of where the true knowledge resides: “That knowledge rests with your Lord alone.” The word muntahāhā comes from intahā, meaning its ultimate end, its final destination, its utmost limit. The knowledge of the Hour is not scattered or partially known by various beings; it is solely and entirely with Allah, terminating at Him. No angel, no prophet, no created being shares in this knowledge. The verse uses the intimate phrase “your Lord” (rabbika), addressing the Prophet directly, which brings comfort to him and, by extension, to every believer. The Lord who nurtures, guides and protects you is the One who holds the timing of the Hour in His wisdom. The exclusivity of this knowledge is emphasised elsewhere, such as in Surah Luqman: “Indeed, Allah has knowledge of the Hour, and He sends down the rain, and He knows what is in the wombs” (31:34). These are the five keys of the unseen that no one else knows. The verse thus re-establishes the correct hierarchy of knowledge: the Creator knows, the creation does not, and the creation must trust the Creator’s wisdom in keeping it hidden.

For the disbelievers, this is a warning: since you do not know when it will come, you cannot afford to delay repentance. For the believers, it is a motivation to live each day as if it could be the last.

The phrase “rests with your Lord” also implies that the matter is settled, complete and perfectly managed. There is no anxiety for the Prophet in not knowing; his Lord has it under control. The surah is now ready to redefine the Prophet’s role in light of this hidden knowledge.

The question of the Hour is not a puzzle to be solved but a reality to be prepared for, and the preparation comes through the warning that the Prophet is commanded to deliver.

=45=

Having removed the burden of knowing the Hour, the verse clarifies the Prophet’s actual mission: “Your duty is only to warn whoever is in awe of it.” The word innamā (only) restricts and focuses the Prophet’s role. He is a mundhir, a warner. His task is not to answer every speculative question or to force people to believe, but to deliver the warning clearly and to let the warning do its work in hearts that are receptive. The warning is effective only for “whoever is in awe of it” (man yakhshāhā).

This is a profound insight. The same message that softens the heart of one person hardens the heart of another. The deciding factor is khashyah: a pre-existing awe, a fear, a reverence for the truth that resides in the person’s soul. Those who already have that seed of awe will hear the warning and take it to heart, increasing in fear of Allah and in righteous action. Those whose hearts are dead, filled with arrogance and love of the world, will hear the same warning and mock it, as the Quraysh did. The verse therefore liberates the Prophet from feeling responsible for the disbelief of the mockers. He cannot guide those who have shut their hearts to awe. His duty is discharged when he warns; the outcome is with Allah. This also connects back to verse 40, which described the righteous as those who “were in awe of standing before their Lord.” The Prophet’s warning is precisely for such people, strengthening their awe and guiding them further. For the deniers who lack this awe, the warning only adds to their evidence against themselves on the Day of Judgement. The verse is a lesson for all callers to Islam: the success of the call is not measured by the number of converts but by the sincerity and clarity with which the message is delivered. The focus should be on nurturing those hearts that tremble at the mention of the Hour, not on chasing the approval of the scoffers. The surah thus ends its main discourse by re-centring the mission on the quality of the audience’s heart, setting up the final, haunting image of the Day when the mockers will realise their error.

=46=

The surah concludes with a striking image that answers the deniers’ obsession with time by showing them how they will perceive time when the Hour arrives: “On the Day they see it, it will be as if they had stayed in the world no more than one evening or its morning.” The deniers, who spent their lives arguing about the timing of the Hour and thinking it far off, will be confronted with a reality that shrinks their entire earthly existence into a fleeting moment.

The word “see it” means they will witness the Hour with their own eyes, and at that moment, their perception of the past will be completely transformed. The long decades they lived, the plans they made, the pleasures they enjoyed, the power they wielded—all of it will seem like a brief stay, no longer than a single evening (‘ashiyyah) or the morning that follows it (ḍuḥāhā). The alternation of evening and morning echoes the earlier mention of Allah bringing forth the daylight after dimming the night. The entire span of a human life will seem as short as that daily transition.

This is not a literal statement about time shrinking, but a psychological and existential truth: in the face of eternity, even a hundred years is an insignificant speck. The disbelievers’ earlier mockery about “when will it be?” will be answered not with a calendar date but with an experience that renders the question meaningless.

The verse also carries a deep sense of tragedy and regret. The same life that they thought was so long and significant, in which they delayed repentance and mocked the warners, is now seen as a wasted opportunity, a mere moment that could have been used for eternal joy but was squandered.

The phrase “one evening or its morning” specifically uses the parts of the day that are transitional, underscoring the impermanence of worldly life. The morning is followed by the full heat of day, just as the worldly life is followed by the full reality of the Hereafter.

The surah ends on this sobering note, leaving the listener with the urgent realisation that the time to prepare is now, not later. The Hour may be hidden, but its sudden arrival will make every moment of heedlessness a source of unbearable remorse for those who did not take the path of awe and purification. With this, the message of the surah is complete: the resurrection is inevitable, the destinies are fixed, and the wise are those who live in the light of that certainty.
