=21=

This verse records Pharaoh's response to the greatest sign: "but he denied and disobeyed ˹Allah˺." Despite witnessing the staff transform into a serpent before his eyes, despite the overwhelming evidence of Moses' truthfulness, Pharaoh chose denial and disobedience. His response was not born of ignorance but of arrogance, for he understood the truth of Moses' message yet refused to submit to it, because submission would have required him to relinquish his claim to divinity and to humble himself before a Lord greater than himself. The verse captures the essence of his crime in two words: denial and disobedience.

The pairing of denial (kadhdhaba) and disobedience (ʿaṣā) is significant, for it describes the two dimensions of Pharaoh's rebellion. His denial was the rejection of the truth in his heart and on his tongue, while his disobedience was the refusal to act upon that truth in his life. Together they represent the complete rejection of the divine message — a rejection not of the intellect, which recognised the truth, but of the will, which refused to yield. This is the archetype of all rejection, repeated in every age by those who see the truth but choose their own desires and their own status over it.

The verse teaches the believer that denial is often not a matter of evidence but of submission. Pharaoh had all the evidence he could ask for, yet he denied because he would not bow. The believer is reminded that faith is not merely the recognition of truth but the submission of the will to it, and that the greatest barrier to faith is often the arrogance that refuses to surrender. The verse also serves as a warning, for Pharaoh's denial and disobedience led to his destruction, and the same fate awaits all who follow his example.

=22=

This verse describes the next stage of Pharaoh's rebellion: "then turned his back, striving ˹against the truth˺." Having denied and disobeyed, Pharaoh did not simply remain passive in his rejection; he actively turned his back on the truth and devoted himself to opposing it. The word "striving" (saʿā) indicates earnest, determined effort, and it reveals that Pharaoh threw himself fully into the campaign against Moses and his message. His rejection was not a momentary lapse but a committed, active rebellion, pursued with all the energy and resources at his disposal.

The image of "turning his back" is significant, for it describes the deliberate turning away from the truth after it has been made clear. Pharaoh did not face the truth and wrestle with it; he turned his back on it, refusing even to look at it, and then busied himself with opposition to it. This is the pattern of the arrogant, who, when they cannot refute the truth, turn away from it and devote themselves to fighting it, hoping that by their efforts they can suppress what they cannot answer.

The verse teaches the believer about the nature of opposition to the truth. Those who reject the truth rarely do so passively; more often, they become active opponents, striving to silence it, to discredit it, and to prevent others from hearing it. The believer is reminded that this opposition is a sign of the truth's power, for falsehood does not need to be opposed with such energy; only the truth, which threatens the foundations of arrogance and falsehood, provokes such a response. The believer is encouraged to remain steadfast in the face of active opposition, trusting that the truth will ultimately prevail, as it did over Pharaoh.

=23=

This verse describes Pharaoh's next move: "Then he summoned ˹his people˺ and called out." Having turned his back on the truth and resolved to oppose it, Pharaoh gathered his people — his ministers, his officials, and the masses — and addressed them in a grand public display. The word "summoned" (ḥashara) conveys the idea of gathering together, while "called out" (nādā) indicates a loud, public proclamation. Pharaoh was not content to reject Moses privately; he sought to rally his entire kingdom against him, turning his rejection into a public spectacle designed to intimidate and impress.

The public nature of Pharaoh's opposition is significant, for it reveals the tactics of tyranny. A tyrant who feels threatened by the truth does not respond with quiet reflection but with a public show of force, seeking to drown out the truth with noise and to overwhelm the solitary voice of the messenger with the collective weight of the masses. Pharaoh's summoning of his people was an act of political theatre, designed to demonstrate his power and to isolate Moses and his message.

The verse teaches the believer about the nature of the struggle between truth and falsehood. The truth often begins with a single voice, while falsehood commands the crowds and the institutions of power. Yet the outcome is never determined by numbers, for the truth of Moses, armed with the word of Allah, ultimately prevailed over the entire apparatus of Pharaoh's state. The believer is reminded that the power of the truth does not lie in numbers but in its divine source, and that the public shows of falsehood are no match for the quiet certainty of faith.

=24=

This verse records the climax of Pharaoh's arrogance: "saying, 'I am your lord, the most high!'" In his public address, Pharaoh declared himself to be the supreme lord of his people, claiming for himself the very attribute that belongs to Allah alone — the Most High. This was the fullest expression of his transgression, the moment when his arrogance reached its peak and he openly usurped the position of the Creator. The claim "I am your lord, the most high" is the ultimate act of rebellion, for it is not merely the rejection of a messenger but the denial of Allah Himself.

The claim of Pharaoh is the archetype of all human arrogance, the attempt of a created being to elevate itself to the position of its Creator. Every form of tyranny, every act of oppression, and every claim of absolute authority is a distant echo of this original rebellion. Pharaoh's declaration reveals what lies at the heart of all falsehood: the refusal to acknowledge any power above oneself, and the desire to make oneself the object of worship and obedience in place of Allah.

The verse teaches the believer about the gravity of arrogance and the reality of Pharaoh's crime. The claim to divinity is the greatest of all transgressions, and it is the sin for which Pharaoh is remembered as the archetype of tyranny. The believer is reminded that true greatness belongs to Allah alone, and that the one who claims it for themselves has set themselves against the Lord of the worlds. The verse also serves as a warning against the arrogance that exists, in lesser forms, in every human heart — the pride that seeks to elevate the self above others and above the authority of Allah.

=25=

This verse describes the fate of Pharaoh: "So Allah overtook him, making him an example in this life and the next." Having declared himself the most high lord, Pharaoh was seized by the punishment of Allah, who overtook him with a punishment that encompassed both this world and the Hereafter. In this world, he was drowned in the sea along with his army, and in the Hereafter, he faces the eternal punishment of the Fire. His claim to divinity was answered by the One Whose power he had denied, and his arrogance was met with a humiliation that reversed his boast.

The word "overtook" (akhadha) conveys the sense of being seized and overtaken by an inescapable force, while "making him an example" (nakāl) refers to the exemplary punishment that serves as a warning to others. Pharaoh's fate was not merely his own destruction but a lesson for all of humanity, a sign that the claims of the arrogant will always be answered by the power of Allah. The phrase "in this life and the next" emphasises the completeness of his punishment, which was not deferred to the Hereafter but began in this world with the drowning and continues in the Hereafter with the Fire.

The verse teaches the believer about the certainty of divine justice. The tyrant who appears invincible in this world is, in reality, utterly vulnerable before the power of Allah, and his apparent success is only a prelude to his downfall. The believer is reminded that the arrogance of the powerful will always be answered, that the truth will always be vindicated, and that the fate of Pharaoh is a warning to all who follow in his path. The verse is a source of hope for the oppressed and a warning to the oppressor.

=26=

This verse draws the lesson from the story of Pharaoh: "Surely in this is a lesson for whoever stands in awe of ˹Allah˺." The story of Pharaoh — his arrogance, his claim to divinity, and his destruction — is described as a lesson (ʿibrah) for those who fear Allah. The word ʿibrah refers to a lesson that is passed across, from one thing to another, conveying the idea that the fate of Pharaoh is a warning that crosses over from history into the present, instructing those who reflect upon it. The story is not merely an account of the past but a living lesson for the present and the future.

The verse specifies that this lesson is for "whoever stands in awe of ˹Allah˺" — the one who fears Allah and is conscious of His power. It is this fear of Allah that opens the heart to receive the lesson, for the one who stands in awe of their Lord will see in the fate of Pharaoh a warning for themselves, while the one whose heart is hardened will dismiss it as mere history. The fear of Allah is thus presented as the key that unlocks the wisdom of the Qur'an's stories, transforming them from narratives into guidance.

The verse teaches the believer the purpose of the Qur'an's historical accounts. They are not told for entertainment but for instruction, and their lessons are available to all who approach them with the fear of Allah. The believer is encouraged to reflect upon the story of Pharaoh, to see in it the fate of arrogance, and to cultivate in their own heart the awe of Allah that makes such reflection fruitful. The story of Moses and Pharaoh thus becomes, for the believer, a personal warning and a call to humility and submission.

=27=

This verse transitions to a new argument for the resurrection: "Which is harder to create: you or the sky? He built it." Having recounted the story of Pharaoh, the sûrah now addresses the disbelievers who doubted the resurrection, challenging them to compare the creation of humanity with the creation of the sky. The question is rhetorical, for the answer is obvious: the sky, with its vastness and majesty, is far greater than the human being. Yet Allah built the sky, and so the One Who built the sky is certainly capable of recreating the human being, whose creation is comparatively trivial.

The verse establishes a principle that runs throughout the Qur'an: the resurrection is not difficult for Allah, for the One Who created the heavens and the earth in their immense grandeur can easily recreate the human being. The disbelievers' doubt about the resurrection stemmed from their failure to appreciate the power of Allah, who they saw at work every day in the vast creation of the sky yet failed to recognise. The sky itself, which they gazed upon constantly, was the evidence of the very power they doubted.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the greatness of creation as a proof of the power of Allah. The sky, the earth, and all that they contain are signs of the Creator's power, and they testify to His ability to bring the dead back to life. The believer is encouraged to contemplate the majesty of the universe, to recognise in it the hand of the All-Powerful, and to find in this recognition the certainty that the promise of the resurrection is true. The argument is simple yet profound: the One Who built the sky can certainly rebuild the human being.

=28=

This verse continues the description of the sky's creation: "raising it high and forming it flawlessly." Having mentioned that Allah built the sky, the verse now describes the manner of its construction: He raised it high above the earth, and He formed it flawlessly, without any defect or imperfection. The vast height of the sky, the precision of its order, and the perfection of its design all testify to the power and wisdom of the Creator, who raised this immense structure and formed it with flawless precision.

The description of the sky as "flawlessly" formed (sawwāhā) is significant, for it points to the perfection of the heavens, in which the stars, the planets, and the celestial bodies move in precise and orderly orbits, without collision or deviation. The sky is not a random collection of matter but a carefully constructed edifice, formed with a precision that the human mind struggles to comprehend. This perfection is a sign of the One Who formed it, and it is a testament to the power that will recreate humanity on the Day of Resurrection.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the perfection of creation as a proof of the Creator's power. The flawless formation of the sky is evidence that the universe is the work of a Master Designer, and the believer is encouraged to contemplate this perfection and to recognise in it the certainty of the resurrection. If Allah formed the sky with such flawless precision, then the recreation of the human being is a simple matter for Him, and the promise of the Hereafter is as certain as the sky above.

=29=

This verse continues the description of the sky: "He dimmed its night, and brought forth its daylight." Having described the raising and perfecting of the sky, the verse now describes the alternation of night and day that Allah established within it. He "dimmed its night" (aghṭasha), covering the sky in the darkness of night, and He "brought forth its daylight" (akhraja ḍuḥāhā), drawing out the light of day. The alternation of darkness and light is a sign of Allah's control over the sky He created, and it is a further testimony to His power.

The imagery of the verse is vivid and precise. The night is described as something that Allah "dimmed" — that is, He brought darkness over the sky, extinguishing the light of day. The daylight is described as something He "brought forth" — drawing it out from the darkness as a treasure is drawn from a hidden place. This language conveys the sense of deliberate, purposeful action, as though the night and day are brought forth by the direct command of the Creator, each in its appointed time and measure.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the alternation of night and day as a sign of Allah's power and wisdom. The rhythm of light and darkness, which governs all of life on earth, is not an accident but a deliberate design, established by the One Who created the sky and controls its affairs. The believer is encouraged to contemplate this rhythm, to recognise in it the hand of the Creator, and to find in it the certainty that the same power that brings forth the daylight from the night will bring forth the dead from their graves on the Day of Resurrection.

=30=

This verse extends the description to the earth: "As for the earth, He spread it out as well." Having described the creation of the sky, the verse now turns to the earth, which Allah spread out after the sky, making it a level, habitable surface for His creation. The word daḥāhā, translated as "spread it out," conveys the sense of expanding and levelling the earth, preparing it as a dwelling place for humanity and all living beings. The earth, with its mountains, valleys, plains, and seas, was formed by the power of Allah and made suitable for life.

The verse establishes the sequence of creation — the sky first, then the earth — and draws attention to the deliberate design of both. The earth was not left as a rough, uninhabitable mass but was spread out and prepared, its surface made level enough for people to walk upon, to build upon, and to cultivate. This preparation of the earth is a sign of Allah's care for His creation, for He did not merely create the earth but shaped it and made it habitable, providing for the needs of all who dwell upon it.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the earth as a sign of Allah's power and mercy. The ground beneath one's feet, which is so often taken for granted, is in fact a carefully prepared gift, spread out by the Creator for the benefit of His servants. The believer is encouraged to contemplate the earth, to recognise in its formation the hand of the All-Wise, and to respond with gratitude for the provision it offers. The verse also reinforces the argument for the resurrection, for the One Who spread out the earth can certainly gather humanity upon it for the final judgment.

=31=

This verse describes the further preparation of the earth: "bringing forth its water and pastures." After spreading out the earth, Allah brought forth from it its water and its pastures — the springs, rivers, and groundwater that sustain life, and the vegetation and pastures that provide nourishment for animals and humans. The water and the pastures are the two fundamental provisions of the earth, and their emergence from the earth is a sign of the Creator's power and mercy.

The verse highlights the interconnectedness of the earth's provisions. The water, brought forth from the depths of the earth, gives rise to the pastures, the vegetation that covers the earth and feeds the animals that feed humanity. This chain of provision, from water to pasture to life, is a testament to the wisdom of the Creator, who designed the earth to sustain His creation in a continuous, interconnected system. Nothing is arbitrary; each provision leads to the next in a chain of mercy that extends to every living being.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the provisions of the earth as signs of Allah's mercy. The water that quenches thirst and the pastures that feed the animals are not mere natural resources but deliberate gifts from the Creator, brought forth by His power for the benefit of His creation. The believer is encouraged to contemplate these provisions, to recognise in them the hand of Allah, and to respond with gratitude and stewardship. The verse also points to the resurrection, for the One Who brings forth water and pastures from the dead earth can certainly bring forth the dead from their graves.

=32=

This verse completes the description of the earth's preparation: "and setting the mountains firmly ˹upon it˺—" After bringing forth the water and pastures, Allah set the mountains firmly upon the earth, anchoring them in place. The mountains, with their immense mass and their deep roots, serve to stabilise the earth, and their placement is described as an act of deliberate design. The em-dash at the end of the verse indicates that the thought continues into the next verse, connecting the setting of the mountains to the purpose they serve.

The setting of the mountains is a sign of Allah's power and wisdom. The mountains, which appear so permanent and immovable, are in fact placed by the command of Allah, their roots extending deep into the earth to hold it firm. They are not random formations but deliberate anchors, set in place by the Creator to stabilise the surface of the earth and to make it habitable. The verse thus presents the mountains as another element in the careful design of the earth, each part serving a purpose in the greater whole.

The verse teaches the believer to reflect upon the mountains as signs of Allah's power. The peaks that rise above the earth, so solid and enduring, are themselves creations, set in place by the One Who has power over all things. The believer is encouraged to contemplate the mountains, to recognise in their firmness the power of the Creator, and to find in this recognition a deepening of faith and certainty. The verse also reinforces the theme of the sûrah, for the One Who set the mountains firmly can certainly raise the dead, and the permanence of the mountains is itself a reminder of the permanence of the Hereafter.

=33=

This verse completes the thought begun in the previous verse: "all as ˹a means of˺ sustenance for you and your animals." The water, the pastures, and the firmly-set mountains — all of these were created by Allah as a provision for humanity and for the animals that serve them. The verse thus ties together the entire description of the earth's preparation, revealing its ultimate purpose: all of it was designed as sustenance, a provision from the Creator for the benefit of His creation. The earth is not an end in itself but a means, a gift prepared by Allah to sustain the life He has placed upon it.

The verse highlights the comprehensiveness of Allah's provision. The water quenches thirst, the pastures feed the animals, the mountains stabilise the earth and store the water, and all of it together sustains both humanity and the animals that share the earth with them. This provision is not limited to one group or one region but extends to all of creation, encompassing every living being in the web of sustenance that Allah has woven. The mention of "your animals" (anʿām) specifically draws attention to the livestock that serve humanity, whose provision is also arranged by Allah.

The verse teaches the believer to recognise the earth and its resources as gifts from Allah, provided for the sustenance of His creation. The believer is encouraged to reflect upon this provision, to respond with gratitude, and to use the resources of the earth responsibly, as a trust from Allah. The verse also reinforces the argument for the resurrection, for the One Who provided the earth as sustenance for this life has certainly prepared a greater provision for the life to come, and the believer is urged to strive for that provision through faith and obedience.

=34=

This verse introduces the final theme of the sûrah: "But, when the Supreme Disaster comes to pass—" The verse turns from the signs of creation to the reality of the Hereafter, warning of "the Supreme Disaster" (aṭ-ṭāmmah al-kubrā), the overwhelming catastrophe that will engulf all of creation on the Day of Judgment. The word ṭāmmah conveys the sense of something that overwhelms and overcomes everything, and the description "Supreme" (kubrā) indicates that this disaster will be the greatest of all, surpassing every catastrophe that has ever occurred in this world.

The em-dash at the end of the verse indicates that the thought continues into the next verse, which will describe what this Supreme Disaster is. The verse thus creates a moment of suspense, drawing the listener's attention to the magnitude of what is coming before revealing its details. The Supreme Disaster is the Day of Judgment, the day when the world as we know it will come to an end and humanity will be brought to account, and the sûrah now turns to describe this day in vivid detail.

The verse teaches the believer to live with the awareness of the Hereafter. The Supreme Disaster is not a distant myth but a certain reality, appointed by Allah and approaching with every passing moment. The believer is encouraged to reflect upon this reality, to prepare for it through faith and good deeds, and to keep it ever before their eyes as the ultimate horizon of their life. The verse serves as a powerful reminder that the pleasures and pursuits of this world are fleeting, and that the only thing that truly matters is one's preparation for the day when the Supreme Disaster will come to pass.

=35=

This verse describes the Supreme Disaster: "the Day every person will remember all ˹their˺ striving." On that Day, when the Supreme Disaster comes to pass, every person will remember all that they strived for in this world — every deed, every effort, every ambition, and every pursuit. The word "striving" (saʿā) encompasses the totality of a person's efforts in life, both good and bad, and the verse declares that all of it will be brought to remembrance on that Day, when the veil of heedlessness is lifted and the reality of one's life is laid bare.

The word "remember" is significant, for it indicates that on that Day, every person will recall their striving with a clarity that was absent in this world. In this life, people are often heedless of the true nature of their deeds, distracted by the concerns of the moment and the illusions of the world. But on the Day of Judgment, this heedlessness will be lifted, and every person will remember exactly what they strove for and why, seeing their life in its true light. This remembrance is itself a form of reckoning, for it confronts the soul with the reality it had forgotten.

The verse teaches the believer to live with the awareness that their striving will be remembered. Every effort, every choice, and every deed is being recorded, and the day will come when all of it will be recalled. The believer is encouraged to strive for that which will benefit them on that Day — the deeds of faith, righteousness, and devotion — and to avoid the striving that leads to regret. The verse is a call to make one's striving in this world a striving for the Hereafter, so that the remembrance of it on that Day may be a source of joy rather than sorrow.

=36=

This verse continues the description of the Day: "and the Hellfire will be displayed for all to see—" On the Day of Judgment, the Hellfire will be brought forth and displayed openly, visible to all who are gathered. The word "displayed" (burrizat) conveys the sense of being brought out into the open, exposed and revealed for all to see. The Hellfire, which had been hidden from human sight during this world, will be made visible on that Day, and its terrifying reality will be undeniable to all who witness it.

The display of the Hellfire serves multiple purposes. For the disbelievers, it is a source of terror, for they will see with their own eyes the punishment they had denied and mocked, and the reality of their fate will be made manifest before them. For the believers, it is a source of gratitude and reassurance, for they will see the punishment from which Allah has saved them, and their hearts will overflow with thankfulness for His mercy. The display of the Hellfire is thus a moment of profound reckoning for all of humanity, a visual confirmation of the reality of the Hereafter.

The em-dash at the end of the verse indicates that the thought continues into the next verse. The verse teaches the believer to take the reality of the Hellfire seriously, for it is not a metaphor or a myth but a real punishment that will be displayed openly on the Day of Judgment. The believer is encouraged to seek Allah's protection from the Fire, to live in a way that keeps them far from it, and to be grateful for the guidance that shows the way to safety. The verse is a reminder that the Hereafter is not hidden forever but will one day be revealed for all to see.

=37=

This verse begins the division of humanity on the Day of Judgment: "then as for those who transgressed," After the display of the Hellfire, the fate of each person will be determined according to their deeds, and the verse begins with those who transgressed — the ones who exceeded all bounds, rebelled against Allah, and followed their desires without restraint. The word ṭaghā conveys the sense of overflowing beyond limits, of arrogance so complete that it acknowledges no boundary and no authority above itself.

The transgression described here is the opposite of the fear of Allah described earlier in the sûrah. The one who fears Allah lives within the limits He has set, while the one who transgresses breaks through every limit, following their own desires as their guide and their own will as their law. This transgression takes many forms — arrogance, oppression, denial of the truth, and immersion in sin — but its essence is always the same: the refusal to submit to the authority of Allah and the insistence on living according to one's own whims.

The verse teaches the believer about the reality of transgression and its consequences. The transgression described here is not a minor failing but a fundamental orientation of the heart, a choice to live without regard for the limits of Allah. The believer is called to guard against this orientation, to live within the boundaries that Allah has set, and to cultivate the fear of Allah that keeps the soul from overflowing into transgression. The verse sets the stage for the division that follows, contrasting the fate of the transgressors with the fate of those who feared their Lord.

=38=

This verse continues the description of the transgressors: "and preferred the ˹fleeting˺ life of this world," The transgressors are further described as those who preferred the life of this world — the fleeting, temporary life of comfort and pleasure — over the eternal life of the Hereafter. Their transgression was rooted in this preference, for they chose the immediate gratification of this world over the lasting reward of the next, trading the eternal for the temporary and the real for the illusory.

The description of the worldly life as "fleeting" is significant, for it captures the essential nature of this world: it passes away quickly, and its pleasures are temporary and ultimately unsatisfying. The word translated as "fleeting" (dunyā) is itself related to the idea of nearness and lowness, indicating that this world is the lower, lesser life compared to the Hereafter. The one who prefers this fleeting life has made a catastrophic choice, exchanging that which endures for that which perishes.

The verse teaches the believer about the importance of priorities. The choice between this world and the Hereafter is the fundamental choice of every life, and the believer is called to prefer the Hereafter, to live in this world as a traveller passing through, and to invest in that which endures rather than that which fades. The verse does not condemn the lawful enjoyment of this world but condemns the preference for it over the Hereafter, the choice to make this fleeting life the ultimate goal. The believer is encouraged to keep their priorities clear, to seek the Hereafter first, and to use this world only as a means to that end.

=39=

This verse declares the fate of the transgressors: "the Hellfire will certainly be ˹their˺ home." For those who transgressed and preferred the fleeting life of this world, the Hellfire will be their home — their final destination and their eternal dwelling. The word "home" (ma'wā) is deeply ironic, for a home is normally a place of comfort, safety, and belonging, yet for the transgressors, their "home" will be the blazing Fire, a place of torment and despair from which there is no escape.

The certainty of this declaration — "will certainly be" — leaves no room for doubt. The fate of the transgressors is not a matter of speculation but of divine decree, determined by their own choices in this world. The Hellfire is not an arbitrary punishment but the natural consequence of a life lived in transgression and preference for the fleeting world, and it is described as their "home" because they will dwell in it forever, with no possibility of leaving. The word "home" also conveys the sense of belonging, as though the transgressors, by their own choice, have made themselves fit only for this dwelling.

The verse teaches the believer about the finality of the Hereafter. The choice made in this world determines the home of the next, and there is no change or reversal after death. The believer is urged to consider carefully which home they are preparing for themselves, and to live in a way that makes their eternal home the Paradise of their Lord rather than the Fire. The verse is a sobering warning, but it is also an invitation, for the door of repentance remains open, and the one who turns back from transgression can still change their final destination.

=40=

This verse presents the contrasting fate of the righteous: "And as for those who were in awe of standing before their Lord and restrained themselves from ˹evil˺ desires," The verse describes the second group — those who feared the moment of standing before their Lord for judgment, and who, as a result of this fear, restrained themselves from following their evil desires. Their fear of Allah was not a vague sentiment but a practical force in their lives, holding them back from sin and guiding them towards righteousness.

The verse identifies two defining qualities of the righteous. The first is "awe of standing before their Lord" — the awareness that they will one day stand before Allah for judgment, and the fear that this awareness produces. The second is "restraining themselves from evil desires" — the discipline of the soul, by which they held back their desires from what Allah has forbidden, refusing to let their whims lead them into transgression. These two qualities — the fear of Allah and the control of desire — are the foundation of the righteous life, and they are the opposite of the transgression described in the preceding verses.

The verse teaches the believer about the essence of righteousness. The righteous person is not one who has no desires, but one who controls their desires through the fear of Allah, restraining the soul from that which displeases Him and directing it towards that which pleases Him. The believer is called to cultivate this fear of Allah and this discipline of the soul, to live with the awareness of the Day of Judgment, and to hold back their desires from every forbidden path. The verse sets up the conclusion that follows, in which the reward of these righteous ones will be declared, contrasting their fate with that of the transgressors.
