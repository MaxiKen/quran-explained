=1=

The surah opens with the separate letters Alif, Lām, Mīm, and Rā’. These disconnected letters appear at the beginning of a number of Quranic chapters, and while their precise meaning is known fully only to Allah, they serve to capture attention and to underscore the miraculous nature of the Book that follows. They are drawn from the very alphabet of the Arabs, yet no one can produce anything comparable to the revelation that comes after them. This serves as an immediate challenge to those who doubt.

The phrase “These are the verses of the Book” identifies the revelations that follow as the clear signs of the Quran, a scripture full of guidance and wisdom. It is not an ordinary composition but the speech of the Almighty. Then the address turns directly to the Prophet, peace be upon him, assuring him that what has been sent down to him from his Lord is the truth. The source is emphasized: it is “from your Lord,” the One who creates, sustains, and commands.

Therefore the message carries absolute certainty, untouched by falsehood. Yet despite this divine guarantee, the verse ends with a painful reality: “but most people do not believe.

” This is not a statement about all people, but about the predominant human response. Many are held back by arrogance, heedlessness, or blind adherence to tradition from accepting what their very hearts recognize as true. The disjunction between the certainty of revelation and the widespread rejection of it becomes one of the central themes of the surah.

The opening thereby prepares the listener for a series of arguments, signs, and parables that will address the root causes of disbelief, calling human beings to reflect on the world around them and on the message itself, so that they might move from doubt to conviction.

=2=

This verse directs our gaze upward to the overwhelming evidence of Allah’s power and wisdom. It declares that it is Allah Who raised the heavens high above the earth, and He did so without pillars that are visible to the eye. The words “as you can see” have been understood in two complementary ways. Some explain that there truly are no pillars at all, meaning the sky is held aloft purely by the command and might of the Creator without any physical support. Others note that the phrase means “as you can see, there are no visible pillars,” implying that there may be supports beyond human perception, but the point is that the heavens stand by Allah’s power, not by anything we can observe. After mentioning the raising of the heavens, the verse tells us that He then established Himself on the Throne in a manner that befits His majesty, a station of supreme authority over creation. He has also subjected the sun and the moon, making them run their courses with perfect consistency, each moving for an appointed term until the Day of Judgement. He conducts the entire affair of the universe: every decree, every event, every movement is under His management. All of these cosmic signs are made clear so that human beings may reach certainty about the meeting with their Lord. The orderly display of the heavens and the relentless precision of the celestial bodies are not random phenomena; they are deliberate signs pointing to the reality of resurrection and accountability. When a person reflects that the One who set these vast systems in motion with such effortless control is fully capable of bringing the dead back to life, the heart begins to accept what the prophets have taught about the Hereafter.

=3=

Turning from the sky to the earth, the verse continues the argument from creation. It is Allah Who spread out the earth, making it habitable and expansive so that living creatures could dwell upon it. Had it been left rugged and entirely impassable, life would not flourish. But by His mercy, He stretched it and prepared it as a resting place. Within that broad expanse, He placed firm mountains that stabilize the earth’s crust, preventing it from shaking violently with its inhabitants. This anchoring function of mountains is a mercy that we often take for granted. Alongside the mountains, He made rivers flow, carving pathways of fresh water that sustain life across vast regions. The water cycle and river systems are among the most tangible signs of a Provider who cares for His creation. Then the verse mentions that He created fruits of every kind in pairs.

The word “pairs” here points to the male and female elements required for reproduction, but it also speaks of the duality and variety that characterize the plant world—sweet and sour, different colours, shapes, and flavours all emerging from the same soil. After this, the verse highlights the alternation of day and night: He covers the day with night, bringing rest after activity, darkness after light. All of these phenomena contain clear signs, but the verse specifies that they benefit only “those who reflect. ” Reflection is not a casual glance; it is the deep, sustained thought that moves from the created thing to the Creator, recognizing His wisdom, power, and deliberate design. Such reflection naturally leads the heart toward faith and gratitude.

=4=

The Quran now draws our attention to an even closer and more intimate sign: the variety on the earth itself. Within one piece of land, you find neighbouring tracts that are side by side yet completely different in their soil composition, fertility, and the types of plants they produce. One piece of ground may be rich and productive, while the plot right next to it is barren and dry, despite receiving the same rain and the same sunlight. The verse mentions gardens of grapevines, various crops, and palm trees. Some of these palm trees stem from a single root system and share a common trunk base, while others stand alone as separate trees. All of them are irrigated with the same water, yet the fruits they bear differ remarkably in taste, colour, aroma, and texture. One water source produces a grape that is intensely sweet and another that is tart; a date that is honey-like and another that is firm and mildly sweet. This diversity within unity is a profound sign. It demonstrates that the outcome does not depend merely on material inputs, because the water is one and the earth is adjacent. The decisive factor is the will and creative command of Allah, who gives each thing its distinct character and quality.

This is why the verse concludes that these are signs “for those who understand.” Understanding here is more than sensory perception; it is the intellectual and spiritual insight that perceives the hand of the Maker in the variety and harmony of the natural world, and that draws from it the lesson that the One who can distinguish between plants with the same water can certainly distinguish between the obedient and the rebellious, and will bring them to separate ends.

=5=

After the powerful display of signs in the natural world, the surah addresses the Prophet directly about a truly astonishing attitude. The opening clause is emphatic: if anything should cause amazement, it is not the signs of creation themselves but the question posed by the disbelievers. They ask, with mocking skepticism, whether when they have become dust and their bodies have completely decayed, they will really be brought back as a new creation. This question reveals a failure to grasp the very point of the earlier verses, for the One who brought them into existence from nothing the first time and who upholds the vast heavens and the intricate earth is certainly able to bring them back again. The verse does not leave their state ambiguous; it delivers three decisive statements about them. First, it is they who have disbelieved in their Lord. Their question is not an innocent inquiry but a symptom of a deeper rejection of His power and their accountability. Second, it is they who will have shackles around their necks.

This is a vivid description of the punishment that awaits them, with chains binding them in humiliation, a fitting recompense for their refusal to be bound by the truth. Third, it is they who will be the residents of the Fire, and they will remain there forever. The triple repetition of “it is they” hammers home the certainty and the justice of this outcome, connecting their present disbelief to their future state. The verse serves as both a warning and a reassurance: a warning to those who mock the resurrection and a reassurance to the Prophet that the disbelief he encounters is truly irrational given the overwhelming evidence.

=6=

The previous verse exposed the disbelief that lies behind the mockery of resurrection. Here we see another dimension of that mockery. The disbelievers do not merely doubt the hereafter; they recklessly challenge it by asking the Prophet to bring on the punishment immediately. Instead of seeking the mercy and grace that Allah offers, they demand the torment be hastened, as if daring Allah to act.

This is an attitude of insolence born of the illusion that the delay in punishment is a sign of weakness or that the threat is empty. The verse responds by reminding them that there have already been many exemplary torments before them. The destroyed nations of the past—the people of Noah, Hūd, Sālih, and others—are evidence that divine punishment is real and can descend when least expected.

Yet even in the face of such provocation, the verse emphasizes two balancing attributes of the Lord. First, He is full of forgiveness for people despite their wrongdoing. His mercy is vast and His readiness to forgive is not cancelled by human sin, so long as the person turns back to Him. Second, He is truly severe in punishment. This severity is not arbitrary; it is the just response to persistent rebellion and mockery of His signs. The combination of these two attributes gives the full picture: no one should mistake Allah’s patience for powerlessness, and no one should despair of His mercy after having wronged themselves. The verse is both a warning to the mockers and an invitation to them to change their stance before the door of forgiveness closes and the torment they so foolishly demand actually arrives.

=7=

Even though the previous verses have laid out the cosmic and earthly signs in abundance, the disbelievers persist in their demands. They ask why no specific miraculous sign is sent down to the Prophet from his Lord, much as earlier nations asked their prophets for visible miracles tailored to their desires. Often this demand is not a genuine search for truth but a tactic of evasion, a way of setting conditions that they imagine will justify their continued disbelief. The verse instructs the Prophet about the nature of his mission: he is only a warner. His role is not to produce miracles on demand or to compel people to believe; it is to convey the message clearly and to warn of the consequences of turning away. The signs that matter most are already present in the revelation and in the creation around them. Then the verse makes a profound statement: “And every people had a guide.” This means that Allah, in His justice, never left any community without someone to guide them to the truth. The guide may be a prophet, a messenger, or the legacy of their teachings.

In the context of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, he is that guide for his people, and his message is the final, universal guidance. The response, therefore, turns the demand for a sign back upon itself: the ultimate sign is the guide and the guidance itself. The presence of a warner and a clear scripture is the most valuable sign any people can receive, and those who reject it will have no excuse when they stand before their Lord.

=8=

After clarifying the role of the Prophet, the surah moves to the perfect knowledge of Allah, which stands in stark contrast to human ignorance and the petty demands of the disbelievers. Allah knows what every female bears. This encompasses every pregnancy across all species, at every moment. He knows the exact moment of conception, the gender of the child, the number of offspring in the womb, and every detail of its development. Even before a mother knows she is pregnant, Allah’s knowledge has already encompassed the life forming within her.

The verse continues: He also knows what increases and what decreases in the wombs. This refers to the entire process of gestation: the growth of the foetus, the periods of development, whether the pregnancy goes to full term or ends early, and every cellular change that occurs within the protective darkness of the womb. No human knowledge, no matter how advanced, can ever fully comprehend or monitor these processes with the exhaustive, simultaneous, and effortless knowledge that belongs to the Creator. Then the verse makes a crucial connection: “And with Him everything is determined with precision.” The intricate knowledge of the womb is not passive observation; it is tied to a precise measure and decree. Every stage, every characteristic, the full span of life, provision, and deeds—all of it is determined with exactness by the One whose knowledge covers all things. This serves as both a comfort to the believer and a warning to the denier. Nothing is random; all is under the meticulous control of the All-Knowing, so the reckoning and resurrection are entirely within His plan.

=9=

Building upon the mention of Allah’s precise knowledge of what is hidden in the wombs, this verse expands the description of His knowledge to encompass everything. He is the Knower of the seen and the unseen. The “seen” refers to the entire observable world, what human senses can perceive, and what is apparent to creatures. The “unseen” includes all that is hidden from creation: the secrets of hearts, the events of the future, the realities of the heavens and the unseen realms, the world of the angels, and the exact moment of the Last Hour. No barrier divides the seen and the unseen for Allah; to Him, all is equally present and fully known. Then two majestic names are given: the All-Great and the Most Exalted. His greatness indicates the perfection of His attributes and the immensity of His being, far beyond anything that human minds can comprehend or language can capture.

The name “Most Exalted” emphasizes His transcendence above all deficiency, similarity to creation, and the limits that constrain everything else. Together these names paint a picture of a Lord whose knowledge is absolute and whose greatness is unmatched, directly countering the false assumptions of those who worship idols that have no knowledge, no power, and no greatness. The believer who absorbs these two verses about Allah’s knowledge will find their heart filled with awe and also a deep sense of accountability. They know that nothing they hide can escape His sight and that the One before whom they stand is unimaginably great, so their worship and their conduct must reflect that realization.

=10=

The all-encompassing knowledge of Allah is now made intensely personal. To Him, it is exactly the same whether a person speaks in secret or aloud, and whether someone conceals themselves in the darkness of night or walks openly in the light of day. Human beings rely on cover: we think that whispered words, closed doors, and the cover of darkness can hide our actions and intentions from others. But this verse demolishes that illusion. The one who speaks secretly, believing that his words are hidden, is as exposed before Allah as the one who shouts from the rooftops. Similarly, the one who moves under the veil of night is just as visible to Allah as the one who strides out under the noonday sun. The point is not merely that Allah sees and hears, but that the categories of “hidden” and “apparent” do not exist for Him. This has profound implications for a person’s spiritual state. It means that sincerity cannot be faked, because Allah knows the hidden intentions behind every public act. It means that secret sins are no safer than open sins, because the One who matters most witnesses them fully. It also means that private acts of devotion and secret charity are fully recorded and rewarded, because the Lord who sees in the dark sees them with the same clarity as what is done before crowds. This verse, therefore, calls the believer to cultivate a constant awareness that their entire life is lived in the open before Allah. It is both a deterrent from wrongdoing and a tremendous encouragement to do good when no one else is watching.

=11=

From Allah’s perfect knowledge, the surah moves to His protection and His laws governing human societies. Every individual has a succession of angels appointed to them, angels who come in shifts, positioned before and behind. They guard and protect the person by Allah’s command, watching over them throughout the day and night, recording deeds and shielding them from many unseen harms until the decreed moment of death arrives. This assurance of angelic presence reminds the believer that they are never alone, and it also reinforces the reality that nothing escapes divine notice. Then the verse articulates a universal principle of tremendous importance. Allah would never change the condition of a people, meaning He would never strip away the blessings, security, honour, and well-being they enjoy, until they change what is within themselves. The change that triggers the loss of divine favour is primarily a change in faith, righteousness, and gratitude. When a people replace obedience with rebellion, justice with oppression, and gratitude with ingratitude, they initiate the process of their own decline.

This principle is a warning and a call to self-reflection at both the individual and the communal level. However, the verse does not end there. It balances this with a reminder of divine sovereignty: if Allah wills to inflict harm on a people as a just punishment, nothing can repel it, and they will find no protector besides Him. Human effort cannot reverse a divine decree when the appointed time of recompense arrives. The verse thus combines hope, responsibility, and reverent fear in a single, tightly woven statement.

=12=

After the foundational truths about knowledge and divine decree, the surah returns to physical signs that people witness directly. Allah is the One who shows humanity the lightning. The flash of lightning across the sky is a powerful and dramatic occurrence, and the verse draws attention to the psychological effect it produces: it inspires both hope and fear. On one hand, lightning often heralds the coming of rain, which revives the earth, fills the wells, and brings sustenance. The farmer, the traveller, and all living beings look to the sky with hope when they see lightning, expecting the mercy of life-giving water. On the other hand, lightning carries an inherent terror. Its sudden, brilliant flash can be blinding, and thunderbolts can strike and destroy. This dual response of hope and fear perfectly mirrors the believer’s attitude toward Allah. The believer lives between hope in His mercy and fear of His punishment, between love and awe. The lightning, then, becomes a natural parable for the human relationship with the divine.

The verse also mentions that Allah produces the heavy clouds, laden with water. The word “heavy” captures the weight of the clouds that are driven by the winds, carrying tons of water suspended in the sky by a command that defies gravity. All of this points to a power that can bring life from a dead land, and by clear extension, can bring life to the dead on the Day of Resurrection. The thunder and lightning, which give this surah its name, are not random meteorological events but deliberate signs carrying a spiritual message for those who are receptive.

=13=

The surah now reveals a deeper layer of meaning behind the thunder. The thunder is not merely a sound; it glorifies Allah with His praise. According to a narration from Ibn ‘Abbās and others, thunder is an angel tasked with driving the clouds, and the sound we hear is the voice of that angel glorifying the Lord. Another interpretation holds that the sound itself is a form of tasbīh, a declaration of Allah’s transcendence that all creation utters, even if we do not understand its language. Alongside the thunder, the angels themselves glorify Allah in awe of Him. Their glorification is not mechanical but filled with profound reverence and fear of His majesty.

This image elevates the storm from a mere natural phenomenon to a symphony of worship that engulfs the heavens. Yet in the midst of this universal adoration, Allah sends thunderbolts, striking with them whomever He wills. These bolts are instruments of destruction for those who have earned His wrath, a demonstration that the same power that sustains can also annihilate.

The verse then expresses a note of astonishment: despite all these signs, the disbelievers still dispute about Allah. They argue about His oneness, His power, and His right to be worshipped alone, while the very thunder proclaims His glory and the angels tremble before Him.

The verse concludes with the affirmation that He is tremendous in might, a might that is irresistible in both its creative and its destructive aspects. This should cause every human being to reflect on their own stance before such a Lord.

=14=

The profound display of Allah’s power in the storm leads to a clear conclusion about worship. Calling upon Him alone is the truth, meaning that sincere supplication and devotion are exclusively His right, and He alone is capable of answering. Any prayer directed to other than Him is fundamentally false.

The verse then exposes the utter helplessness of the idols that the pagans invoke besides Allah. These idols, whether they are statues, celestial bodies, or imagined beings, cannot respond in any way whatsoever. They do not hear, they possess no power, and they cannot bring benefit or ward off harm even for themselves. To illustrate this futility, the Quran presents a remarkable parable. The situation of the idolater is like a person who stretches out his two hands toward water, calling out for the water to reach his mouth. The water is an inanimate object; it cannot hear the call, it cannot move by itself, and it will never rise up to meet the man’s lips no matter how desperately he begs. Similarly, the idols are utterly unresponsive, and the one who prays to them is reaching out toward something that can never deliver. The verse seals the image with the statement that the calls of the disbelievers are only in vain, or literally, in error and misguidance.

The word “only” emphasizes that there is no benefit, no partial answer, and no hope in such supplication. The lesson is as practical as it is theological: every human being must direct their deepest hopes, fears, and requests to the One who actually hears and has the power to respond.

=15=

While the previous verse established that calling upon Allah alone is the truth, this verse expands the picture to a cosmic scale. To Allah alone bow down in submission all those who are in the heavens and the earth. This prostration is not limited to voluntary acts of worship. The verse makes a profound distinction: they bow down willingly or unwillingly. The believers, the angels, and those who recognize the truth submit willingly out of love, awe, and understanding. The disbelievers and the rebellious, on the other hand, submit unwillingly in the sense that they are subject to the universal laws that Allah has decreed. They cannot escape birth, death, the pull of gravity, the functioning of their own bodies, or the decrees that govern their lives. In their very physical existence they are prostrating to His command even while their hearts and tongues refuse.

The verse then adds a beautiful and often overlooked detail: as do their shadows, morning and evening. The shadow of every object and every living being stretches out and contracts, moves and stills, in perfect compliance with the laws of light and the motion of the earth. Shadows cannot rebel; they shift from right to left, lengthen and shorten, solely by Allah’s order.

The mention of morning and evening ties this universal submission to the daily rhythm of life. Every sunrise and every sunset witnesses the bowing of shadows, a silent, continuous testimony that all of creation is inherently oriented toward its Creator. The verse calls humans to align their voluntary worship with the involuntary worship of their own shadows.

=16=

The surah now presents a powerful rational argument in the form of a dialogue. The Prophet is instructed to ask the idolaters a direct question: Who is the Lord of the heavens and the earth? They could not answer with the names of their idols because deep down they acknowledged that Allah was the Creator and Sustainer of the universe. So the Prophet is told to answer on their behalf: Say, “Allah! ” Having established this admission, the next question exposes the contradiction: Why then have they taken besides Him lords that cannot even benefit or protect their own selves? If the idols cannot manage their own affairs, how can they possibly manage the affairs of their worshippers? The logical flaw is laid bare.

The verse then uses two powerful analogies. The blind and the sighted are not equal, and darkness and light are not equal. One who worships an idol is like a blind person stumbling in darkness, while the believer walks with sight in the light. The two states cannot be equated. The argument then shifts to another angle: have these false partners ever created anything like Allah’s creation, causing confusion between the two? The obvious answer is no. They have created nothing, so why are they worshipped alongside the true Creator?

The verse concludes with a definitive declaration: Allah is the Creator of all things, and He is the One, the Supreme. His oneness is unmatched, and His power over all creation is absolute. This verse trains the mind to think clearly, to reject contradiction, and to see that pure monotheism is the only intellectually and spiritually coherent position.

=17=

This verse is one of the Quran’s most vivid parables, drawing from everyday phenomena to explain the nature of truth and falsehood. Allah sends down rain from the sky, and the water rushes into the valleys. Each valley flows according to its capacity: a wide, deep channel carries a great volume of water, while a narrow one carries less. The flowing water picks up foam and scum that rise to the surface and float along. This foam is temporary and insubstantial, soon to disappear.

The verse then draws a parallel to the process of smelting metal. When people heat ore in the fire to make ornaments or tools, a similar slag or dross rises to the top, an impurity that is skimmed off and discarded. The pure metal remains and is what actually benefits people. Allah explicitly states that this is how He compares truth to falsehood. The rain and the pure metal represent the truth: it is substantial, lasting, and beneficial. The foam and the slag represent falsehood: it appears on the surface, sometimes swelling impressively, but it is ultimately worthless and is quickly swept away or discarded. When truth and falsehood clash, falsehood may froth and bubble and momentarily catch the eye, but it has no solid foundation.

In the end, what benefits people remains on the earth—truth, justice, and righteousness survive, while falsehood vanishes. This parable comforts believers that the apparent strength of disbelief is merely foam on the surface of a mighty torrent of truth, and it assures them that persistence in the truth, even when it seems small, will yield lasting fruit.

=18=

The parable of truth and falsehood leads naturally to the consequences of accepting or rejecting the truth. The verse first describes the fate of those who respond to the call of their Lord. This response is not merely a verbal affirmation but a comprehensive obedience that follows from recognizing the truth depicted in the previous parable. For them is the finest reward, al-husnā, which encompasses all that is good and beautiful in the Hereafter, especially the vision and pleasure of Allah. Then the verse turns to the other side. Those who do not respond to Him, who turn away from the call of truth and follow the foam of falsehood, will face a terrifying reality. Even if they owned everything in the world twice over—all its treasures, wealth, and luxuries doubled—they would certainly offer it all as a ransom to save themselves from the punishment.

This image is repeated throughout the Quran to stress that no amount of worldly wealth can buy escape from divine justice. The verse intensifies the warning: they will face a strict judgment, one in which every deed is examined with precision and no injustice is done. Their final abode will be Hell, and the verse ends with a chilling exclamation: “What an evil place to rest!

” The word “rest” carries bitter irony, for Hell is a place of eternal torment without any true rest or peace. The verse thus completes the picture that the parable began: just as the foam is cast away, so the rejecters of truth will be cast into a punishment from which there is no ransom and no escape.

=19=

This verse sharpens the contrast between those who respond to the truth and those who do not by using the powerful metaphor of sight and blindness. It asks a rhetorical question: can the person who knows with certainty that what has been revealed to the Prophet from his Lord is the truth be compared to someone who is blind? The knowledge here is not mere information but a deep conviction that illuminates the heart and guides behaviour. The one who possesses this knowledge sees reality as it truly is: they recognize their Creator, understand the purpose of life, and walk a straight path. The blind one, by contrast, is spiritually blind. Despite having functioning eyes, they cannot perceive the light of revelation. They stumble through life, mistaking falsehood for truth and the transient for the eternal. The two are utterly unequal, just as a seeing person and a blind person cannot be equated in their ability to navigate the physical world.

The verse then makes a crucial observation: none will be truly mindful of this difference except people of reason, ulū al-albāb. These are people who possess a core of sound intellect, unclouded by desires and arrogance. They use their reason to reflect on the signs and to connect the evidence of creation with the message of revelation. The verse is both a compliment to those who believe and an implicit call to those who hesitate. It urges every person to examine whether they are living with true sight or with a blindness that will lead them to the abyss described in the preceding verses.

The following verses will now describe the qualities of these people of reason in beautiful detail.

=20=

Having mentioned the people of reason who take heed, the surah begins to describe them so that no one is left merely with an abstract concept. The first quality of these ulū al-albāb is that they honour Allah’s covenant and they never break the pledge. The covenant of Allah mentioned here encompasses the primordial covenant taken from the souls of all human beings when they testified that Allah is their Lord, as well as the covenant established through the sending of prophets and scriptures, which requires belief, obedience, and the worship of Allah alone without partners. To honour the covenant means to remain faithful to that original testimony and to the demands of the revealed guidance.

The verse emphasizes the totality of their loyalty: they never break the pledge. The word “never” indicates a consistent pattern of life, not a sporadic or selective faithfulness. They do not break their promise to Allah when tested, nor do they abandon their religious duties when circumstances become difficult. This quality is foundational because all other virtues flow from a heart that is true to its covenant with its Creator. In a world where human agreements are often broken and commitments are treated lightly, these believers stand out as people whose word, once given to Allah and to others, is their bond.

The mention of the covenant also reminds us that the relationship between Allah and the believer is not a vague sentiment but a defined, solemn pledge with responsibilities and, by Allah’s grace, a magnificent reward. The description of these people of reason will continue in the verses that follow, painting a complete portrait of the ideal believer.
