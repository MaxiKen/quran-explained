=21=

The description of the people of reason continues from the previous verse, where they were characterised as those who honour Allah’s covenant without breaking it. Now we learn that they maintain whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained. This refers first and foremost to the ties of kinship, the bonds of blood and family that Allah has commanded to be upheld. The Prophet, peace be upon him, warned that severing these ties leads to grave consequences, and he promised that whoever wishes to have their provision expanded and their life blessed should keep good relations with their relatives. But the ties go beyond family: they include all relationships of faith, community, and justice that Allah has commanded to be nurtured. The believers do not treat these bonds casually; they actively guard and strengthen them. Alongside this outward action, they possess an inward state: they stand in awe of their Lord.

The Arabic word used here, khashyah, is a profound fear rooted in knowledge of Allah’s majesty and one’s own shortcomings. It is not a terror that makes one despair, but a reverential awe that keeps the heart humble and the limbs obedient.

Furthermore, they fear a strict judgment, an evil reckoning. Even though they are righteous, they do not feel secure from the scrutiny of the Day of Resurrection. They live with a balanced consciousness, hoping in Allah’s mercy yet trembling at the thought of answering for their deeds. This internal fear fuels their external obedience and ensures that their maintenance of ties is not merely social formality but an act of sincere devotion.

Thus the verse ties together social responsibility with deep spiritual awareness, showing that true piety integrates inner devotion with outward conduct, all flowing from a heart that never feels safe from accountability.

=22=

The portrait of the people of reason grows richer with each quality mentioned. Here they are described as those who endure patiently, and their patience is not the grudging tolerance of one who has no choice; it is a patience that actively seeks the face of their Lord and His pleasure. This transforms every trial, every act of self-restraint, and every perseverance in obedience into a form of worship laden with hope. Then the verse mentions the central pillar of their daily lives: they establish the prayer. The prayer is not merely performed occasionally but established with consistency, mindfulness, and in its proper manner, becoming the fixed point around which the rest of life revolves. Alongside prayer, they donate from what Allah has provided for them, spending their wealth in charity both secretly and openly.

The mention of both modes indicates a wisdom that knows when discretion is better to protect sincerity and when public giving can encourage others. Then comes a quality that tests the limits of human nature: they respond to evil with good. When they are wronged, their response is not vengeance but forgiveness, kindness, and even active goodness toward the one who harmed them.

This is one of the highest stations of character, and it requires immense spiritual strength. The Prophet exemplified this when he forgave the people of Ta’if who had pelted him with stones, praying for their guidance rather than their destruction. For all of these, the verse declares a magnificent promise: it is they who will have the ultimate abode.

The word used, ‘uqbā ad-dār, signals the final, permanent home of peace and honour in the Hereafter, a reward that exceeds all imagination.

=23=

The ultimate abode is now named and described. It is the Gardens of Eternity, a place of perpetual residence where life does not end and joy does not fade. The believer will not enter this paradise alone. The verse contains a deeply comforting promise: they will be joined by the righteous among their parents, their spouses, and their descendants. This shows that faith and good deeds create bonds that transcend this world, reuniting families in a perfect state where no grief or separation can touch them. A person who strove for righteousness while yearning for their loved ones to be guided will see their hope fulfilled in the most beautiful way.

This promise was profoundly moving to the companions, for it meant that the separation caused by death was temporary and that the family unit, purified of all worldly strains, would be restored in eternal bliss. Moreover, the angels will enter upon them from every gate.

The image is one of honour and ceaseless welcome. The angels do not merely pass by; they come to the believers as honoured guests would be received, appearing from every entrance of their lofty dwellings. Their entry signals safety, divine acceptance, and the beginning of a dialogue of peace. The atmosphere is one of sublime serenity where the saved believer is surrounded by family, embraced by angelic beings, and immersed in the continuous manifestation of Allah’s mercy. This verse gives the believer a vivid picture to hold onto during the trials of this world, transforming the abstract hope of paradise into a warm, personal, and relational reality.

=24=

The angels’ greeting is recorded here, and every word of it carries meaning. “Peace be upon you” is the salutation of salam, a word that denotes safety, security, and complete well-being. When it comes from the angels on behalf of the Most Merciful, it is the final assurance that all danger, all fear, and all grief have passed forever. The reason given for this peace is “for your perseverance. ” This tells us that the patient endurance the believers displayed in the world—the sabr that the earlier verse mentioned—is the direct cause of this honour. Every difficulty they bore, every temptation they resisted, every act of obedience they persisted in, and every wrong they forgave has been noted and now repaid with eternal peace. The angels then exclaim, “How excellent is the ultimate abode!

” This is not a question but a declaration of admiration. They marvel at the splendour Allah has prepared for the believers. The exclamation also serves to heighten the joy of the inhabitants, confirming through the testimony of the angels themselves that this reward is truly magnificent. The verse, therefore, links the struggle of this life with the rest of the next in a single, seamless chain: perseverance now leads to peace then. For a believer facing hardship, this verse is a balm. It assures them that their pain is not unnoticed, their patience is not wasted, and their final home will be one about which even the angels, who have witnessed the grandeur of the heavens, will exclaim in wonder.

This is the tremendous outcome of being among the people of reason.

=25=

After the luminous description of the people of reason and their reward, the surah now presents the contrast. The balance of the Quran always places light against darkness so that the path is unmistakably clear. Those who violate Allah’s covenant after it has been affirmed are the direct opposite of the ones who honoured the covenant. They made a pledge of faith and obedience, either through their innate nature or through their verbal acceptance of the message, yet they broke it deliberately. This breach is not a moment of weakness but a settled pattern of treachery. They also break whatever ties Allah has ordered to be maintained, severing the bonds of kinship and community that the righteous work so hard to preserve. Their hearts are hard, and their relationships are marked by division, selfishness, and disregard for divine command.

Moreover, they spread corruption in the land. This corruption takes many forms: it includes moral decay, injustice, violence, and leading others away from the path of Allah. Their very presence is a source of harm rather than benefit. The result for such people is stark and twofold. First, they will be condemned; the curse and alienation from Allah’s mercy fall upon them. Second, they will have the worst abode. Just as the righteous received the best home, these will receive the most terrible dwelling, which is the Fire of Hell. The symmetry is deliberate: faithful maintenance of covenants and ties leads to the finest reward; violation and corruption lead to the worst punishment. The verse is a sobering warning that human choices in this life shape the eternal dwelling in the next with absolute justice.

=26=

The surah now turns to one of the greatest tests that lead people to violate their covenant: wealth and worldly provision. Allah gives abundant sustenance to some and limited provision to others. This distribution is not arbitrary but based on His perfect wisdom, testing some with wealth to see if they are grateful and others with poverty to see if they are patient. The problem arises when the disbelievers become prideful and exult in the pleasures of this worldly life, thinking that their wealth is a sign of divine favour or their own power, and failing to see it as a temporary test. Their hearts become attached to what is fleeting, and they mock those who have been given less.

The verse then delivers the divine assessment that recalibrates all values: the life of this world, compared to the Hereafter, is nothing but a fleeting enjoyment. The Arabic term used, matā‘, describes a small provision taken on a journey, something quickly consumed and left behind. No matter how vast one’s worldly riches, they are insignificant when measured against the eternal duration of the afterlife. If the entire world were a single meal, the Hereafter would be an endless feast; the comparison is not even close. This perspective liberates the believer from envy and despair. The wealthy believer sees their wealth as a trust to be spent for the Hereafter, and the poor believer understands that their true wealth is yet to come. The verse strips the glamour from worldly life and invites every soul to invest in what lasts.

=27=

The demand for a tangible miracle surfaces again, echoing verse 7. The disbelievers persist in their request for a special sign, believing that such a spectacle would compel them to believe. They fail to see that the entire Quran is an enduring intellectual and spiritual sign, and the universe around them is full of wonders. Allah instructs the Prophet to answer with a profound theological truth that addresses the root of guidance and misguidance. He is to say that Allah leaves to stray whoever He wills, and He guides to Himself whoever turns to Him.

This statement upholds divine sovereignty while simultaneously affirming human responsibility. The one who is left astray is the one who has chosen to turn away, who has no sincere will to find the truth, and Allah, knowing this state, seals that person in their chosen misguidance. As for the one who “turns to Him,” who repents, seeks truth earnestly, and directs their heart toward Allah, Allah guides that person to Himself.

The word “turns” translates anāba, which implies a continuous returning, a reorientation of the whole being. The answer thus reframes the issue: the problem is not a lack of signs but a lack of the sincere turning that opens the heart to the signs already given. The miracle of guidance is not in mountains moving but in a heart that turns and is met by divine grace. This verse protects the believer from the false idea that guidance is arbitrary, while also preventing arrogance, because guidance remains ultimately in Allah’s hands and is given to those who humbly seek it.

=28=

The description of those who are guided continues. They are the ones who believe, and their belief is not a cold, intellectual assent but a living faith that has settled deep into their hearts. The consequence of this faith is that their hearts find comfort in the remembrance of Allah. The remembrance, dhikr, encompasses the recitation of the Quran, the pronouncement of His names and praises, prayer, and the conscious awareness of His presence throughout the day. When the heart is agitated by fears, anxieties, grief, or the turbulence of life, it is the remembrance of Allah that brings it back to a state of stillness and peace.

This is not merely a psychological suggestion but a spiritual law: the heart is created to know and love its Creator, and when it turns to what it was made for, it finds its missing piece. The verse then emphasizes this truth with an independent, emphatic statement: Surely in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find comfort.

The Arabic construction conveys absolute certainty. No heart, no matter how troubled, is beyond the reach of this tranquillity if it sincerely engages in dhikr. The Prophet, peace be upon him, exemplified this when he would tell Bilal to call the prayer so that they could find rest in it. He lived in constant remembrance, and his heart was the most tranquil of all, even amidst the greatest trials. This verse offers a direct remedy to modern anxiety and spiritual emptiness. When people chase comfort in entertainment, substances, or material gain, they find only temporary distraction. The lasting comfort, the true sakīnah, descends only through the remembrance of the One who holds all hearts in His hand.

=29=

This short, powerful verse serves as the summary and the promise for the guided ones described in the previous two verses. Those who combine sincere belief with righteous deeds—the two inseparable pillars of salvation—are given a two-part reward.

The first part is tūbā, translated as bliss. This Arabic word carries connotations of goodness, joy, and a blessed state that is both comprehensive and enduring. Some scholars have interpreted it as a tree in Paradise, or the specific delight and congratulations the believers receive. Whatever its exact nature, it represents the pinnacle of happiness and divine approval.

The second part is an honourable destination, a beautiful place of return. This destination is Paradise itself, but the phrasing emphasizes not just the place but the honour with which they are received there. They do not creep into forgiveness; they are welcomed with dignity, their faces bright, their records clean, and their status elevated. The linking of belief and good deeds throughout these verses reinforces that faith that does not translate into action is incomplete, and actions without faith lack foundation. The beauty of this verse lies in its simplicity. It offers a compact, memorable formula for the ultimate success. Every believer can hold onto it: if I truly believe and strive to do good, then tūbā and a beautiful return await me. The promise is immediate enough to provide hope in this life and grand enough to sustain the soul through any hardship. It is a verse that the companions would have cherished, and it serves as a constant reminder that our small, sincere efforts are seen, valued, and will be rewarded beyond measure.

=30=

This verse turns directly to the Prophet, peace be upon him, to clarify his mission and the painful response he receives. Allah tells him that he has been sent to a community in the same pattern as earlier communities received their messengers. There is a long chain of prophethood, and Muhammad is its final link, sent with the same essential call to a people who need guidance. His task is to recite to them what has been revealed to him, so the Quran is the centerpiece of his mission. The response, however, is staggering: they deny the Most Compassionate. The disbelievers of Makkah were known to reject even the name “Ar-Rahmān,” the Most Compassionate, as they did not want to acknowledge this attribute of Allah. They preferred their tribal idols. Their denial of the Most Compassionate was a denial of the very source of mercy and guidance. The Prophet is then instructed to declare his own firm stand: “He is my Lord! There is no god worthy of worship except Him.” He is to affirm pure monotheism regardless of their rejection. Then he adds, “In Him I put my trust, and to Him I turn in repentance. ” This is a profound model for every caller to truth. When people reject the message, the response is not frustration or compromise but a renewed personal attachment to Allah through trust and repentance. Tawakkul, or trust, means handing over all affairs to Him, and inābah, or turning in repentance, means constantly returning to Him with devotion, seeking His help, and purifying one’s own state.

The verse teaches that the rejection of others should drive the believer closer to Allah, not further into despair. The Prophet’s mission is secure because his trust is in the Lord of the universe.

=31=

The disbelievers’ demand for spectacular signs is met with this powerful statement. The Quran itself possesses such immense spiritual power that, were any recitation capable of causing mountains to move, splitting the earth, or making the dead speak, it would be this very Quran.

However, the manifestation of such cataclysmic signs is not the purpose of the revelation; its primary purpose is to guide hearts. The mountains will indeed be moved on the Day of Judgement, but here, the verse emphasizes that the transformative power of the Quran is moral and spiritual, not a tool for human spectacle. Then the verse delivers a humbling question: have the believers not yet realized that if Allah had willed, He could have guided all of humanity? This comforted the early Muslims who wondered why so many refused to believe. Forced universal guidance is not the divine plan. Instead, humans are given free will, and the truth is made clear so that belief is a meaningful choice.

The verse then shifts to a warning about the immediate consequences of disbelief: disasters will continue to afflict the disbelievers or strike close to their homes as a recompense for their deeds. This refers to the ongoing trials, defeats, and calamities that serve as reminders and punishments in this life, all leading up to the ultimate promise of the Hereafter.

The verse concludes by affirming that Allah never fails in His promise. The delay is not a cancellation; every threat and every promise will come to pass at its appointed time. The believer is thus taught patience, while the disbeliever is warned that the apparent respite is a test, not an escape.

=32=

To comfort the Prophet, peace be upon him, in the face of the mockery mentioned throughout the surah, this verse reveals that the pattern of ridicule is not new. Other messengers before him were similarly mocked by their people. The path of prophethood is paved with rejection, sarcasm, and hostility. But Allah did not leave those mockers unpunished indefinitely. He delayed them for a while, granting them time to repent, to reflect, and to exhaust their excuses. This respite is a manifestation of divine patience and mercy, but it is also a test. When they persisted in their arrogance, the seizure came.

The Arabic word “akhadhtuhum,” translated as “seized them,” conveys a sudden, firm, and inescapable grip. The punishment descended, and the mockery ceased.

The verse then poses an exclamatory question: “And how horrible was My punishment!” This is not a question seeking information but a rhetorical emphasis on the severity of what befell those nations. It serves as a warning to the mockers among the Quraysh that the same divine law applies to them. The delay they enjoy is not evidence of safety but an opportunity. For the Prophet and the believers, this verse provides immense solace. It reminds them that they are walking a well-trodden path of honour, that Allah’s justice is certain, and that the laughter of the disbelievers will turn to anguish. The believer is not to be shaken by mockery but to recall the fate of the ancient peoples and trust that the Lord who avenged His earlier messengers will uphold His final messenger and his followers.

=33=

The argument returns to the core of monotheism by challenging the very notion of associating partners with Allah. The verse begins with a rhetorical question: Is there any equal to the Vigilant One Who knows what every soul commits? The implied answer is a resounding no. Allah is al-Qayyūm, the Self-Subsisting Sustainer, who watches over every soul and records every deed. No idol, no false god, possesses even a fraction of this knowledge or vigilance.

Yet despite this clear truth, the pagans have set up partners with Allah. The Prophet is instructed to demand: “Name them!” Let them identify these gods, describe their attributes, their powers, their lineage. They cannot, because they are empty figments of imagination.

The next question is razor-sharp: “Or do you mean to inform Him of something He does not know on the earth?” This is a devastating irony. Are they claiming to know of partners that Allah Himself is unaware of? It exposes the absurdity of their position. Then the third option: “Or are these gods just empty words?” They are names without reality, sounds with no substance, a hollow claim with no truth behind it.

The verse then explains the psychological state that makes such irrationality possible: the disbelievers’ falsehood has been made so appealing to them that they have been turned away from the Path. Their desires and the delusions of Satan have beautified their shirk to the point where they cannot see its emptiness.

The verse ends with a sobering truth: whoever Allah leaves to stray will have no guide. When a person persistently chooses misguidance, Allah seals that choice, and no one can bring them back. This underscores the urgency of turning to the truth while the door is open.

=34=

After detailing the misguidance of the idolaters, this verse pronounces their sentence. It states that for them there is a punishment in this worldly life. This refers to the various calamities, defeats, humiliations, and afflictions that befall the stubborn disbelievers as a consequence of their rebellion. For the Quraysh, this included the devastating defeat at Badr and other trials.

However, the punishment of this world, no matter how severe, is only a foretaste. The verse then declares that the punishment of the Hereafter is truly far worse.

The Arabic phrasing emphasizes the magnitude of the difference. Hellfire is incomparably more intense, more lasting, and more comprehensive in its suffering than any worldly pain. The two punishments are not in the same category. This should shake any rational person into reconsidering their path, for enduring a temporary difficulty or giving up a sinful pleasure is trivial compared to eternal torment.

The verse concludes with the chilling statement: “And none can shield them from Allah.” No protector, no intercessor that they falsely imagined would save them, no wealth, no social status, and no power will be able to defend them when the divine punishment arrives. The absolute helplessness of the disbelievers on that Day is a recurring Quranic theme, and it strips away all the false confidence they had in this life. The believer reads this and feels both a healthy fear that drives them to obedience and a deep gratitude that Allah has saved them from such a fate by guiding them to faith. It is a verse that cuts through complacency.

=35=

The surah now provides a summary description of Paradise that balances the warning of the previous verse. The Paradise that is promised to the righteous, those who embody the qualities of taqwa, is a place of abundance and permanence. Its most famous feature is that rivers flow beneath its gardens and dwellings. Water in the arid landscape of Arabia was the symbol of life, luxury, and refreshment, and the idea of eternal, flowing rivers captures the heart’s deepest longing for peace and provision.

The verse then highlights two specific elements: its fruit is eternal, and its shade is likewise everlasting. In this world, fruits are seasonal, they spoil, and they eventually cease. Shade shifts with the sun, and trees lose their leaves. In Paradise, there is no autumn, no decay, and no deprivation. The blessings are constant and unchanging, yet always enjoyable.

This is the ultimate outcome for the righteous, the final destination that their faith and deeds have earned them by Allah’s mercy. The verse then presents the stark contrast in a single, terse sentence: “But the outcome for the disbelievers is the Fire!” The abruptness of this statement after the flowing description of Paradise creates a powerful emotional jolt. It is a pattern used throughout the Quran: Paradise is painted in alluring detail, and then the alternative is named with terrifying brevity, as if to say that the mere mention of the Fire should be enough to make any soul flee from it. The verse leaves the listener with a clear choice and an urgent incentive to be among the righteous.

=36=

The surah now addresses the reception of the Quran among the People of the Book. Among those who were given the Scripture in previous generations, there are believers who truly recognize the truth. When the Quran was revealed, they rejoiced because they found in it the confirmation of what was in their own scriptures and the culmination of the prophetic promise. This includes individuals like ‘Abdullāh ibn Salām, a Jewish scholar who embraced Islam, and the Christian delegation that recognized the truth when they heard the Quran. Their joy is a sign for the skeptics.

However, the verse also notes that some factions among them deny a portion of the revelation. They accept parts that align with their desires and reject others, especially those verses that clearly affirm the prophethood of Muhammad and the purity of monotheism. The Prophet is then instructed to declare his simple, unwavering mission: “I have only been commanded to worship Allah, associating none with Him. To Him I invite, and to Him is my return.” This declaration leaves no room for compromise or hidden motives. His message is purely the worship of the One God, and his ultimate accountability is to Allah alone.

The verse teaches that the truth of the Quran is attested to by the sincere among previous religious communities, and it calls the Prophet to stand firm on this clear foundation, unaffected by the selective acceptance or rejection of any group. The final words, “to Him is my return,” reflect the eschatological perspective that governs all his actions.

=37=

This verse addresses the nature of the Quran itself and the seriousness of adhering to it. Allah has revealed the Quran as an authority, a decisive criterion that separates truth from falsehood, and He has revealed it in clear Arabic so that its first audience can understand it directly and grasp its profound meanings.

The Arabic language was chosen as the vehicle for the final revelation, not out of ethnic preference, but so that the message could penetrate hearts without linguistic barriers for its initial recipients, and so that its eloquence could stand as a lasting miracle. However, with this great gift comes a strict warning. The Prophet is told that if he were to follow the desires of the disbelievers after the knowledge that has come to him—knowledge of pure monotheism, of the divine law, and of the truth—then he would find no protector or shield against Allah. While the address is to the Prophet, the warning extends to the entire community. No one, no matter how elevated their status, is exempt from the consequences of compromising the truth. The verse reinforces the absolute integrity of the revelation. It cannot be bent to please human whims.

The Arabic Quran is an immovable standard. This also serves as a source of strength for the believers: they possess a revelation whose authority is not derived from human approval but from divine origin. They are not to dilute it to gain acceptance. The warning is severe precisely because the trust is immense, and it teaches that true security lies not in aligning with the powerful or the majority, but in steadfastly holding onto the revealed knowledge, no matter the pressure.

=38=

This verse serves multiple purposes. First, it consoles the Prophet by reminding him that the messengers before him were also fully human. Allah blessed them with wives and children. They lived normal human lives, married, had families, and faced the same human needs and relationships. This counters any argument that a prophet should be an angel or a supernatural being untouched by ordinary life. The Prophet’s humanity, including his marriage and offspring, is consistent with the pattern of all previous messengers. Second, the verse repeats a key principle: no messenger had the authority to bring a sign independently. Signs come only by Allah’s permission. This addresses the repeated demand of the disbelievers for miracles, telling them that the Prophet does not control the extraordinary; his task is to convey the message, and Allah sends signs according to His wisdom. Third, the verse grounds everything in divine decree: “Every destined matter has a set time.

” The word “ajal” refers to an appointed term. Every event, every manifestation of punishment or victory, the rise and fall of nations, and the lifespan of every individual and every scripture’s dominance, all run according to a timeline established by Allah. The believers are being taught patience; the delay in seeing the full triumph of truth is not neglect but precise timing.

For the disbelievers, it is a warning that their respite is measured, and when their term arrives, they cannot delay it by a single moment. The verse connects the life of the Prophet to the broad fabric of prophetic history and divine timing, giving his mission a cosmic context.

=39=

This verse expands on the idea of divine decree mentioned in the previous verse. Allah eliminates and confirms what He wills. This refers to His absolute authority over the decrees, laws, and events that unfold. He can abrogate previous rulings, change circumstances, erase a punishment that was decreed due to a person’s repentance, or confirm a blessing that was promised. The scholars have understood this verse in multiple valid ways. Some relate it to the abrogation of certain laws within the scriptures; Allah may replace one commandment with another according to His wisdom. Others view it in the context of the Preserved Tablet, where the angels record the decrees. There are matters that are conditional, such as a person’s lifespan being extended by maintaining family ties or a provision being increased by charity. Allah eliminates or confirms these conditional matters based on human actions, while the ultimate, unchangeable decree resides with Him in the Master Record, the Umm al-Kitāb. This concept should not be misunderstood as Allah changing His mind, which is impossible for the All-Knowing. Rather, it means that within His eternal knowledge, certain outcomes are contingent upon actions, and He brings those to pass in real time. The verse inspires both hope and fear. A sinner should never despair, because sincere repentance may eliminate a decreed punishment. A righteous person should remain humble and not feel secure, understanding that ultimate outcomes are written with Allah. The verse deepens the believer’s trust in divine wisdom while emphasizing that the final, unalterable source of all decrees is the Master Record with Allah, above all change and beyond full human comprehension.

=40=

This verse provides profound relief and clarity regarding the Prophet’s responsibility. The threats of punishment that have been repeatedly mentioned in the surah may or may not be witnessed by the Prophet during his lifetime.

The verse presents two possibilities: either Allah will show him some of the punishment He has promised the disbelievers during his life—as indeed happened with the defeat of the Quraysh at Badr and other victories—or Allah will cause the Prophet to die before that punishment fully materializes. In either case, the Prophet’s duty does not change. His task is solely the delivery of the message, the clear and complete conveyance of the revelation. He is not responsible for the results, nor is he required to witness the final judgment.

The word “balāgh” denotes a communication that reaches its target, and the Prophet fulfilled this to perfection. The verse then declares, “Judgment is for Us. ” The accountability of people, the punishment of the rejecters, and the final reckoning belong entirely to Allah. This absolves the Prophet of the psychological burden of having to see every denier punished in his lifetime. It also teaches the believers that their duty mirrors this: they must convey the truth with wisdom and kindness, but they cannot force outcomes. The ultimate judgment of hearts and the execution of justice are reserved for the Lord of the worlds. This verse, therefore, is a foundation for a balanced understanding of activism in faith: do your part with excellence, then leave the rest to Allah with full trust. It frees the soul from despair over delayed justice and from the temptation to take matters into its own hands.
