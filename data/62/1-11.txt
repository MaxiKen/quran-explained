=1=

This surah opens, just as the previous one did, with a declaration that everything in the heavens and the earth continuously glorifies Allah. This sets the tone of universal submission and divine majesty. The glorification is described as constant, uninterrupted, and all-encompassing. Every particle, every angel, every creature sings the praise of its Creator, acknowledging His perfection and His freedom from any fault.

The verse then attaches four majestic names of Allah: the King, the Most Holy, the Almighty, and the All-Wise. “The King” indicates that absolute dominion and sovereignty belong to Him alone; He rules over the entire universe without any partner or rival. “The Most Holy” is Al-Quddus, meaning the One who is utterly pure and free from any imperfection, and who blesses and purifies creation. “The Almighty” is Al-‘Aziz, the One who possesses irresistible power and authority, whom none can overpower. “The All-Wise” is Al-Hakim, the One whose every decree and command is based on infinite wisdom and purpose. By combining these four names, the verse presents a complete picture of a sovereign who is not only powerful but also pure and wise. This introduction, much like in Surah Al-Ḥadīd and Al-Ḥashr, connects the following themes to these divine attributes.

In the context of this Medinan surah, the believers are being prepared to receive instructions about the Friday prayer and to understand the immense favour of prophethood. Because Allah is the King, His commands concerning the sacred assembly must be taken seriously. Because He is the Most Holy, the gathering for the remembrance of Allah purifies the believers. Because He is the Almighty, no worldly business or distraction can benefit a person if they disobey Him. And because He is All-Wise, the legislation of the Friday congregation, the sending of the Messenger to the unlettered people, and even the test of the caravan diversion are all full of deep wisdom. This opening thus anchors the entire surah in the glorification and attributes of Allah, reminding the listener that the teachings to come are from the Almighty King, not a mere human suggestion, and that our response to them is part of our own glorification of Him.

=2=

After the universal praise, Allah reminds the believers of the immense favour He has bestowed upon them specifically. The term “the illiterate people” translates “al-ummiyyīn,” referring to the Arabs, who were largely a nation without a scripture, not generally literate in the sense of having received divine books before. From among themselves, Allah raised a messenger—Muhammad, peace be upon him—who was also unlettered, not schooled by any human teacher.

This is a response to those who might have questioned how a prophet could emerge from a people without a scholarly tradition. The four key functions of the Messenger are then listed. First, he “recites to them His revelations,” meaning he conveys the verses of the Quran, the very words of Allah. Second, he “purifies them,” which includes cleansing them from idolatry, moral vices, and spiritual diseases through tazkiyah. Third, he “teaches them the Book,” providing them with a deep understanding of the Quran, its laws, and its meanings. Fourth, he teaches them “wisdom,” which many scholars, including Imam Shafi’i, understood as the Sunnah, the practical application of the Quranic teachings in life.

The verse then states, “for indeed they had previously been clearly astray,” highlighting that before this Messenger came, the Arabs were wandering in the darkness of polytheism, tribal feuds, and moral decadence. This phrasing shows that the sending of the Prophet was not a random favour but a rescue from manifest error. This verse parallels the commendation of the disciples in the previous surah by honouring the companions of Muhammad, who received this purification and teaching directly from the Prophet. It also sets the stage for the criticism of those who were given the Torah but failed to uphold it, contrasting the successful mission among the ummiyyīn with the failure of previous communities.

The emphasis on purification and wisdom indicates that faith is not mere information but transformation.

=3=

This verse continues the statement from the previous one, broadening the scope of the Messenger’s mission. He was not sent only to the immediate Arabs of his time, but also to “others of them who have not yet joined them.” There is a beautiful interpretive depth here. Some scholars say it refers to the non-Arab peoples—Persians, Romans, and all other nations—who would later embrace Islam and join the community of faith, becoming spiritual “brothers” of the original Arabs. The Prophet himself, when asked about this verse, placed his hand on Salman al-Farsi and said, “If faith were at the Pleiades, men from among these would surely attain it,” indicating the Persians. Others understand it as referring to all future generations of believers until the Day of Judgment. The verse thus prophesies the vast expansion of Islam beyond the Arabian Peninsula, a prediction that came true within a few decades.

The phrase “join them in faith” indicates that these later people will become part of the same Ummah, sharing in the purification, teaching, and guidance. The verse ends with “For He is the Almighty, All-Wise,” reaffirming that this expansive plan is backed by divine power and wisdom. The Almighty is fully capable of spreading the faith to lands and peoples far away, overcoming all barriers. The All-Wise designs the mission to include all times and places, so that no nation is left without the call. This universal dimension serves as a great honour for the companions: they were the nucleus of a global community. It also consoles them that their struggle will bear fruit beyond what their eyes can see. The connection to the surah’s theme is clear: the Friday congregation that will be commanded later is not just for a tribal gathering but for a worldwide, multi-ethnic community united by this Messenger. The believers are part of something far larger than themselves, and the favour of prophethood will keep radiating until the end of time. It humbles the Arabs not to boast of exclusivity and opens the hearts of all to welcome every new believer as part of the same blessed family.

=4=

The verse emphatically states, “This is the favour of Allah,” referring back to the sending of the Messenger with all the functions mentioned: reciting revelations, purifying, teaching the Book and wisdom, and including the later generations. Favour, or “fadl,” is a gift given out of pure generosity, not as a payment for something deserved. The unlettered Arabs did not have any prior merit that obligated Allah to send a prophet among them; it was sheer divine grace. The statement “He grants it to whoever He wills” establishes that prophethood and guidance are matters of Allah’s choice. No human being can dictate where mercy should land.

This is a direct rebuttal to any claims of exclusive entitlement to prophethood. It also answers those among the Jews who rejected the Prophet because he was not from the Children of Israel; Allah chooses whom He wills, and His favour is not confined by lineage.

The final phrase, “And Allah is the Lord of infinite bounty,” uses the term “al-fadl al-‘azim”—tremendous, boundless grace. This assures that Allah’s generosity is not limited; He bestows enormous favours, and the sending of the final Messenger is one of the greatest of those favours. The believers are being reminded not to take this for granted. After this reminder, the surah will contrast the proper response with the failure of those who were previously given scripture.

The verse also connects to the upcoming command regarding Friday prayer: attending the remembrance of Allah is part of that divine favour, and those who neglect it are rejecting a portion of that bounty. The word “favour” here ties directly to verse 11, where the Prophet is instructed to say, “What is with Allah is far better than amusement and merchandise. ” Thus, the entire surah pivots on recognizing the immense value of the divine favour versus the cheap attractions of this world.

This is not just a historical statement but a living reminder that our presence in the Muslim community, our access to the Quran, and the guidance we receive are all gifts of infinite bounty that deserve gratitude and active response.

=5=

After presenting the immense favour of the final Messenger, the surah draws a stark contrast with the previous nations, specifically the Jews who were given the Torah. They were “entrusted” with it—charged with the duty to study it, practice it, and convey its message. But they failed to uphold that trust. Their failure did not mean they lost the physical books; they had them and read them, but they did not live by them or recognize the prophesied Prophet within them. The example given is vivid: they are like a donkey carrying heavy volumes of books on its back. The donkey feels the weight but has no understanding of the knowledge it bears, gaining nothing from the load except fatigue. So too, these people carry the scripture outwardly, but its wisdom does not penetrate their hearts, their conduct does not change, and they receive no benefit.

This is a severe indictment of those who possess sacred knowledge but do not act upon it. The verse then exclaims, “How evil is the example of those who reject Allah’s signs!” It is a terrible parable because it portrays a state of utter failure—having the greatest treasure yet being as oblivious as a beast of burden. The underlying message is clear: if the Muslims, who have been given the Quran, were to neglect their duty and fail to practice its teachings, they too would risk resembling this donkey.

The verse concludes, “For Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people. ” The wrongdoing here is their persistent rejection and failure, which leads to a state where divine guidance is withdrawn. This verse links directly to the earlier theme in Surah As-Saff about the Israelites harming Moses and deviating. It explains that the favour of a scripture is not enough; one must carry it with understanding and action. It also serves as a warning to the believers attending the Friday sermon: do not be physically present but spiritually absent, like a donkey bearing a weight without comprehension. The purpose of the remembrance of Allah is to transform, not merely to perform a ritual.

=6=

The argument now turns to challenge the Jews directly. Many of the Jews in Medina proudly claimed that they were the beloved sons of God and His chosen people, and that Paradise was reserved exclusively for them. To expose the hollowness of this claim, the Prophet is commanded to issue a bold challenge: “If your claim is true and you are certain of your special status and guaranteed salvation, then wish for death.” The reasoning is simple: if someone is absolutely sure that Paradise awaits them after death, they would long to meet that destiny and be freed from the hardships of this world.

In fact, the Quran elsewhere says that the Hereafter is better and more lasting than this life. So the mere fact that they cling to life and dread death proves that deep down they know their claim is false. This challenge was delivered publicly, and it completely silenced them. History records that none of them dared to accept this invitation. They knew that wishing for death while being false in faith would be an act of self-destruction.

This is what the next verse will confirm: they will never wish for it. The challenge is a rational argument that turns their own pretension against them. It also serves as a profound psychological exposure: the love of this world and fear of meeting Allah are signs of a lack of true faith and good deeds.

For the Muslim community, this verse is a lesson that genuine faith makes the believer unafraid of death, seeing it as a passage to Allah’s mercy. It also warns against making empty claims of spiritual superiority while leading a life that contradicts those claims. The verse connects to the “unutilized knowledge” theme: the Jews had the Torah and claimed to be special, but their failure to act on it made them afraid of death, demonstrating that their claim was a lie. The challenge remains a timeless proof of the Quran’s divine origin, as nobody could take up the call.

=7=

This verse states the divine judgment that the Jews will never ever wish for death. The reason is explicitly given: “because of what their hands have done.” Their hands have committed sins, including altering the scriptures, concealing the truth about the Prophet, breaking covenants, and committing injustices. Their conscience knows that their record is heavy with misdeeds. So their fear of death is not merely an instinctive love of life; it is a terror of the accountability that follows. They know, deep within, that they are not prepared for the Hereafter.

The phrase “what their hands have done” is a Quranic idiom that refers to all the evil deeds they have perpetrated. The verse then adds, “And Allah has perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers. ” This serves as a warning that no matter how much they hide their fears and pretend confidence, Allah is fully aware of their state. He knows that their refusal to wish for death is proof of their wrongdoing.

This is also a comfort to the believers: they need not be impressed by the arrogant claims of those who oppose the truth, because Allah knows the hidden reality. The continuation of the argument from the previous verse dismantles any remaining excuse. If the Jews truly believed they were God’s chosen, they would not hesitate. Since they hesitate, it is an admission against themselves. This logical approach is typical of Quranic debate: it uses their own belief in a future life against them.

The verse also reinforces the surah’s emphasis on action over empty claims. It ties back to the donkey carrying books; those who bear knowledge without action end up terrified of the consequence of that knowledge. For the believers, this verse encourages a life of righteous deeds that make one look forward to meeting Allah with hope rather than dread. The “wrongdoers” here are those whose knowledge and claims are not matched by righteous action, and Allah’s knowledge encompasses them all.

=8=

The challenge is followed by a solemn reality check. The death that they flee from and so fiercely avoid even wishing for is inescapable. It will seize them no matter how carefully they try to preserve their lives.

This is a universal truth: every soul shall taste death. The Prophet is instructed to announce this fact to them.

The phrase “will inevitably come to you” means that no fortress, wealth, or caution can delay the appointed time. Then, after death, they will be “returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen.” Allah is the One who knows all that is visible and all that is hidden—public deeds, secret thoughts, and even the unspoken fears in their hearts. At that moment, He will inform them fully of everything they used to do. This “informing” is the precise judgment where every deed is laid bare. The statement is at once a warning and a call to reflect. The Jews’ reluctance to wish for death was based on their dread of this very disclosure. For the believers, however, this verse is a powerful reminder that no one escapes death, and the only sensible preparation is to live a life that one will not be ashamed to have revealed.

The verse also connects to the broader surah theme: since death is certain and the return to Allah is inevitable, it is utter foolishness to prefer the fleeting business of this world over the remembrance of Allah. The Friday prayer and sermon are a weekly rehearsal of this truth, pulling the believer away from worldly pursuits to face the ultimate reality. The description of Allah as “Knower of the seen and unseen” also emphasizes that sincerity in faith is essential; one cannot hide hypocrisy from Him. This verse acts as a bridge between the criticism of the Jews and the instructions for the believers, reminding everyone that all will stand before Allah and account for how they responded to the divine favours, including the favour of the Messenger and the scripture.

=9=

With this verse, the surah turns to the practical regulation that gives it its name. After establishing the foundation of faith, the honour of the Muslim community, and the fate of those who neglect divine trust, Allah addresses the believers with a direct command concerning the Friday congregation. The “call to prayer” refers to the adhan that is pronounced when the Imam sits on the pulpit for the sermon. The command is to “proceed diligently” (fas‘aw) to the remembrance of Allah.

The Arabic “sa‘y” does not necessarily mean running in haste, but to head with purpose, priority, and mental focus. The “remembrance of Allah” includes the Friday sermon (khutbah) and the prayer itself, which are filled with praise, glorification, and recitation of the Quran. The instruction to “leave off your business” means to suspend all trade, buying, selling, and worldly transactions.

The verse then explains the wisdom: “That is best for you, if only you knew. ” The true benefit of attending the congregation—spiritual cleansing, divine reward, community unity, and enlightenment—far outweighs whatever immediate profit they might miss by closing their shops for an hour. This verse was revealed at a time when the Muslim community in Medina needed to be shaped into a distinctive, disciplined ummah. The weekly gathering became a pillar of social and spiritual life, an opportunity for the entire community to renew their faith, learn from the Prophet, and stand shoulder to shoulder in prayer.

The phrase “if only you knew” appeals to the believers’ deeper understanding; human beings often overvalue immediate material gain and undervalue long-term spiritual gain. So Allah invites them to a knowledge-based re-evaluation of priorities.

This command also directly counters the previous behaviour of the Jews who clung to worldly life and feared death. The believer is trained to willingly drop worldly pursuits when called by the Creator.

The verse establishes the obligation of Friday prayer and emphasizes its importance by making it a test of faith and priority.

=10=

This verse provides the necessary balance. The command to leave business during the Friday prayer does not imply a monastic rejection of worldly life. As soon as the prayer is completed, the believers are permitted, even encouraged, to “disperse throughout the land and seek the bounty of Allah.

” This is a clear directive that engaging in trade, agriculture, and other lawful professions is not only allowed but is a means of seeking Allah’s provision. The phrase “bounty of Allah” dignifies work and trade by linking it to divine grace. A merchant closing his shop for the prayer and then reopening it does so with the understanding that his livelihood is in Allah’s hands, not just his own effort.

The verse then adds a crucial spiritual dimension: “And remember Allah often so you may be successful.” Even while engaging in business and worldly activities, the heart should remain attached to Allah through dhikr—remembrance. The ultimate success (falah) is not measured by the amount of profit one makes during the day, but by whether one remained God-conscious, honest, and prayerful while seeking that provision. This destroys the false division between the sacred and the secular. A believer’s whole day can become worship: the morning is dedicated to the congregation, and the remainder is spent in productive work laced with the remembrance of Allah.

The verse also teaches that success is a holistic concept encompassing both this world and the Hereafter. The command to remember Allah often after the prayer indicates that the Friday prayer should have a lasting effect on the believer’s conduct throughout the week. It connects back to the “favour” mentioned earlier: the true success lies in recognizing and acting upon that favour, not in being distracted by the caravan. The surah’s message thus integrates the spiritual and the material, correcting those who would neglect prayer for business and those who would neglect worldly duties in the name of false piety, guiding the community to a balanced, successful path.

=11=

This final verse records a historical incident that served as a live lesson for the community, revealing the underlying tension between faith and worldly distraction. The Prophet, peace be upon him, was delivering the Friday sermon in Medina when a trade caravan arrived with the beating of drums and loud fanfare, announcing its goods. At that time, the city was experiencing economic hardship, and the people were eager for provisions. Upon hearing the noise, a significant number of the congregation, including many who were standing in prayer rows, left the mosque and rushed toward the caravan, leaving the Prophet standing on the pulpit with only a handful of companions remaining, perhaps as few as twelve. This incident was a test that exposed the weakness of some believers whose hearts were still overly attached to worldly gain. The verse says “they ˹almost all˺ flocked to it,” indicating that the majority left. The Prophet was not left entirely alone, but it was a stark moment. Allah then instructed the Prophet to say to them, “What is with Allah is far better than amusement and merchandise. ” The reward, blessing, and spiritual sustenance that Allah grants through the prayer and the remembrance are infinitely superior to the fleeting entertainment and the temporary profit of the caravan.

The word “amusement” refers to the drums and excitement, while “merchandise” refers to the goods for sale. Both are declared to be inferior to what Allah has reserved for those who remain steadfast in His worship.

The verse concludes with the profound reassurance, “And Allah is the Best Provider. ” It reminds the believers that their sustenance is ultimately not dependent on rushing to a caravan; Allah, the Best of providers, will give them much more than they might miss. This ending ties the surah together beautifully: the favour of Allah, the teaching, the purification, and the congregation are worth infinitely more than any worldly attraction. The lesson remains eternally relevant: whenever the call to Allah conflicts with the pull of profit or entertainment, the believer must remember that what is with Allah is better and more lasting.
