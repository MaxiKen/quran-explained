=1=

The surah opens with two Arabic letters, Ḥâ and Mĩm. These are among the disjointed letters that appear at the beginning of twenty-nine chapters of the Quran. Their precise meaning is known fully only to Allah. Throughout Islamic scholarship, however, several profound wisdoms have been drawn from them. One widely held view is that they serve as a challenge to the Arabs of the time, who prided themselves on their eloquence. The Quran is composed of the very same letters they used in their daily speech, yet they cannot produce anything like it. The letters are a silent announcement that this Book is constructed from familiar letters but carries a miraculous quality. Another interpretation connects these letters to the names and attributes of Allah, with Ḥâ pointing to the All-Wise and Mĩm to the All-Mighty. Some companions and early scholars also saw them as a means of capturing the listener's attention. When the Prophet recited a surah beginning with such letters, the disbelievers who had been trying to drown out the recitation would fall silent out of curiosity, allowing the verses that followed to enter their ears. This opening forms a natural link to the next verse, which swears by the Book itself. The letter symbols remind us that the Quran cannot be reduced to mere ink and sound; it contains a divine secret that human minds cannot exhaust. These letters also indicate that the revelation is a direct communication from the unseen realm.

For the believer, encountering Ḥâ-Mĩm at the head of a chapter creates a sense of awe and readiness to receive the weighty message that follows. Thus, the short verse functions as a doorway into a profound discourse about faith, denial, and the Creator’s signs, all of which unfold in the subsequent verses.

=2=

Here Allah swears an oath by the Quran itself, describing it as the clear Book. In the Arabic expression, the term used for "clear" is mubîn, which can mean something that is itself clear and also something that makes things clear. The Quran is luminous in its own truth, free from contradiction and obscurity, and it clarifies the path to guidance for humanity. Swearing by a created object is a rhetorical device that elevates the status of that object and draws the listener’s heart toward it. When a speaker swears by something immensely valuable, the hearer understands that what follows is of tremendous importance. In this case, the oath announces that the Quran is the decisive criterion between truth and falsehood. The description "clear" also counters the claim of the Meccan pagans who dismissed the Prophet’s message as poetic ravings or sorcery. The Book is anything but ambiguous; its legal rulings, moral principles, and stories of the past are laid out with transparent clarity for those who approach it sincerely. This verse, coming right after the mysterious letters, establishes that although some knowledge is hidden with Allah, what He has chosen to reveal is made plain. The oath connects directly to the next verse, which explains the purpose of revealing the Quran in Arabic. The clarity of the Book is also linked to its language. It is clear because it is delivered in the tongue of its first audience, allowing them to grasp its meanings directly without foreign intermediaries.

Thus, "By the clear Book" is a powerful opening that invokes the sacred text as a witness, a guide, and an undeniable sign from the Almighty.

=3=

After swearing by the clear Book, Allah now explains a fundamental aspect of its clarity: it has been revealed in the Arabic language. The phrase "Certainly, We have made it a Quran in Arabic" underscores a deliberate divine choice.

The word "Quran" itself means recitation, and linking it to "Arabic" emphasizes that this recitation is in a living language understood by its first hearers. The purpose is explicitly stated: "so perhaps you will understand. " This points to God’s mercy in sending messengers with the language of their people. If the Quran had been revealed in a foreign tongue, the Quraysh would have had an excuse, saying they could not comprehend its message. By revealing it in clear Arabic, that excuse is removed. This does not limit the Quran to Arabs alone, as its message is universal; rather, the Arabic language serves as the vehicle through which the timeless guidance is delivered. The expression "perhaps you will understand" shows that understanding the words is within reach, but it also hints that true comprehension requires reflection and a sound heart. Not everyone who hears Arabic words will automatically grasp the depths of their meaning; only those who ponder will benefit.

The verse also subtly refutes the pagans’ objection that a mere man could not bring such a Book. The Quran’s linguistic perfection and wisdom testify that it is from Allah, and the Arabic medium allowed its immediate audience to taste that miracle directly. This understanding then leads to the next verse, which reveals the heavenly source of the Quran, preserved in the Master Record. So the Arabic Quran is not an earthly invention but a faithful reproduction of a transcendent original.

=4=

Having spoken of the Arabic Quran accessible to human understanding, Allah now reveals its supreme origin. The verse tells us that this Book exists in the "Master Record" with Allah, a term scholars identify with the Preserved Tablet, the divine register that contains the decrees and the original scripture from which all revealed books descend. Describing it as "highly esteemed" elevates the Quran far above any human speech; it is exalted in rank, majestic in status, and honoured in the highest assembly of angels. Nothing of falsehood can approach it.

The second attribute, "rich in wisdom," means that every verse, every ruling, and every story is placed with perfect knowledge and purpose. There is no frivolity or imperfection in it.

The Arabic word ḥakīm (wise) also implies that the Quran is firmly constructed, containing profound truths that govern human life. By linking the earthly Arabic recitation to the heavenly source, the verse establishes that the Prophet did not compose the Quran himself. He simply conveyed what was already inscribed in the presence of Allah. This serves as a powerful response to the Meccan disbelievers who mocked the revelation. It also provides comfort to the believers: the guidance they hold in their hands has its root in an unchangeable, protected record with their Lord. The flow of these opening verses builds a complete picture: mysterious letters stir attention, the oath proclaims the Book’s clarity, the Arabic language makes it understandable, and its celestial origin invests it with absolute authority. The stage is now set for the warning that follows, where the arrogant rejection of such a glorious Reminder is shown to be utterly senseless.

=5=

After establishing the majestic nature of the Quran, the discourse takes a sharp rhetorical turn. Allah asks a question that exposes the foolishness of the Meccan pagans’ attitude. The "Reminder" here is the Quran itself, a name that highlights its function of awakening heedless hearts.

The question implies: would it be reasonable for Us to deprive you of this Reminder and withhold it from you just because you are a people who exceed all bounds in disbelief and sin? The answer is obviously no. Divine mercy is not governed by human rebellion. Despite their transgressions, Allah sent them the most honourable revelation as a chance to reform.

This verse addresses a subtle psychological assumption of the disbelievers. Some of them felt that a man like Muhammad, an orphan without great wealth, was unworthy of receiving revelation. They thought the Reminder should have come to a prominent leader. Allah dismantles their arrogance by pointing out that far from being unworthy of the Reminder, they were the ones in desperate need of it due to their transgressions. Had Allah turned away the Quran from them, they would have been left in utter darkness without any excuse on the Day of Judgment.

The verse also contains a warning: persistent transgression can lead to the heart being sealed, but God’s patience and mercy provide opportunity after opportunity. The next verses then recount the histories of past nations who received prophets and mocked them, showing that the Quraysh were not the first to react arrogantly. The link is clear: the sending of the Reminder to a transgressing people is a consistent pattern of divine mercy, but when rejection becomes entrenched, destruction follows as a lesson for others.

=6=

Allah now invites the Prophet and his followers, as well as the disbelievers, to reflect on history. The rhetorical "Imagine" draws the mind to the numerous prophets dispatched to earlier civilizations.

The phrase "those destroyed before" refers to communities such as the people of Noah, ‘Ad, Thamud, and the towns of Lot, whose ruins the Arabs passed during their trade journeys. The point is that the Quraysh are not unique in receiving a warner; the divine practice has always been to send human messengers to guide people before punishment descends. This continuity shows that Allah does not wrong anyone. He provides ample warning through clear signs. By mentioning the sheer number of prophets sent, the verse indicates God’s immense patience. He did not punish earlier peoples without first sending successive warners to call them to repentance. The Meccans should not think they are being singled out unfairly when Muhammad calls them to monotheism. Rather, they are part of a long human story.

The verse also consoles the Prophet, reminding him that his experience of rejection is not new. Many prophets before him faced similar opposition. The historical reflection sets the stage for the next verse, which reveals how those earlier nations received their prophets. So the mention of "how many" functions both as a warning and an encouragement: a warning to the disbelievers that the pattern of punishment is real, and an encouragement to the believer that patience in the face of mockery has always been the prophetic path.

=7=

This verse completes the historical picture hinted at previously. It states a universal law: mockery was the default response of the arrogant to every prophet. The wording "no prophet ever came to them without being mocked" leaves no exception. From Noah, who was ridiculed for building an ark on dry land, to Moses, called a sorcerer, to Jesus, accused of illegitimate birth, the pattern is unbroken. The Meccans’ mockery of the Prophet Muhammad, their calling him a madman or a poet, is therefore not evidence against his truthfulness; rather, it aligns perfectly with the way transgressors have always treated those sent to reform them. This provides deep consolation to the Prophet and the early Muslims who endured humiliation. It also contains a subtle threat: if the past peoples mocked their messengers and were then destroyed, what awaits those who now mock the final messenger?

The verse also highlights a psychological trait of those who reject the truth. Instead of engaging with the message rationally, they resort to mockery because it allows them to dismiss it without serious thought. Laughter and ridicule become a shield against the prick of conscience.

Yet this mockery does not harm the prophets; it only seals the fate of the mockers. By connecting this verse to the preceding one, we see a cycle: a prophet is sent, he is mocked, and then divine judgment intervenes.

The next verse will describe that judgment upon a generation mightier than the Quraysh, making the warning explicit.

=8=

Having established the pattern of mockery, Allah now announces its consequence. He destroyed the former nations, even though they were "far mightier" than the Meccans. The polytheists of Mecca were not a global superpower; they were a relatively small tribal society. The civilizations before them—like ‘Ad with their lofty pillars, Thamud who carved mountains, and Pharaoh with his army—possessed far greater physical strength, technology, and worldly dominion.

Yet when they persisted in denial, their might could not save them from a single divine command. This demolishes any sense of security the Quraysh might have felt because of their limited power and wealth. The statement also serves as evidence that the Quranic warnings are not empty threats; they are based on historical realities whose traces could still be observed.

The second sentence, "The examples of their predecessors have already been related," refers to the detailed stories recounted elsewhere in the Quran. These examples are not mere entertainment; they are object lessons. By calling them "examples," the verse hints that the fate of the Meccans is foreshadowed in those narratives. The listener is meant to understand that history repeats itself when human behaviour repeats itself. This verse acts as a bridge to the next section, which confronts the Meccans’ contradictions regarding the Creator. Despite their denial of the message, they actually acknowledge Allah as the Creator of the heavens and earth, a glaring inconsistency that will be exposed next.

=9=

This verse unveils a profound paradox in the belief system of the Meccan pagans. The Prophet is instructed to question them about the origin of the universe, and Allah declares with certainty what their answer will be. They will acknowledge that the heavens and the earth were created by "The Almighty, All-Knowing." They used these very divine names, recognising Allah’s supreme power and complete knowledge. This shows that even in their polytheism, they never attributed the fundamental act of creation to their idols. They knew deep down that Lat, ‘Uzza, and Manat had no share in bringing the cosmos into existence. The verse exposes the irrationality of their position: how can one worship stones, trees, or angels alongside the very Being who alone created everything? This rhetorical exposure forces them to confront their own inconsistency. The description "Almighty, All-Knowing" is perfectly chosen. Creation requires unmatched power and also exhaustive knowledge to design the universe with all its intricate laws. These attributes belong solely to Allah.

The verse also teaches an important principle of da‘wah: beginning with common ground. By starting from what they already admitted, the Prophet could lead them logically to the oneness of worship. If Allah alone created, then He alone deserves worship.

The next several verses will expand on the implications of this creative power, listing specific blessings in the earth and sky that should lead any rational person to grateful monotheism. Thus, verse 9 acts as a pivot from the historical warnings to a fresh argument based on the signs within creation.

=10=

Following the admission that Allah created the heavens and the earth, the discourse now elaborates on His specific favours to humanity, beginning with the earth. The phrase "laid out the earth for you" paints a picture of a surface spread out like a comfortable carpet, prepared for human habitation. The earth is not a chaotic, hostile wilderness by default; it has been made suitable for life with stability, soil, and a temperate climate. Then the verse mentions that He "set in it pathways. " These pathways can refer to the natural routes that run through mountains, valleys, and flatlands, making travel possible. They also include the instinctive sense of direction that humans and animals use, as well as the roads people construct over time. The purpose clause "so that you may find your way" carries both a literal and spiritual meaning. Literally, the paths allow traders and travellers to reach their destinations without losing themselves.

Spiritually, the ease of navigating the physical world is a metaphor for the guidance of revelation, which enables humans to navigate the moral and spiritual landscape of life. If Allah made external signs to keep us from being lost in the desert, He has surely provided internal guidance to keep us from being lost in error.

The mention of pathways also reminds the Meccans of their own livelihood, which depended on the two great trade caravans to Yemen and Syria. Their survival was possible only because of the earth’s God-given pathways. This gracious provision should have softened their hearts toward the Provider, making their ingratitude all the more astonishing.

The next verse continues with the counterpart blessing: water from the sky.

=11=

From the earth’s pathways, the focus shifts skyward to rain, the life-giving water. Allah sends down rain "in perfect measure," meaning with precise quantity and according to a wise plan. It is not sent as a destructive flood but in drops, in due season, sufficient to nourish the land without overwhelming it. This measured descent points to the Creator’s knowledge and mercy; He knows exactly what each region needs. The result is described with the sudden shift from "He" to "We," a common Quranic style denoting majesty: "with which We give life to a lifeless land." The barren, dry earth is brought to life with vibrant green vegetation. This observable miracle of revival in nature is then immediately linked to the reality of resurrection. The transition "And so will you be brought forth from the grave" is a logical argument from the familiar to the unfamiliar. The pagan Arabs often dismissed the idea of life after death as impossible, asking who could revive decayed bones. Here Allah draws their attention to a parallel they witness every year: dead ground becomes alive with plants. The One who performs this yearly revival has the power to bring humans out of their graves. The rain, then, is both a physical blessing and a visual proof of the afterlife.

In the context of the surah, which warns the deniers, this verse comforts the believers that just as dry land awaits the rain, justice and resurrection await the oppressed. The pattern of linking natural signs to spiritual truths continues seamlessly into the next verse about the pairs in creation and the provision of transport.

=12=

The catalogue of divine favours broadens to encompass a fundamental principle of creation: all things are created in pairs. Male and female, night and day, heaven and earth, sweet and bitter—this duality runs through the entire cosmos. It reveals a design of complementarity and interdependence. Nothing stands alone; everything finds its completion with its counterpart.

This principle serves as evidence that the universe is not a random accident but a deliberate system crafted by an All-Wise Creator. For the Meccans, the mention of pairs also subtly refutes their idea that Allah had taken daughters while they themselves preferred sons. It exposes their inconsistency even in natural matters.

The verse then moves to a specific application of God’s provision: "and made for you ships and animals to ride. " Ships, which float on the sea by God’s command, and animals such as camels, horses, and donkeys, are both mentioned together as means of transport. The pairing of ships and animals is itself significant. The Arabs were mostly land travellers dependent on camels, the "ships of the desert," but the parallel with sea ships reminds them that all means of conveyance are divine gifts. The camel, with its unique design for desert life, and the ship, with its ability to harness wind and water, both reflect the same intelligent Maker. This verse sets the stage for the next one, which describes the proper human response when settling on these mounts: remembering the Lord's blessing and declaring His glory.

=13=

This verse completes the purpose of providing mounts. The phrase "so that you may sit firmly on their backs" indicates that the stable mounting of animals and the safe boarding of ships is not a human achievement but a divine favour. Allah designed the camel’s back to be broad and suited for carrying loads, and He subjected the ship to the laws of buoyancy. Without these built-in properties, humans could not travel securely. Once settled on their conveyance, believers are instructed to engage in two inward and outward actions: remember the Lord’s blessings and utter a specific supplication. The act of remembering (dhikr) here ties the physical journey to the spiritual journey toward God. Instead of being puffed up with pride over one’s possessions, the believer acknowledges the source of the gift. The prescribed words are, "Glory be to the One Who has subjected these for us, for we could have never done so on our own. " To glorify Allah is to declare Him far above any weakness or partner, and to admit human powerlessness.

The word "subjected" indicates that these creatures and objects, though mighty, are made obedient to human use by a higher power. The humble rider confesses that even with all human intelligence, harnessing a strong animal or building a sailing ship would have been impossible without the raw materials, physical laws, and inspiration provided by Allah. This supplication, taught in the Quran, became the practice of the Prophet and his companions whenever they mounted for travel, transforming a mundane act into an act of worship.

The next verse then completes the spiritual attitude by directing the traveller's ultimate hope toward the return to the Lord.

=14=

After acknowledging the blessing of transport, the rider is to direct his or her heart to the ultimate journey. The conjunction "and" links the previous declaration of God’s glory with this statement of eschatological hope and responsibility. "Surely to our Lord we will all return" is a confession that every journey in this world, no matter how long, ends with the final return to the Creator for judgment. This thought tempers the pleasure of travel. While sitting comfortably on a mount, the believer does not forget that this comfort is temporary and that a far greater homecoming awaits. For the Meccan pagans who denied the resurrection, this verse inserted into a travel supplication serves as a subtle rebuke. Every journey is a reminder of the final journey; just as a traveller eventually reaches home, so every soul will come back to the One who created it.

The phrase also instills moral accountability. If one is returning to the Lord, then the journey of life must be conducted in a manner that pleases Him. The prayer on the mount becomes a mini-lesson in theology: Tawḥīd (God’s oneness in subjecting creation), divine favour (remembering blessings), and the Hereafter (return to the Lord). These four verses, from the creation of pairs to the return, form an integrated reflection on travel, blending physical observation with spiritual truth.

The surah now pivots sharply from these signs of God’s generosity to the absurd claim of the polytheists that Allah has offspring, a claim utterly incompatible with the majesty of the Creator just described.

=15=

Despite all the clear signs of Allah’s sole power and beneficence, the disbelievers commit an astonishing contradiction. The verse begins with "Still," indicating that their action continues in the face of overwhelming evidence. They have "made some of His creation out to be a part of Him," referring to their claim that angels are the daughters of Allah. By attributing offspring to Him, they make created beings a portion of the Creator, blurring the absolute distinction between the Eternal and the contingent.

This is a grave slander, because a "part" of God would imply division, need, and similarity to creation—all of which are impossible for the Self-Sufficient Lord. The verse immediately pronounces a verdict: "Indeed, humankind is clearly ungrateful.

" The Arabic word for ungrateful here is kafūr, which also carries the meaning of being extremely unthankful and covering up the truth. The use of "humankind" generalizes the trait, reminding us that this tendency to be ungrateful is a deep-rooted human flaw. After being blessed with the earth as a resting place, rain from the sky, ships, and animals, the natural response should have been pure monotheism and gratitude. Instead, they invented a false lineage for their Benefactor. This ingratitude is not a minor slip; it is a brazen denial of the obvious. The verse transitions from the travel supplications straight into the myth of the angels as daughters, drawing a stark contrast between the believer’s humble glorification of Allah and the pagan’s disrespectful fabrications.

The next verses will intensify the argument by exposing the psychological and logical absurdity of their daughter-god claim.

=16=

The interrogation continues with a piercing question that exposes the pagans’ double standard and the absurdity of their belief. The verse asks: out of all that He created, has Allah chosen for Himself daughters, while He selected sons for you?

The Arabic phrasing drips with irony. The Quraysh, like many pre-Islamic Arabs, viewed daughters as less valuable than sons. When a man received news of the birth of a daughter, his face would darken with shame, as the Quran describes elsewhere.

Yet they dared to assign to Allah what they themselves despised. The question implies that if offspring were possible for God—and it is absolutely not—then sons would be more fitting according to their own standards, yet they claim He has daughters. This reveals the sheer irrationality of their myth.

Furthermore, the verse underlines that the angels are simply part of God’s creation. The phrase "from what He created" reminds the pagans that they themselves admit the angels are created beings, not the Eternal Creator. So how can a created being become a divine offspring? The very category they use contradicts their own theology.

The question also has an emotional dimension. By saying "favoured you with sons," the verse forces the pagan Arab to confront his own chauvinistic preference, showing that he attributes to God what he would be embarrassed to have for himself.

Thus, the verse methodically dismantles the false doctrine from multiple angles: logic, their own social values, and the fundamental Islamic principle that nothing is comparable to Allah. This sets up the next verse, which vividly describes their disgust at the birth of daughters, further highlighting their hypocrisy.

=17=

This verse paints a striking psychological portrait to shame the pagan Arabs. "The good news" is used ironically here. Normally, glad tidings bring joy, but the news in question is the birth of a daughter, which is precisely what they attribute to Allah, the Most Compassionate. Notice the deliberate use of the name "the Most Compassionate" (Ar-Raḥmān) in this context. The pagans disliked the name Ar-Raḥmān, yet the Quran persistently applies it to Allah to emphasize His encompassing mercy, a mercy they reject by their ingratitude. The reaction described is intensely negative: "his face grows gloomy, as he suppresses his rage." The face darkens with shame and anger. He is embarrassed to face his community and is filled with inner fury, but he holds it back, suppressing his rage rather than expressing joy.

This is the man’s response to a daughter. Now the Quran asks, through its rhetorical force, how can you assign to the Lord of the Worlds something that you yourselves find so humiliating? The emotional reaction captured here serves as irrefutable psychological evidence. They do not merely dislike having daughters; they are devastated by it.

Yet they comfortably claim that angels are Allah’s daughters. This contradictory behaviour demonstrates that their claim is not based on reason or revelation but on blind cultural tradition.

The verse also implicitly defends the dignity of women: the Quran does not endorse the pagan view that daughters are a source of shame; rather, it uses their own inconsistent standard against them. The next verse continues the argument by describing the nature of those whom they call daughters of God.

=18=

The interrogation intensifies by challenging the suitability of the beings they assign as God’s children. The verse describes two characteristics of those they call angels and consider female: they are "brought up in fineries" and they are "not commanding in disputes.

" This description reflects the Arab perception of women in their society. They saw women as being raised amid ornaments and adornments, and they believed women were less capable of articulating clear, forceful arguments in disputes and conflicts. The Quran is not endorsing this view as an eternal truth about women; rather, it is using the pagans’ own stereotype against their theological claim. The argument is: according to your own standards, you consider such beings weak and dependent. How then can you attribute them to the Almighty, who is the All-Powerful and Self-Sufficient? Would the Supreme Lord really have offspring characterized by what you yourselves deem a deficiency? The rhetorical question exposes the absurdity of projecting human gender roles and perceived weaknesses onto the divine realm. The angels are noble, powerful servants of Allah; they are not as the pagans imagined. But even if we temporarily grant the pagan perception for the sake of argument, their claim is still incoherent. The verse silences the objector by trapping him in his own cultural logic. It also continues to highlight the injustice of their claim: they disgrace their own daughters while exalting these supposed daughters of God.

The next verse will explicitly state the reality that the angels are honourable servants, not females or divine offspring, and will question the source of their knowledge.

=19=

This verse directly refutes the pagan belief. The opening "Still they have labelled" indicates that despite all logical arguments, they persist. The angels are correctly identified as "servants of the Most Compassionate," not His children.

The term "servants" (‘ibād) immediately demolishes the idea of any familial relationship. Servants are owned, obedient, and have no share in the master’s essence. The title "the Most Compassionate" is repeated, emphasizing that His mercy should lead to gratitude, not to fabricating offspring for Him.

The verse then says they have labelled the angels "as female." The Quran refutes this by asking, "Did they witness their creation? " This is a powerful epistemological challenge. Where did they get this information? They were not present when the angels were created, so they have no sensory knowledge. They have no scripture that informs them of this either. Their claim is pure conjecture and hearsay, inherited from a polytheistic culture that deified the angels.

The question underscores a fundamental Islamic principle: matters of the unseen cannot be spoken about without divine revelation. The verse then delivers a stern warning: "Their statement will be recorded, and they will be questioned!" Every word they utter in this slander against Allah is being written down meticulously by the recording angels. On the Day of Judgment, they will be held accountable for this great lie.

This is a direct threat and a comfort to the Prophet, assuring him that these false statements are not forgotten by God. The surah now moves to their next flimsy argument, the excuse of divine will, showing how they shift from one lie to another.

=20=

After their claims about angels were refuted, the pagans resort to another argument to justify their idol worship. They say, "Had the Most Compassionate willed, we would have never worshipped them.

" This is a fatalistic statement that shifts blame onto Allah. They claim that their idolatry exists because God allowed it, as if His will constitutes His approval and removes their responsibility.

This is a misuse of the concept of divine decree. The Quran immediately rebuts them: "They have no knowledge in support of this claim." Their argument is not based on any revealed book or rational proof; it is a mere assumption they use to cover their stubbornness. The truth is that Allah’s will encompasses everything, including human misguidance by His permission, but this does not negate human free will and accountability. God has clearly shown the right path through messengers and endowed humans with the ability to choose. When they choose idol worship, they do so by their own volition, and then they deceptively invoke God’s will to escape blame. The verse delivers a harsh verdict: "They do nothing but lie." Their claim is a fabrication against Allah. This lie is twofold: first, they lied by attributing daughters to Him, and now they lie by claiming that He is pleased with their worship of idols. The surah is systematically dismantling every layer of their falsehood. This verse marks the end of the first section of the surah, which has moved from the magnificence of the Quran and the signs of creation to the exposure of pagan irrationality.

The next verses will continue this theme by questioning whether they have any scripture to support their inherited beliefs.
