=81=

After exposing the secret schemes of the polytheists, the surah now instructs the Prophet to deliver a powerful rhetorical rebuttal to their claim that God has children. The command "Say" is a direct address to Muhammad, equipping him with the precise words to counter the false belief that angels are God's daughters. The statement is framed as a conditional: "If the Most Compassionate really had offspring, I would be the first worshipper.

" This is a masterclass in argumentation. The Prophet does not simply say "God has no offspring" in this instance; instead, he uses a hypothetical that exposes the absurdity of their claim from within their own logic.

The name "the Most Compassionate" is used deliberately, echoing the earlier arguments where the pagans disliked this name yet attributed daughters to Him. The word "really" implies that the condition is false and impossible, but even if it were true, the Prophet declares he would be the first to worship that offspring.

However, the context makes it clear that this is an ironic challenge. The Prophet is already the most devoted worshipper of Allah alone. He would never worship a created being. The implication is: if such an offspring existed, he, as the foremost servant of God, would have known about it and been commanded to worship it. Since he has been commanded only to worship the One God, no such offspring exists. The statement also demonstrates that the Prophet's refusal to worship idols is not out of stubbornness but out of strict obedience to divine truth. If God had genuine children, the Prophet would be the most obedient in recognizing them. This argument leaves the pagans with no ground to stand on. It exposes that their claim is not based on any divine command but on their own invention.

The verse also carries a subtle lesson in dealing with false claims: sometimes presenting the logical conclusion of a false belief can highlight its absurdity more effectively than a simple denial. The next verse will transition from this conditional argument to a direct glorification of God, declaring Him far above such fabrications.

=82=

After the conditional statement, the Quran immediately moves to declare the absolute perfection of God. The verse begins with "Glorified is the Lord," which translates the Arabic "subḥāna," a term that negates any imperfection, defect, or unworthy attribute from God. It is the believer's response to any false claim about the Divine. The glorification is then linked to three majestic descriptions: "the Lord of the heavens and the earth, the Lord of the Throne. " The mention of the heavens and the earth encompasses the entire visible universe, over which God has absolute ownership, authority, and nurturing care. The "Throne" represents the highest point of creation, the symbol of God's supreme dominion and majesty. By specifying the Lord of the Throne in addition to the heavens and earth, the verse emphasizes that God's sovereignty is not limited to the physical cosmos but extends to the unseen, supreme realm.

The phrase "far above what they claim" is a comprehensive rejection of all the false attributions the polytheists make. Whether they claim He has daughters, sons, partners, or any likeness to creation, He is transcendently beyond all of it. This verse serves as a healing balm after the tense arguments. The believer's heart finds rest in glorifying God and acknowledging His utter uniqueness. It also teaches the proper adab (etiquette) when discussing divine attributes: always couple any negation of false claims with a positive affirmation of God's perfection. The verse connects to the earlier description of the Quran as originating from the Master Record; the Lord of the Throne is the same One who revealed the wise Book. For the Meccans, the mention of the Throne was also a reminder of the unseen realm they had no knowledge of, yet they made claims about angels. This glorification corrects their vision, lifting their gaze from the idols on the ground to the Throne above the heavens.

The next verse will issue a temporary instruction to the Prophet regarding these stubborn deniers, advising a period of leaving them to their heedlessness until the promised Day arrives.

=83=

After the clear glorification of God and the refutation of their claims, the surah issues a directive that marks a shift in tone. The particle "So" connects this instruction to the preceding truth. Since the matter has been clarified, and they still persist in their falsehood, the Prophet is told to leave them. "Let them indulge in falsehood and amuse themselves.

" This is not an encouragement but a warning phrased as a permissive command. They are to be left to wade deeper into their vain talk, their idol worship, and their worldly amusements.

The Arabic verbs suggest plunging into something with abandon and playing around. This reflects the divine law that when a people persistently reject clear guidance, they are given over to their chosen path until the appointed time of reckoning.

The phrase "until they face their Day, which they have been warned of" identifies the endpoint of this period. "Their Day" is the Day of Judgment, which was repeatedly mentioned in the surah. It is the Day when the Hour takes them by surprise, when friends become enemies, and when the wicked face eternal torment. The warning had been delivered; now the appointment is fixed.

This verse also provided the Prophet and the believers with emotional release. They were not to exhaust themselves in endless argument with those who had made up their minds. Their duty of conveying was fulfilled; the outcome was now in God's hands. There is a deep lesson here about the balance between da‘wah and personal well-being. One does not endlessly chase those who mock and twist the truth. After clear communication, one entrusts them to God.

The verse also carries a subtle promise: their "indulgence" is temporary, and the Day will come when all excuses will vanish. This prepares the way for the final majestic verses of the surah, which reaffirm God's sole divinity, knowledge, and the eventual return to Him.

=84=

The surah now delivers a foundational declaration of tawḥīd, the absolute oneness of God. "It is He Who is the only God in the heavens and the only God on the earth." The repetition of "God" emphasizes that His divinity is not divided into jurisdictions. He is not a local deity for a specific territory; He is the sole object of worship for the inhabitants of the heavens—the angels—and the inhabitants of the earth—humans and jinn. Any deity worshipped besides Him is false, having no real sovereignty or creative power anywhere in the universe. This directly refutes the pagan idea of a supreme God with subordinate intermediaries.

The verse then provides the logical basis for this exclusive divinity: "For He is the All-Wise, All-Knowing." The wisdom and knowledge of God are perfect and all-encompassing. Wisdom implies that all His decrees, laws, and acts of creation are placed in perfect order and for a perfect purpose. Knowledge implies He is aware of everything, including the innermost thoughts and the secret schemes mentioned earlier. Because He is All-Wise, He does not create in vain or share His authority with imperfect creatures. Because He is All-Knowing, He is fully aware of who worships Him and who associates partners with Him. These two attributes are the pillars of His sole right to divinity. An idol made of stone possesses neither wisdom nor knowledge; it cannot even defend itself. How can it share divinity with the One who possesses absolute wisdom and knowledge? The verse ties together the earlier arguments about creation: the Creator of the heavens and earth is necessarily their sole God. The Meccans themselves admitted He was the Creator; they must now admit He is the only God. This verse provides the believer with a concise, powerful summary of monotheism.

The next verse will continue the glorification, adding the dimension of the Hour and the return, further cementing God's absolute sovereignty.

=85=

The surah continues its crescendo of divine praise. "Blessed is the One" uses the Arabic "tabāraka," a word that signifies abundance of goodness, permanence, and transcendence. God is the source of all blessing, and His being is infinitely exalted. The description that follows explains why He is Blessed: "to Whom belongs the kingdom of the heavens and the earth and everything in between." This absolute ownership encompasses every atom, every galaxy, every being, and every event. Nothing falls outside His dominion.

This is the practical meaning of His Lordship. The phrase "everything in between" includes the air, the clouds, the birds, and all the unseen realms between the sky and the earth. Next, the verse singles out a specific aspect of divine knowledge: "With Him alone is the knowledge of the Hour. " The timing of the Day of Judgment is a secret held exclusively by God. No prophet, no angel, no created being has any share in this knowledge.

This is a direct answer to the frequent questions about when the punishment or the Hour would occur. It places the timing completely beyond human inquiry, emphasizing that the focus should be on preparedness, not on calculating dates.

The verse then concludes with the ultimate return: "And to Him you will all be returned." This is the summation of the entire surah's argument. Everything originates from Him, everything belongs to Him, and everything returns to Him. The resurrection is not a strange, isolated event; it is the natural conclusion of a universe under the complete sovereignty of its Creator. The Meccans who are left to amuse themselves will ultimately be returned to Him for judgment. This verse instills in the believer a profound sense of trust and accountability.

The next verse will address the false objects of worship and the concept of intercession, clarifying who can and cannot intercede on that Day.

=86=

After affirming God's sole ownership and knowledge, the surah addresses a critical theological point that the pagans used to justify their idol worship. They would often say, "We worship them only so they may bring us closer to God," treating their idols as intercessors. This verse demolishes that argument. "Those objects of worship they invoke besides Him have no power to intercede." The idols of stone and wood, the angels, the jinn, or the prophets they had deified—none of them possess the independent authority to intercede on behalf of anyone. Intercession on the Day of Judgment is entirely under God's control. No one can intercede except with His permission and for those with whom He is pleased.

The verse then introduces a crucial exception: "except those who testify to the truth knowingly." This refers to those who have borne witness to the truth of God's oneness with full knowledge and conviction. According to many commentators, this includes the angels, the prophets, and the righteous believers, who will be granted the honour of interceding for others by God's permission. Importantly, even their intercession is dependent on God's leave and is itself a testimony to the truth of tawḥīd.

The phrase "testify to the truth" means they bear witness that there is no god but Allah. This testimony is not a mere verbal statement; it is "knowingly," with deep understanding and certainty. So, the verse teaches that idolaters cannot hope for intercession from their false gods because those gods are either inanimate objects or beings who will themselves disown the worship. True intercession is reserved for the camp of tawḥīd. This clarifies the common misconception that intercession could come from anyone other than God's authorized servants. It also encourages believers to uphold the testimony of faith sincerely.

The next verse will return to the logical inconsistency of the Meccans, asking them who created them, and exposing how they are deluded away from the truth.

=87=

The surah circles back to a foundational argument that was mentioned earlier in verse 9, bringing the discussion full circle. The Prophet is told to question the Meccan pagans about their own origin: "If you ask them who created them, they will certainly say, 'Allah!'" As with the creation of the heavens and the earth, the polytheists had no hesitation in acknowledging that their own personal existence came from the one true God. No idol was credited with fashioning them in the womb, giving them sight and hearing, or designing their intricate bodies. This admission is the very heart of the case against them.

The verse then asks, "How can they then be deluded from the truth?" The Arabic word "yu'fakūn" conveys the idea of being turned away, diverted, or deceived. Given that they know their Creator, how is it that they end up worshipping stones and attributing offspring to God? The logical gap between acknowledging Allah as the sole Creator and then worshipping others is inexplicable except through delusion. This delusion is the result of blindly following forefathers, the whispering of satanic associates, and the love of worldly status. The verse expresses astonishment at their mental and spiritual state. It is as if a person acknowledges that the sun gives light, yet spends his day searching for a candle. For the believers, this verse is a reminder to maintain intellectual and spiritual consistency. The One who created us deserves our exclusive worship, love, and obedience. Any diversion from this is a form of delusion.

The question "How can they then be deluded?" is both a lament and a challenge. It invites the listener to ponder the tragic irony of their condition.

The surah now nears its end, and the next verse captures the Prophet's heart-felt cry to his Lord concerning these stubborn people, a cry that God acknowledges and responds to with final instructions.

=88=

The penultimate verse of the surah reveals a deeply personal and emotional moment between the Prophet Muhammad and his Lord. The verse is structured as a divine acknowledgment of the Prophet's lament.

The opening phrase "Allah is Aware of" indicates that God has full knowledge of the Prophet's words, his feelings, and the context in which he uttered them. The cry itself is recorded: "O my Lord! Indeed, these are a people who persist in disbelief.

" This is a statement of fact combined with a sense of sorrow and perhaps a plea for divine judgment. The Prophet had exhausted all efforts, faced continuous mockery, and seen the truth rejected time and again by his own people. The verb "persist in disbelief" translates a form that means they are continuing, they are not just disbelieving but are entrenched and unwilling to change. The Prophet's cry is not a curse; it is a report to his Lord, entrusting the matter to Him. It echoes the cries of previous prophets: Noah's lament that he was defeated, Lot's plea for help, and Moses's prayer against Pharaoh. God records this cry in the Quran not to rebuke the Prophet but to honour him and to show that the Lord hears the sincere complaints of His servants. It also serves to humanize the Prophet, showing his vulnerability and his complete reliance on God. The early Muslims in Mecca, hearing this, would feel their own hearts reflected in the Prophet's words. The verse is a profound lesson in how to handle persistent rejection. One turns to God with the truth of the situation, expressing the reality without animosity, and leaves the outcome to Him.

The final verse of the surah will provide the divine response to this cry, a response filled with wisdom, patience, and a promise of future clarity.

=89=

The surah concludes with a direct divine command to the Prophet, forming a bookend with the earlier instructions about holding firmly to the revelation. The particle "So" links this command to the preceding cry. Because they persist in disbelief, and because the matter is in God's hands, the Prophet's immediate duty is clear: "bear with them and respond with peace.

" The Arabic "faṣfaḥ" translated as "bear with them" means to turn the page, to forgive gracefully, to overlook, and to avoid confrontation. It is a command of magnanimous forbearance. This does not imply approval of their disbelief; it means refraining from personal retaliation and from being consumed by their insults. The addition "and respond with peace" goes further. The Prophet is to greet them with "salām" (peace) as a way of disengaging from futile arguments and entrusting their judgment to God.

This is the greeting of farewell to those who reject the message after it has been made clear. It communicates that the messenger has done his part, bears no personal enmity, and leaves the final outcome to the Lord.

The verse then ends with a powerful, ominous promise: "They will soon come to know." This is an ellipsis that leaves the object unstated but vividly understood. They will come to know the truth of the Quran, the reality of the Hour, and the consequences of their disbelief. This could be in this life, as happened at Badr and the conquest of Mecca, or in the Hereafter, which is certain.

The phrase "soon" indicates the nearness of this realization from God's perspective, even if it seems delayed to humans. This ending ties the entire surah together. It began with the clear Book and the warnings, moved through arguments and historical examples, and concludes with a call to forbearance, peace, and a firm reliance on the coming divine judgment.

For believers, it is a lesson in patience, trust, and the proper etiquette when faced with obstinate rejection. The surah ends not in defeat but in calm assurance and quiet strength.
