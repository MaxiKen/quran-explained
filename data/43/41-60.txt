=41=

The surah continues its direct address to the Prophet Muhammad, offering him further reassurance about the ultimate fate of the obstinate disbelievers. The verse opens with the phrase "Even if We take you away from this world," which refers to the possibility of the Prophet’s death before seeing the punishment descend upon his Meccan opponents. The disbelievers might have thought that if Muhammad died, they would be free from the threat he constantly warned them about. They might have assumed that his death would signal the failure of his mission and the end of the matter. This verse decisively refutes that assumption. Allah declares that the Prophet's departure from this world will not grant the disbelievers any immunity. The words "We will surely inflict punishment upon them" are absolute and emphatic. The punishment can take the form of worldly destruction, as happened to many previous nations, or the torment of the Hereafter, or both. The point is that divine justice is not dependent on the presence of any particular human messenger. The Prophet’s role is to deliver the message. Once delivered, the accounting is in God’s hands. Whether the punishment comes during his lifetime or after his death, it is certain to come.

This verse also addresses a subtle psychological anxiety among the early Muslims. They were a small persecuted minority, and the Prophet was their leader, teacher, and protector. The thought of losing him could have caused them to despair. God reassures them that the mission does not hinge solely on the Prophet’s physical presence. The Lord of the mission is eternal and will ensure its triumph and the punishment of the rejecters. This instills a sense of reliance upon Allah rather than upon individuals. It also carries a warning to those who mock the message: do not think that the death of the warner will save you. The One who sent the warner remains.

The next verse adds the complementary possibility—that the Prophet may in fact witness their punishment during his own lifetime.

=42=

This verse presents the other side of the same coin, creating a balanced picture of divine decree and power. While the previous verse addressed the scenario of the Prophet passing away before the punishment, this one addresses the scenario in which he lives to see it. "Or if We show you what We threaten them with" refers to the possibility that during his lifetime, the Prophet might witness the very punishment Allah has promised the deniers.

Historically, this indeed happened. The Prophet witnessed the decisive defeat of the Quraysh at the Battle of Badr, where their leaders were killed. Later, he entered Mecca victorious, humbling those who had driven him out. So he was shown, in his own lifetime, a foretaste of the punishment in this world. But the verse makes a deeper point with the statement "We certainly have full power over them." Regardless of whether the punishment is shown to the Prophet or delayed, Allah’s power over the disbelievers is absolute, constant, and unchallenged. The timing is a matter of divine wisdom, not of any inability or hesitation. If the punishment is delayed, it is not because God cannot act, but because He has a plan.

The phrase "full power" (qādirūn) indicates perfect capacity and overwhelming strength. The disbelievers are completely within His grip, whether they realize it or not. For the believers, this is a source of confidence. The apparent strength of the forces of falsehood is an illusion; they are powerless before their Creator. The two verses together (41-42) thus form a complete doctrine: the outcome is certain regardless of whether the messenger witnesses it or not, because the source of the power is God, not the messenger.

This teaching frees the Muslim activist from an obsession with immediate visible results. One works for the sake of God, knowing that the results, in this life or the next, are guaranteed by the One who never fails.

The next verse then turns to the imperative command that flows from this certainty: the Prophet is to hold firmly to the revelation.

=43=

After the reassurance about divine punishment, the surah issues a direct and loving command to the Prophet. The particle "So" links the instruction to the preceding truths. Because the fate of the deniers is secure in God’s hands, and because His power is absolute, the Prophet’s sole focus should be on steadfastness. "Hold firmly to what has been revealed to you" uses the Arabic verb istamsik, which implies grabbing onto something with all one’s strength, refusing to let go despite all distractions and pressures. What he is to hold onto is the Quran, the revelation. In the face of mockery, persecution, and the temptation to compromise, the Prophet is to cling to the Book as his anchor. There is a beautiful balance here: the previous verses told him that the punishment of his enemies is God’s responsibility; this verse tells him that his responsibility is unwavering adherence to the message. He is not to be swayed by their insults or their demands for a different kind of messenger.

The verse then provides the reason and the reassurance: "You are truly on the Straight Path. " This confirmation is immensely powerful. The entire Meccan dispute was about whether Muhammad was guided or astray. The Quraysh called him a madman and a sorcerer. Here, the Lord of the universe affirms that he is upon the Straight Path, the path of truth, righteousness, and salvation.

The word "truly" emphasizes the certainty of this fact. It also implies that holding onto the revelation is the very definition of being on the Straight Path; to deviate from the revelation is to deviate from the path.

For believers today, this verse is a timeless instruction. In a world full of ideologies, doubts, and moral confusions, the believer’s safety lies in gripping the Quran firmly, not as a cultural artifact but as the living guide for life. The Straight Path is not a subjective feeling; it is objectively defined by the revealed Book.

The next verse expands on the nature of the Quran as a source of honour, and reminds both the Prophet and his people that they will be questioned about this gift.

=44=

After commanding the Prophet to hold firmly to the revelation, the surah now declares the immense status of the Quran. "Surely this Quran is a glory for you and your people." The Arabic word "dhikr" translated here as "glory" carries multiple rich meanings: it can mean honour, renown, reminder, and exaltation. The Quran is a source of immense honour for the Prophet Muhammad, as it is the eternal miracle given to him, and through it, his name is exalted across the world. But the verse also says it is a glory for "your people," meaning the Quraysh in the first instance, and by extension the entire Arab nation, and ultimately the Muslim ummah. The fact that the final revelation was revealed in their language and through one of their own is a tremendous honour. No other nation received this final testament directly in its own tongue.

However, this honour comes with a weighty responsibility: "And you will all be questioned about it." The gift of the Quran is not a mere privilege; it is a trust. On the Day of Judgment, the Prophet’s people will be asked how they responded to this Book. Did they believe in it? Did they recite it? Did they live by its teachings? Or did they neglect it, mock it, and turn away? The questioning will include gratitude for the honour and accountability for the deeds. This verse served as both a boost to the morale of the early Muslims, affirming their unique status, and a stern warning to the pagan Quraysh who treated the Quran with contempt.

For Muslims today, it is a reminder that having the Quran in our homes and on our tongues is a great honour, but we must live it, or else it may become a witness against us. The "glory" is conditional upon faithful adherence.

The next verse then transitions to a challenge: ask the followers of previous scriptures whether God ever sanctioned idol worship, further solidifying the argument for pure monotheism.

=45=

The surah now provides the Prophet with a powerful method to validate his message against the background of all previous revelation. The command "Ask the followers of the messengers that We already sent before you" is an instruction to consult the scriptures and the learned among the Jews and Christians who were present in Arabia. The core of the question is: did God ever command the worship of any deity besides Himself?

The question is rhetorical, because the answer is clearly no. Every single prophet, from Noah to Jesus, came with the fundamental creed of monotheism. No authentic scripture ever sanctioned idol worship or the deification of angels, prophets, or saints. The specific mention of "the Most Compassionate" in the question is significant. The Meccan pagans recognized Allah as the Supreme Creator but associated partners with Him, and they even disliked the name "the Most Compassionate," as mentioned earlier. By framing the question in this way, the Quran emphasizes that the very God they acknowledge—the Most Compassionate—never allowed any partners. This verse is not a call to adopt the altered scriptures of the People of the Book, but to use the unanimous testimony of all prophetic traditions against idolatry. It establishes a principle of continuity: the message of Muhammad is not a new religion but the restoration of the original, universal faith. If even the distorted scriptures do not contain a command to worship other gods, where did the Meccans get their religion? It must be a baseless invention.

The verse also serves as a da‘wah technique: engage with others on the basis of common recognition of previous messengers, and demonstrate that your call aligns with theirs. The following verses then shift to a specific historical example, the story of Moses and Pharaoh, which parallels the struggle between Muhammad and the Meccan elite, providing both a warning to the disbelievers and a lesson in the pattern of prophetic confrontation.

=46=

The surah now presents the detailed case of Moses as a powerful historical parallel. The opening "Indeed, We sent Moses" draws the Meccans’ attention to a well-known story. The Arabs, through their contact with Jews and Christians, were familiar with the broad outlines of the Exodus. Moses was sent "with Our signs," which included the staff, the shining hand, and the subsequent plagues. His target audience was "Pharaoh and his chiefs," the political and economic elite of Egypt. This directly mirrors the Prophet Muhammad’s mission to the chiefs of the Quraysh. The parallel is striking: both messengers were sent to the ruling class of a powerful, polytheistic society. Moses’s opening declaration was, "I am a messenger of the Lord of all worlds. " This statement is a direct challenge to Pharaoh’s claim to divinity. Pharaoh had declared himself the supreme lord of Egypt, but Moses announced that there is a Lord of all the worlds, far above Pharaoh.

The phrase "Lord of all worlds" encompasses every creation, negating any localized or tribal deity. For the Meccans, this was a reminder that Muhammad’s message of a universal Lord was the same as Moses’s. The verse sets the stage for the conflict: a single, unarmed prophet, armed only with divine signs, confronting a tyrannical superpower. The chiefs are mentioned alongside Pharaoh because the elite class often plays a crucial role in suppressing a prophet’s message, as their vested interests are threatened.

The next verse will describe the immediate reaction of Pharaoh and his court when Moses presented the signs: they did not respond with serious reflection but with scornful laughter, exactly as the Quraysh mocked Muhammad’s recitation and miracles.

=47=

The verse captures the initial response of the Egyptian elite to Moses’s mission. "As soon as he came to them with Our signs" emphasizes that their reaction was immediate and unthinking. There was no pause for contemplation. The signs—the staff transforming into a serpent, the hand becoming luminous—were stunning displays of divine power, far beyond human magic.

Yet the response of Pharaoh and his chiefs was not fear or belief but "they laughed at them. " Mockery is the defense mechanism of the arrogant. When a person is confronted with a truth that threatens his power and lifestyle, laughter can be a way to belittle the evidence and avoid engaging with it seriously. By laughing, Pharaoh and his chiefs were signaling to each other and to the public that Moses was not a threat but a joke.

This is identical to the Meccan response described earlier in the surah, where they called the Quran magic and rejected it. The verb "laughed" implies a group behavior; they encouraged each other in scoffing. This collective mockery increases the pressure on the prophet and creates a hostile environment where it becomes socially unacceptable to take him seriously. The verse serves as a profound consolation to the Prophet Muhammad. He was facing laughter and ridicule in Mecca; now he learns that the great Moses faced the exact same treatment. It is part of the prophetic package.

For believers, this verse underscores the pettiness of the disbelievers’ tactics. Laughter does not refute an argument; it merely exposes the one laughing as unwilling or unable to engage with the truth. The surah will now explain that despite the mockery, the signs kept coming, each greater than the last, demonstrating God’s patience and the increasing severity of His warning before the final punishment.

=48=

This verse highlights the escalating nature of divine signs given to Pharaoh and his people. The phrase "every sign We showed them was greater than the one before" indicates a progression of miracles and plagues, each more severe and more astonishing than the previous. The staff turning into a serpent was mighty, but the plagues that followed—floods, locusts, lice, frogs, and blood—were even more devastating and undeniable. This escalation served a dual purpose. First, it removed any excuse of ignorance. With each new sign, the truth became more manifest. Second, it was an act of mercy, giving them repeated opportunities to recognize their error and turn back.

The verse then says, "Ultimately, We seized them with torments so that they might return to the Right Path. " The word "torments" here refers to the plagues and afflictions that struck Egypt. Importantly, the purpose clause "so that they might return" shows that even these punishments were intended as wake-up calls, not instant destruction. Allah did not immediately drown them; He sent successive hardships to break their arrogance and lead them to repentance. This reveals a profound aspect of God’s justice: even when He punishes, He leaves the door open for reform. The torments were corrective, not merely retributive. For the Meccans listening, this was a clear message. The drought and famine they had experienced, as mentioned elsewhere in the Quran, were similar wake-up calls. If they responded with humility and faith, the punishment would be lifted. The pattern of Moses’s story is a mirror held up to the Quraysh: signs have come, they mocked, and if they persist, the gradual seizing will intensify.

The next verse records the specific plea Pharaoh’s people made when the torments became unbearable, a plea that revealed their continued lack of sincere faith.

=49=

The verse describes the response of Pharaoh’s people when the plagues struck. Overwhelmed by suffering, they turned to Moses, but their address reveals their deep-seated arrogance. They call him "O mighty magician!" Even in their moment of desperation, they refused to acknowledge him as a prophet.

The term "magician" was the label they used to explain away his miracles, and they continued using it. Yet they added "mighty," because they recognized his power and perhaps his elevated status before God. Their request was, "Pray to your Lord on our behalf, by virtue of the covenant He made with you." This shows a curious mixture of belief and disbelief. They admitted that Moses had a special connection with his Lord, the God of the covenant, but they themselves did not commit to worshipping that God directly. They wanted Moses to act as an intermediary to remove the torment.

The phrase "by virtue of the covenant He made with you" refers to the prophetic promise that those who believe will be saved, and possibly the covenant of protection and guidance. Their final pledge was, "We will certainly accept guidance. " This is a conditional promise made under duress. They would become guided, they said, if the torment was removed. The Quran records this to expose the psychology of those who only turn to God when in trouble. Their faith was not genuine; it was a bargaining chip to escape pain.

This is exactly the attitude the Meccans displayed when they said, "If only he would pray for us, we might believe." The next verse will reveal the tragic outcome of this false promise: as soon as the pressure was lifted, they reverted to their old ways without any intention of honoring their word.

=50=

The human tendency to forget God when ease returns is starkly illustrated here. The verse uses the phrase "as soon as," indicating that their reversion was immediate. There was no period of genuine reflection or gradual change of heart. "We removed the torments from them" highlights God’s mercy in responding to the prayer of Moses and lifting the plague.

However, the lifting of the trial was a test in itself. Would they use the respite to reform, or would they return to their tyranny and disbelief?

The answer is recorded with devastating brevity: "they broke their promise." The promise they had made—to accept guidance and to believe—was shattered.

The Arabic word for "broke" implies a complete and deliberate violation. They did not merely forget; they actively reneged. This pattern is well-known in human history. In times of crisis, people cry out to God with sincere promises of change.

Yet when the crisis passes, the majority slip back into their old habits as if the promise never happened. Pharaoh’s people had now exhausted the sequence of signs and plagues. Their repeated promise-breaking had sealed their fate. The divine educational process had been completed, and they had failed the test at every stage. For the Meccans, this was a direct warning. They had witnessed signs and made similar conditional offers. If they continued breaking their implicit promises of accepting guidance if a clear messenger came, they would meet the same fate.

The verse also teaches believers the importance of faithfulness to one's word, especially vows made to God. A promise made in distress must be honored in ease.

The surah now shifts focus to Pharaoh himself, who, instead of learning from the plagues, escalated his arrogance to new heights, openly challenging Moses and belittling him before the people.

=51=

The scene shifts from the plagues to a public address by Pharaoh. After the torments were lifted and Moses’s prayer was answered, Pharaoh felt the need to reassert his authority and downplay the power of Moses. He "called out to his people," making a grand, theatrical proclamation. His words are filled with arrogance: "O my people! Am I not sovereign over Egypt?" He appeals to his undisputed political and territorial dominion. Egypt was the superpower of the age, and Pharaoh was its absolute ruler. Then he draws attention to a specific feature: "as well as all these streams flowing at my feet? " The Nile and its tributaries were the lifeblood of Egypt. By claiming ownership over the rivers, Pharaoh was not just boasting about real estate; he was implying that he controlled the very source of life and prosperity that the people depended on. In the ancient Egyptian worldview, the Pharaoh was considered a god, the giver of the Nile’s bounty. His rhetorical question "Can you not see?" is an appeal to their physical perception. He asks them to observe the obvious—his kingdom and its rivers—and conclude that his power is supreme.

This is a classic tactic of tyrants: distract people with the material world they can see, so they forget the invisible, higher power. Pharaoh contrasts his visible, tangible kingdom with the unseen Lord that Moses speaks about. The verse exposes the psychology of worldly power. Pharaoh did not engage with the content of Moses’s message; he deflected by pointing to his own splendor.

The next verse will reveal how he then personally attacked Moses, using class prejudice and ableism to belittle God’s messenger.

=52=

Pharaoh’s arrogance now takes an intensely personal turn. After boasting about his kingdom, he compares himself directly to Moses. He asks his people, "Am I not better than this nobody?

" The Arabic word "mahīn" translated as "nobody" carries the connotation of being contemptible, weak, and of no social standing. Pharaoh is drawing on class snobbery. Moses, though raised in Pharaoh’s palace, had fled Egypt as a fugitive and returned as a shepherd-prophet with no army or wealth. By calling him a nobody, Pharaoh appeals to the elite’s disdain for the apparently lowly. His second attack is on Moses’s speech: "who can hardly express himself. " This refers to the speech impediment that Moses had, which he himself mentioned in his supplication to Allah when he said, "And untie the knot from my tongue so that they may understand my speech." Pharaoh exploits this physical difficulty to argue that Moses is not even capable of clear communication, so how can he be a messenger with an important message?

This is a profoundly ableist and arrogant argument. A speech impediment has no bearing on the truth of a person’s message or their intellectual capacity.

Yet to a crowd swayed by superficial appearances, it might be an effective smear. The verse reveals the moral bankruptcy of Pharaoh’s position. When a person cannot refute the truth with logic, he resorts to ad hominem attacks, mocking appearance, status, or disability. This paralleled the Meccans’ attacks on the Prophet Muhammad for not being a wealthy leader.

The next verse continues Pharaoh’s speech, where he lists the material expectations he believes a true messenger should have met, further exposing his worldly criteria for truth.

=53=

Pharaoh continues his tirade with a list of demands that, according to his materialistic worldview, would authenticate a messenger from God. "Why then have no golden bracelets of kingship been granted to him?" In ancient Egypt, and in many ancient cultures, golden bracelets were a sign of royalty, authority, and divine favour. Pharaoh’s reasoning was: if Moses is truly sent by the Lord of all worlds, his Lord would have adorned him with the trappings of worldly power. He should have arrived with wealth and a royal entourage.

This is the exact same logic used by the Meccan chiefs when they asked, "If only this Quran was revealed to a great man from one of the two cities." The second demand is, "or angels come with him as escorts!" Pharaoh expected that a messenger from God would not come alone and vulnerable but would be accompanied by visible angelic warriors, serving as his bodyguards and proving his divine mandate through sheer celestial force. This demand had been made by the Quraysh as well; they asked why angels were not sent down with Muhammad. Both Pharaoh and the Meccans failed to understand the nature of the worldly test. Faith must be based on free will and recognition of truth through signs, not on overwhelming force that compels belief. If angels came as visible escorts, the test of faith would be nullified.

Moreover, the lack of worldly pomp is actually a sign of the messenger’s truthfulness; he relies entirely on God, not on earthly wealth and armies. Pharaoh’s speech, recorded in these verses, is a masterclass in tyrannical propaganda, combining class contempt, personal mockery, and a false standard of divine authentication.

The next verse will summarize the tragic result of this propaganda: the people, who should have thought critically, were fooled and followed him.

=54=

The Quran provides a concise evaluation of Pharaoh’s propaganda and its effect. "And so he fooled his people." The Arabic verb "istakhaffa" means to make light, to deceive, to treat as lightweight and manipulate. Pharaoh, through his boasts and mockery, managed to make the truth seem insignificant and his own power seem paramount. He played on their emotions, their national pride, and their fear of losing social standing. As a result, "they obeyed him." The Egyptian masses, instead of rallying behind the prophet who brought liberation and monotheism, chose to remain in the comfort of their familiar oppression, obeying their tyrant.

This is a profound lesson in mass psychology. Eloquent, powerful leaders can easily sway populations away from truth by appealing to base instincts.

The verse then passes a divine judgment on the entire people: "They were truly a rebellious people." The word "fāsiqīn" denotes those who have left the bounds of obedience to God and have plunged into moral corruption. Their rebellion was not just in disobeying the messenger; it was in their entire lifestyle and their willing submission to a false god. This condemnation shows that the people were not innocent victims of Pharaoh; they were complicit. They chose him, they believed his lies, and they shared in his rebellion. For the Meccans, the parallel was unmistakable. The chiefs of Quraysh were manipulating the masses to reject Muhammad, and the masses were obeying, thus earning a share of the sin. The verse serves as a warning to all people to think for themselves and not blindly follow charismatic leaders who stand against God’s messengers.

The next verse will now describe the final, decisive consequence of this entrenched rebellion: divine wrath and total destruction.

=55=

This verse marks the end of the long divine patience with Pharaoh and his people. The phrase "when they enraged Us" uses a strong Arabic expression that indicates they had crossed every limit, and their persistent disobedience, mockery, and persecution had provoked the divine anger to its fullest measure.

This is an anthropomorphic expression, whose reality is known to Allah, but it conveys the idea that God’s forbearance had reached its appointed term. Their repeated promise-breaking, their slander of the messenger, and their attempted annihilation of the Children of Israel had filled the cup of their sins. The punishment is described succinctly but terrifyingly: "We inflicted punishment upon them, drowning them all." The act of drowning is particularly poignant because Pharaoh had boasted about the rivers flowing at his feet. The very water he considered a symbol of his sovereignty became the instrument of his destruction.

The phrase "them all" confirms that the punishment was comprehensive; not just Pharaoh, but his army and the rebellious elite who supported him perished in the sea. There was no escape for those who had mocked and defied God. This event is one of the most dramatic examples of divine justice in human history. For the Prophet Muhammad and the believers in Mecca, this verse was a powerful promise. The Lord who drowned Pharaoh is the same Lord who will deal with the persecutors in Mecca. The patterns of history repeat: arrogance, followed by divine warning, followed by mockery, and finally, if there is no repentance, total destruction.

The next verse will explain that this destruction was not just an end but a beginning—a lasting lesson for all future generations.

=56=

The destruction of Pharaoh and his people was not merely an isolated historical event; it served a permanent educational purpose. The verse states, "And We made them an example and a lesson for those after them.

" The word "example" (salafan) implies a precedent, a case study that later generations can refer to. The word "lesson" (mathalan) means a parable or a striking illustration. Together, they indicate that the story of Pharaoh has become a timeless warning. Every future tyrant who considers himself powerful should remember the one who said, "Am I not sovereign over Egypt?" and is now at the bottom of the sea. Every person who mocks a prophet should recall that the mockers of Moses perished. The lesson is not restricted to a specific era; it transcends time. The Quran frequently tells the story of Moses and Pharaoh precisely because its lessons are so universally applicable.

The phrase "for those after them" encompasses the Meccans directly, as they are the immediate audience, and all humanity until the Day of Judgment. The preservation of this story in the Quran means that Pharaoh’s humiliation continues to be recited and pondered. In a sense, Pharaoh’s worldly power was completely erased, and the only legacy he now has is as a symbol of arrogant disbelief and its consequences.

For believers, this verse instills a sense of history and a firm belief in moral causality. The world is not ruled by chance; the fall of tyrants is a divine law.

The surah now makes a sharp transition, moving from the well-known story of Moses to a contemporary argument the Meccans were making. They had begun to use Jesus, the son of Mary, as a debating point to justify their own idol worship, and the Quran will now address that argument in detail.

=57=

This verse introduces a new and specific incident that occurred in Mecca. The context, as explained by many commentators, is that when the Quranic verse was revealed stating that the idolaters and what they worship will be fuel for Hell, the Meccans sought to trap the Prophet in an argument. They said, "We accept that our idols will be in Hell, but what about Jesus, whom the Christians worship? Will he be in Hell too? " They thought they had found a clever rebuttal because Jesus is a revered prophet in Islam. When they presented this argument, "your people broke into joyful applause.

" The Arabic word for "applause" here implies loud laughter, shouting, and clamor. They were overjoyed, thinking they had cornered the Prophet. They assumed that either he would say Jesus is in Hell—offending the Christians—or he would admit an exception, which they would then use to justify their idols. The verse highlights their attitude: they were not interested in a sincere search for truth. They were citing the son of Mary merely as a tool for debate, a point to score.

The mention of "son of Mary" rather than his common title of Messiah emphasizes his human birth, which is central to the Islamic rebuttal. Jesus was a human prophet born of a woman, not a divine being. The Meccans’ clamor reveals their deep-seated desire to discredit the message rather than understand it.

The next verse will record their specific question and immediately expose its insincerity, explaining that they are a people who love to dispute without genuine faith.

=58=

The verse explicitly quotes the argument the Meccans were raising with great excitement. "They exclaimed, 'Which is better: our gods or Jesus?'" This question was a trap. Their logic was that since Christians worship Jesus and yet Jesus is considered a righteous servant of God in the Quran, then perhaps their own worship of idols could also be excused, or at least it would mean that Jesus and their idols share a similar fate. If Jesus were to be placed in Hell, they would have cried blasphemy against a respected prophet. The Quran immediately dismisses the sincerity of their inquiry: "They cite him only to argue. " Their purpose was not to learn about Jesus or to seek guidance; it was to engage in polemics and to score rhetorical points.

The word "argue" (jadala) implies a contentious, quarrelsome debate that aims at victory, not truth. The verse then delivers a diagnosis: "In fact, they are a people prone to dispute.

" This is a fundamental character trait of those who reject faith. When faced with clear signs, they resort to endless argumentation, finding obscure comparisons and hypothetical scenarios to distract from the main message. The Meccans were not genuinely confused about the status of Jesus compared to their idols; they knew deep down that Jesus was a prophet and their idols were stones. Their question was a smokescreen. The Quran’s response, which will unfold in the next verses, is to set the record straight about Jesus. He was not a god; he was a blessed servant and a sign for the Children of Israel, and his true message was monotheism, exactly what Muhammad was calling to. The debate tactic of the Meccans thus backfired, as it provided the Quran with an opportunity to reaffirm the core Islamic creed about Jesus.

=59=

The Quran now directly addresses the Meccans’ misuse of Jesus by defining his true status with absolute clarity. "He was only a servant." The Arabic particle "in" (translated here as "only") carries a restrictive force: Jesus was nothing more than a servant. This completely negates the Christian deification of Jesus and any attempt by the pagans to equate him with divine beings.

The word "servant" (‘abd) is the highest honour for a human being; it signifies total submission to God and complete freedom from any claim to independent divinity. Jesus himself is quoted in the Quran as saying, "Indeed, I am the servant of Allah." Next, Allah says, "We showed favour to" him. This favour includes his miraculous birth, his prophethood, the Gospel, his miracles of healing the blind and raising the dead by God’s permission, and his elevation. All these were divine gifts, not evidence of his own divinity. Then the verse states, "and made as an example for the Children of Israel." Jesus was a sign and a lesson specifically sent to the Israelites, calling them back to the true spirit of the Torah. His mission was primarily to the lost sheep of Israel, as a reformer. This refutes the Meccan argument on multiple levels. First, Jesus was a servant, not a god, so the comparison with their idols is baseless because idols are false gods, while Jesus was a human prophet. Second, Jesus was sent to a specific nation with a specific message, which aligned perfectly with the message of all prophets: worship Allah alone.

Therefore, Jesus himself would be the first to condemn the worship of anyone besides Allah. The Meccans’ attempt to use Jesus as a justification for idolatry is thus shown to be completely illogical.

The next verse will confront a deeper claim: the idea that God could have had offspring or that angels could replace humans, establishing a powerful principle about divine will and creation.

=60=

This verse expands the discussion from Jesus to a broader theological principle. After clarifying that Jesus was a servant and a sign, Allah now addresses the human arrogance that leads to polytheism. "Had We willed, We could have easily replaced you all with angels.

" This statement is directed at all humanity, including the Meccans. God declares that if He had wished, He would not have sent human prophets at all; He could have populated the earth with angels instead of humans, and angels could have succeeded one another as inhabitants. The point is that the presence of humans, the sending of human messengers, and the existence of a human like Jesus are all results of a deliberate divine will, not an accident or a sign of any human’s intrinsic divinity. The angels, who are pure and obedient servants, could have been the dwellers of earth, but God chose to create humans with free will and to test them. This verse indirectly answers those who objected to a human prophet like Muhammad or who deified a human like Jesus. God says: if I wanted the messengers to be angels, I could have easily done so, and I could have replaced you all with angels. But in My wisdom, I chose a different arrangement.

The phrase "succeeding one another on earth" implies successive generations of angels, just as humans succeed one another. This underscores God’s absolute power; the current human race is not irreplaceable. The verse humbles human beings by reminding them that their existence on earth is not a necessity but a temporary test. If they fail, God’s power is not diminished; He can bring forth other creatures. This also ties back to the discussion about Jesus: his miraculous birth without a father does not make him divine, because God’s creative power is such that if He willed, He could replace all of humanity with a completely different kind of creature, without any process of normal procreation. The surah will now continue with the next verse, which speaks of Jesus’s second coming as a sign of the Hour, linking back to the refutation of those who dispute about him.
