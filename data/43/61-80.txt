=61=

After clarifying the true nature of Jesus as a servant and a sign, the Quran now reveals a profound eschatological truth. The pronoun “his” refers to Jesus, the son of Mary, and “his second coming” translates the Arabic word “nuzūl,” indicating a descent from the heavens. This verse informs us that the return of Jesus to earth before the end of time is a certain sign that the Hour, the Day of Judgment, is approaching.

The phrase “truly a sign for the Hour” leaves no room for ambiguity; it is divinely established knowledge. This event is part of the unseen, revealed through the Prophet Muhammad, and it serves as a bridge between the discussion of Jesus’s first mission and the final reckoning.

The verse then delivers two urgent commands: “So have no doubt about it, and follow me.” The doubt prohibited here encompasses skepticism about the Hour itself and about the return of Jesus as its precursor. The believers are to accept these truths with firm conviction and not allow the arguments of those who dispute to shake their faith. The command “follow me” is placed on the lips of the Prophet Muhammad, calling all humanity to his guidance, which is the same guidance brought by Jesus and all previous prophets.

The following declaration, “This is the Straight Path,” encapsulates the entire message. The Straight Path is the path of monotheism, submission to God, and preparation for the inevitable Hour. This verse directly answers the Meccans who were using Jesus for their polemics. Instead of arguing about his status, they should recognize him as a sign pointing to the ultimate reality and then follow the final messenger who confirms that reality. The certainty of the Hour and the second coming should inspire a sense of urgency and steadfastness, moving the believer from pointless debate to sincere action.

=62=

Following the clear command to follow the Straight Path, this verse issues a critical warning about the primary obstacle on that path. The address “do not let Satan hinder you” directly engages the listener’s will. Hindrance from Satan can take many forms: whispering doubts, beautifying sins, promoting procrastination, and inciting disputes that distract from the essential truths just mentioned.

The Arabic term “ṣadd” implies turning someone away and creating a barrier. Satan’s goal is to block humanity from the Straight Path, especially after the clear signs of the Hour and the coming of Jesus have been mentioned. The justification for this vigilance is given immediately: “for he is certainly your sworn enemy.

” The word “sworn” emphasizes an ancient, declared, and permanent enmity that dates back to the moment Satan refused to prostrate to Adam. This enmity is not passive; it is active, cunning, and relentless. Recognizing Satan as an enemy is the first step toward being on guard against him. A believer who forgets this enmity becomes an easy target. The verse links back to the earlier passage in the surah about those who turn away from the Reminder and are assigned a satanic associate. Here, the remedy is proactive: do not let him hinder you in the first place. The instruction also echoes the temptation of Adam in Paradise, where Satan succeeded in making him slip. Now, in the earthly life, the battle continues, but the believers are equipped with guidance and warned about the enemy’s tactics. Since Satan’s ultimate objective is to make people miss the Straight Path and thus face ruin on the Hour, this verse functions as a spiritual alarm. It connects the theological discussion about Jesus and the Hour directly to the personal struggle of every individual, reminding that guidance is not just intellectual assent but a constant, vigilant walk along a path where an enemy lurks.

=63=

Returning to the first coming of Jesus, this verse provides the authentic content of his message, contrasting it with the distortions that arose later. The phrase “When Jesus came with clear proofs” refers to the miracles and the pure gospel that validated his prophethood. His declaration begins with a summary of his mission: “I have come to you with wisdom.” Wisdom encompasses the profound understanding of God, the purpose of life, and the moral and spiritual teachings that guide human conduct. It is not mere knowledge but the ability to apply truth in the most beneficial way. Jesus then explains the function of his message: “and to clarify to you some of what you differ about.” The Children of Israel had fallen into deep sectarian disputes over the interpretation of the Torah, over legal rulings, and over theological concepts. Jesus came to settle some of these disagreements, bringing them back to the spirit of the law.

The word “some” indicates that not all disputes were resolved in his mission; the final and complete clarification awaited the coming of the Prophet Muhammad. After stating his mission, Jesus gives the same fundamental command all prophets give: “So fear Allah, and obey me.” Fear of Allah is the basis of all goodness, a consciousness that prevents transgression. Obedience to the messenger is the practical path of that fear, because the messenger delivers God’s commands. This verse dismantles any notion that Jesus came to establish a new religion divorced from the law or to claim divinity. He came as a wise teacher, a clarifier of disputed matters, and a caller to the worship of the One God. The Meccans who were arguing about Jesus are thus invited to hear his own words, which perfectly align with the Quranic message.

The next verse records the core of his theological call, the absolute oneness of God.

=64=

Continuing the words of Jesus, this verse presents the uncompromising monotheism that formed the heart of his message. Jesus declares, “Surely Allah alone is my Lord and your Lord.” By saying “my Lord and your Lord,” he places himself and his followers on the same level of servitude before God. He does not claim a special divine status; he is a servant, and they are servants.

The emphasis “alone” is a direct refutation of any partners, whether that be the deification of Jesus himself, the Holy Spirit, Mary, or any idol. The command that flows from this is immediate and clear: “so worship Him alone.” Worship, in its comprehensive Islamic sense, includes prayer, supplication, obedience, love, and reverence. None of these can be directed to anyone or anything besides Allah. Jesus then seals his message with the identical phrase used by the Prophet Muhammad in the earlier verse: “This is the Straight Path.

” The use of the same phrase across two prophets shows the unity of the divine message. The Straight Path is not a new invention of Muhammad; it was the path of Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and all the prophets.

This is a devastating response to the Meccans’ attempt to use Jesus to justify their idolatry. Jesus himself explicitly commands the worship of Allah alone and defines that as the Straight Path.

Therefore, those who worship Jesus, or Mary, or idols, are deviating from the very path Jesus established. The verse also beautifully links to the earlier command to the Prophet to hold firmly to the Quran and to say “follow me, this is the Straight Path.” Here, Jesus says the same. The religion of all prophets is one, and its essence is tawḥīd. The surah will now describe how, despite this crystal-clear message, the followers of Jesus fractured into sects and fell into disagreement, earning a stern warning.

=65=

After quoting the pure monotheistic call of Jesus, the Quran addresses the historical reality that followed. The word “Yet” signals a tragic turn. Despite the clarity of Jesus’s message, the communities that came after him “differed among themselves about him.” The “various groups” refer to the Christian sects that emerged, each holding a different, often contradictory, doctrine about the nature of Jesus. Some claimed he was God, some said he was the son of God, some said he was one of three in a trinity, while others denied his crucifixion or held other divergent views. These differences were not based on any ambiguity in Jesus’s own teaching but arose from later theological speculation, political influences, and the interpolation of pagan ideas. The verse is not only about historical Christianity but serves as a warning against the very thing the Meccans were doing: splitting into factions and disputing about a prophet instead of accepting the clear guidance he brought.

The verse then pronounces a severe warning: “so woe to the wrongdoers when they face the torment of a painful Day. ” The word “woe” is an exclamation of impending destruction and grief. The wrongdoers are those who knowingly distorted the truth about Jesus or stubbornly clung to false beliefs after the truth had been made clear. The “painful Day” is the Day of Judgment. The torment will be agonizing, and their regret will be immense when they see the prophet they argued about disowning their fabrications. This verse connects the discussion back to the surah’s central theme of warning the deniers. It tells the Meccans that their current arguments and divisions about the Prophet Muhammad mirror the earlier divisions about Jesus, and if they persist, a similar “woe” awaits them.

=66=

The verse shifts from the specific dispute about Jesus to the broader, universal attitude of heedlessness. The rhetorical question “Are they waiting” exposes the foolishness of those who persist in disbelief and argumentation. They act as if they have all the time in the world, as if the Hour is a distant, theoretical event that they need not worry about. The phrasing “take them by surprise” describes the sudden, unforeseen arrival of the Hour. The Day of Judgment will not come with a gradual warning that allows people to prepare at the last minute. It will occur in an instant, while people are going about their daily business, utterly unprepared. The expression “when they least expect it” emphasizes the element of complete surprise.

This is a recurring Quranic theme: the Hour will come suddenly, like a thief in the night, as mentioned in other scriptures and hadith. The disbelievers who mock the concept of the Hereafter and engage in endless theological disputes are, in reality, standing on the edge of an abyss. Their arguments and their rejection do not delay the Hour by a single second. This verse serves as a wake-up call. Instead of arguing about Jesus or idols, they should be preparing for the inevitable event that will render all those arguments meaningless.

The question also implies a sense of impending doom. The Meccans had been warned again and again, yet they continued to dispute. The verse suggests that the only thing left for them is to wait for the sudden seizure of the Hour, which will come upon them while they are lost in their worldly distractions. This sets the stage for a vivid contrast between the fate of the righteous and the wicked on that Day, which unfolds in the following series of verses.

=67=

The surah now draws back the curtain on the social reality of the Day of Judgment. The verse begins with a powerful statement about the transformation of earthly relationships. “Close friends will be enemies to one another on that Day.

” The word “close friends” translates the Arabic “akhillā’,” which refers to intimate, devoted companions who shared their deepest secrets and shaped each other’s lives. On the Day of Judgment, those friendships that were based on anything other than faith and righteousness will turn into bitter enmity. The friends who encouraged each other in sin, who mocked the prophets together, who found comfort in collective disbelief, will suddenly see each other as the cause of their ruin. Each will blame the other for leading them astray. This reversal is a recurring theme in the Quran, as seen in verses where people say, “I wish I had not taken so-and-so as a friend. ” Then the verse introduces a crucial exception: “except the righteous.” The righteous (muttaqūn) are those who were mindful of God. Their friendship was based on love for God and mutual support in truth and patience. That bond survives death and is actually perfected in the Hereafter. Instead of turning into enmity, their friendship becomes an eternal source of joy. This exception provides hope and guidance. It tells the believers that the beloved companionship they enjoy for God’s sake will not be lost; it will be elevated.

For the disbelievers, however, the verse is a stark warning: the social circles they prized so highly in Mecca, the alliances of the chiefs, the camaraderie of the marketplace, will all implode into mutual hatred when the reality of the punishment dawns. The next verse will describe the direct divine address that the righteous will receive, in sharp contrast to the regret and enmity of the wicked.

=68=

The surah transitions from the general statement about the righteous to the specific, intimate words they will hear from their Lord on the Day of Judgment. The address begins with “O My servants,” a phrase of immense honour and affection. The direct possessive “My” connects the servant to the Master in a bond of love and recognition. While the disbelievers are experiencing the breakdown of all their worldly friendships, the righteous are receiving a personal greeting from the Lord of the Worlds. The content of the address is a comprehensive negation of all forms of distress: “There is no fear for you Today, nor will you grieve.” Fear relates to the future; the righteous will be told that they have nothing to worry about regarding what lies ahead. The terror of the Day, the horrors of the Reckoning, the possibility of punishment—all are removed from them. Grief relates to the past; they will not sorrow over the trials of worldly life, the loved ones they lost, or the missed opportunities, because all of that has been compensated and surpassed.

The word “Today” emphasizes the immense shift; this specific Day, which is so terrifying for others, is a day of peace for them. This divine assurance is the ultimate fulfillment of the Quranic promise that the allies of God will have no fear nor grief. The verse encapsulates the entire reward in a few words: complete emotional and psychological security. For the early Muslims facing persecution, the promise that one day they would hear these words directly from God was a source of strength that far outweighed the temporary anxiety inflicted by the Quraysh.

The next verse provides the identity of these fortunate servants, linking the blessing to their faith and submission in the world.

=69=

This verse defines the qualities that made the righteous deserving of the divine address of peace. It begins with “those who believed in Our signs.” The signs encompass the verses of the Quran, the evidences in the natural world, and the miracles given to the prophets. Their belief was not a vague acknowledgment but a deep, convinced faith that penetrated their hearts and influenced their actions.

The second quality is “and fully submitted to Us.” The Arabic word is “muslimūn,” derived from the same root as Islam. It denotes a complete, willing surrender of one’s entire being to God. This submission is not just an external ritual but an inner state of obedience, humility, and trust. The verse pairs belief and submission, showing that they are inseparable. True belief necessarily leads to submission, and submission is the outward manifestation of inner faith.

The use of the past tense (“believed” and “submitted”) anchors their eligibility in their worldly life choices. They are not given this honour arbitrarily; they earned it through their commitment while on earth.

The word “fully” captures the comprehensiveness of their submission; it was not selective or partial. They did not accept some commands and reject others, nor did they worship God while clinging to idols or their own desires.

This verse also subtly answers the earlier Meccan argument about blind following. The righteous are those who saw the signs, used their intellect to believe, and then surrendered their will. They did not merely follow forefathers.

For believers today, the verse presents a clear blueprint: the path to safety on the Day of Judgment is through active belief in the revealed signs and a total, uncompromising submission to God. The next verse will pronounce the ultimate invitation, the call to enter Paradise in a state of joy.

=70=

The divine address to the righteous continues with the long-awaited command. After reassuring them that there is no fear or grief, the Lord invites them into His eternal hospitality. The single word “Enter” is a gate opening to infinite bliss.

The mention of “you and your spouses” is a beautiful recognition of the bonds of marriage that were formed in faith and righteousness. The spouses who shared the journey of belief and submission will be reunited in Paradise, not as former partners with their worldly flaws, but as purified, joyful companions. This inclusion also speaks to the emotional completeness of Paradise. The righteous will not enter alone; their loved ones who qualify will be with them.

The word “rejoicing” describes their state as they cross the threshold. Their faces will be radiant with happiness, their hearts overflowing with gratitude and delight. This rejoicing is the perfect counterpart to the grief and fear that have been removed. It is a positive, active joy, not just the absence of pain. This verse fulfills the deep human longing for lasting happiness, security, and the continuation of loving relationships. For the persecuted believers in Mecca, who had lost family members due to their faith, the promise of entering Paradise with their believing spouses was a powerful consolation.

The verse also contrasts sharply with the fate of the wicked described later, who will be driven into Hell in disgrace. The sequence of these verses—the enmity of worldly friends, the divine address of peace, the description of their faith, and the entry into Paradise—forms a coherent and moving portrait of the final outcome.

The next verses will paint a picture of the physical delights that await them inside, using imagery that resonates with human desires while pointing to realities far beyond our comprehension.

=71=

The surah now offers a glimpse into the material beauty of Paradise, described in terms the human mind can grasp. “Golden trays and cups will be passed around to them” depicts a scene of luxurious hospitality. Gold in this world is a rare and cherished metal, a symbol of wealth for which people strive and compete. In Paradise, it is a common material for serving ware, passed around casually among the believers by immortal youths. This imagery conveys a sense of honour, abundance, and freedom from toil. The believers are not scrambling for gold; it circulates among them as they recline in comfort. Then the verse expands the description to encompass the infinite scope of Paradise’s pleasures: “There will be whatever the souls desire and the eyes delight in.

” This is a comprehensive, all-inclusive promise. Every longing that a purified soul could conceive will be fulfilled. Every beautiful sight that could bring joy to the eyes will be present.

This statement covers both the physical and the spiritual; the delights of the eyes include magnificent gardens, rivers, and companions, while the desires of the souls include peace, love, knowledge, and nearness to God. The phrase assures the believers that Paradise is not a place of mere physical gratification but a realm where every deep, purified human aspiration finds its fulfillment.

The verse ends with the crown of all blessings: “And you will be there forever.” The Arabic “khālidūn” emphasizes eternal duration without any interruption, decline, or death. This permanence is what elevates Paradise above any worldly pleasure, which is always tainted by the fear of its end. The combination of golden vessels, fulfilled desires, and eternal stay creates a complete picture of the ultimate reward, making the fleeting enjoyments of the disbelievers, which were described earlier, seem utterly pathetic in comparison.

The next verse will explain that this is not a random gift but a direct reward for past deeds.

=72=

After describing the delights of Paradise, the verse grounds that reward in divine justice. The phrase “That is the Paradise” points back to all the previously mentioned joys, identifying them collectively as the inheritance of the righteous. The passive construction “you will be awarded” translates an Arabic verb that means to be made to inherit or to be given as a due. The imagery of inheritance is powerful: just as an heir receives property as a legal right, the believers will receive Paradise as their rightful portion. This concept is further emphasized by the clause “for what you used to do.” This establishes that Paradise, while ultimately a mercy from God, is not given arbitrarily. It is tied to the deeds performed in the worldly life.

The phrase “what you used to do” encompasses every act of faith, every prayer, every charity, every moment of patience, every struggle against temptation, and every act of kindness. The verse refutes any notion of salvation without works. While entry into Paradise is by God’s mercy, the degree and the specific honour are correlated with deeds. This balance between divine grace and human effort is central to Islamic theology. The believers are given credit and honour for their deeds, which God, in His mercy, has accepted and rewarded manifold. This verse serves as both a comfort and a motivation. For the believers listening in Mecca, their small acts of worship and their endurance of persecution were accumulating into this eternal inheritance. They were not suffering in vain; every difficulty was a deposit toward Paradise. The surah will now continue the description of Paradise by mentioning the abundant food, completing the picture of provision and delight before pivoting to the grim contrast of the wicked’s fate.

=73=

The imagery of Paradise continues with a specific mention of its culinary delights. The verse says, “There you will have abundant fruit to eat from.” Fruit in the Quranic description of Paradise symbolizes several things: it represents the effortless provision of the best and most refreshing foods; it points to beauty and a variety of colours and tastes; and it recalls the original Garden of Eden where Adam and Eve were permitted to eat freely from all trees except one.

The word “abundant” indicates that there is no scarcity, no seasonality, and no toil required to harvest. The fruit is always ripe, always within reach, and always exactly to one’s liking.

The phrase “to eat from” implies continuous enjoyment without any negative consequences, such as illness or satiety that diminishes pleasure. In the harsh desert environment of Arabia, where fresh fruit was a luxury and often scarce, the promise of abundant fruit was particularly attractive. The Meccans who prided themselves on the trade caravans that brought goods from Syria and Yemen would understand that Paradise offers a provision that does not require travel, risk, or money.

Furthermore, this mention of fruit connects to the broader Quranic theme of the believers’ reward as a fulfilment of the metaphor of a good tree with deep roots and ever-giving branches, like the palm tree. The verse is short, but its placement is strategic. After mentioning the psychological and spiritual rewards, the Quran returns to a tangible, sensory delight to make the picture complete. This balance appeals to the whole human being, which is both soul and body.

The surah now abruptly shifts from the serene picture of Paradise to the horrifying reality of the wicked in Hell, creating a powerful contrast that heightens the impact of both.

=74=

With a strong contrastive particle “Indeed,” the surah pivots from the righteous to the wicked. The “wicked” are the opposites of the righteous described earlier; they are those who disbelieved in the signs, refused to submit, and persisted in wrongdoing. Their fate is stated with stark brevity: they “will be in the torment of Hell forever.

” The word “torment” encompasses both physical and psychological suffering. The mention of “forever” seals their fate with terrifying finality. Unlike the fleeting punishment of this world or the corrective plagues described earlier in the story of Pharaoh, the Hellfire is an eternal abode for the unrepentant disbelievers. This permanence is one of the most distressing aspects of Hell, as it extinguishes all hope of relief. The contrast with Paradise is deliberate and structured. In Paradise, the believers are told they will be there forever as a source of joy and security. In Hell, the eternity of the stay is the ultimate despair.

The verse also serves as a powerful rebuttal to the Meccan pagans who denied the reality of the Hereafter. Their mockery and their claim that they would never be resurrected are answered with this decisive statement. The “wicked” are not a small, isolated group; they include the arrogant leaders, the blind followers, the mockers of prophets, and all those who, after the truth had been made clear to them, chose to reject it. For the believers, this verse intensifies gratitude for salvation and reinforces the fear of God’s punishment.

The next verse will elaborate on the nature of the torment, emphasizing that it is not a light or temporary sentence but an unrelenting agony.

=75=

This verse adds two crucial details about the torment of Hell. The first is that “it will never be lightened for them.

” The word “never” is an absolute negation. The punishment will not be reduced in intensity even for a moment. In worldly suffering, pain often fluctuates; there are moments of relative ease or numbness. But in Hell, the torment is constant and unremitting. This permanence of full intensity crushes any hope that the punishment might become bearable over time.

The second detail is even more psychologically devastating: “and there they will be overwhelmed with despair. ” The Arabic word for “despair” (mublisūn) describes a state of utter hopelessness, silence, and dejection. A person who despairs sees no way out, no future, no rescue.

This is the complete opposite of the state of the righteous, who are told they will never grieve. In Hell, the inhabitants are not just in physical pain; they are consumed by the psychological torment of knowing that their situation is eternal and will never change. The combination of unrelenting physical pain and absolute despair creates a picture of total ruin.

This verse also refutes the false hopes that some people entertain—that perhaps Hell is purgatorial and temporary, or that God’s mercy will eventually override His justice for everyone. While God’s mercy is vast, His justice is also true, and the Quran clearly states the eternal nature of punishment for those who die in unrepentant disbelief. The Meccans who mocked the concept of a painful Day are being told exactly what that Day entails.

The following verse will establish the justice behind this terrible punishment, removing any suggestion that God is unfair.

=76=

The Quran preemptively answers the question that might arise in the listener’s mind: how can a merciful God impose such an eternal and severe punishment? The verse declares, “We did not wrong them.” The negation is emphatic. God’s attribute of absolute justice is upheld. The punishment of Hell is not an act of divine tyranny or random cruelty; it is a perfectly measured consequence of their own actions and choices. Then the verse explains the true source of the wrong: “but it was they who were the wrongdoers.

” The Arabic word for wrongdoers, “ẓālimūn,” comes from a root meaning to put something in the wrong place. They committed the ultimate wrong by placing worship and obedience where they did not belong, by denying the One who created them, by oppressing the messengers, and by corrupting their own souls. The wrong was self-inflicted. God had sent prophets, revealed books, and provided intellect and free will. They used all these gifts to reject the truth and persist in rebellion.

Therefore, their punishment is not an injustice but the natural harvest of what they planted. This verse is crucial for understanding the Islamic view of divine justice. God’s punishment is not arbitrary; it is the recompense for deeds.

The phrase also connects back to the surah’s earlier arguments: the Meccans were given a Reminder, they were shown signs in nature, they acknowledged the Creator, yet they associated partners with Him and mocked the warner. They were the wrongdoers. No one can blame God for their fate.

For believers, this verse instills a deep sense of taqwā, an awareness that our deeds have real and eternal consequences. It also closes the door on the sort of fatalism the Meccans expressed when they said, “Had the Most Compassionate willed, we would not have worshipped them.” Here, God affirms that He does not wrong, and the responsibility lies squarely with the human being who chose to do wrong.

The next verse will capture a desperate plea from the inhabitants of Hell and the crushing reply they receive.

=77=

This verse presents a chilling dialogue within Hell. The inhabitants, overwhelmed by the ceaseless torment and despair described earlier, will call out to Mâlik, the chief angel appointed as the keeper of Hell. His name means “owner” or “master,” reflecting his authority over the Fire. Their cry is a plea for annihilation: “Let your Lord finish us off. ” They are so broken that they no longer ask for relief or mercy; they ask for death, for an end to existence itself, because they perceive that non-existence would be better than their current state. This reveals the unimaginable severity of the punishment. But Mâlik’s reply, coming after what some narrations describe as a long delay that intensifies their agony, is decisive and final: “You are definitely here to stay.

” The Arabic word “mākithūn” means those who remain, who abide, who will never leave. The response does not offer a glimmer of hope; it confirms the permanence of their condition. The very thing they wished for—an end—is denied. This dialogue illustrates the complete helplessness of the damned. Even the angel in charge of their punishment has no authority to help them and can only affirm the decree. The verse is a powerful reminder of the importance of the worldly life as the only opportunity for choosing one’s eternal abode. The cry for death in Hell is echoed elsewhere in the Quran, where they will say, “O Malik, let your Lord put an end to us,” and he will reply, “Indeed, you will remain.” The Meccan pagans, who questioned the Prophet about the Hereafter with sarcasm, are given a preview of the utter regret that awaits them.

The next verse will reinforce the reason for their predicament, stating plainly that the truth was brought to them, but they despised it.

=78=

The surah now addresses the inhabitants of Hell, or by extension the Meccan disbelievers, explaining the root cause of their punishment. The phrase “We certainly brought the truth to you” is a divine declaration. The truth refers to the pure monotheistic message, the Quran, and the clear signs. It was not hidden or ambiguous; it was delivered by a trustworthy messenger, with evidence and in their own language.

The emphasis on “certainly” confirms that no room for excuse remains. Then comes the diagnosis: “but most of you were resentful of the truth.

” The Arabic word “kārihūn” means those who detest, who find something loathsome. It is not that they were merely indifferent or lazy; they actively disliked and resented the truth. Why would anyone resent the truth? Because the truth threatened their power, their luxurious lifestyles, their inherited customs, and their pride. The call to monotheism demanded a complete overhaul of their social and spiritual order. To admit the truth would be to admit that their forefathers were wrong and that they had to submit to a man they considered socially inferior. So they resented the message and resented the messenger. This resentment led them to mock, to persecute, and to stubbornly refuse.

The phrase “most of you” indicates that while some did accept, the majority chose resentment over acceptance. This verse contains a profound psychological insight: often, people reject faith not because of intellectual doubt but because of an emotional aversion to its implications. The cure for this resentment is a sincere re-examination of one’s heart, humility before the Creator, and the recognition that what God commands is for our own good. The surah will now shift back to the contemporary Meccan scene, addressing the polytheists’ secret scheming against the Prophet and revealing that God is fully aware of their plots.

=79=

After detailing the fate of the wicked and the root cause of their rejection, the surah returns to challenge the Meccans directly. The verse begins with an interrogative: “Or have they mastered some evil plan?

” The word “mastered” (aḥkamū) implies making something firm and precise. The question is rhetorical and sarcastic. Do the disbelievers think they have come up with a foolproof, watertight scheme to defeat the truth, to silence the Prophet, or to escape divine punishment? The historical context includes the Quraysh’s secret meetings in Dār al-Nadwah, where they plotted to assassinate, imprison, or exile the Prophet. They thought they were clever and that their plotting would succeed.

The verse then delivers a powerful counter-statement: “Then We too are surely planning. ” God’s “planning” (makr) is His perfect, hidden response to the schemes of the wicked. It is not that God plans in the way humans do, with deception or uncertainty; rather, it is His definitive countermeasure that nullifies their plots and brings about their failure in ways they never anticipated. The battle is not between equal opponents; it is between the finite scheming of mortals and the infinite wisdom of the Almighty. This verse is both a threat to the disbelievers and a reassurance to the believers. The believers should not worry about the elaborate conspiracies of the powerful, because God’s plan is overarching and will inevitably prevail. The history of the Prophet’s life is a testament to this: the Quraysh’s best-laid plans were foiled, the Prophet emigrated safely, and Islam spread despite all their efforts.

The verse teaches that trust in God’s plan is the believer’s ultimate security. The next verse continues the exposure of the disbelievers’ hidden activities, revealing that even their secret conversations are under divine surveillance.

=80=

The surah continues to dismantle the arrogance of the polytheists by exposing the illusion of their privacy. “Or do they think that We do not hear their evil thoughts and secret talks?” This question confronts their hidden world. The “evil thoughts” refer to the whispers, the doubts, the schemes they harbor in their hearts. The “secret talks” refer to the hushed conversations where they plot against the Prophet, mock the believers, and plan their next moves. They felt secure in the secrecy of Dār al-Nadwah and their private gatherings, assuming that their conspiracies were hidden from the God they claimed to believe in. The verse answers its own question emphatically: “Yes We do!” God’s hearing encompasses everything, public and private, spoken and unspoken. Nothing escapes His knowledge. This revelation is deeply unsettling for those who live as if God is unaware of their inner and outer lives. To further emphasize the meticulous record-keeping, the verse adds: “And Our messenger-angels are in their presence, recording it all.” The angels designated to record deeds are always with every human being. They write down every word, every plot, every secret.

The word “it all” stresses that nothing is omitted. On the Day of Judgment, the record will be presented, and they will be stunned to find even their most private conversations documented. This verse serves as a powerful deterrent against sin committed in secret. A person might hide from other people, but never from God and His recording angels. For the Prophet, this was a great comfort; he was not alone in facing their conspiracies, and the evidence of their crimes was being compiled. For the Meccans, it was a dire warning that their hidden war against truth was fully monitored. The surah will soon reach its climax, instructing the Prophet on how to respond to their false claims and concluding with the ultimate declaration of God’s oneness and the fate of the deniers.
