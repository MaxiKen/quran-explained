=21=

The argument now shifts to a direct challenge. After the pagans claimed that their idolatry was willed by Allah, the Quran asks: did they receive any prior scripture that permits such worship?

The question "Or have We given them a Book before this" is rhetorical; the answer is clearly no. They have no divine text that sanctions taking angels as daughters or worshiping idols.

The phrase "to which they are holding firm" exposes their predicament. If they had such a book, they would be clinging to it as evidence. But they have nothing except inherited customs. This question also serves to highlight the exclusivity of divine guidance. True religion must be based on revelation from God, not on ancestral tradition. The pagans were not People of the Book; they had no scripture among them before the Quran. Hence, their claims about the unseen are baseless.

This verse also implicitly points to the Quran as the Book they now need to hold firmly to, yet they reject it. The absence of any earlier scripture proves that their polytheism is a human invention.

The question also creates a link with the next verse, which will reveal the real basis of their religion: blindly following forefathers. By juxtaposing the absence of a divine book with the presence of blind imitation, the Quran draws a sharp contrast between revealed truth and cultural inheritance. The interrogation "Or have We given them a Book..." functions like a courtroom cross-examination, dismantling the opponent's defense. It reminds the believer that no matter how many generations have practiced a religion, without a revealed basis it remains an error. This prepares the ground for the story of Abraham, who broke with his forefathers precisely because they had no divine warrant for their worship. So verse 21 is not just a refutation but a call to examine the foundations of one's faith.

=22=

The answer to the previous question is revealed here. They do not rely on any book; they rely on ancestral tradition.

The verse begins with "In fact," correcting any assumption that they have a proof. Their actual argument is: "We found our forefathers following a particular way, and we are following in their footsteps. " The Arabic word "ummah" translated as "particular way" can also mean a community, religion, or path. They appeal to the authority of the past. In their minds, the longevity of a practice validates its truth.

This is the classic argument of those who reject a prophet. They feel comfortable in the familiar, and the new call to monotheism disrupts the social and spiritual order inherited from their fathers. The Quran records this statement not to endorse it but to expose its weakness. Following forefathers simply because they were forefathers negates the human faculty of reason. It also betrays a lack of sincere concern for truth. If the forefathers were astray, then the followers share their misguidance. The verse captures a timeless human dilemma: the tension between tradition and truth. The Prophet Muhammad faced the same objection that Noah, Hud, and Salih faced before him.

The mention of "footsteps" suggests meticulous imitation, not even a slight deviation. They pride themselves on preserving the exact path. This blind allegiance is what the Quran repeatedly condemns. The verse links to the next one, which universalizes this phenomenon, showing that every nation's elite reacted exactly the same way. It also sets the stage for Abraham's story, who broke free from exactly this kind of thinking. The lesson for all believers is that a claim to truth cannot rest solely on the shoulders of ancestors; it must be rooted in evidence from the Creator.

=23=

The Quran now consoles the Prophet and broadens the picture. What the Meccans say is not original; it is the same argument thrown at every warner in history.

The phrase "Similarly, whenever We sent a warner" establishes a consistent divine pattern. A warner is a prophet who alerts his people to the consequences of their sins. The response from the "spoiled elite" is nearly verbatim what the Quraysh said. These elites, living in luxury, are most resistant to change because they benefit from the status quo. Reform threatens their position, so they cling to the ancestors' way as a shield.

The mention of "spoiled elite" (mutrafūn) is significant. It suggests that material comfort often hardens hearts against spiritual truth. Their rejection is not intellectual but social and economic. They do not ask about the evidence; they simply default to tradition. By noting this pattern, Allah reassures the Prophet Muhammad that the rejection he faces is not due to any deficiency in his message. It is the predictable reaction of the arrogant across the ages.

The verse also serves as a warning to the Meccan leaders: you are walking exactly the path of those who were destroyed. The similarity of the wording underscores the inevitability of the same outcome. This historical survey leads directly to the next verse, where the warner's response is recorded. The pattern shows that God sends prophets with clear signs, but the elite block the way by invoking the fathers. The continuity of the argument across millennia demonstrates that satanic deception operates through recurring patterns. The believer can take comfort in knowing that the struggle is not new, and victory ultimately belongs to the truth.

=24=

This verse presents the rational counter-argument of every prophet. The warner does not simply accept the appeal to ancestors; he asks a devastating question: "Even if what I brought you is better guidance than what you found your forefathers practicing?

" The word "better guidance" (ahdā) implies that the new message is clearer, more comprehensive, and leading more directly to the truth. The question forces the listener to consider the possibility that their forefathers were mistaken. It tests their sincerity. Are they truly seeking guidance, or are they just blindly attached to the past?

The answer is immediate and tragic: "We totally reject whatever you have been sent with." They do not even pause to compare the two ways. The rejection is absolute and unconditional. This reveals that their commitment to ancestral tradition has become an ideology that precludes any evaluation of evidence.

The phrase "totally reject" (kāfirūn) comes from the root of disbelief, showing that their denial is active and stubborn. Historically, this exchange repeated with Noah, Hud, Salih, and others. For instance, the people of ‘Ad told Hud that his message was foolishness, and they refused to abandon what their fathers worshipped. The verse exposes the irrational core of blind following. It teaches an important lesson for all believers: when presented with truth, we must examine it on its own merits, not dismiss it because it contradicts what we are accustomed to. The warner’s question is a logical tool that Muslims today can use when inviting others to Islam. Reason demands that we follow the best guidance, regardless of its source.

The next verse will describe the inevitable outcome of such stubborn rejection.

=25=

The consequence of their stubbornness is now declared. The particle "So" links the punishment directly to their rejection. Because they refused to even consider the better guidance, and instead clung to their forefathers' way, Allah's justice overtook them. "We inflicted punishment upon them" summarizes the various forms of destruction: floods, earthquakes, screaming blasts, and other torments.

The verse then invites the Prophet and anyone who hears the Quran to "See then what was the fate of the deniers! " The instruction to "see" is not merely about physical sight; it is a call to reflect on the ruins and the historical accounts that serve as lessons. The fate of those who disbelieved is well-known: obliteration in this world and eternal punishment in the next. This verse serves as a stern warning to the Quraysh. Their denial matches the pattern of previous nations exactly, so they should expect a similar outcome if they persist. The rhetorical "See" also implies that the traces of these destroyed peoples are still visible. The Arabs passed by the dwellings of Thamud and the cities of Lot during their travels. These ruins were not hidden; they were open-air museums of divine judgment.

For the believer, this verse reinforces the certainty of God's justice. No nation is allowed to mock the prophets and reject truth indefinitely. The punishment may come in this life or the next, but it is inevitable. The surah will now pivot to a positive example, the story of Abraham, who broke free from ancestral blindness and established the true faith, providing a sharp contrast to the deniers just mentioned.

=26=

From the general pattern of destroyed nations, the Quran now presents the specific, shining example of Abraham. The address "Remember, O Prophet" links the story to the Meccan context. Abraham is the forefather the Arabs claimed to follow, yet he disowned the idolatry they practiced. The verse says he declared "to his father and his people. " His father, Azar, was an idol-maker, and his people were steeped in astral worship. Abraham's declaration begins with a complete dissociation: "I am totally free of whatever gods you worship.

" The Arabic word barā’ means absolute disavowal, an unshakeable break. This was not a private thought but a public proclamation. Abraham did not compromise with his community's polytheism, even though it meant standing against his own family and the entire society. He severed all spiritual ties with their false deities. This act of disowning false gods is the essence of monotheism, embodied later in the declaration of faith: there is no god but Allah. Abraham’s example directly refutes the Quraysh’s claim that they follow the religion of Abraham. If they truly followed him, they would also declare freedom from idols, not worship them.

The verse also carries a lesson about the boundaries of family loyalty. Abraham loved his father and prayed for him, but when it came to faith, he drew a clear line. Truth must override tribal and familial bonds. The declaration is not yet complete; it requires a positive affirmation of what he does worship, which follows in the next verse. This two-part statement captures the essential Islamic formula: rejection of falsehood and affirmation of the One God.

=27=

Abraham’s disavowal is not merely negative; it is anchored in a positive attachment to the Creator. He makes an exception: "except the One Who originated me.

" The word "originated" (faṭaranī) refers to the initial creation out of nothing, the bringing into existence from non-being. By singling out this attribute, Abraham points to the most intimate proof of God's sole divinity. The One who created him from nothing is alone worthy of worship. This argument is also found when Abraham debates his people, telling them that the Lord who created him will guide him.

The phrase "and He will surely guide me" expresses absolute trust. Abraham does not yet know the full details of his life’s path, but he is certain that the Creator who gave him existence will provide the guidance he needs.

This is a profound statement of reliance. It also answers the fear that leaving ancestral religion means being lost. Abraham declares that true guidance comes only from Allah, not from forefathers. This verse combines theology with personal relationship. Abraham’s God is not a distant cosmic force but "the One Who originated me." The connection between creation and guidance is central to Islamic thought: the One who gave us life also gives us the roadmap for that life through revelation. The Meccans, who acknowledged Allah as Creator, should logically follow the same conclusion as Abraham. The verse seamlessly completes the earlier argument about creation. Since Allah originated them, He alone should be worshipped and He will surely guide. Abraham’s certainty "He will surely guide me" became manifest in his prophetic mission, his trials, and his eventual status as the leader of monotheism.

The next verse reveals that Abraham intended this declaration to endure.

=28=

Abraham was not content with his own salvation; he ensured that his proclamation of monotheism remained alive in his progeny. The verse says "he left this enduring declaration," referring to the words "I am totally free of whatever you worship, except the One Who originated me, and He will surely guide me." He made it a lasting legacy, a "kalimah bāqiyah" (enduring word) among his descendants. This means he taught his children and their descendants to uphold this principle. The purpose is explicitly stated: "so they may always turn back to Allah." The act of turning back (yubṣirūn or yarji‘ūn) implies that whenever they stray, this declaration will be a beacon calling them back to pure monotheism.

Historically, the Arabs were descendants of Abraham through Ishmael, and they once adhered to monotheism before Amr ibn Luhayy introduced idolatry. Thus, Abraham’s legacy was meant to be a safeguard, but they neglected it. The verse highlights Abraham’s deep concern for his offspring, a trait all believers should emulate. He wanted his lineage to remain on the straight path until the Day of Judgment. This "enduring declaration" was later fully realized in the final message of Islam, which revives the pure Abrahamic creed. The presence of the Prophet Muhammad among Abraham’s descendants is the ultimate fulfilment of this prayer. The verse subtly rebukes the Meccans: you are the descendants of Abraham, yet you have abandoned his word. Return to it. This provides a strong connection to the next section, which describes how the Meccans, despite being Abraham’s descendants, were given enjoyment and then rejected the truth when it came. The enduring word still exists in the Quran, calling them back.

=29=

The discourse now shifts from Abraham’s legacy directly to the contemporary Meccans. The "In fact" (bal) indicates a contrast: rather than heeding Abraham’s enduring word, they became immersed in worldly enjoyment. Allah states, "I had allowed enjoyment for these Meccans and their forefathers.

" The word "enjoyment" refers to the pleasures of worldly life: health, wealth, children, and long lifespans. This was not a sign of divine approval but a period of respite.

The phrase "until the truth came to them" marks the end of the era of excuse. The truth is the Quran, and it came "along with a messenger making things clear." The Prophet Muhammad did not come with ambiguities; he presented clear signs, a clear book, and a clear call. The arrival of truth after a long period of respite left them with no argument. This verse mirrors the wisdom in divine delay. Allah gives nations time to reflect, enjoy, and perhaps turn in gratitude.

Yet the Meccans used their respite for deeper immersion in idolatry and persecution of the believers. The mention of "their forefathers" ties back to the earlier argument about blind following. The forefathers also enjoyed long periods without a warner, but that period of ignorance ended when the messenger arrived. Now the children are accountable for their own response. The verse prepares for the tragic reaction described next. Despite the clarity of the message, they called it magic. This reveals that the problem was never a lack of evidence; it was an unwillingness to abandon false pride and ancestral customs. The enjoyment they were given became a trial they failed.

=30=

The response of the Meccans stands in stark opposition to the clarity of the message. The verse begins with "But," indicating a disappointing turn. When the truth, described in the previous verse, arrived, they did not pause to examine it. Their immediate reaction was to label it "magic. " This term was carefully chosen because it acknowledged the extraordinary impact of the Quran while denying its divine origin. A magician was seen as a skilled deceiver who captivates minds with illusions. By calling the Quran magic, they tried to explain its profound effect on listeners without admitting it was from God.

The second part of their statement, "we totally reject it," is a blanket denial. The Arabic kāfirūn again emphasizes active disbelief. They refused to consider even the possibility that it might be true. This reaction mirrors the pattern of earlier nations mentioned in the surah. The verse exposes the psychology of rejection: when people are confronted with a truth that threatens their way of life, they often resort to dismissive labels rather than engaging intellectually.

The word "magic" served as a convenient shield. If the Quran was magic, they had no obligation to follow it. This also reveals their arrogance. Despite admitting in their hearts that Muhammad was truthful before his prophethood, once he brought a message that demanded their submission, they turned to accusations. The verse connects to the next objection they raised: if this were truly a divine revelation, it should have been sent to a more prominent man. Their rejection was not just verbal; it was rooted in social snobbery and a false sense of superiority.

=31=

The Meccan elite added another layer to their rejection, revealing their materialistic criteria for prophethood. They argued that the Quran should have been revealed to a "great man" from one of the two cities, meaning Mecca and Taif. By "great man," they meant someone of high social status, immense wealth, and political influence. According to some reports, they specifically had in mind al-Walid ibn al-Mughirah in Mecca or ‘Urwah ibn Mas‘ud in Taif. These were prominent chiefs whose word carried weight. In their view, a messenger of God should naturally be a person already honoured by society. Muhammad, despite his noble lineage, was an orphan who had not amassed great wealth and did not hold a leadership position. This objection reveals their fundamental misunderstanding of prophethood. God chooses messengers based on their spiritual and moral qualities, not their worldly rank. By grumbling that the revelation did not come to a rich man, they were essentially appointing themselves as distributors of divine mercy. The verse sets up the powerful response in the next verse, which asks whether it is their prerogative to decide who receives God's mercy. Their objection also exposed their hypocrisy: they were unwilling to follow a man who lived among them and whose honesty they had previously acknowledged, because he did not match their superficial standards. This verse encapsulates the arrogance of the elite who believe that truth must come dressed in worldly splendour. The Quran's response will completely overturn this false value system.

=32=

This verse is a direct and devastating reply to their objection. It begins with the rhetorical question, "Is it they who distribute your Lord’s mercy?

" The answer is an emphatic no. Prophethood is a divine mercy, and only Allah decides where to place it. They have no authority to suggest who should receive revelation.

The verse then turns their own worldly logic against them. It states that Allah Himself has distributed their very livelihood—a far lesser thing than prophethood—and in doing so, He has created differences in rank and wealth. Some are rich, some poor; some are employers, others are employees. This social and economic hierarchy is solely by God's decree, intended so that "some may employ others in service." Human society functions through interdependence. If they do not control even the distribution of material provision, how can they dare to dictate who receives the highest spiritual favour? The argument is airtight. They accept Allah as the provider of sustenance, so they must accept Him as the chooser of messengers.

The verse concludes with a powerful reorientation: "your Lord’s mercy is far better than whatever wealth they amass." Prophethood, the Quran, and guidance are treasures infinitely more valuable than the gold and silver they hoard. Their fixation on worldly status blinds them to the real riches.

This verse teaches believers to never measure a person's worth by their bank account. The division of livelihoods is a worldly test, not a sign of divine favour.

The next section will further illustrate this by describing the fleeting nature of material luxury compared to the eternal blessings of the Hereafter.

=33=

After explaining that God’s mercy is better than wealth, the Quran now reveals a stunning divine wisdom behind the lack of worldly splendor for disbelievers. The verse begins with a hypothetical: "Were it not that people might be tempted to become one community of disbelievers." Allah withholds extravagant worldly luxuries from the deniers out of mercy to the believers and to humanity at large. If disbelievers had silver roofs and stairways as a reward for their disbelief, people would assume that God favours their path, and many weak in faith would apostatise, desiring that visible wealth.

The phrase "one community of disbelievers" indicates that the temptation would be so overwhelming that nearly everyone would abandon faith for materialism. The verse then lists what God could have given them: homes with silver roofs and silver stairways. Roofs of silver represent an unimaginable level of luxury, and stairways to ascend from level to level signify extreme opulence.

Yet Allah, in His wisdom, denies them this. He keeps their life modest or their wealth in forms that do not utterly dwarf the believers' portion, to prevent a trial too severe. This does not mean disbelievers never have wealth; they do, but it is not the exaggerated, miraculous opulence described here.

The verse teaches believers to never envy the disbelievers' possessions, because their wealth is either a test or a distraction, and God could make it far greater if He wished. The absence of such excessive luxury is actually a mercy, preserving the distinction between truth and falsehood.

The description continues in the next verse, adding more items to the list of hypothetical luxuries.

=34=

Continuing the description of the hypothetical worldly luxuries that Allah has withheld, this verse mentions "silver gates and thrones to recline on." Gates of silver suggest grand entrances and a stately, secure home. Thrones or couches to recline on indicate a life of ease and authority. In ancient cultures, reclining on thrones was a posture of royalty and comfort. If the disbelievers had such homes, they would be mistaken for kings living in divine favour. The picture being painted is one of complete material splendour: silver above their heads in the roofs, silver beneath their feet in the stairways, and silver around them in the gates, while they relax on luxurious seats.

Yet all of this is denied to them for the reason given in the previous verse. The Quran is teaching a profound lesson about the nature of dunya (the worldly life). Its adornments are so worthless in Allah's sight that He gives them even to those whom He is displeased with, as a form of gradual punishment or test. But He holds back the truly mind-boggling luxury that would confuse the test. The believer who lives in a simple house should reflect on this: had God wanted, the disbelievers' mansions could be made of silver, but such things have no weight on the scales of the Hereafter. The short verse, combined with the next, forms a series of conditional clauses that build up the image of earthly treasure, only to shatter its value in the final statement. This rhetorical technique moves the heart away from attachment to gold and silver, redirecting it toward the lasting reward with the Lord.

=35=

The list of hypothetical luxuries concludes with "ornaments of gold." Gold, even more than silver, symbolizes the ultimate worldly treasure. The verses 33-35 together describe a palace of silver and gold that any human would marvel at. Then comes the abrupt and powerful reversal: "Yet all this is no more than a fleeting enjoyment in this worldly life. " The Arabic word matā‘ (enjoyment) implies a temporary provision that will soon pass.

The word "fleeting" emphasizes its short duration. Compared to the eternal life to come, even a palace of solid gold is like a cheap trinket that a traveller uses for a brief moment and then leaves behind.

The verse then delivers the true value proposition: "But the Hereafter with your Lord is only for those mindful of Him. " The word "mindful" translates muttaqīn, those who have taqwa, God-consciousness. The bliss of Paradise is reserved exclusively for them, not for the disbelievers, no matter how wealthy they are in this life. This verse completely dismantles the Meccans' objection about why Muhammad was not a wealthy man. If gold and silver were the goal, Allah could have given it to the disbelievers. But since those things are worthless, the Prophet's lack of them is a sign of his honour, not a deficiency. The true treasure is the Hereafter, which is earned through taqwa.

The surah now shifts to the spiritual consequences of rejecting this truth: the one who turns away from the Reminder will be assigned a satanic companion, illustrating that the real loss is not the absence of silver roofs but the corruption of the soul.

=36=

This verse describes a terrifying spiritual law. Whoever deliberately ignores "the Reminder of the Most Compassionate," meaning the Quran, Allah assigns to them a "devilish one" (shayṭān) as a qarīn, an inseparable companion.

The phrase "turns a blind eye" translates the Arabic ya‘shu, which means to be dim-sighted or to pretend not to see. It is not an innocent oversight; it is a willful neglect.

The name "the Most Compassionate" is again used, highlighting the tragedy of turning away from the source of all mercy. The consequence is that a satanic associate is appointed to him. This association is not random; it is a punitive measure decreed by Allah in response to the person’s own choice. The shayṭān becomes his intimate friend, whispering evil, beautifying sin, and blocking the path to repentance.

This is the opposite of the righteous companionship that believers are encouraged to seek. The verse shows that there is no neutral ground: one either follows the Reminder or falls under the sway of Satan.

The use of "We place at the disposal of each" indicates that this is a divine punishment fitting the crime. Because they chose to blindly follow their own desires and forefathers instead of the clear Book, they are given a guide who leads them further into blindness.

This principle also explains how some people who are intelligent in worldly matters remain utterly misguided in faith. Their constant rejection creates a spiritual blindness, and Satan capitalizes on it.

The next two verses will elaborate on how this devilish companion deceives them and the regret that follows at the moment of death or judgment.

=37=

This verse explains the primary function of the satanic associate. The devils "will certainly hinder them from the Right Way." They erect barriers, sow doubts, and make falsehood appear beautiful. The Right Way is the path of monotheism and obedience to Allah. The hindrance is active and persistent.

However, the most dangerous aspect is captured in the second half: "while they think they are rightly guided." The person does not realize he is being deceived. He believes he is on the correct path.

This is the peak of misguidance, because someone who knows he is lost might search for the truth, but someone who thinks he is guided will never question his direction. The satanic companion ensures that evil deeds are presented as good, and false beliefs are rationalized. This verse explains the psychology behind the Meccan elite’s stubbornness. They genuinely believed that the religion of their forefathers was correct and that Muhammad was the one in error. Their satanic associates had made their disbelief seem like wisdom. The verse serves as a warning to all believers to constantly check their hearts and actions against the Quran. Self-deception is a real danger; we might think we are following guidance while actually being hindered. The only safeguard is sincere, continuous reliance on the Reminder they are turning away from. The tragic irony is that the very Book they abandoned could have broken the spell, but they refused it. This sets the stage for the intense regret described in the next verse, when the veils are lifted and the disbeliever finally sees his associate for what he was.

=38=

The scene shifts to the Day of Judgment, when the blinds are removed. "When such a person comes to Us" refers to the disbeliever being brought before Allah for reckoning, accompanied by his devilish companion. At that moment, the truth becomes undeniable. The disbeliever turns to his satanic associate and utters words of profound regret: "I wish you were as distant from me as the east is from the west!" The east and the west are the two farthest points imaginable, symbolizing an unbridgeable separation. The friendship that seemed so sweet in the world now reveals itself as the cause of his destruction. He then exclaims, "What an evil associate you were!

" This is a devastating admission. The one he trusted, listened to, and took as a close friend was actually leading him to hellfire. This dialogue echoes another Quranic verse where Satan disowns his followers on the Day of Judgment, saying he only called them and they responded. The verse here captures the intense emotional pain: betrayal, regret, and despair. It also reveals the ultimate loneliness of misguidance. The close bond that defied God's commands is shattered by the reality of punishment. This regret, however, does not change the outcome.

The verse teaches that friendships built on anything other than faith and righteousness will turn into enmity on that Day, except the bonds among the pious. It is a powerful motivation to choose our close associates wisely in this life, to ensure that we are not led astray while thinking we are guided.

The next verse contains the divine response to this regret.

=39=

The regretful wish of the disbeliever to be far from his satanic companion is answered with a stern statement from Allah. The verse says it will be said to both the human and his devilish associate: "Since you all did wrong, sharing in the punishment will be of no benefit to you this Day." This means that wishing for separation or blaming one another is useless now. They are both guilty. The human chose to follow, and the devil chose to mislead. They are partners in crime, so they will be partners in punishment.

The phrase "sharing in the punishment" indicates that they are all suffering together, and this shared suffering does not ease the individual’s pain. In worldly terms, shared hardship can sometimes bring comfort, but in Hell, the torment is so intense and the reality so clear that togetherness offers no solace. The verse nullifies any hope that the disbeliever might have of shifting blame entirely. It also rebuffs the satan's possible excuse. All parties are held accountable for their own deeds. This pronouncement underscores the absolute justice of Allah. No one will be punished for the sin of another without their own complicity. The entire scene from verse 36 to 39 presents a coherent sequence: turning away from the Reminder leads to a satanic companion who deceives, and the final awakening comes too late, when remorse and blame are futile. This serves as a powerful warning to the Meccans who are actively turning away from the Quran and mocking it.

The surah then turns to console the Prophet, reminding him that he cannot force guidance on those who have made themselves deaf and blind.

=40=

The surah now addresses the Prophet directly with a rhetorical question that provides comfort and perspective. After describing the terrible fate of those who turn away, Allah asks: "Can you make the deaf hear, or guide the blind or those clearly astray?" The deaf, the blind, and those clearly astray are metaphors for the disbelievers who have shut their spiritual senses. They have ears but do not hear the truth with acceptance; they have eyes but do not see the signs with insight; and they are wandering in manifest misguidance.

The question is phrased to elicit a clear "no." The Prophet, despite his deep desire for their guidance, cannot force his message into hearts that have been sealed. The ability to grant true hearing and sight belongs to Allah alone. This verse serves to alleviate the Prophet’s grief over their rejection. He is not responsible for their choice; his duty is only to convey.

The verse also contains a hidden critique of the disbelievers, comparing them to the deaf and blind, which is far more severe than physical disability because it is self-inflicted. They have deprived themselves of the greatest gift by their arrogance.

The phrase "clearly astray" (ḍalālin mubīn) leaves no room for ambiguity; their error is not subtle but manifest to any observer. This rhetorical question links back to the preceding verses about the devilish associate; those who are led astray become so hardened that even the Prophet's clear call cannot penetrate.

The next section of the surah will continue to reassure the Prophet about the eventual outcome, whether he witnesses their punishment or departs this life before it occurs.
