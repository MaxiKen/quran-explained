=81=

This verse captures the dark joy of the hypocrites who deliberately stayed behind from the Tabūk expedition. The phrase “rejoiced for doing so in defiance of the Messenger of Allah” shows that their staying was not a matter of necessity but an act of rebellion. They took pleasure in their disobedience, feeling they had outsmarted the Prophet and avoided a difficult journey.

Moreover, they “hated the prospect of striving with their wealth and their lives in the cause of Allah. ” This reveals that their hearts had no love for jihad or sacrifice; they saw it as a burden to be avoided. The historical context is the blistering summer heat of the Tabūk campaign. The hypocrites discouraged one another, saying, “Do not march forth in the heat.” It was a seductive argument because the physical discomfort was real. They used a worldly reality—the harsh weather—to justify abandoning a divine command. Allah instructs the Prophet to respond not by denying the heat but by redirecting their attention: “The Fire of Hell is far hotter!” The comparison is devastating. They were trying to escape temporary, physical heat by embracing an action that leads to eternal, unimaginable heat. The sentence “If only they could comprehend” expresses pity mixed with condemnation; their minds are so trapped by immediate comfort that they cannot foresee the ultimate consequence. For the sincere believers, this verse is a lesson in priorities: no worldly discomfort, no matter how intense, can compare to the punishment of the Hereafter. It also exposes the psychology of the hypocrites who seek to make disobedience attractive by magnifying present hardships. The reply given to them is a powerful rhetorical tool that believers can use internally whenever tempted to abandon a duty because of difficulty—the heat of Hell is far hotter than any struggle in this world.

=82=

This verse follows the hypocrites’ joy at staying behind with a chilling prophecy. The phrase “let them laugh a little” is a divine concession to their current state of false security. Their laughter is the laughter of those who think they have escaped hardship. It is a small, fleeting amusement tied to this world.

The second half, “they will weep much,” shatters that illusion. The weeping is not a brief moment of regret but a prolonged, intense grief. This refers primarily to the punishment of the Hereafter, where the hypocrites will shed tears of anguish and remorse. It also manifested in this world through the exposure of their hypocrisy, their loss of trust, and the constant fear they lived under. The “little” and “much” are placed in sharp contrast to show the vast discrepancy between the temporary pleasure of sin and the eternal pain it brings.

The phrase “as a reward for what they have committed” is deeply ironic. The word “reward” usually implies compensation for good deeds, but here it is used for the recompense of evil. Every act of mockery, every encouragement to abandon jihad, every moment of defiance is being stored up and will be returned to them in the form of endless sorrow. This verse serves as a powerful warning not to take delight in disobedience or in escaping religious obligations, because that laughter is short‑lived and will be replaced by tears.

For believers, it is also a comfort: those who mock and avoid sacrifice may seem happy now, but their true state is one of impending grief.

=83=

The verse anticipates a future scenario. After the Tabūk expedition, the Prophet would return to Medina, and some of those hypocrites who had stayed behind might come forward and ask to join future military campaigns. Perhaps they saw the success and spoils, or they wanted to clear their names.

However, Allah instructs the Prophet to refuse them permanently. The command is emphatic: they will never be allowed to march out with him or fight an enemy alongside him. The reason given is their previous choice: “You preferred to stay behind the first time.” This was a deliberate decision, not an inability, and it revealed their true character. The punishment is to be consigned permanently to the group of those who legitimately remain behind—the women, children, and sick. They are to be treated as if they are incapable, stripped of the honour of participating in jihad. This was a severe social and spiritual sanction. It demonstrates that repeated, unrepentant hypocrisy can lead to being excluded from the ranks of the believers in matters of trust and honour. For the community, it was a purification; the army would no longer be infiltrated by those whose hearts were not in the struggle.

The verse also highlights the principle that opportunities for sacred service can be lost through one’s own choices. When a person repeatedly refuses a call to good, a time may come when the door is closed and the chance is gone. This should inspire believers to seize the moments of obedience when they are presented.

=84=

This verse establishes a definitive break with the hypocrites even in death. The Prophet is forbidden from performing the funeral prayer for any of them and from standing at their graveside. The funeral prayer is a profound act of intercession and a sign of the community’s bond with the deceased. To deny it is to declare that the bond of faith does not exist. The reason is explicitly stated: they “lost faith in Allah and His Messenger and died rebellious.” Their outward affiliation with Islam did not save them because their inner state was one of rejection.

The term “rebellious” translates fāsiqūn, indicating they departed from the path of obedience. This verse was applied when ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy, the head of the hypocrites, died. His son, a sincere Muslim, asked the Prophet to pray for him and to give his shirt as a shroud. The Prophet initially inclined to do so, but this verse descended and he refrained. The prohibition serves multiple purposes: it demonstrates that faith is the only basis for spiritual solidarity; it removes any ambiguity about the status of known hypocrites; and it acts as a deterrent for those who might think they can live a life of hypocrisy and still receive the honours of a believer at death. For the community, it emphasises that loyalty is to faith, not to personalities or family ties.

The verse also reminds every Muslim to be deeply concerned about the state in which they will die, for an apparently Muslim life that ends in hidden kufr is of no avail.

=85=

This is the second time in the sūrah that the Prophet is warned not to be impressed by the wealth and children of the hypocrites, the first being verse 55. The repetition underscores its importance. The hypocrites possessed considerable riches and many offspring, which in the eyes of society signalled success and divine favour. Allah warns that these are not signs of His pleasure; rather, they are instruments of torment. The torment occurs in this world through the anxiety, greed, and fear of loss that accompany wealth, and through the worry and grief that children can cause when they stray or when they are lost.

Moreover, the attachment to these blessings prevents them from repenting and turning to Allah, so their souls depart from this world while they are still disbelievers. This is the ultimate disaster: to leave the world with the kalimah of tawḥīd absent from the heart. The verse dismantles the materialistic worldview that equates prosperity with divine approval.

For believers, it is a reminder not to envy the worldly success of those who are distant from Allah, because that success is part of their trial and potential destruction. The truly blessed person is the one whose wealth and children bring them closer to Allah and help them attain a good end, not the one who simply possesses them in abundance.

=86=

This verse highlights the immediate reaction of the wealthy hypocrites to the revealed commands. When a sūrah would descend containing the call to faith and jihad, the wealthy among them would immediately seek exemption. They did not wait to understand the command or to assess their capacity; their first instinct was to avoid the obligation. Their request, “Leave us with those who remain behind,” shows that they wanted to be counted among the helpless—the women, children, and invalids—who were legitimately excused. This was a deliberate lowering of their status to escape duty. Their wealth, which should have made them foremost in contributing, became the very thing that made them cling to comfort and privilege. The verse exposes the correlation between material abundance and spiritual cowardice when that abundance is not grounded in faith. It also shows that the Qur’anic revelation was a continuous test, sifting the sincere from the insincere.

For believers, this is a warning about the subtle danger of wealth: it can make a person seek comfort and exemption from the sacrifices that faith demands. It also teaches that one’s response to the call of Allah at the moment it is heard reveals the true condition of the heart.

=87=

The choice of the hypocrites is described as a preference. They actively chose to remain with those who were incapable of fighting. This was not a forced situation but a deliberate option.

The phrase “with the helpless” further emphasises the shame of their position; able‑bodied, wealthy men grouping themselves with those who are physically unable. Then the verse reveals the spiritual cause behind this choice: “their hearts have been sealed.” The sealing of the heart is a consequence of persistent rejection and sin. When a person continually turns away from guidance, the heart gradually loses its ability to receive truth. The seal is not an arbitrary punishment but the natural result of their own actions. As a result, “they do not comprehend.” They cannot grasp the gravity of their situation, the importance of jihad, or the reality of the Hereafter. Their intellect functions in worldly matters, but regarding spiritual truth, they are blind.

This is one of the most frightening descriptions in the Qur’an, and it calls every believer to guard their heart against hardness. The verse implies that if a person repeatedly chooses comfort over divine command, a time may come when they no longer even see their choice as wrong; they lose the capacity for sincere repentance.

=88=

The contrast with the previous verse is absolute. While the hypocrites stayed behind with the helpless, the Messenger and the sincere believers strove with both their wealth and their lives. This pairing of financial and physical sacrifice is the hallmark of true faith. Their striving was not reluctant but eager, and it covered every dimension of their being. The result is a comprehensive promise: “They will have all the best.

” The Arabic expression al‑khayrāt means all good things—victory, honour, provision, and above all, the spiritual goodness of nearness to Allah. The verse then declares, “it is they who will be successful.

” This is the true success, the f alāḥ, which is not measured by worldly gains but by achieving eternal salvation and the pleasure of Allah. The verse serves as an inspiring conclusion to the section, refocusing the believers’ attention on the right models. The Prophet and his Companions are the standards; their sacrifice is the path to all good.

For every believer who feels the pull of comfort and the temptation to stay behind, this verse stands as a clear sign: real success belongs to those who strive.

=89=

The success promised in the previous verse is now detailed. The reward is not abstract; it is a prepared, ready destination. Allah Himself has prepared these Gardens, indicating the care and honour with which they have been made. “Gardens under which rivers flow” is the Qur’an’s most frequent description of Paradise, evoking an image of perpetual shade, water, and abundance. The eternality of the stay is emphasised with “to stay there forever,” removing any fear of ending or expulsion. Then the verse seals it with “That is the ultimate triumph.

” The word “triumph” (fawz) conveys the idea of winning the greatest prize after a difficult contest. The struggle with wealth and lives was the contest, and the reward is this unimaginable victory. This verse gives the believers a vision that makes every sacrifice in the path of Allah light. It also stands in stark contrast to the fate of the hypocrites described earlier. For the Companions returning from Tabūk or preparing for future struggles, this was a continuous source of hope. For all believers, it reaffirms that the true measure of triumph is not a military victory or worldly gain, but entry into Allah’s eternal Gardens with His pleasure.

=90=

The sūrah now widens its scope to include the Bedouin tribes living around Medina. When the call to Tabūk was made, some of these nomads came to the Prophet with excuses, seeking permission to stay behind. Their excuses were varied, but they were a mix of genuine and false. The verse, however, focuses on a group that did not even bother to present an excuse: they simply remained behind, being “untrue to Allah and His Messenger. ” Their absence was a direct act of betrayal, without even the pretence of justification. The verse warns that “the unfaithful among them will be afflicted with a painful punishment.” This includes those who lied in their excuses and those who stayed behind arrogantly.

The mention of the Bedouins is significant because they were often new to Islam, living on the outskirts of the community, less exposed to the teachings and less integrated. Their behaviour during Tabūk was a test of their sincerity. The verse demonstrates that Allah’s judgment is based on truth and faithfulness, and that no one can escape punishment by simply hiding in the desert or offering flimsy excuses. It also serves as a reminder that the commands of Allah apply to all believers, urban or nomadic, and that those who claim faith while being untrue to it face severe consequences.

=91=

After condemning those who made false excuses, the verse establishes the categories of people who are genuinely exempt. The “weak” include the elderly and the physically frail. The “sick” are those with illnesses that prevent them from undertaking the arduous journey. “Those lacking the means” are the poor who cannot find mounts or provisions even if they desired to go. These groups bear no sin for staying behind.

However, the verse adds a crucial condition: “as long as they are true to Allah and His Messenger.” Their inability must be genuine, and their hearts must be sincere. They should not be secretly pleased to be exempt, nor should they spread discouragement. They are considered among the “good-doers” (muḥsinīn) because their intention is pure and they would go if they could.

The verse then closes with two divine names: All‑Forgiving and Most Merciful, indicating that any shortcoming in their effort that falls short of the ideal is covered by Allah’s immense mercy. This verse is a powerful example of the balance in Islamic law: obligations are matched to capacity, and sincerity of the heart is the ultimate criterion. It also provides comfort to those who are forced by legitimate circumstances to miss out on great acts of worship; their reward is secure if their hearts are with the believers.

=92=

This verse describes one of the most moving scenes of the Tabūk preparations. A group of sincere, poor believers approached the Prophet and asked for animals to ride so they could join the expedition. The Prophet, peace be upon him, had to turn them away because there were simply no mounts available. Their response was not relief but profound grief. They turned away “with eyes overflowing with tears,” weeping not from disappointment of a personal desire, but from the sorrow that they could not contribute to the cause of Allah. They had nothing to give and no way to go. This verse exonerates them completely from any blame. Their tears are a testimony to their deep faith. In the books of sīrah, these men are known as the “Weepers” (al‑Bakkā’ūn). They included people like ʿAbdullāh ibn Mughaffal and others. The Prophet later managed to find mounts for some of them, but the verse immortalises their state of sincere longing. This teaches that the value of a believer’s intention can surpass the value of the action itself. Their inability did not diminish their rank; rather, their tears elevated them. It also shows the immense love the Companions had for jihad and their fear of missing out on the reward. For all believers, it sets a standard: when a good deed is desired but blocked by genuine incapacity, the tears of that sorrow are a sign of a living heart.

=93=

After excusing the sincere, the verse returns to the blameworthy. The blame is exclusively on the wealthy and able who came to the Prophet asking to be excused. They had the means—health, mounts, and provisions—but they chose to seek exemption. Their request was not based on necessity but on preference. “They preferred to stay behind with the helpless” repeats the earlier judgment, highlighting the shame of their choice. Then the verse states that “Allah has sealed their hearts so they do not realize.” This sealing is the result of their own persistent dodging of responsibility. Their repeated exposure to the truth, combined with their refusal to act on it, caused a spiritual insensitivity. They do not realize the gravity of their situation or the eternal loss they are incurring. The verse thus draws a clear line: capacity creates responsibility. Those who have means and still seek to avoid sacrifice are in a state of grave spiritual danger. It also warns that the habit of making excuses can harden the heart until it no longer feels the sting of its own failures.

=94=

The verse anticipates the moment when the victorious army returns to Medina. The hypocrites who stayed behind will rush to present their excuses, trying to justify their absence. The believers are instructed to reject these excuses outright. The command “Make no excuses, for we will not believe you” is a powerful refusal to engage with their lies. The reason is that Allah has already informed the believers of the true state of the hypocrites’ faith. Divine revelation has exposed their inner reality, so no human testimony can overturn that.

However, the verse does not end with a complete closure. It adds that their future deeds will be observed by Allah and His Messenger.

This is both a warning and an opportunity: they are being watched, and if they genuinely change, that will be known. But if they persist, they will be returned to the Knower of the seen and unseen on the Day of Judgment, and then they will be fully informed of what they used to do. The verse combines firmness with a reminder of ultimate accountability. It teaches that the community is not required to be naive; when Allah has clarified a matter, believers can act on that knowledge.

Yet the final judgment remains with Allah, and the door of sincere repentance is not declared shut for individuals.

=95=

The hypocrites will resort to their usual tactic of swearing by Allah, hoping that their solemn oaths will persuade the believers to stop reproaching them and to leave them in peace. Their goal was to be left alone—to be socially accepted without having to account for their actions. The divine instruction is surprising: “So leave them alone.

” This is not an acceptance of their excuses, but a form of punishment. It means to boycott them, to withdraw from engaging with them, to treat them as if they are irrelevant. The verse immediately explains why such a harsh measure is appropriate: “they are truly evil,” an abomination in character. Their place is Hell, and that is the fitting reward for their deeds. This response was more painful to the hypocrites than open confrontation, because it denied them the social validation they craved. The lesson is that there are times when the best course with insincere people is not argument but a dignified disengagement, leaving them to their own corruption and to the judgment of Allah.

=96=

This verse reinforces the point about the hypocrites’ oaths. Their swearing is a calculated effort to regain the believers’ favour. They want the believers to be pleased with them so they can enjoy the benefits of community membership. But the verse declares that human pleasure is irrelevant if Allah’s pleasure is absent. Even if every single believer were somehow satisfied with their excuses, Allah would never be pleased with the fāsiqūn, the rebellious who have left the path of obedience.

This is a profound recalibration of priorities. The ultimate goal is not to please people but to please Allah. The hypocrites’ entire strategy was based on managing public perception, but they neglected the One whose opinion truly matters. For the sincere believers, this verse is a warning against seeking human approval at the expense of divine approval. It also liberates them from the need to win the favour of people whose hearts are corrupt, because that favour is worthless in the divine scales.

=97=

This verse offers a sociological observation about the Bedouins. Because they lived far from the centre of learning in Medina, their exposure to the Qur’an and the Prophet’s teachings was less frequent.

Consequently, their disbelief and hypocrisy, when it existed, was “far worse” and more hardened. They were “less likely to know the limits revealed by Allah,” meaning the boundaries of what is lawful and unlawful, the obligations and prohibitions. This was due to the harsh desert environment, which often produced rough manners, and their distance from the community of knowledge.

However, the verse does not excuse them; it simply explains the reason for the severity of their condition. It then reminds that Allah is All‑Knowing of their states and All‑Wise in His legislation and in differentiating between people based on their circumstances.

This verse teaches the importance of being near to the sources of sacred knowledge. Isolation from the community and from regular exposure to revelation can lead to a coarsening of faith and a weakening of moral sensitivity. It also calls for understanding that people’s level of religious comprehension varies, and those with less knowledge may need more patience and teaching, though they remain accountable for what they have received.

=98=

The verse now exposes the deep malice of some Bedouins. When they spent anything in charity or for the expedition, they considered it a fine or a loss, not an investment for the Hereafter. They gave reluctantly, feeling they were being forced to pay. Worse, they awaited misfortunes to strike the believers—hoping for defeat, for disaster, for the mission to fail. Their hearts were so twisted that the success of Islam was a source of pain to them. The Qur’an responds with a powerful curse: “May ill-fortune befall them!

” This is a prayer that the very misfortune they wish upon the believers should rebound upon them. It is a rare instance of a direct curse in the Qur’an, indicating the severity of their enmity.

The verse concludes by stating that Allah is All‑Hearing of their words and All‑Knowing of their intentions. This is both a comfort to the believers—that the hidden malice is not hidden from Allah—and a warning to the hypocrites that their secret hopes are fully recorded. For the believers, it reinforces the idea that donations in the path of Allah should be given with a generous heart, expecting reward, not treating them as a financial loss. The attitude of the giver is a key indicator of faith.

=99=

The picture of the Bedouins is now balanced. Just as there were hard‑hearted hypocrites among them, there were also sincere believers. These nomads truly believed in Allah and the Last Day. Their spending was not seen as a loss but as qurubāt—a means of drawing nearer to Allah. They also deeply valued the prayers of the Messenger, peace be upon him. The Prophet would pray for those who brought charity, asking Allah to bless them. For these Bedouins, that supplication was a treasured gift, a spiritual connection that they sought. The verse assures them that their donations will indeed bring them closer to Allah.

The word “certainly” emphasises the certainty of this promise. Their physical distance from Medina did not prevent their hearts from being close.

The verse concludes by saying Allah will admit them into His mercy, and that He is All‑Forgiving and Most Merciful. This is a beautiful testament to the fact that true faith can flourish anywhere, even in the remote desert. It also highlights the importance of having the right intention in charity: to seek closeness to Allah, not to avoid criticism or show off. The prayers of the righteous are a blessing, and seeking them is a sign of humility and faith. This verse gives hope to all those who are physically far from centres of learning but whose hearts are attached to Allah and His Messenger.

=100=

This verse crowns the discussion of the different groups by mentioning the very best of the community. The “foremost” (al‑sābiqūn al‑awwalūn) are the earliest and most devoted among the Muhājirūn and the Anṣār—those who embraced Islam in Mecca before the Hijrah and those who welcomed them in Medina. This includes the participants of Badr and those who pledged allegiance under the tree. Their rank is the highest.

The verse then extends the honour to “those who follow them in goodness,” meaning all subsequent generations of believers who follow the path of the earliest Companions in faith and righteous deeds, until the Day of Judgment. The greatest reward mentioned is not the Gardens but “Allah is pleased with them and they are pleased with Him.” This mutual contentment is the pinnacle of spiritual attainment. Allah’s pleasure is the source of all bliss, and their pleasure is the result of receiving His boundless favour. The Gardens are then described as already prepared, a gift waiting for them. The eternity of the stay and the declaration that it is the ultimate triumph bring the passage to a majestic close. For the believers, this verse establishes the hierarchy of honour and motivates them to align themselves with the path of the early generations. It affirms that the true triumph is not in wealth or power but in earning Allah’s pleasure and entering His Paradise.
