=121=

Building upon the previous verse's promise that no hardship in Allah's cause goes unrewarded, this verse extends that assurance to every financial sacrifice and every physical movement undertaken for the sake of Allah. The phrase "whenever they make a donation, small or large" is profoundly inclusive. Nothing is too minor to be noticed. Whether a believer gives a mountain of gold or merely a handful of dates, it is recorded. This was a direct comfort to both the wealthy Companions who had given huge sums for the Tabūk expedition and the poor who had brought whatever meagre amount they could, such as the half a measure of dates that the hypocrites had mocked. Both donations, so vastly different in material terms, are equally "written to their credit" when given with sincerity.

The verse then adds "or cross a valley," referring to the actual physical marching through difficult terrain. Every step taken, every valley traversed, every mile covered in fatigue and heat is not just a military movement but a spiritual act being recorded by the angels.

The phrase "it is written to their credit" implies a meticulous divine accounting, where nothing is lost. The purpose stated is "so that Allah may grant them the best reward for what they used to do." The "best reward" means a recompense far exceeding the actual deed. A single coin given sincerely can be multiplied into a mountain of reward. This verse instils in the believer a sense that every moment of the struggle, and every expenditure, is an investment with guaranteed, multiplied returns. It demolishes the notion that mundane logistics of a campaign are separate from worship.

For believers across time, this transforms daily acts of sacrifice—giving charity even when it is hard, commuting for a good cause, enduring tiredness in service of others—into deeds of immense spiritual worth. It encourages a life where one consciously dedicates even the smallest acts to Allah, knowing that He records and rewards them beyond imagination.

=122=

After the intense call to jihad and the rebuke of those who stayed behind, this verse establishes a crucial balance. It explains that not every believer needs to go out on every military expedition.

The phrase "it is not necessary for the believers to march forth all at once" relieves the community from the burden of total mobilisation for every campaign. The early Muslim community was small, and if all had left, the city would have been defenceless and the sources of learning would have dried up. So the verse commands that from each tribe or group, only a party should go forth for jihad. The rest—those who remain—are to engage in a different but equally important form of struggle: "to gain religious knowledge then enlighten their people when they return to them.

" This is the foundation of the Islamic duty of seeking knowledge and teaching it. The Arabic term li-yatafaqqahū fī d-dīn means to deeply understand the religion, to attain fiqh, which is profound comprehension that goes beyond surface information. Those who stay behind are not idle; they are students whose task is to learn the Qur'an and the teachings of the Prophet as they are being revealed, and to then educate the returning soldiers. The reason is "so that they too may beware of evil." The warriors returning from battle need spiritual refreshment and guidance, and the community as a whole needs constant reminders to maintain taqwā.

This verse establishes that knowledge is a form of jihad, and that a community must have a segment dedicated to scholarship and teaching. The early Muslims implemented this by sending delegations to the Prophet to learn and then return to their tribes as teachers. Later, this verse became the basis for the obligation of seeking religious knowledge as a communal duty (farḍ kifāyah). It ensures that the faith is preserved and transmitted, and that military strength is balanced with spiritual and intellectual strength. Without this balance, a community can become powerful in arms but weak in understanding, which leads to eventual ruin.

=123=

This verse gives a strategic command to the Muslim state. The directive is to fight those disbelievers who are geographically close and pose an immediate threat. The early Muslims were to prioritise the hostile tribes and forces in Arabia before turning to distant enemies.

The phrase "let them find firmness in you" translates ghilẓah, which means toughness, severity, and unyielding resolve. The enemy should encounter not weakness or hesitation but a community that is firm in its principles and formidable in its defence. This firmness is not cruelty but a projection of strength that deters aggression. The verse immediately balances this instruction with a reminder that "Allah is with those mindful of Him." The firmness shown must be within the bounds of taqwā, not a descent into barbarism or transgression. The believer fights firmly but justly, and the presence of Allah's support is tied to maintaining that God‑consciousness. This verse was particularly important in the context of the Byzantine frontier and the remaining hostile tribes in Arabia. It outlined a military posture that was proactive and strong, yet always anchored in moral limits. For today's believers, this verse is a call to be unapologetically firm in upholding truth and justice, not to be pushed around or bullied into compromising principles, but at the same time to remain deeply mindful of Allah in every action. The combination of firmness and taqwā is the hallmark of a balanced Muslim community.

=124=

The sūrah now returns to the behaviour of the hypocrites when the Qur'an was being revealed. A new revelation was a test. The hypocrites would turn to each other and ask with sarcasm, "Which of you has this increased in faith?" They meant the question as a joke, implying that the so‑called believers were just gullible, being moved by mere words. This mockery was a sign of their spiritual deadness. The verse immediately supplies the true answer: for the believers, the revelation does increase their faith. Hearing the words of Allah, with their proofs, warnings, and promises, strengthens their conviction and deepens their understanding. The increase of faith is a reality; imān is not static. It goes up with obedience and remembrance and decreases with sin and neglect.

Furthermore, the believers "rejoice" in the revelation. The new sūrah brings them joy because it is guidance, mercy, and a connection to their Lord. The hypocrites cannot comprehend this joy.

This verse teaches that the response to the Qur'an is a diagnostic of the heart. When a person hears a verse and feels boredom, irritation, or a desire to ridicule, that is a symptom of a serious spiritual sickness. When the heart is drawn closer, feels awe, and experiences an increase in eagerness to obey, that is the mark of faith. For the believers, it is a call to approach the Qur'an with an open, receptive heart, seeking the increase in faith that is promised to those who truly listen.

=125=

The same revelation that benefits the believers harms the hypocrites. The phrase "sickness in their hearts" refers to the disease of doubt, hypocrisy, and malice. For them, each new sūrah does not increase guidance but "increased them only in wickedness upon their wickedness.

" The word rijs means filth, impurity, and spiritual defilement. The revelation exposes their secret thoughts, warns of punishment, and demands sacrifices they are unwilling to make. Instead of submitting, they react with more hatred, more mockery, and more plotting, layering fresh evil on top of their existing evil. The verse predicts their end: "they die as disbelievers." Their persistent rejection and their refusal to let the Qur'an purify them lead to a state where they leave this world in a state of kufr.

This is a terrifying warning that exposure to divine truth can either be a cure or a poison, depending on the heart's disposition. The same sun that softens wax hardens clay. If a person approaches the Qur'an with arrogance and a desire to find fault, it only hardens their heart further. This verse calls for deep self‑examination each time one reads or hears the Qur'an: is it increasing me in submission and love, or in resistance and resentment? The difference is the difference between life and spiritual death.

=126=

This verse points to the repeated tests and exposures that the hypocrites faced. Every year, perhaps through a military campaign, a scandal, a death, or the exposure of one of their own, they were confronted with the reality of their hypocrisy and the truth of Islam. "Tried once or twice every year" could refer to the various expeditions in which their true colours were shown, or to the moments when the Prophet confronted them with revelations exposing their secrets.

Yet despite these repeated wake‑up calls, they failed to repent and failed to take heed. The phrase "they neither repent nor do they learn a lesson" indicates a complete spiritual numbness.

This is a universal pattern: a person who is on a wrong path will often receive multiple warning signs—a sudden difficulty, a friend's advice, a sermon that stings, a near‑miss with disaster. These are opportunities from Allah to turn back. The tragic condition is when these tests come and go and the person does not change, because the heart has become sealed. The verse is a call to be alert to the tests and reminders in one's own life, and to treat them as urgent signals for reflection and repentance before the heart becomes incapable of responding.

=127=

The scene described is vivid. When a sūrah is revealed in the Prophet's presence, and the believers listen with rapt attention, the hypocrites are uncomfortable. They glance nervously at each other, checking if any believers are observing their reactions. Their concern is not with the divine message but with human observation. "Is anyone watching you?" reveals their paranoia. If they sense they are not being watched, they slip away quietly, unable to bear the weight of the revelation.

The verse then gives the spiritual reason: "Allah has turned their hearts away." Their hearts are not merely misguided; they have been actively diverted from the truth by Allah as a punishment for their own choices.

The phrase "because they are a people who do not comprehend" indicates the root cause: their refusal to use their intellect in the way Allah intended, to reflect and understand, has led to a divine seal. This verse teaches that when a person's primary concern becomes the judgment of people rather than the judgment of Allah, they lose the ability to benefit from revelation. It also shows that avoiding the company of the righteous and the settings where the Qur'an is recited is a sign of a diseased heart. The believer should be eager to be in such gatherings, not looking for an exit.

=128=

After all the stern commands, exposures, and warnings, the sūrah draws to a close with a tender description of the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. This verse is placed here as a soft landing after the difficult journey of Sūrah At‑Tawbah. The address is to all humanity, or specifically to the Quraysh, and by extension to the entire Ummah. "A messenger from among yourselves" highlights his humanity and his connection to the people. He is not an angel or a stranger; he shares their lineage, their language, and their daily struggles. This makes his example imitable and his compassion relatable.

The verse then lists three of his most beautiful qualities. First, he is deeply "concerned by your suffering.

" The word ʿazīz means severe or hard; whatever causes the believers hardship is heavy upon him. He feels their pain and is distressed by the obstacles they face. Second, he is "anxious for your well-being.

" The word ḥarīṣ indicates a burning desire; he is extremely eager for their guidance and salvation. He wants them to enter Paradise and escape the Fire, and his entire mission is driven by this urgency. Third, he is "gracious and merciful" specifically to the believers. Ra’ūf and Raḥīm are two names of Allah, here applied to the Prophet in a derived sense. He is exceptionally gentle, kind, and forgiving with those who follow him.

This description was meant to soften the hearts of the believers after the intensity of the surah and to remind the hypocrites and polytheists of the treasure they were rejecting. The verse calls every believer to love the Prophet with a deep love based on these qualities, and to strive to embody a fraction of that concern, eagerness, and mercy towards others.

=129=

The sūrah that began with a declaration of dissociation from the treaty‑breakers ends with a declaration of ultimate reliance. After the beautiful description of the Prophet’s character, Allah instructs him on what to say if the people still turn away from his message. Their rejection is painful, but the Prophet is not left in despair. The formula given is a fortress for the soul. “Allah is sufficient for me” is ḥasbiya Allāh, a statement of complete contentment and dependence. Allah is enough; no human support, no worldly power is needed when He is on one’s side. “There is no god worthy of worship except Him” is the foundation of tawḥīd, reminding the Prophet that his mission is to affirm this truth regardless of the response. “In Him I put my trust” is tawakkul, the active entrusting of all affairs to Allah after doing one’s best.

The verse concludes with “He is the Lord of the Mighty Throne,” emphasising Allah’s supreme dominion over the entire universe. The Throne is the greatest of all created things, and its Lord is the One who controls all.

This is a source of immense comfort: the rejection of a few tribes is nothing before the Lord of the Mighty Throne. This verse was among the last to be revealed in this sūrah, and it served as a powerful reminder to the Prophet and to all believers that when facing opposition, the ultimate response is to turn to Allah, reaffirm one’s faith, and place absolute trust in the Lord of all creation. It is the perfect seal for a sūrah that dealt with enmity, hypocrisy, and the struggle for the soul of the community, bringing the focus back to the unshakeable bedrock of reliance on Allah alone.
