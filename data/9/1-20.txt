=1=

This opening verse of Sūrah At-Tawbah comes without the usual basmalah, a deliberate omission that signals the gravity of what follows. The declaration is a complete dissolution of treaty obligations: “a discharge from all obligations” means that Allah and His Messenger are no longer bound by the pacts that had been made with those polytheists who repeatedly broke their word. The historical context is vital here. After the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah, several Arab tribes entered into alliances with the Muslims, but many of them—most notoriously the Quraysh and their confederates—violated the terms openly. They attacked allied tribes, conspired against the believers, and proved that their agreements were worthless. As the time for the pilgrimage approached in the ninth year after Hijrah, the Muslims needed to secure the sacred precincts and establish that the era of dealing treacherously with treaties was over.

The verse addresses the believers who were party to those treaties, reminding them that the discharge comes directly from Allah and His Messenger, so it carries ultimate authority. When we break the wording into its parts, “a discharge from all obligations” is a sweeping cancellation, not a selective adjustment.

The phrase “by Allah and His Messenger” leaves no room for negotiation; it is a divine and prophetic decree. The expression “to the polytheists you have entered into treaties with” identifies the recipients: those idolaters with whom the Muslims had formal agreements. This verse is not a sudden breach of faith by the Muslims; rather it is a public annulment of pacts that the other side had already rendered void through their own actions. The principle here is that treaties are sacred in Islam, but they cannot be a one‑way street. When the other party systematically destroys the basis of trust, the injured side has the right to declare the agreement null. This discharge was proclaimed on the greatest day of pilgrimage, making it a matter of public record heard by all of Arabia. It also serves as a prelude to the four‑month grace period mentioned in the next verse, during which those who wished to make amends or leave peacefully could do so. The underlying message is one of justice: continued peace with those who keep their word, and a clear severance from those who do not.

=2=

The four‑month period granted here is an extension of the discharge announced in the first verse, giving the polytheists time to reflect, relocate, or accept Islam without being attacked. “You may travel freely through the land” is a guarantee of safe passage, a remarkable provision in a context where warfare was the norm after treaty violations. This safe‑conduct allowed those who were hostile to move about unmolested and either return to their own territories or come to a decision about faith. The four months began, according to the most authoritative reports, on the day of the greater pilgrimage when the declaration was read aloud, the tenth of Dhul‑Ḥijjah, and ran until the tenth of Rabīʿ al‑Thānī. So it was not an arbitrary period; it was tied to the sacred months and the pilgrimage season, during which fighting was already prohibited by Arab custom and later by Islamic law.

The second part of the verse introduces a sobering warning: “but know that you will have no escape from Allah.” No matter where they travelled or how they evaded the Muslim forces, they could not flee Allah’s grasp.

This is a call to spiritual reckoning, reminding them that physical safety within those four months does not mean ultimate impunity. The final phrase, “Allah will disgrace the disbelievers,” announces that persistent rejection will end in humiliation, either in this world through defeat or in the Hereafter through punishment. The disgrace here is not arbitrary; it is the natural consequence of defying truth after it has been made clear. The offer of safe travel combined with a solemn warning reveals the Qur’anic approach: clarity, opportunity, and then accountability. It also dismantles any misconception that the subsequent command to fight was an ambush; the polytheists were given every chance to rectify their stance.

=3=

This verse details the public proclamation that took place on the day of the greater pilgrimage, the Day of Sacrifice during the Ḥajj. The Prophet, peace be upon him, first sent Abū Bakr to lead the pilgrimage and then dispatched ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭālib to read these verses aloud to the assembled crowds at Minā.

The phrase “to all people” shows the universal scope of the announcement: it was directed not only at the treaty‑breakers but at every tribe present, ensuring that no one could later claim ignorance. The core message is that Allah and His Messenger are now “free of the polytheists,” meaning the obligations of protection and alliance are dissolved. Immediately after this severance, the verse opens the door to reform: “if you repent, it will be better for you.” This demonstrates that the purpose was never mere expulsion or destruction, but transformation. Repentance, sincere turning away from idolatry and towards the worship of the One God, would bring them all that is good. “But if you turn away” indicates stubborn refusal, and the warning follows that they will have no escape from Allah. Then comes the striking instruction: “give good news to the disbelievers of a painful punishment.

” This is an ironic expression; the phrase “good news” is used for something terrifying to underscore that what awaits them is certain and fully deserved. It also emphasizes the Prophet’s role as a warner. The historical report that ʿAlī stood and recited, “No disbeliever will enter Paradise, and no polytheist may perform pilgrimage after this year, and no one shall circumambulate the House naked,” highlights how the declaration translated into concrete rulings that purified the sacred precincts and affirmed the exclusivity of tawḥīd.

=4=

Even amidst the sweeping annulment of treaties, Allah carves out a clear exception for those who remained faithful. The verse specifies two conditions: they honoured every term of their treaty, and they did not support any enemy against the believers. Those who met both criteria were to be treated with full justice; the believers were commanded to honour their treaty with them until its natural expiration. This refutes any claim that Islam promotes blanket hostility or aggression. The principle is that integrity is reciprocal. The command “honour your treaty with them” is a binding directive, not a recommendation.

The verse then grounds this command in a quality beloved to Allah: taqwā, mindfulness of Him. “Surely Allah loves those who are mindful” means that keeping one’s word even with non‑Muslims when they have kept theirs is an act of piety that attracts divine love.

This is consistent with the general Quranic insistence on fulfilling contracts and covenants. The historical background includes certain tribes that had pacts with the Muslims and never violated them, such as some clans of Khuzāʿah or Banū Ḍamrah. They were allowed to live in peace until their treaty periods ended.

This verse also teaches believers that moral conduct is not conditional on the faith of the other party; justice and faithfulness are absolute values in Islam. It sets a standard that governs international relations: agreements must be observed, and only a proven breach by the other side justifies termination.

=5=

This verse is one of the most frequently misquoted passages when removed from its context. The opening, “But once the Sacred Months have passed,” refers directly to the four‑month period of safe passage given earlier. The polytheists intended here are specifically those who broke their treaties and were given that grace period. The commands that follow are military instructions: to engage them wherever they are found, to capture, besiege, and ambush them. These are tactical measures appropriate to a state of war against a treacherous enemy, not a licence for indiscriminate violence. The specific mention of “kill the polytheists” must be read with the restriction “who violated their treaties” understood from the flow of the verses. After the Sacred Months elapsed, those who had persisted in hostility were to be fought. Crucially, the verse immediately pivots to the door of mercy: “But if they repent, perform prayers, and pay alms-tax, then set them free. ” The very people who were to be fought become untouchable the moment they embrace faith, establish the prayer, and give the zakāh. The three conditions are marks of genuine conversion and entry into the Muslim community.

The phrase “set them free” means all military actions against them must cease. “Indeed, Allah is All-Forgiving, Most Merciful” explains why: divine forgiveness overwhelms past transgressions once repentance occurs. This verse, far from being a call to perpetual warfare, presents a clear path to peace. The Prophet, peace be upon him, applied this in practice: those who accepted Islam were left unharmed, and even those who did not but sought protection were granted safety, as the very next verse shows.

=6=

This verse provides an immediate balance to the preceding military command. Even during a state of conflict, if a single polytheist comes seeking protection—literally, “seeking your neighbourhood”—the Prophet is ordered to grant it. The purpose is profoundly merciful: “so they may hear the Word of Allah.” The safe conduct is not merely physical shelter; it is an opportunity for the person to listen to the Qur’an, to understand its message, to ask questions and witness Islam firsthand.

The command then goes further: after they have heard, if they do not accept the faith, the believers must “escort them to a place of safety.” The individual must be delivered to a location where they are secure from harm, not mistreated or coerced. The reason given is “for they are a people who have no knowledge.” Their hostility and idolatry are rooted in ignorance, and exposure to the truth is the remedy.

This principle shaped Islamic rules of engagement: a non‑combatant who seeks amān must be protected and given the chance to learn. The verse demonstrates the very heart of the Islamic mission—to convey the message with wisdom and to prioritise guidance over vengeance. It also shows that the goal of struggle is not the elimination of a particular people but the removal of the barriers between them and the truth.

=7=

The rhetorical question “How can such polytheists have a treaty” expresses astonishment that serial treaty‑breakers could expect their agreements to remain honoured indefinitely. The exception “except those you have made a treaty with at the Sacred Mosque” points to those who entered into a pact in the vicinity of the Masjid al‑Ḥarām, which includes the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah. Some of those tribes, like the Banū Khuzāʿah, kept their word. For them, the instruction is clear: “as long as they are true to you, be true to them. ” The reciprocity is absolute. The verse does not demand naivety; it demands faithfulness matched with faithfulness. Again the verse concludes by linking this conduct to the love of Allah for the mindful. This teaches that fulfilling treaties is a direct expression of taqwā. It also conveys that the Muslim community is to be a model of integrity; when they give their word, they uphold it, even if the world around them does not. This sets the stage for the next verse which explains why the habitual breakers cannot be trusted.

=8=

This verse answers its own question by exposing the inner reality of the treaty‑breakers. The idea of entering into a new treaty with them is unthinkable because if they gained power, they would discard all considerations of kinship and pact. “No respect for kinship or treaty” means that neither blood ties nor formal agreements would restrain them from harming the believers.

This is not conjecture; it was proven by their repeated betrayals, such as the Quraysh’s attack on the Banū Khuzāʿah, which directly violated the terms of Ḥudaybiyyah. The verse then notes that whatever pleasant words they speak are merely flattery: “They only flatter you with their tongues.” Outwardly they may profess good intentions to buy time, but “their hearts are in denial.” Their internal state is one of rejection and hatred. “Most of them are rebellious” highlights their deep‑seated defiance of moral norms. The verse gives believers a realistic understanding of the enemy they faced. It also warns against being deceived by sweet talk when actions prove otherwise. The lesson is timeless: evaluate agreements not by rhetoric but by consistent behaviour, and when a party shows by its track record that it respects neither family ties nor contracts, it cannot be trusted until it demonstrates genuine change.

=9=

Here the Qur’an summarizes the motivation behind the polytheists’ treachery. They exchanged the eternal truth for a “fleeting gain.” That gain could be worldly power, tribal pride, economic advantage, or social status.

The phrase “over Allah’s revelations” shows that they were presented with clear signs, yet they turned away because of immediate worldly interests. Not only did they stray themselves, they actively “hindered others from His Way,” obstructing people from embracing Islam through propaganda, threats, and warfare. The exclamation “Evil indeed is what they have done!” is a divine judgment on their actions. It reinforces that the severance of ties was not because of personal animosity but because of their fundamental opposition to the truth and their effort to prevent its spread.

This verse also serves as a warning to any who put worldly profit ahead of divine guidance and who use their influence to block others from faith.

=10=

This statement is both a repetition and a reinforcement of what was said earlier, but now it becomes a definition of their character. They do not observe any protective bond, whether of blood or covenant, with the believers.

The phrase “It is they who are the transgressors” turns the tables on any claim that the Muslims were the aggressors. Transgression means crossing the boundaries set by God and by human decency. The polytheists’ habitual violation of pacts and family ties qualifies them as the true transgressors, fully deserving of the annulment of their treaties. This also prepares the ground for the next verse, which opens the door to repentance and brotherhood once again.

=11=

The contrast could not be sharper. The same people who were described as treacherous and rebellious can become “your brothers in faith” through three steps: sincere repentance from shirk and past wrongs, the establishment of regular prayer, and the giving of the alms‑tax. These three are not merely external acts; they indicate a complete internal transformation and entry into the Muslim community. The moment they do this, all previous enmity is erased, and they are granted the full rights of brotherhood.

The phrase “your brothers in faith” is one of the warmest expressions in the Qur’an, conveying equality and affection. Then the verse adds that Allah makes His revelations clear “for people of knowledge,” meaning those who truly understand will appreciate the wisdom of this balanced approach—firmness against persistent treachery, but immediate embrace when genuine change occurs. It also implies that superficial readers might misinterpret the preceding verses because they lack the knowledge of context and the overarching Quranic principles of mercy and justice.

=12=

This verse addresses a further scenario: even after entering into a pact or accepting Islam, some may break their oaths and actively assault the religion by slandering it or attacking the community. In that case, the directive is to fight “the champions of disbelief,” the leaders and instigators who drive the hostility and who have a proven record of never keeping their word.

The phrase “who never honour their oaths” identifies them as irredeemable in their current state. The purpose is again made clear: “so perhaps they will desist. ” The fighting is not for annihilation but to bring an end to their aggression. This restrained and purposeful approach permeates the entire passage. It also shows that the Muslim community must be ready to defend itself against those who would destroy it from within or without, yet even then the goal remains the cessation of harm, not vengeance.

=13=

The rhetorical question aims to awaken the believers’ sense of justice and courage. The polytheists are described with three severe crimes: they broke their oaths, they plotted to expel the Messenger from Mecca, and they initiated the aggression. The conspiracy to expel the Prophet refers to the Quraysh’s plan to assassinate or banish him from his homeland, the very event that led to the Hijrah. “And attacked you first” reminds the believers that they were not the aggressors; the battles of Badr and Uḥud were fought because the Quraysh came out to destroy the Muslim community.

The question “Do you fear them?” addresses the hesitation that might arise from worldly considerations—the enemy’s numbers, wealth, or reputation. It then redirects that fear: “Allah is more deserving of your fear.” If you are true believers, your primary concern should be Allah’s command and His displeasure, not the enemy’s might.

This is a powerful call to prioritize divine authority over human intimidation. It connects directly to the next verse’s promise of divine support.

=14=

This is the divine command to engage in battle, accompanied by five promises. “Allah will punish them at your hands” means the believers become the instruments of Allah’s justice in this world. The punishment is the defeat they will suffer. “Put them to shame” adds public disgrace to their loss. “Help you overcome them” promises victory. “And soothe the hearts of the believers” addresses the emotional wounds inflicted by years of persecution, treachery, and loss. The believers had suffered greatly, and there is a recognition that seeing justice served brings healing. This soothes the grief and anger that had built up over time. The verse, therefore, is not glorifying violence for its own sake but explaining that the battle against the treaty‑breakers was a necessary step toward establishing peace, security, and emotional closure for the oppressed community.

=15=

The soothing mentioned in the previous verse is now specified as “removing rage from their hearts.” The deep‑seated anger and bitterness that the believers harboured against those who had betrayed and killed their loved ones would be removed by Allah through the victory He granted.

This is both a psychological and spiritual healing. The phrase “And Allah pardons whoever He wills” injects a note of divine mercy even into the discussion of battle. Among those enemies, some would eventually repent and be forgiven, as indeed happened with many Quraysh leaders after the conquest of Mecca. Allah’s will in pardoning is based on His knowledge and wisdom, not on human expectation.

The closing attributes, “All-Knowing, All-Wise,” assure that every decision—who is punished, who is forgiven—is made with perfect understanding and purpose. This prevents believers from feeling that they must take personal vengeance, because ultimate judgment belongs to Allah.

=16=

This verse shifts the focus inward to the Muslim community itself. The command to fight serves as a test. The believers should not assume that their mere profession of faith is sufficient; Allah will test them to distinguish the sincere from the insincere. “Proving who among you truly struggles in His cause” means that through the call to arms, the true mujāhidīn will be separated from those who hold back. Added to this is the test of loyalty: they must not take intimate allies other than Allah, His Messenger, and the believers.

In the context of the upcoming campaign to Tabūk, this was a direct challenge to those who secretly sympathised with the hypocrites or the enemy. The verse ends by reminding that Allah is fully aware of all actions, so no one can hide their true intentions.

This is a spiritual call to self‑examination before the external battle begins.

=17=

The polytheists used to boast of their service to the Sacred Mosque, providing water to pilgrims and caring for the Kaʿbah. This verse strips them of that claim. It is not fitting for them to maintain the mosques of Allah because their open disbelief cancels any merit their actions might have had. “Their deeds are void” means all their charitable and religious services are rendered worthless in the sight of Allah due to their association of partners with Him. The announcement that they will dwell in the Fire forever is a stark warning that no amount of worldly service can compensate for the absence of true faith. This verse laid the groundwork for the purification of the Sacred Mosque from idolatry, which was completed when Mecca was conquered and the idols were removed.

=18=

The true caretakers of the mosques are defined by five qualities: belief in Allah and the Last Day, establishment of prayer, payment of zakāh, and fearing none but Allah. The phrase “fear none but Allah” is particularly significant because it excludes those who serve the mosques for worldly prestige or out of fear of people. The maintenance of mosques includes both physical upkeep and spiritual leadership.

The verse then says, “It is right to hope that they will be among the truly guided,” which means that those who possess these qualities can reasonably expect to be counted among the rightly guided. It is an encouragement for believers to take up the responsibility of building and serving places of worship with pure intentions. This also contrasts sharply with the previous verse’s condemnation, establishing that mosques are for the people of tawḥīd, not for those who associate partners with Allah.

=19=

This verse directly rebuts a specific boast that some Quraysh leaders made. Historical reports mention that al‑ʿAbbās or others prided themselves on their service to the pilgrims and the upkeep of the Sacred House, considering it equivalent to faith and jihad.

The question challenges that assumption: could providing water and maintaining the Mosque possibly stand on the same level as believing in Allah and the Last Day and striving in His cause? The answer is emphatic: “They are not equal in Allah’s sight. ” Acts of service without īmān are hollow and carry no weight.

The verse then declares that Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people, meaning those who persist in this false equivalence and who reject true faith are left to their misguidance. The principle is clear: the value of any deed is rooted in the belief that motivates it. This also reassures the believers that their sacrifices of faith, emigration, and jihad far outweigh any worldly service the pagans might offer.

=20=

The verse sets the true hierarchy of honour. Three qualities are listed: belief, emigration for the sake of Allah, and striving with both wealth and lives. This specifically describes the earliest Companions, the Muhājirūn, who left their homes and possessions and fought in the path of Allah. “Greater in rank in the sight of Allah” is the real measure of superiority, not lineage, wealth, or tribal standing.

The phrase “It is they who will triumph” is an absolute promise of success, whether in this world through victory or in the Hereafter through eternal reward. This verse serves as an uplifting conclusion to the section, motivating the believers to press forward and not be disheartened by the enemy’s claims or numbers. It also seamlessly leads into the next passage, where the reward of such believers is further detailed. For the listener, it reinforces the message that the sacrifices demanded by Allah are never in vain; they elevate a person’s rank and guarantee ultimate triumph.
