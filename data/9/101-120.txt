=101=

After praising the foremost believers in the previous verse, the sūrah returns to the reality of hidden opposition. The verse states plainly that hypocrisy was not confined to the city; it existed among the nomadic tribes around Medina and among the settled residents of the city itself.

The phrase “they have mastered hypocrisy” translates the Arabic maradū ʿalā n-nifāq, meaning they became deeply entrenched, skilled, and persistent in it. Hypocrisy became their second nature.

The verse then addresses the Prophet with a limitation of his knowledge: “They are not known to you; they are known to Us.” This is a profound reassurance. Despite the Prophet’s insight and the signs of hypocrisy that sometimes surfaced, there remained a segment of hypocrites whose true state was completely hidden from him. Allah alone knew them. This protected the Prophet from the burden of judging hearts and allowed him to deal with them based on their outward profession.

The phrase “We will punish them twice in this world” has been interpreted in several ways by the Companions and early scholars. Some said it refers to the humiliation they suffered through exposure and the constant fear of being found out, combined with a painful illness or death. Others said the first punishment is the distress of seeing Islam’s victory and their own isolation, and the second is the punishment of the grave. Still others linked it to being forced to pay zakāh and participate in campaigns they hated. Regardless, the double punishment in this world is followed by a “tremendous punishment” in the Hereafter.

This verse teaches the believers that unseen hypocrisy is a grave spiritual disease, but it also reassures them that ultimate judgment rests with the All‑Knowing. It warns that persistent insincerity brings suffering even in this life, not just the next.

=102=

This verse introduces a group distinct from the entrenched hypocrites. These were believers who had stayed behind from the Tabūk expedition but did not resort to lies and false excuses. Instead, they openly confessed their sin, acknowledging their failure without justification. The verse says they “mixed goodness with evil”—their earlier righteous deeds were tarnished by this serious lapse, but the foundation of faith was still present. Because of their honesty, the verse offers a powerful word of hope: “It is right to hope that Allah will turn to them in mercy.

” The Arabic particle ʿasā used here, when coming from Allah, indicates a near certainty. Among those who fell into this category were individuals who tied themselves to the pillars of the mosque in Medina, vowing not to free themselves until the Prophet and Allah forgave them. One well‑known example is Abū Lubābah, who had earlier committed a different error and bound himself to a pillar until his repentance was accepted. Their confession was not a private whisper but a public acknowledgment, demonstrating true remorse. The verse closes with two divine names, All‑Forgiving and Most Merciful, to emphasise that the door of divine pardon is wide open for those who are truthful about their faults.

This passage teaches that the worst state is not sin itself but the refusal to admit it. Honest confession, even after a major failure, can lead to complete forgiveness, while denial and excuses lead to the hardening of the heart.

=103=

After their confession, Allah instructs the Prophet to take ṣadaqah from their wealth. This charity serves two purposes: to purify them from the stain of their sin and to cleanse their wealth and souls.

The word tuhirruhum indicates a cleansing of their spiritual state, and tuzakkīhim means to increase and bless them in goodness. The act of giving charity was a tangible proof of their repentance and a means of making amends.

The verse then commands the Prophet to pray for them. His supplication was not a mere formality; it was a source of profound comfort (sakan) for their troubled hearts. Knowing that the Messenger of Allah was praying for their forgiveness brought them peace and relief. This incident shows the unique role of the Prophet as an intercessor and spiritual father to his community, and it underlines the importance of sincere charity accompanied by the prayers of the righteous.

The verse ends by stating that Allah is All‑Hearing of their confessions and All‑Knowing of their intentions, so no sincerity goes unnoticed. In a broader sense, this established the principle that charity is a means of purification from sins, and the leader’s prayer for the community is a blessing. It also teaches that those who err can transform their mistake into an opportunity for good by giving generously and seeking the prayers of the pious.

=104=

This rhetorical question redirects the focus of those who repented—and indeed all believers—to the ultimate source of acceptance. While the Prophet was commanded to take their charity and pray for them, the verse reminds everyone that it is Allah alone who truly accepts repentance.

The phrase “receives their charity” means He takes it with His right hand, as mentioned in a hadith, nurturing it until it grows like a mountain. The acceptance of charity is a divine act; the Prophet’s role is a means, but the actual acceptance is entirely in Allah’s hands. The verse uses two intensive names of Allah: at‑Tawwāb, the One who constantly turns in mercy to those who repent, and ar‑Raḥīm, the Most Merciful.

This is meant to inspire hope in the hearts of sinners. No matter how grave the error, the door of return is open, and the Lord is eager to forgive. The rhetorical question also serves as a gentle rebuke to those who might focus too much on the external validation of their repentance through the Prophet’s prayer while forgetting the inner relationship with Allah.

The verse teaches that repentance and charity must be directed sincerely to Allah, and that His acceptance is the only real guarantee of forgiveness.

=105=

At first glance, “Do as you will” seems like an open permission, but it is a powerful and stern warning. It follows the encouragement to repent and give charity, and it addresses those who might waver between sincerity and hypocrisy.

The phrase means: act as you choose, but know that your actions are under constant surveillance. Allah observes everything, the Messenger is informed of the community’s true state through revelation, and the believers will witness the outward fruits of your deeds. This triple observation leaves no room for hidden misdeeds.

The verse then points to the ultimate return to Allah, the Knower of the seen and the unseen, who will inform every soul of the full reality of its actions on the Day of Judgment. This is not a licence to sin; it is a threat wrapped in a command. It is similar to the statement, “Do whatever you want, for I know what you are doing. ” The effect on the listener is meant to be profound introspection. For the believers, it instils a constant awareness of accountability. For the hypocrites, it warns that their pretence will eventually be exposed. The verse is a masterful psychological tool that employs the concept of surveillance—both divine and communal—to inspire righteous behaviour.

=106=

This verse introduces the case of the three famous Companions who remained behind from Tabūk without a valid excuse but did not lie about it. They were Kaʿb ibn Mālik, Murārah ibn ar‑Rabīʿ, and Hilāl ibn Umayyah. Unlike the hypocrites who offered false excuses and were dealt with firmly, and unlike those who confessed and gave charity, these three were placed in a state of suspense. The verse says they are “left for Allah’s decision,” meaning the Prophet received no revelation concerning their fate. The entire Muslim community was ordered to boycott them; no one was to speak to them. They were neither punished nor forgiven, and the uncertainty was agonising. The verse states that Allah may either punish them or turn to them in mercy, and He is All‑Knowing about their inner truth and All‑Wise in the way He is dealing with them. This period of suspension lasted for fifty days, and it served as a profound purification for their souls.

The verse teaches that divine mercy is not to be taken for granted; even sincere but negligent believers can be subjected to severe spiritual trials to refine them. It also shows that Allah’s wisdom sometimes delays relief to deepen a servant’s repentance and reliance upon Him.

=107=

This verse exposes the story of Masjid aḍ‑Ḍirār, the Mosque of Harm. A group of hypocrites, led by Abū ʿĀmir ar‑Rāhib, a man who had fought against Islam and fled to the Byzantines, built a mosque near Qubā’. They claimed it was for prayer, especially for the sick and on rainy nights, and they asked the Prophet to come and pray there, thereby giving it his seal of approval. But the Qur’an reveals their four hidden objectives: to cause harm to the believers by drawing them away from the true mosque, to promote disbelief through secret meetings, to divide the community, and to serve as a safe house for the enemy, Abū ʿĀmir, who was plotting to return with a Byzantine army.

The phrase “they will definitely swear” indicates their deceptive oaths that their intentions were purely good. But Allah bears witness that they are liars.

The verse teaches the grave danger of religious institutions being used as fronts for subversive activities. It also warns against being deceived by fine‑sounding religious language when the underlying aim is to split the community. The intention behind any sacred work is what determines its value in the sight of Allah.

=108=

In this verse, the Prophet is categorically forbidden from praying in the Mosque of Harm. The command “Do not ever pray in it” serves to delegitimise the structure entirely. The Prophet’s prayer would have been seen as validation, so this prohibition was essential.

The verse then contrasts it with the mosque “founded on righteousness from the first day. ” According to many Companions, this refers to the Mosque of Qubā’, which the Prophet himself helped build when he first arrived in Medina during the Hijrah. Its very foundation was laid upon taqwā—God‑consciousness and sincerity. Others said it could refer to the Prophet’s Mosque itself.

The phrase “in it are men who love to be purified” specifically praised the people of Qubā’, who were exceptionally meticulous in personal cleanliness, using water for purification. When the Prophet asked them what they did, they told him they used stones and then water, and he confirmed this was the source of the praise. Allah’s love for those who purify themselves encompasses both physical purity and spiritual purification from sins.

The verse establishes that the sanctity of a mosque is not in its structure but in the sincerity of its founders and the purity of its congregation. It also highlights the high status of the Qubā’ Mosque and the virtue of cleanliness.

=109=

This verse presents a powerful parable to illustrate the contrast between the two mosques. The first building is founded on taqwā—the fear of Allah and the desire for His pleasure. This foundation is solid, rooted in the firmest ground, and it leads the builder upwards towards acceptance and Paradise.

The second building is founded on the very edge of a cliff that is actively crumbling and giving way. The foundation is so unstable that it cannot support the structure, and inevitably the whole building collapses, plunging the builder into the depths of Hellfire. The imagery is terrifying: a person who thought he was constructing something beneficial discovers that his work is the cause of his destruction. The rhetorical question “Which is better?” leaves the answer obvious.

The verse concludes by stating that Allah does not guide the wrongdoing people, meaning those who build their actions on deceit and hypocrisy are left to the consequences of their own choices. This parable applies not only to mosques but to all deeds. Any action, no matter how outwardly impressive, if built on insincerity, arrogance, or a desire to harm, is like that unstable cliff edge. It will collapse under the weight of divine scrutiny and take its builder to ruin.

=110=

The physical structure they built did not go away immediately; even after exposure, it remained a monument to their treachery. The verse says that this building “will never cease to fuel hypocrisy in their hearts.” It served as a constant source of doubt and insincerity, a reminder of their failed plot and their stubbornness in not repenting.

The phrase “until their hearts are torn apart” is a graphic description of death or of the ultimate destruction of their inner selves. Some scholars say it means they will die as hypocrites, their hearts consumed by the very division they sought to sow. Others say it refers to the moment when the mosque itself was torn down by the order of the Prophet after his return from Tabūk. When they saw their work demolished, their hearts were shattered with grief and rage.

The verse concludes with Allah being All‑Knowing of their hidden schemes and All‑Wise in the command to destroy their mosque and expose them. The lesson is that a sinful project, even if it stands physically, continues to corrode the souls of those behind it. The only cure is to demolish the wrong and turn to Allah in repentance.

=111=

After exposing the mosque of hypocrisy, the sūrah shifts to one of the most uplifting verses in the entire Qur’an. The commercial metaphor is used to describe the relationship between Allah and the believers. Allah, the Creator and Owner of everything, purchases from the believers their own lives and wealth—which He initially gave them—and in return grants them Paradise.

This is an extraordinary act of divine generosity. The believers pay with their mortal, fleeting existence and receive eternal, perfect bliss. The transaction is carried out through jihad: they fight and are killed or kill, but in both cases the price is paid and the reward guaranteed.

The verse then declares that this promise is a binding truth, recorded not only in the Qur’an but also in the earlier scriptures—the Torah and the Gospel. This emphasizes the unity of the divine message and the centrality of sacrifice for God’s cause across all dispensations. The rhetorical question, “Whose promise is truer than Allah’s?” is an unanswerable challenge. It is followed by a command to rejoice in this bargain. The believers should feel not regret but immense joy for having made the most profitable trade imaginable.

The verse concludes by calling this “the ultimate triumph. ” No worldly gain, no victory on any battlefield, compares to the success of securing Paradise through this divine transaction. For the Companions and for all believers, this verse transforms the sacrifices of life and wealth from a loss into the wisest investment.

=112=

This verse describes the believers who are party to the great transaction. The list of their qualities begins with repentance, indicating that a believer is not sinless but is one who constantly turns back to Allah. They are devoted to worship, a life of servitude. They praise Allah in all conditions, which some commentators link to saying alḥamdulillāh, but it encompasses a general attitude of gratitude and recognition of Allah’s perfections.

The term as‑sā’iḥūn, translated here as “who fast,” literally means those who journey or travel. Many early scholars, including Ibn ʿAbbās, explained that it refers to those who fast, because fasting is a spiritual journey away from desires. Others said it refers to those who travel for jihad or in pursuit of knowledge.

The next qualities relate to prayer: bowing and prostrating. Then the social dimension appears: they encourage good and forbid evil, and they observe the limits set by Allah, meaning they respect His boundaries in all aspects of life.

The verse ends with a command to give good news to such believers. This good news is the Paradise of the previous verse. The qualities listed show that the faith is comprehensive, covering inward repentance, acts of worship, and social responsibility. It contrasts sharply with the hypocrisy described earlier.

=113=

This verse establishes a definitive boundary in the believers’ relationships with polytheists, particularly deceased relatives. The Prophet and the believers are forbidden from praying for the forgiveness of those who died upon shirk.

The phrase “even if they were close relatives” underscores that the bond of faith supersedes the bond of blood when it comes to matters of the Hereafter. The condition “after it has become clear to the believers that they are bound for the Hellfire” means when a person dies in a state of clear disbelief, without repentance, their fate is sealed, and intercession for their forgiveness is no longer appropriate. This verse was revealed in relation to the Prophet’s desire to pray for his uncle Abū Ṭālib, who had protected him but did not embrace Islam before death. When the Prophet sought to ask forgiveness for him, this verse and others prohibited it. Some believers also wished to pray for their pagan parents.

The verse teaches that while kindness and justice to living non‑Muslim relatives are commanded, seeking divine forgiveness after their death on shirk is a transgression of the limits set by Allah. This preserves the purity of the faith and emphasises the seriousness of dying in a state of disbelief.

=114=

An objection could arise: did not Abraham pray for his father’s forgiveness, and is he not a model for the believers? This verse clarifies the context. Abraham’s prayer was not an ongoing practice; it was based on a specific promise he had made to his father that he would ask Allah to forgive him, hoping his father might yet believe. But when it became absolutely clear to Abraham—whether through his father’s death on disbelief or through divine revelation—that his father was an unchangeable enemy of Allah, he immediately dissociated himself and stopped the prayer entirely.

The verse then praises Abraham as “tender-hearted” (awwāh) and “forbearing” (ḥalīm). Awwāh is a term indicating deep compassion, frequent sighing out of fear and love of Allah, and heartfelt supplication. His forbearance is his patience in the face of his people’s opposition. This verse makes clear that Abraham is not a precedent for praying for polytheists after their death. His prayer was conditional, temporary, and abandoned once the truth was manifest. So the prohibition in the previous verse remains intact, and Abraham’s example in this specific matter is not to be followed, while his other noble qualities are to be emulated.

=115=

This verse articulates a fundamental principle of divine justice: Allah does not hold people accountable for a prohibition until it has been clearly communicated to them. A community that has received guidance is not declared astray or punished for a matter that was ambiguous or unknown to them. Only after the limits are made explicitly clear, through revelation, can those who violate them be considered deviant. This verse was revealed to comfort those believers who had previously prayed for their deceased polytheist relatives before the prohibition of verse 113 was sent down. They feared they had committed a grave sin. This verse assures them that since the prohibition had not yet been clarified, they would not be held accountable for their past actions. It also explains why Abraham’s earlier prayer was not held against him.

The verse establishes that ignorance of a ruling is a valid excuse before the law is known, but once knowledge comes, obedience is obligatory. It also reminds that Allah has full knowledge of everything, so His timing in revealing laws is perfectly wise.

=116=

This verse reinforces the absolute sovereignty of Allah, which underlies all the commands and prohibitions in this passage. Everything in the heavens and the earth is under His dominion. He is the sole giver of life and death; no one can escape His decree.

The verse then draws the practical conclusion: since Allah alone holds all power, besides Him humanity has no true guardian or helper. This demolishes any reliance on polytheist relatives, wealth, or human allies. The verse connects directly to the preceding prohibition of seeking forgiveness for polytheists and the call to complete loyalty to Allah. It teaches that all protection and aid come from the Creator, so to compromise faith for the sake of worldly ties is to abandon the only real source of help. For the believers, this is a source of strength and independence from the pressures of family and society.

=117=

This verse reflects on the Tabūk expedition, known as the “time of hardship” (ʿusrah). It was a moment of extreme difficulty due to heat, distance, and scarcity of provisions. The believers who marched out were the sincere Muhājirūn and Anṣār. The verse says that Allah turned in mercy to the Prophet first, then to the Emigrants and the Helpers.

The mention of the Prophet, who is sinless, indicates Allah’s gracious acceptance of his efforts and his leadership during the hardship. For the believers, it acknowledges that “the hearts of a group of them had almost faltered.” Some of them experienced severe doubt and anxiety, wavering under the immense pressure. But they did not give in, and Allah accepted their repentance for any inner weakness. He then turned to them in mercy. The verse describes Allah as Ra’ūf (Ever Gracious) and Raḥīm (Most Merciful) specifically “to them,” showing a special divine care for that generation.

This is a beautiful reassurance that even the greatest Companions were human, susceptible to moments of weakness, but divine mercy enveloped them because of their sincerity and ultimate perseverance.

=118=

The story of the three—Kaʿb ibn Mālik, Murārah ibn ar‑Rabīʿ, and Hilāl ibn Umayyah—is narrated in great detail in the authentic hadith. After being boycotted for fifty days, their psychological state became unbearable. The verse describes how “the earth, despite its vastness, seemed to close in on them.

” This is a powerful image of claustrophobic guilt; they felt trapped, with no escape. Their souls were “torn in anguish,” a deep inner torment. In that state, they realised with absolute certainty that there is no refuge from Allah except in Allah Himself. No human being, no cave, no distance could save them from His judgment. So they turned entirely to Him in supplication and repentance. Then, at the end of this agonising trial, Allah turned to them in mercy.

The phrase “so that they might repent” indicates that the trial itself was designed to guide them to a profound, transformative repentance. The verse seals their story with the names at‑Tawwāb and ar‑Raḥīm. Their enduring legacy in the Qur’an is a testament to the fact that even grave neglect of duty, when met with absolute truthfulness and sincere repentance, leads to divine forgiveness and eternal honour.

=119=

After the story of the three, whose salvation came through their truthfulness, the Qur’an addresses all believers with a concise, comprehensive command. “Be mindful of Allah” is the instruction to cultivate taqwā, a constant awareness of Allah that leads to obedience and avoidance of sin. The command “and be with the truthful” links taqwā directly to one’s social environment. It is not enough to have a personal intention of righteousness; one must keep the company of the ṣādiqīn—those who are truthful in their speech, sincere in their actions, and who do not waver in their commitment to the truth. The three were ultimately saved because they aligned themselves with truthfulness, refusing to lie as the hypocrites did.

This verse teaches that character is deeply influenced by companionship. To remain steadfast in faith, a believer must associate with those whose words and deeds match their inner reality. The verse is a timeless call for integrity and wise selection of friends and community.

=120=

This verse addresses the obligation of supporting the Messenger and participating in the struggle. It states that it was not fitting for the residents of Medina and the surrounding Bedouins to stay behind while the Prophet marched out, or to consider their own lives more precious than his. The life of the Prophet was the most precious to the community, and his safety and mission were of paramount importance.

The verse then offers a profound spiritual consolation: every hardship endured in the path of Allah is not wasted. Thirst, fatigue, and hunger—the common sufferings of a military campaign—are recorded as righteous deeds. Even the mere act of entering enemy territory in a way that frightens the disbelievers, or inflicting any loss upon them, is written as a good deed. Nothing is too small to be rewarded. “Surely Allah never discounts the reward of the good-doers” assures the believers that their sacrifices, no matter how seemingly insignificant, are fully preserved and magnified in the divine scales. This verse transforms the physical pains of struggle into acts of worship and sources of reward, motivating the believers to never hold back from supporting the cause of Allah and His Messenger.
