=41=

After reminding the believers of Allah’s support for His Prophet in the most desperate moment—alone in the cave—this verse issues a comprehensive call to action. The address “O believers” draws the community back into a collective responsibility. The command to “march forth whether it is easy or difficult” removes every possible excuse. Ease and difficulty cover all circumstances: youth and old age, wealth and poverty, hot weather and cold, personal comfort and hardship. The expedition of Tabūk was particularly difficult; it was the height of summer, the distance was great, the enemy was the superpower of the age, and the harvest was ripe.

Yet Allah commands them to go regardless. The phrase “strive with your wealth and your lives” pairs financial sacrifice with physical sacrifice. In the battle of Tabūk, the wealthy Companions like ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān gave enormous sums, while others gave their physical effort and risked their lives. The verse declares that this striving is “best for you,” a compact statement that reorients their entire calculus. The benefit is not for Allah but for their own souls, in this world through honour and victory, and in the Hereafter through immense reward.

The closing words “if only you knew” are a gentle lament, indicating that human perception is often blinded by immediate comfort, unable to grasp the vastness of divine recompense. This verse is a timeless call to the believers to remain in a state of readiness to sacrifice for their faith, refusing to let comfort become an obstacle to obedience.

=42=

This verse exposes the true nature of the hypocrites who stayed behind. The “gain” refers to immediate worldly booty, like that of a nearby raid. If the campaign had been close and promised quick material reward, they would have eagerly participated. But the journey to Tabūk was long, the spoils uncertain, and the danger high, so “the distance seemed too long for them. ” Their reluctance was not based on genuine inability but on a cost‑benefit analysis of worldly profit. The verse predicts their behaviour after the believers return: they will swear by Allah, insisting that they truly wanted to go but lacked the means. Their oaths are elaborate but empty. The divine judgment is stark: “They are ruining themselves. ” Their lies and desertion do not harm Allah or the Prophet; they destroy their own souls.

The final phrase, “And Allah knows that they are surely lying,” strips away all pretense. No human court might convict them, but the Knower of the unseen has already pronounced the verdict. This teaches that sincerity cannot be faked before Allah, and that outward excuses will not avail when the heart is full of worldly calculation. It also warns believers against judging by appearances, because some people may swear eloquently while their hearts are far from the truth.

=43=

This gentle rebuke of the Prophet, peace be upon him, reveals his immense mercy and the high standard to which he was held. He had allowed some of the hypocrites to remain behind when they came with excuses, not wanting to be harsh. The verse opens with “May Allah pardon you,” indicating that his decision, though motivated by compassion, was not the ideal course of action at that moment.

The question “Why did you give them permission before those who told the truth were distinguished from those who were lying?” points to the wisdom of waiting. Had he not excused them, the genuine believers who had valid reasons would have been forced to reveal themselves honestly, and the liars would have been exposed by their persistence in making false excuses. By granting permission early, the lines between the sincere and the hypocritical were blurred. This was a learning moment for the Prophet and, through him, for all leaders: leniency is good, but it should not come at the cost of clarifying the truth and exposing falsehood in critical matters of communal obligation. The rebuke also demonstrates the Qur’an’s honesty in recording even a corrective addressed to the Messenger, confirming that he is a human who is guided, and that ultimate perfection belongs only to Allah. For the community, it is a lesson that leadership requires discernment and sometimes firmness to protect the collective integrity.

=44=

This verse sets the benchmark for genuine faith. True belief in Allah and the Last Day produces a natural willingness to sacrifice. The ones who truly hold these beliefs “do not ask for exemption” from jihad with their wealth and lives. Their hearts are so convinced of the reward and the importance of the struggle that they would not seek to be excused except under the most dire and obvious necessity, and even then they would come with heavy hearts, not with excuses. The verse distinguishes between the faithful, who feel the weight of obligation, and the hypocrites, who eagerly seek loopholes.

The second sentence, “And Allah has perfect knowledge of those who are mindful of Him,” serves as a reassurance that Allah knows the inward state of every person. Even if a believer’s situation genuinely prevents him from going, his sincere intention and taqwā are fully known to Allah, and he will be rewarded accordingly. The verse thus encourages believers to cultivate an attitude of readiness and to leave the judgment of hearts to Allah, while making it clear that a pattern of seeking exemption without valid cause is a sign of weak faith.

=45=

This verse draws the logical conclusion from the previous one. The only people who would seek to be excused from the struggle are those who lack genuine faith. They “have no faith in Allah or the Last Day” in a true, operative sense; whatever they claim with their tongues is contradicted by their actions.

Additionally, their “hearts are in doubt. ” They are not serene disbelievers but are “torn by their doubts,” wavering between belief and disbelief, never finding peace. This state of uncertainty makes every sacrifice seem too costly, because they are not convinced of the eternal reward. The verse masterfully diagnoses the psychological root of reluctance: a heart unsettled by doubt cannot commit to a costly course of action. For the sincere believer, this serves as a warning to guard against doubts and to seek certainty through knowledge and reflection. It also helps the community understand that the problem with the hypocrites was not merely laziness but a fundamental spiritual sickness. The cure is not harsher commands but a renewal of īmān that removes the hesitation caused by doubt.

=46=

This verse adds another layer of exposure. The hypocrites’ lack of intention is proved by their lack of preparation. A person who genuinely wants to travel for a campaign gathers provisions, arranges a mount, and gets ready. They did none of this, betraying their insincerity. Then the verse reveals a deeper divine decree: “But Allah disliked that they should go.” Because of their evil intentions, Allah did not want their presence among the sincere believers. Their going would not have added strength; it would have brought corruption. So “He let them lag behind,” creating circumstances—such as their own laziness and cowardice—that resulted in their staying. The passive phrase “it was said to them” may refer to the inner voice of their own nafs or the whisper of Shayṭān, or it could be the decree of Allah that they are consigned to remain with the helpless—the women, children, sick, and elderly who legitimately stayed. In any case, they were grouped with those who could not fight, which was a humiliation for able‑bodied men. This teaches that sometimes being prevented from a good deed is itself a punishment for hidden evil. The outward failure to act is a reflection of a prior inward failure.

=47=

The wisdom behind their being held back is now explained. If the hypocrites had joined the Tabūk expedition, they would have added no military value, only trouble. They would have “scrambled around,” moving through the ranks, whispering doubts, spreading rumours about the difficulty of the journey, the strength of the enemy, and the foolishness of the campaign. Their goal was to spread discord and break the unity of the believers. The verse acknowledges a vulnerability within the Muslim camp: “some of you would have eagerly listened to them.” Not all believers are equally strong; there are those with weak faith who can be swayed by persuasive talk. The hypocrites knew how to target such individuals. By keeping them away, Allah protected the army from internal collapse.

The verse concludes that Allah has perfect knowledge of the wrongdoers; He knows their methods, their targets, and the harm they could cause. For the believers, this is a reassurance that Allah’s management of affairs, even when it appears as a setback, is rooted in deep wisdom. It also warns the community to be vigilant about internal subversion, then and now.

=48=

The current plotting is not new. The verse recalls their past attempts at causing discord. Earlier in Medina, the hypocrites had tried to undermine the Prophet’s authority, spread false rumours about his wives, and incite division between the Muhājirūn and the Anṣār. They “devised every possible plot,” scheming in every way their malice could invent. But all their efforts failed because “the truth came and Allah’s Will prevailed.” The truth refers to the clear proofs of Islam and the divine support given to the Messenger. No matter how intricate their schemes, they could not stop the unfolding of Allah’s plan. The result was “much to their dismay,” because the very thing they hated flourished. This historical reminder strengthens the Prophet and the believers, showing that the enemy’s conspiracies are ultimately futile. It also prepares them for the next round of hypocritical excuses and complaints. The lesson is that falsehood can plot extensively, but when Allah wills a matter, no human scheme can overturn it.

=49=

Another specific excuse of the hypocrites is recorded here. One of them, said to be al‑Jadd ibn Qays, came to the Prophet and asked for exemption from the Tabūk expedition, arguing that if he saw the beautiful Byzantine women, he would be tempted and fall into sin. So he presented himself as a man who feared temptation and wanted to protect his faith. The divine response cuts through the pretence: “They have already fallen into temptation. ” By staying behind with insincere intention, by seeking to avoid a sacred duty, and by making false excuses, they are already deep in fitnah. Their supposed fear of sin is a mask for their cowardice and disobedience.

The verse then declares that Hell will surely engulf the disbelievers, a sharp verdict that connects their action to the root cause—disbelief. This teaches that one cannot justify disobedience by claiming to avoid a lesser temptation. The greater temptation is abandoning Allah’s command. It also warns against the subtlety of the nafs, which can dress up desertion as piety. The sincere believer checks his motives and prioritises obedience over rationalisations.

=50=

The emotional state of the hypocrites regarding the Prophet’s mission is laid bare. When victory or booty comes to the Muslims, the hypocrites are grieved. They hate to see Islam flourish because it threatens their status and exposes their insincerity.

In contrast, when a hardship or defeat occurs—such as the initial confusion at Ḥunain or any temporary setback—they feel a perverse joy. They then say, “We took our precaution in advance,” meaning that their decision to stay behind was wise and saved them from suffering. They “turn away, rejoicing,” utterly detached from the pain of the believers. This exposes their complete alienation from the community. They are not neutral; they are emotionally invested in the failure of the believers. For the Prophet and the sincere, this is a painful revelation, but it also equips them to understand that the hypocrites’ words and actions are driven by this deep‑seated enmity. It is a lesson that a true believer’s heart beats with the community; their joy is our joy, and their suffering is our suffering. The hypocrite’s heart is the opposite.

=51=

In the face of the hypocrites’ gloating over disasters, the Prophet is instructed to teach the believers a formula of unshakeable faith. The phrase “Nothing will ever befall us except what Allah has destined for us” affirms absolute divine decree. Whether victory or defeat, ease or hardship, everything is within the knowledge and will of Allah. This does not lead to passive fatalism but to courageous action, because the believer knows that his ultimate safety is not in human planning but in Allah’s protection. “He is our Protector” means Allah is the Mawlā, the one who takes charge of the believers’ affairs and secures their ultimate good. The response to the hypocrites’ smugness is not anger but a deepening of trust. The concluding instruction, “So in Allah let the believers put their trust,” makes tawakkul the defining characteristic of the community. This verse was a powerful source of strength for the Companions on the difficult Tabūk campaign, and it remains a refuge for believers in all times of hardship. It silences the inner whisper that says disaster means abandonment and replaces it with the conviction that everything is from the Wise, the Merciful.

=52=

The Prophet is instructed to contrast the two outlooks with striking clarity. The believers, in their march, expect one of only two outcomes, both of which are the best: victory, which brings honour and the spread of the faith, or martyrdom, which leads directly to Paradise. For a believer, both are a triumph. This reframes the entire concept of loss; there is no scenario in which a sincere warrior for Allah’s cause ultimately loses. The hypocrites, however, can only await torment. That torment may come “from Him,” through a natural disaster, a lightning bolt, or a sudden illness, or “at our hands,” through the believers being commanded to fight them or through the execution of justice.

This is not a prayer for their destruction but a statement of fact about the consequences of their hypocrisy. The challenge “So keep waiting! We too are waiting with you” is a firm declaration of confidence. It leaves the matter in Allah’s hands and refuses to be intimidated by the hypocrites’ schemes. For the believers today, this verse offers a complete reframing of life’s trials: a believer’s path always leads to good, and the enemies of truth have nothing to wait for but ruin, unless they repent.

=53=

This verse addresses the hypocrites’ potential attempt to give charity in order to prove their loyalty or to silence criticism. The Prophet is told to declare bluntly that their donations will never be accepted. The reason is not the amount or the outward act but the state of the givers: “for you have been a rebellious people.

” The word fāsiqūn implies persistent, wilful disobedience and departure from faith. An act of worship is only accepted when it springs from īmān and sincerity. The hypocrites lack both. This verse was a devastating blow to any attempt by them to pretend to be part of the community through financial contributions. It also establishes the crucial principle that Allah looks at hearts and intentions, not merely at the external deed. A charity given to earn a reputation, or to cover up hypocrisy, is worthless in the divine scales.

This teaching safeguards the community from being impressed by the wealth of the insincere and reminds every Muslim that the purification of the soul is a prerequisite for any good deed to be accepted.

=54=

The previous verse’s verdict is now explained in detail. Three reasons are given for the rejection of their charity. First, they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger; their kufr cancels all deeds. Second, their prayer, which is the pillar of religion, is performed only “half-heartedly,” with laziness, without true devotion, and often to be seen by others. Third, their spending is always accompanied by resentment; they give not out of love for Allah but grudgingly, feeling it as a loss rather than an investment for the Hereafter. These three points paint a picture of a person whose entire religious life is a hollow shell. They do the motions but the soul is absent.

The verse teaches that outward acts without a believing and willing heart are rejected. It also serves as a mirror for believers to examine their own prayers and charity: do we give cheerfully? Do we pray with presence of heart? The hypocrisy condemned here can have degrees, and the remedy is to constantly renew one’s faith and intention.

=55=

The hypocrites often possessed considerable wealth and many children, which in the societal view were signs of success and divine favour. Allah warns the Prophet not to be impressed by these outward trappings. What appears as a blessing is actually an instrument of punishment for them. Their wealth brings them anxiety, greed, and fear of loss. Their children may be a source of worry and grief.

Moreover, their attachment to these things keeps them from faith, so that when death comes, they depart from this world still disbelievers, having squandered their test. This is a profound redefinition of blessing and punishment. A comfortable life that leads to eternal loss is the worst form of torment. The verse redirects the believers’ gaze from material comparisons to the ultimate outcome. It teaches that we should never envy the prosperity of those who are distant from Allah, because that prosperity might be their ruin. True blessing is that which brings one closer to Allah and leads to a good end.

=56=

The hypocrites constantly swore oaths to convince the Muslims that they were truly part of the believing community. They would say, “We are with you, we believe as you believe.” But the verse declares, “they are not.” Their inner state is completely detached. The only motivation for their pretense is fear—fear of being treated as enemies, fear of losing protection, fear of being exposed and killed. Their Islam is a political cloak. This verse explains the phenomenon of religious affiliation based purely on social or political convenience. It was a warning to the Muslim community not to take every outward profession at face value, especially when the person’s actions and the company they keep indicate otherwise. At the same time, it directs the Prophet to leave their inner judgment to Allah, while dealing with them based on their outward claims, unless clear evidence of treachery emerges.

=57=

This vivid image reveals the inner distress of the hypocrites. They feel trapped in their double life, constantly afraid of exposure. If they could locate any secure shelter—a fortress, a mountain cave, an underground passage—that would allow them to escape the Muslim community and its demands, they would bolt towards it without a moment’s hesitation. Their presence among the believers is not a matter of choice but of painful necessity. This shows that the hypocrites were not comfortable fellow‑travellers; they were prisoners of their own lies. For the believers, this was a consolation: the very people who caused them trouble were themselves suffering a kind of torment, and their outward association was not a sign of genuine belonging.

The verse also teaches that living a lie is a heavy burden; the hypocrite can never experience the peace of a single, honest life. The believer, in contrast, finds rest in the unity of his inner and outer self.

=58=

Another trait of the hypocrites surfaces: their attitude towards the distribution of charity and public funds. The Prophet was in charge of distributing zakāh and spoils. Some hypocrites, and also some new Muslims with weak faith, were critical of how he allocated these funds. If they received a share, they were pleased and considered it fair. If they were not given anything, they became enraged and accused the Prophet of injustice. Their standard was purely self‑interest, not principle. This behaviour reveals a transactional relationship with the faith: they support the leader only as long as they personally benefit. The verse exposes this ugly trait and serves as a warning to all believers to examine their own motives when they receive or do not receive worldly benefits from the community. The sincere believer trusts the leader’s judgment and is content, knowing that the ultimate reward is with Allah.

=59=

This verse presents the beautiful alternative to the ugly greed of the previous verse. Instead of complaining, the believers should be content with whatever they receive, recognizing it as a provision from Allah. The words they should say are an expression of profound trust: “Allah is sufficient for us!

” This is hasbunā Allāh, a declaration of complete reliance. They affirm that Allah will give them from His bounty in the future, and so will His Messenger through just distribution. And their ultimate desire is directed to Allah alone, “To Allah alone we turn with hope,” indicating that their ambition is not for a few coins but for His pleasure and reward.

This is a lesson in the etiquette of receiving: gratitude, trust in the provider, and redirecting one’s highest hopes towards the Hereafter. The verse teaches that inner contentment is a priceless treasure that frees a person from the tyranny of greed and the bitterness of unmet expectations.

=60=

After addressing the hypocrites’ criticism of alms distribution, Allah lays down the definitive categories for zakāh expenditure. This is the only verse in the Qur’an that details these categories, and it thus forms the foundation of the Islamic economic system of obligatory charity. The verse lists eight categories. First, the poor (fuqarā’) and the needy (masākīn), who are those without sufficient means. Then, those employed to administer the zakāh, meaning the collectors and distributors, who may be paid for their work. The fourth category is “those whose hearts are attracted to the faith,” new Muslims or influential people whose inclination towards Islam can be strengthened through financial support. Fifth, for freeing slaves, which includes ransoming captives and assisting in emancipation. Sixth, those in debt who cannot repay. Seventh, “for Allah’s cause,” which scholars have understood primarily as jihad and all works that serve the religion’s public good.

Finally, the needy traveller stranded away from home. The verse concludes with “an obligation from Allah,” stressing that this is a divine decree, not a matter of human discretion. The two names All-Knowing and All-Wise assure that the allocation is perfectly designed to meet the needs of society. For the believers, this verse transforms zakāh from a vague charity into a structured system of social justice, ensuring that wealth circulates to those who need it most.
