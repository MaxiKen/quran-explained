=21=

After establishing in the previous verse that those who believe, emigrate, and strive with their wealth and lives are greater in rank and will triumph, this verse reveals the substance of that triumph. The good news comes directly from “their Lord,” a possessive that conveys intimacy and honour.

The first element of the glad tidings is “His mercy,” a comprehensive divine compassion that envelops the believers and admits them into realms of favour they could never earn by deeds alone. The Prophet, peace be upon him, taught that no one enters Paradise by their actions except through Allah’s mercy. This mercy is the foundation of all that follows. Next is “pleasure,” which is riḍwān, the state of Allah being eternally pleased with them.

This is the highest aspiration of the believers, for divine displeasure would render any blessing bitter. The pleasure of Allah is the crown of heavenly reward, mentioned in other verses as “the greatest triumph. ” Finally, “Gardens with everlasting bliss” describes the physical abode of delight.

The word “everlasting” emphasises that the bliss is uninterrupted, never fading, never ending. The gardens are not merely orchards but realms of perpetual joy, with whatever the soul desires.

The sequence moves from the unseen spiritual gift of mercy, to the relational gift of divine pleasure, to the tangible gift of Paradise. This good news was particularly important for the Companions who had left behind their homes, families, and wealth, because it assured them that what awaited them was incomparably better. It also serves to motivate those who might hesitate when called to struggle, reminding them that the promise of their Lord is true and generous. For all believers, this verse paints a picture of the ultimate success that makes any worldly sacrifice insignificant.

=22=

The phrase “to stay there for ever and ever” reinforces the permanence of the bliss mentioned before. Human experience in this world is defined by transience; every joy eventually ends. But the dwellers of Paradise will never be expelled, will never die, and will never experience decline.

The repetition “for ever and ever” in the translation captures the Arabic emphasis on absolute eternity. This permanence is a crucial aspect of the reward because the fear of loss diminishes enjoyment. Knowing that the Gardens are an everlasting home eliminates any anxiety about the future.

The verse then concludes with a general statement: “Surely with Allah is a great reward.” This directs attention away from the specific descriptions and towards the Giver. The reward is not merely gardens and pleasures; it is fundamentally something that lies with Allah, in His presence. The adjective “great” encompasses everything that human language cannot fully capture. This sentence also serves as a seal on the section, reminding the believers that the storehouses of divine generosity are limitless. It implicitly contrasts the fleeting gains the hypocrites and polytheists cling to with the immense, lasting reward reserved for the faithful.

The placement of this reassurance before the stern instructions that follow about family ties and the call to battle is significant: it anchors the believer’s heart in what truly matters, so that the difficult commands become easier to bear.

=23=

With the magnificence of the reward established, Allah now addresses a deeply sensitive area of loyalty. The call “O believers” prepares the hearts for a command that cuts across the strongest natural bonds. Parents and siblings represent the closest circle of blood relation, and the term “trusted allies” translates awliyā’, implying intimate friendship, protection, and deep loyalty. The condition “if they choose disbelief over belief” is critical: it is not a blanket command to sever family ties, but a prohibition against making those who actively prefer kufr over īmān one’s closest confidants and protectors, particularly when such alliances could compromise faith or the community’s security. During the early period in Medina, some Muslims were torn between their love for their disbelieving relatives and the demands of their new faith, and some even passed sensitive information to them.

This verse draws a clear boundary. To prioritise allegiance to disbelieving kin over the bonds of faith is described as “the true wrongdoers.

” The word ẓālim here is profound: it indicates a person who puts something in the wrong place, the ultimate injustice being to place the rights of creation above the rights of the Creator. The verse does not cancel the obligation of kind treatment towards parents—as emphasised elsewhere—but it subordinates that relationship to the supreme loyalty owed to Allah, His Messenger, and the believing community. This was a necessary realignment of identity for the nascent Muslim society, ensuring that the community’s cohesion was not undermined by divided hearts.

=24=

This verse expands the list of potential competing loves. The Prophet is instructed to put forward a comprehensive inventory of everything a person naturally holds dear: parents, children, siblings, spouses, the wider clan, accumulated wealth, business ventures whose downturn one fears, and comfortable dwellings. Each of these represents a legitimate attachment, but they become a test when they outweigh the love for Allah, His Messenger, and jihad in His path.

The phrase “more beloved to you” shifts the discussion from mere outward alliance to the heart’s deepest affection. The command to “wait until Allah brings about His Will” carries a severe warning: those who persist in loving these worldly attachments more than the divine cause should expect a painful consequence when Allah’s decree unfolds. It might be defeat, punishment, or simply being left behind from the ranks of the truly faithful. “Allah does not guide the rebellious people” identifies them as fāsiqūn, those who step outside the bounds of obedience. This verse was a powerful address to the Muslims who hesitated to join the Tabūk expedition because it was harvest time, the weather was hot, and the journey was long. It reminded them that all the things they feared to leave behind were from Allah in the first place, and that no worldly benefit compares to what lies with Him. The verse calls for an honest self‑examination of one’s priorities, a lesson that remains urgently relevant.

=25=

To reinforce the lesson that reliance must be on Allah, not on material advantages, the verse recalls a recent historical event. The Muslims had experienced numerous victories, but the Battle of Ḥunain served as a humbling corrective. Ḥunain took place after the conquest of Mecca, when the Muslims numbered twelve thousand. Some of them felt a sense of self‑confidence, saying, “We will not be defeated today because of small numbers.” The verse captures this: “when you took pride in your great numbers. ” But that multitude “proved of no advantage.” The enemy, the Hawāzin and Thaqīf tribes, launched a surprise attack in a narrow valley, and the Muslim army was thrown into confusion. The earth, vast as it was, “seemed to close in on you,” a psychological description of the terror and claustrophobia of the ambush. The result was that many turned back in retreat, leaving the Prophet with only a small group of steadfast Companions. This vivid recollection is not to shame the believers but to teach them a fundamental principle: numbers and worldly strength do not guarantee success. Victory is from Allah alone. This served as a direct parallel to the call for Tabūk, where some might have been overconfident or, conversely, daunted by the enemy’s might. By recalling Ḥunain, Allah reminds them that the same power that turned their defeat into victory then can do so again, provided they place their trust in Him rather than in their resources.

=26=

After the chaos described in the previous verse, the tide turned through direct divine intervention. “His reassurance” translates sakīnah, a profound inner calm and tranquillity that descends from Allah. This sakīnah settled first upon the Messenger, who remained steadfast, and then spread to the believers around him. It replaced the panic with firmness and courage. The sending down of “forces you could not see” refers to the angels who fought alongside the believers, a reality mentioned elsewhere and confirmed by the Prophet, peace be upon him. Their presence was invisible to the human eye but tangible in its effects. The disbelievers were then punished, many being killed or captured, and their camp was plundered. The concluding phrase, “Such was the reward of the disbelievers,” ties the event to a universal law: those who oppose Allah’s Messenger and His cause will eventually face defeat and punishment. The verse is deeply reassuring to the believers facing the daunting campaign to Tabūk, because it demonstrates that when human means fail, Allah’s aid arrives in ways beyond expectation. It also underscores the importance of internal state—when the heart is filled with divine serenity, external circumstances can be overcome.

=27=

Even after the punishment of the disbelievers at Ḥunain, the door of repentance remained open. “Allah will turn in grace” refers to tawbah, the acceptance of repentance. Many of the defeated Hawāzin embraced Islam after the battle, and the Prophet, peace be upon him, treated them generously, returning captives and granting gifts.

The phrase “whoever He wills” indicates that divine mercy is not arbitrary but is guided by His wisdom; those who genuinely turn to Allah are the ones He wills to forgive. The two concluding names, All-Forgiving and Most Merciful, are perfectly placed after the recounting of both punishment and grace. They remind the believers that Allah’s mercy can overtake even those who have been enemies. For the Muslims preparing for Tabūk, this served as a hope that their own shortcomings could be forgiven and that even the enemy might one day become brothers. It also prevented the believers from developing a triumphalist vengeance mentality, because the ultimate aim is that people come to faith and receive mercy.

=28=

This verse establishes a permanent ruling for the sanctity of the Sacred Mosque. The spiritual impurity of the polytheists is not physical dirt but the ritual and doctrinal impurity stemming from their idolatry. Because of this, they were forbidden from approaching the Masjid al‑Ḥarām after that year—the ninth year of Hijrah. This meant the end of the pagan pilgrimage and their naked circumambulation of the Kaʿbah. The prohibition was essential to purify the House of Allah for the worship of the One God alone.

However, the Quraysh and other tribes’ pilgrimages had brought considerable trade and economic benefit to Mecca. Anticipating the worry that this ban might cause financial hardship, the verse immediately addresses the believers’ potential fear of poverty: “If you fear poverty, Allah will enrich you out of His bounty, if He wills.

” This is a direct challenge to rely on Allah’s provision rather than on the revenues generated by idolatrous practices. The enrichment promised came true; the Islamic pilgrimage brought far greater numbers and prosperity, and the spoils of subsequent battles enriched the community. The verse closes with two names that underscore the perfection of this decree: All-Knowing, because He knows the future economic implications, and All-Wise, because the command serves a profound spiritual purpose. The verse thus combines ritual purity with trust in divine provision.

=29=

This verse extends the call to engage in combat, but now it is directed towards a specific category: the People of the Scripture who meet three conditions. They do not truly believe in Allah and the Last Day according to the monotheistic creed, they do not forbid what Allah and His Messenger have prohibited, and they do not adopt Islam as their religion. The command to fight them is until they pay the jizyah, a tax levied on non‑Muslims living under Islamic rule.

The phrase “willingly submitting” translates ʿan yadin, which has been interpreted as paying it by hand in a state of submission and not through an intermediary, symbolising their acceptance of Muslim governance. “Fully humbled” translates ṣāghirūn, meaning they acknowledge their status as subjects under the protection of the Islamic state. This verse was the legal basis for the jizyah system. It is crucial to note that this fighting is not aimed at forced conversion; the jizyah allows them to retain their religion while living under the security and justice of Muslim rule. The campaign of Tabūk was directed against the Byzantine Empire and its Arab allies, who were largely Christians. This verse clarified the objectives: hostilities would cease if they embraced Islam or agreed to pay the jizyah. This provides a framework for a pluralistic society where non‑Muslims are protected in exchange for this tax, which exempts them from military service and zakāh. The verse underscores that the ultimate goal is either their entry into the truth or their peaceful integration under a just order.

=30=

The verse exposes the theological distortions that led these communities away from pure monotheism. It reports that some Jews claimed Ezra was the son of God—a belief not mainstream today but attested in certain ancient Jewish sects. The Christians are cited for their doctrine of the divine sonship of the Messiah. Both assertions are labelled “baseless assertions,” mere words unsupported by truth or revelation.

The phrase “only parroting the words of earlier disbelievers” indicates that this is not an original deviation but an imitation of the pagans who also assigned sons to God. The exclamation “May Allah condemn them!” is a powerful expression of divine displeasure.

The closing rhetorical question, “How can they be deluded from the truth?” expresses astonishment that they could be so far misled. The verse aims to highlight the gravity of such claims and to prepare the believers to see the battle not as mere political expansion but as a struggle to uphold tawḥīd against entrenched falsehood. It also serves as a warning to Muslims not to fall into exaggerated veneration of righteous figures.

=31=

This verse clarifies a profound form of idolatry that crept into the previous scriptures’ communities. Taking rabbis and monks as lords does not mean they formally prayed to them, but that they gave them the authority to declare what is lawful and unlawful, obeying them even when it contradicted God’s commands. An authentic hadith relates that when this verse was revealed, ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim, a former Christian, said to the Prophet, “We did not worship them.” The Prophet replied, “Did they not make lawful what Allah had made unlawful and you followed them, and make unlawful what Allah had made lawful and you followed them?” ʿAdī acknowledged this, and the Prophet said, “That is worship of them. ” Alongside this, the Christians’ deification of the Messiah is mentioned.

The verse then reaffirms the core of all divine messages: they were commanded to worship only One God. The kalimah, “There is no god worthy of worship except Him,” is proclaimed, followed by the declaration that He is far above the partners they ascribe. This directly ties the combat against the Byzantines and their allies to the restoration of pure worship. It also reminds Muslims that blind obedience to scholars in matters that contravene clear revelation can become a subtle form of shirk, a warning that remains exceptionally relevant.

=32=

The imagery of light and extinguishing it is powerful. Allah’s light refers to His religion, His revelation, and the guidance brought by the Prophet.

The phrase “with their mouths” suggests that their weapons are words—slander, argument, blasphemy, conspiracy. They blow at the light as if to put it out, but their breath is feeble against a divine blaze. The verse emphatically declares that Allah will perfect His light, meaning Islam will spread, its message will be completed, and its truth will be made manifest. “Even to the dismay of the disbelievers” indicates that the opposition’s hatred will not prevent the divine plan. This verse gave immense confidence to the Companions as they faced the superpower of the time, the Byzantine Empire. For the modern believer, it is a reassurance that the truth of Islam is not dependent on human approval and that the efforts to suppress it will ultimately fail. The light here is not merely intellectual but spiritual, penetrating hearts and societies.

=33=

The previous verse’s promise of the perfection of light is now explained. The means by which Allah perfects His light is the sending of the Messenger with guidance and the religion of truth. “True guidance” is the Qur’an and the Sunnah, which lead to every good. “The religion of truth” is Islam, the complete way of life that conforms to reality and justice. The divine purpose is to make this religion prevail over all religions—meaning it will be dominant, its proofs superior, and its adherents honoured. This prevalence was fulfilled in the Arabian Peninsula during the Prophet’s lifetime and later spread far beyond. “Even to the dismay of the polytheists” reiterates that the enemies’ displeasure is irrelevant; the divine promise stands. This verse is a declaration that Islam is not one option among many but the final, complete revelation destined for supremacy. For those struggling in the path of Allah, it is a vision that defines the ultimate goal: the global establishment of truth, not for the pride of a nation but so that Allah’s guidance can reach all humanity.

=34=

After exposing the theological deviations, the sūrah turns to the financial corruption of religious leaders. Many rabbis and monks—the same class that was wrongly taken as lords—devour people’s wealth through bribery, selling indulgences, or unjust judgments. They also hinder others from the path of Allah by keeping them away from Islam.

The verse then broadens to include all who hoard gold and silver and refuse to spend in Allah’s cause. Hoarding (kanz) is the accumulation of wealth without fulfilling its due obligations, particularly zakāh. The command to “give good news of a painful torment” is, as earlier, a severe irony. This verse was a direct critique of the wealthy who withheld support from the Tabūk expedition and of the hypocrites who encouraged that attitude. It establishes the principle that wealth is a trust and must circulate for the good of the community. The hoarding of precious metals is specifically mentioned because they were the primary forms of saved wealth. This remains a timeless warning that amassing riches while neglecting the rights of Allah and the needy invites a dreadful punishment.

=35=

The torment described is terrifyingly graphic. On the Day of Resurrection, the very gold and silver they hoarded will be heated in Hellfire until it becomes blazing hot, and then it will be pressed against their foreheads, sides, and backs. The specific mention of these three body parts may correspond to the ways they turned away from obligation: the forehead representing arrogant refusal, the sides their turning away, and the back their retreat from spending. A hadith in Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim describes a similar punishment for those who withhold zakāh from camels and cattle, confirming that this is not metaphorical but real. The words spoken to them, “This is the treasure you hoarded for yourselves. Now taste what you hoarded!” are a direct echo of their own greed. The treasure they thought they were preserving for their benefit has become the instrument of their torture. This verse, along with the previous one, is a powerful motivation to give generously in Allah’s cause, especially when the community is in need, as during the Tabūk campaign. It shifts the perspective from short‑term financial loss to eternal consequence.

=36=

This verse anchors the Islamic calendar in the divine order. The twelve lunar months are decreed from the very creation of the universe, a celestial law that the pre‑Islamic Arabs knew but manipulated. Among these twelve, four are sacred: Rajab, Dhul‑Qaʿdah, Dhul‑Ḥijjah, and Muḥarram. During these months, fighting was forbidden, a tradition that Islam largely upheld except in cases of self‑defence. “That is the Right Way” refers to the correct religion and the adherence to the divinely fixed calendar. The command “do not wrong one another during these months” emphasises that the sanctity must be observed, and transgressions in them are even graver.

However, the verse immediately adds a practical reality: the polytheists, while sometimes respecting the sacred months, fought the Muslims collectively and often violated them. So the believers are instructed to “together fight the polytheists as they fight against you together,” meaning that in the face of a united enemy that disregards sacred limits, the Muslims may also engage in collective action even during these months if necessary. The verse closes by reminding that Allah’s aid is with those who maintain taqwā, ensuring that any military action remains bound by righteousness.

=37=

The practice of nasī’ was the pre‑Islamic manipulation of the calendar. The Arabs, addicted to raiding and warfare, found it inconvenient to observe a three‑month truce, so they would shift the sacredness from one month to another, postponing, for example, Muḥarram’s sanctity to Ṣafar to allow fighting. The verse calls this “an increase in disbelief” because it added the sin of altering divine law to the sin of idolatry. They were led far astray by it because they thought they were cleverly managing their affairs, but in reality they were compounding their rebellion.

The phrase “only to maintain the number of months sanctified by Allah” highlights their superficial adherence to the form while destroying the substance. They kept the count of four sacred months but violated the specific months that Allah had designated. “Their evil deeds have been made appealing to them” explains the psychology of sin: persistent transgression blinds a person until they see their evil as good. The verse declares that Allah does not guide such people, meaning they are left to their own delusion. This condemnation of calendar tampering served to re‑establish the pure lunar calendar for the Muslim community and underscored the principle that divine commands must be followed exactly as prescribed, not modified for convenience.

=38=

The address now returns directly to the hesitant believers during the call to Tabūk. The tone is one of gentle rebuke and awakening. “What is the matter with you” expresses disappointment at their sluggishness. To “march forth in the cause of Allah” meant joining the expedition against the Byzantine forces. Their response was to “cling firmly to the land,” meaning they were deeply attached to their homes, farms, and comforts. The rhetorical question “Do you prefer the life of this world over the Hereafter?” cuts to the heart of the issue. It presents a binary choice: temporary worldly ease or eternal reward.

The verse then provides the rational basis for the correct choice: “The enjoyment of this worldly life is insignificant compared to that of the Hereafter. ” Whatever they feared losing—shade, water, family, business—is utterly trivial when weighed against the everlasting bliss of Paradise. This comparison was meant to shift their perspective and loosen the grip of worldly attachments. It speaks powerfully to all believers who find it difficult to sacrifice for their faith, reminding them that every worldly comfort is fleeting, while what lies with Allah is immeasurable.

=39=

The warning intensifies dramatically. Refusal to march forth will bring a painful punishment, whether in this world through humiliation and defeat, or in the Hereafter through the Fire, or both. This punishment is a direct consequence of disobedience. More starkly, Allah threatens to replace them with other people—a community that will be more obedient, more willing to sacrifice. This replacement happened historically to some extent; the early followers who showed reluctance were later supplanted in leadership by those who were more steadfast, and the message spread through others.

The phrase “You are not harming Him in the least” is a profound statement of Allah’s self‑sufficiency. Human obedience or disobedience does not affect Him; it only affects the people themselves. This demolishes any arrogance or sense that Allah needs our worship. It is purely for our own benefit. The verse closes with “Allah is Most Capable of everything,” reminding them that the threat is not empty; He can easily bring about the change He warns of. This served to shake the believers out of their complacency and impel them towards action.

=40=

This verse delivers the ultimate rebuttal to any hesitation to support the Prophet. It begins by stating that the believers’ failure to help would not diminish him, because Allah has already proven He can support him alone. The reference is to the Hijrah, the most vulnerable moment of the Prophet’s mission. When the disbelievers drove him out of Mecca, he was only one of two—just him and Abū Bakr. In the cave of Thawr, with the pursuers outside, the Prophet reassured his companion with the immortal words, “Do not worry; Allah is certainly with us.

” This is a lesson in absolute trust. Allah responded by sending down His sakīnah upon the Prophet, a divine calm that stilled the heart. Unseen forces—angels—were sent, and the outcome was that the disbelievers’ word was made lowest, utterly defeated, while the Word of Allah, the kalimah of tawḥīd, remained supreme. The two names Almighty and All-Wise seal the narrative: Almighty because no power can oppose Him, and All-Wise because every event, including the ordeal in the cave, was part of a perfect plan. For the believers facing Tabūk, this was a reminder that the same God who protected His Messenger in that desperate situation would grant victory again, regardless of the enemy’s apparent strength. It also honours Abū Bakr’s companionship, which earned him the title “the second of two. ” The verse teaches that Allah’s support is not dependent on numbers; it is rooted in faith and reliance upon Him.
