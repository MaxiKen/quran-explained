=61=

This verse exposes another form of verbal attack that the hypocrites used against the Prophet, peace be upon him. The phrase “He listens to anyone” was a sarcastic remark. They meant that the Prophet was gullible, that he would believe whatever anyone told him without verification, and that his ear was open to every rumour and excuse. It was a way of belittling his judgment and suggesting that he could be easily manipulated. The divine response instructs the Prophet to clarify the truth. “He listens to what is best for you” is a profound correction. The Prophet did listen attentively, but only to that which is good, truthful, and beneficial for the community. His listening was not a weakness but a mercy. He heard people’s problems, their confessions, their excuses, and he responded with wisdom.

The verse then adds three qualities that define his character. First, “He believes in Allah,” meaning his trust and faith are in his Lord, not in gossip. Second, he “has faith in the believers,” meaning he trusts the sincere among them and treats them with goodwill. Third, “he is a mercy for those who believe among you.” This recalls the Qur’anic description of the Prophet as a mercy to the worlds. His compassionate listening, his patience with people’s stories, and his refusal to rush to judgment were all manifestations of that mercy.

The verse ends with a stern warning: those who hurt the Messenger with such words will face a painful punishment. The crime of hurting the Prophet is not merely a personal offence; it is an attack on the conduit of revelation and leadership.

This verse also teaches the believers the etiquette of speaking about the Prophet and the seriousness of even casual disparagement. It calls for deep respect and love for the one through whom they received guidance.

=62=

The hypocrites were masters of the strategic oath. They swore by Allah to the believers, attempting to convince them of their loyalty and innocence after they had made offensive remarks or stayed behind from battle. Their goal, as the verse states, was “to please you.” They sought social acceptance, the avoidance of conflict, and the material benefits of being seen as part of the community. The verse redirects their focus and exposes their misplaced priorities. The pleasure they should have been seeking was that of Allah and His Messenger. True believers orient their entire lives around earning divine and prophetic approval; human approval is secondary. The clause “if they are true believers” implies that their actions prove they are not genuine. A sincere heart cannot prefer the pleasure of people over the pleasure of Allah. This verse serves as a sharp self‑assessment tool for every Muslim. Are we performing religious acts, saying the right words, or making certain commitments primarily to please those around us—family, peers, society—or to please Allah? The hypocrites’ oaths were technically true in wording but false in intent, and the Qur’an exposes that hypocrisy cannot be papered over with solemn words. The lesson is that the direction of the heart in all actions must be towards Allah and His Messenger, not towards the shifting approval of human beings.

=63=

This verse presents a rhetorical question meant to shake the conscience of the hypocrites. Did they truly not understand the consequence of their opposition?

The phrase “whoever opposes Allah and His Messenger” encompasses all forms of hostility, whether it is verbal mockery, secret plotting, or open defiance. The punishment is eternal Hellfire. The Qur’an describes it as “the ultimate disgrace,” a comprehensive shame that dwarfs any worldly honour they might have sought through their scheming.

The word “disgrace” is particularly pointed because the hypocrites’ whole strategy was to avoid disgrace in this world by pretending to be believers. Yet they were earning the greatest possible disgrace in the Hereafter. This verse demonstrates that a short‑sighted focus on worldly reputation while neglecting eternal consequences is the height of foolishness. It also serves as a warning to all believers that opposition to divine authority, even in subtle forms, is a path to ruin. The verse encourages a healthy fear of Allah’s punishment, which is a necessary component of faith that keeps a person from moral compromise.

=64=

The hypocrites lived in constant dread of exposure. They knew that the Qur’an was being revealed gradually and that it often addressed the hidden secrets of people. They feared that a sūrah would come down and lay bare what was in their hearts—their mockery, their disbelief, their plots. This verse captures that anxiety and turns it into a challenge. The Prophet is told to say, “Keep mocking!

” This is not a permission but a threat. Continue with your scornful talk, because Allah will certainly expose it.

The phrase “bring to light what you fear” is a definite promise. Shortly after, verses were indeed revealed that unmasked their words and deeds, such as the incident where some of them joked, “We have not seen anyone like our reciters—more greedy, more lying, and more cowardly,” referring to the Prophet and the believers. The Qur’an recorded their words and answered them. So their worst fear came true. This teaches that no secret is hidden from Allah, and that the truth will eventually surface. It is also a comfort to the sincere believers who might be distressed by the whispered mockery of the insincere; they are assured that Allah is aware and will deal with it.

=65=

The verse now relates the actual incident. When confronted about their sarcastic remarks, the hypocrites immediately tried to downplay their words. They claimed it was just idle talk, mere joking to pass the time on the journey. They used this as a defence to avoid being held accountable. The response demolishes their excuse.

The question “Was it Allah, His revelations, and His Messenger that you ridiculed?” leaves no room for ambiguity. The subject of their jesting was not a mundane matter; it was the most sacred realities. Mocking Allah, the Qur’an, or the Prophet is not an act of casual levity; it is a sign of deeply rooted kufr.

The verse teaches that words are not weightless. Speech about religious matters carries immense responsibility. In a famous hadith, a man said something he thought insignificant, but it caused him to be thrown into Hell for seventy years. The hypocrites’ attempt to trivialise their blasphemy is a timeless tactic: when people are caught in offensive speech about the faith, they often say, “I was only joking,” or “You’re too sensitive. ” This verse sets the standard that certain subjects are never to be treated with mockery, and that doing so indicates a heart that has not truly internalised belief.

=66=

The divine verdict is delivered. “Make no excuses” means that their justifications are utterly rejected. The judgment has been passed: they have committed kufr after having outwardly professed īmān.

This is a declaration of apostasy. The phrase “lost faith after your belief” indicates that they had once entered the fold of Islam, at least outwardly, but their mockery and hypocrisy expelled them from it.

The verse then mentions a division among them: some might be pardoned—meaning those who sincerely repented and amended their ways—while others would be punished for their persistence in wrongdoing. This is a consistent Qur’anic pattern: the door of repentance remains open even for the gravest sins, but those who refuse it face certain punishment.

The verse also shows that collective guilt is not applied blindly; individuals are judged by their own actions. This was a warning to the hypocrites of Medina that their time for excuses was over, and they needed genuine repentance or they would face the consequences.

=67=

The Qur’an now paints a comprehensive portrait of the hypocritical character, applying to both men and women. They are all from the same cloth, sharing these traits. First, they encourage evil (munkar) and forbid good (maʿrūf).

This is the exact opposite of the believers’ command. Where the believers call to virtue, the hypocrites promote vice and discourage righteousness. Second, they withhold what is in their hands, meaning they are miserly with their wealth, refusing to spend in charity or for the common good. Their greed is a hallmark.

The verse then describes a terrifying spiritual law: “They neglected Allah, so He neglected them. ” Because they ignored Allah’s remembrance, commands, and rights, Allah left them to their own devices, withdrawing His guidance and care. This is a form of divine punishment that comes through abandonment.

The verse concludes that the hypocrites are indeed the fāsiqūn, those who have strayed far from obedience. This description serves as a mirror for believers to examine themselves: do we subtly encourage evil through our silence or actions? Do we close our hands when the community needs help? The neglect of Allah is the root cause, and it leads to a downward spiral.

=68=

The fate of the hypocrites is now stated without ambiguity. They are grouped together with the open disbelievers, receiving the same promise of eternal Hell.

The phrase “it is sufficient for them” indicates that Hell is ample as a punishment and as an abode; it requires no addition. “Allah has condemned them” means they are under divine curse, banished from mercy. The punishment is “never-ending,” underscoring the severity of their crime. Hypocrisy, which corrupts the community from within, is treated with the utmost seriousness because it destroys trust and faith in the hearts of the weak. For the believers, this verse is a warning and a reassurance: a warning not to let any trace of hypocrisy enter their hearts, and a reassurance that the hidden enemies who cause so much pain will not escape justice. The clarity of the threat is meant to bring the sincere back to Allah and to isolate the corrupt.

=69=

The hypocrites are now compared to earlier nations that were destroyed. The comparison highlights their false sense of security.

The previous nations were stronger, richer, and had more children, yet they were annihilated when they persisted in disbelief. “They enjoyed their share” means they took their portion of worldly pleasures, and “you have enjoyed your share” indicates the hypocrites are doing the same. Both also engaged in idle talk—vain conversation, mockery of the prophets, and immersion in trivialities. The outcome for those earlier peoples was that their deeds became void, worthless, and they were losers in both worlds. This serves as a direct warning: if the hypocrites follow the same path, they will meet the same end. History is full of lessons, but only those with insight learn.

The verse teaches that material strength is no protection against Allah’s punishment. It also warns against the danger of being absorbed in the fleeting pleasures of this world and in empty talk, which distracts from the remembrance of Allah and the serious business of the soul.

=70=

The rhetorical question is a call to reflect on well‑known historical examples. Each mentioned nation had a messenger who brought clear proofs—miracles and divine guidance. The people of Noah were drowned, ʿĀd were destroyed by a fierce wind, Thamūd by a thunderous blast, the people of Abraham defied him and were defeated, the people of Midian (Shuʿayb’s people) suffered an earthquake, and the overturned cities of Lot were rained upon with stones and flipped. The verse emphasises that their destruction was not an act of divine injustice. “Allah would have never wronged them.” Rather, they wronged themselves by rejecting the truth and committing evils. This establishes that divine punishment is always a consequence of human choices. The hypocrites of Medina had heard these stories recited in the Qur’an, so they had no excuse. The verse calls every generation to study the past, not as mere tales but as warnings. The pattern is clear: when people mock the messengers and persist in rebellion, they seal their own fate. No one is too powerful to escape.

=71=

After the dark portrait of the hypocrites, the sūrah turns back to the beauty of the true believing community. The relationship among believers is described with the word awliyā’, translated as “guardians” or “allies.” This means they support, protect, and care for one another like a single body. This mutual guardianship is not based on blood or tribe but on faith.

The verse then lists their qualities, directly contrasting them with the hypocrites. The hypocrites encouraged evil and forbade good; the believers encourage good and forbid evil. The hypocrites were miserly; the believers establish prayer and pay zakāh. The hypocrites neglected Allah; the believers obey Allah and His Messenger. This parallel structure makes the distinction crystal clear. The promise for such believers is that they will be enveloped in Allah’s mercy. “It is they who will be shown Allah’s mercy” indicates a special, continuous manifestation of divine compassion. The verse closes by reminding that Allah is Almighty, so He can reward them, and All-Wise, so His plan for the community is perfect. This verse lays the foundation for a caring, morally active society where faith translates into positive social action.

=72=

The reward for the believing men and women is now detailed in a manner that fills the heart with hope. The first promise is of Gardens with flowing rivers, symbolising unending delight and abundance. They will dwell there eternally, never to be expelled. Within those Gardens, they are promised “splendid homes,” pure and beautiful dwellings in the Gardens of Eternity, a place of settled peace. But then the verse elevates the reward to its peak: “and—above all—the pleasure of Allah.

” The Arabic structure uses the word “wa riḍwānun mina Allāhi akbar” meaning the pleasure of Allah is greater than all that. This is the supreme gift, for the soul of the believer longs not just for comfort but for the knowledge that its Lord is pleased with it.

The verse concludes, “That is truly the ultimate triumph. ” All worldly triumphs—victory in battle, accumulation of wealth, fame—are shadows compared to this. For the Companions who had sacrificed everything for the cause, this verse was a balm and a motivation. It also makes clear that women are full partners in this spiritual journey and its reward, explicitly mentioned alongside men.

=73=

The command to the Prophet now combines the two fronts of opposition. Jihad against the disbelievers is a clear command, but the verse also commands struggle against the hypocrites.

However, the form of this struggle differed. Against the disbelievers, it could be military. Against the hypocrites, it was through argument, exposure, legal measures, and a firm, uncompromising stance. The Prophet was never commanded to execute hypocrites for their hidden disbelief as long as they maintained outward peace; instead, he struggled against them by ignoring their excuses, withholding positions of trust, and not praying for their dead. “Be firm with them” means no leniency, no accepting their false justifications.

The mention of Hell as their home reinforces the gravity of their state. This verse established a dual policy that protected the Muslim community from both external military threats and internal subversion.

=74=

This verse refers to a specific incident. During the return from Tabūk, a group of hypocrites plotted to assassinate the Prophet by pushing him off a mountain pass. They also made blasphemous remarks. When confronted, they swore by Allah they had said nothing. But Allah reveals the truth: they uttered a blasphemy, committed apostasy, and attempted a plot they failed to execute.

The verse then expresses astonishment at their ingratitude: instead of being grateful for the wealth and prosperity that came through Islam, they repay Allah and His Messenger with resentment and treachery. “It is only through resentment that they pay Allah and His Messenger back for enriching them out of His bounty!

” This is an indictment of the ugliest form of ingratitude, where blessing is answered with rebellion. The verse, however, leaves the door open: “If they repent, it will be better for them.” But if they persist, a painful torment awaits in this world—through exposure and defeat—and in the Hereafter, with no protector. This shows that even the gravest crimes can be forgiven with sincere repentance, but that stubborn refusal leads to complete ruin.

=75=

A new category of hypocrites is introduced. These were people who, in times of poverty, made solemn pledges to Allah. They promised that if Allah granted them wealth, they would become generous givers of charity and would join the ranks of the righteous. This vow was made in a moment of desire and perhaps sincerity.

However, vows made to Allah are binding, and the heart’s true state is tested when the wealth actually arrives. This verse sets up the story that follows, teaching that a promise made in need can be easily forgotten in prosperity. It serves as a warning about the danger of making commitments to Allah that one is not fully prepared to keep.

The verse also teaches that charity and righteousness are the expected responses to divine generosity, not hoarding and forgetfulness.

=76=

The test came and they failed. Allah granted them from His bounty; they became wealthy. Instead of fulfilling their vow, they withheld their wealth, refusing to give the charity they had promised. Worse, they “turned away indifferently,” meaning they abandoned the whole attitude of devotion and obedience that had led them to make the vow. Their hearts, now attached to their new riches, grew cold.

This is a classic tale of how wealth can corrupt. The poverty that once made them turn to Allah was replaced by a self‑sufficient arrogance. This verse exposes the hypocrisy of conditional religiosity: a person claims they will be pious if only given certain means, but when the means arrive, they reveal their true attachment to dunyā. For the sincere believer, it is a call to fulfil one’s pledges and to recognise wealth as a test, not a reward in itself.

=77=

As a direct consequence of their broken vow and false promises, Allah punished them with a spiritual disease: hypocrisy was made to settle deeply in their hearts. It became a permanent stain, a persistent sickness that would remain with them until death, when they would meet their Lord and face the full reckoning. The verse explicitly states the cause: “for breaking their promise to Allah and for their lies.” This reveals a crucial psychological and spiritual law. Each unfulfilled vow, each lie told in the name of faith, hardens the heart and makes insincerity easier. Over time, the heart becomes so accustomed to falsehood that it can no longer distinguish truth from falsehood. The punishment is built into the sin.

This is a terrifying warning to take one’s words, especially solemn oaths and promises to Allah, with the utmost seriousness. It also teaches that sincere repentance is urgent; delay allows the disease to become chronic.

=78=

The rhetorical question shatters any illusion of privacy. The hypocrites whispered their plots, made their secret vows, and concealed their mockery, thinking no one knew.

The verse reminds them—and all humanity—that Allah knows their secret thoughts (sirr) and their most private conversations (najwā). He is the Knower of the unseen (ʿallām al‑ghuyūb).

This is a fundamental attribute of Allah, and reflecting on it is meant to instil both fear and comfort: fear for those who hide evil, and comfort for the oppressed that their sighs and secret tears are fully known. For the believer, the awareness that Allah knows every hidden thought is the strongest motivation for sincerity. It is impossible to be a hypocrite if one truly internalises that Allah’s knowledge penetrates every layer of concealment.

=79=

This verse recounts another ugly incident. The Prophet called for charity for the Tabūk expedition. The wealthy Companions like ʿUthmān and ʿAbd al‑Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf gave enormous sums. The hypocrites accused them of showing off, saying they only gave to be seen. Meanwhile, poorer believers gave whatever small amount they could, such as a handful of dates or half a measure of grain. The hypocrites mocked them, saying, “Allah has no need of this paltry donation.” So the hypocrites slandered both the generous rich and the struggling poor. The response is severe: their mockery will be turned back on them. On the Day of Judgment, they will be the ones ridiculed and disgraced. The painful punishment awaits them. This verse protects the dignity of sincere donors, whether they give much or little. It condemns the cynical attitude that belittles people’s sacrifices. It also teaches that the value of charity is in its sincerity, not its amount, and that mocking a believer’s good deed is a very serious sin.

=80=

This verse was revealed concerning the leader of the hypocrites, ʿAbdullāh ibn Ubayy. When he was dying, his son, a sincere Muslim, asked the Prophet to pray for his forgiveness and to give his shirt for the burial shroud. The Prophet, out of his immense mercy, was inclined to do so. But this verse decisively forbade it. The number “seventy” is not a literal limit but an Arabic expression indicating a very large number; it means no amount of intercession will avail for them. The reason is that “they have lost faith in Allah and His Messenger.” Their persistent disbelief and hypocrisy have barred them from forgiveness. The verse closes by stating that Allah does not guide the rebellious people, those who have chosen to exit the bounds of faith. This was a crucial lesson for the Prophet and the community: divine mercy is immense, but it is not for those who die in a state of kufr and rebellion after the truth has been made clear. It also established the ruling that funeral prayers are not to be performed for known hypocrites. The verse highlights the balance between the Prophet’s compassionate nature and the divine limits that must be respected.
