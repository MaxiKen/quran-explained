=41=

The series of rhetorical challenges continues, now confronting yet another possible source of the deniers' arrogance. The verse asks whether they have access to the unseen record, the divine tablet upon which all decrees and knowledge are inscribed, such that they can simply copy from it and present it to the people.

The Arabic word "ghayb" refers to the unseen, all that is hidden from human perception, including the future, the decrees of destiny, and the true nature of reality. The deniers acted as if they had insider knowledge, as if they could confidently declare what would and would not happen, who was rightly guided and who was astray, and that there would be no resurrection or judgment. This verse exposes the absurdity of their confidence. They have no ladder to the heavens, as the previous verse established, and they have no access to the book of the unseen. Their claims are baseless speculations, not informed certainties. If they truly had access to the unseen record, they would be able to copy it out, to produce a document that details the hidden truths they claim to know. Their inability to do so is glaring. This argument is deeply relevant to the modern age, where many claim that science or reason gives them the right to speak authoritatively about what exists beyond the material world, denying God, the soul, and the Hereafter.

Yet science, by its very method, is limited to the observable and measurable. It has no access to the unseen. The deniers of every era act as if they have a secret book that disproves the prophets, but they have no such book. The verse invites the honest person to recognise the limits of human knowledge and to turn to the only reliable source of information about the unseen: divine revelation. The Quran is the true record from the unseen, revealed by the One who knows the unseen. The deniers reject it while pretending to possess knowledge they do not have.

The question is a powerful reminder that humility before the unknown is the beginning of wisdom. To reject the prophet’s news of the unseen while having no alternative source is not rationality; it is arrogance.

=42=

The verse now addresses a darker reality: the Quraysh were not merely arguing; they were actively plotting against the Prophet. They held secret meetings in Dar al-Nadwa, their council chamber, to discuss how to neutralise him. They considered imprisoning him, exiling him, or assassinating him. This verse refers to those schemes and delivers a thunderous response. If they are plotting, then let them know that their plot will backfire. The disbelievers themselves will be the ones ensnared and destroyed by their own scheming.

The Arabic word "makr" means plotting, scheming, devising a plan in secret to harm someone. Allah is informing the Prophet that He is aware of their plots and that He is the ultimate planner. The very schemes they are weaving to extinguish the light of Islam will become the means of their own humiliation and defeat.

This is a consistent pattern in the Quran. The people of Noah schemed, and they were drowned. The brothers of Joseph schemed, and they ended up bowing before him. Pharaoh schemed against Moses, and he was drowned while Moses was saved. The Quraysh schemed to kill the Prophet, and their scheme directly led to the Hijra, which became the foundation of the first Islamic state and the eventual triumphant return to Mecca. The verse gave the Prophet and the believers immense comfort. They were living under the shadow of these plots, feeling vulnerable and outnumbered. This verse assured them that the plotters were not operating in secret as far as Allah was concerned. He was watching, and He was counter-plotting with a plan far superior to theirs. The principle taught here is timeless. When the people of truth face the conspiracies of the powerful, they should not despair. The history of the prophets shows that divine counter-scheming always prevails.

The verse also serves as a severe warning to anyone who plots against the righteous and the messengers. Their own trap will close upon them. The best course is to abandon scheming and submit to the truth, for no scheme can succeed against the One who knows all secrets and controls all outcomes.

=43=

This verse brings the series of rhetorical questions to its climax. After dismantling all their flimsy excuses and exposing their intellectual and moral bankruptcy, the Quran asks the ultimate question: do they have a god other than Allah? Is there any deity, any power, any entity that can rival or replace Allah in His lordship, His provision, His knowledge, and His judgment?

The question is left unanswered because the answer is self-evident. There is no god but Allah. Every argument the deniers have offered has been refuted. Every source of their arrogance has been stripped away. They are left standing before the undeniable truth of divine oneness.

The verse then declares the transcendence of Allah: "Glorified is Allah far above what they associate with Him." The Arabic "subhana" is an exclamation of glorification, declaring Allah's absolute perfection and His complete freedom from any partner, equal, or rival. Whatever false gods, idols, or intermediaries they associate with Him are utterly beneath His majesty. This glorification is a natural response to the realisation of tawhid. When the mind grasps that Allah is the sole Creator, the sole Sustainer, the sole Owner of the unseen, and the sole true God, the heart bursts into praise and exaltation. This verse is the conclusion of a long argument that began with the oaths at the opening of the surah. All the oaths, all the descriptions of punishment and reward, all the challenges, have been leading to this point: the affirmation that there is no god but Allah, and that He is far above the shirk that the deniers practice.

For the believer, this verse is a renewal of faith. It reminds them that their God is the true God, exalted above all falsehood, and that worshipping Him alone is the most rational, the most dignified, and the most liberating act a human can perform.

The verse also serves as a transition, closing the argumentation and preparing the Prophet for the concluding instructions that will follow in the remaining verses of the surah.

=44=

This verse delivers a penetrating insight into the psychology of hardened deniers. Even if a piece of the sky were to fall upon them as a deadly punishment, they would still rationalise it away rather than accept the truth and repent.

The Arabic word "kisf" refers to a fragment or a piece, something broken off. The image is terrifying: a segment of the celestial canopy detaching and plummeting toward them, an undeniable sign of divine wrath.

Yet, instead of acknowledging it as a sign and turning to Allah, they would dismiss it as merely a pile of clouds. They would find a naturalistic explanation to soothe their consciences and avoid the implications.

This is a profound commentary on the human capacity for self-deception. When the heart is determined to reject the truth, no amount of evidence will sway it. The same phenomenon can be observed today. People witness the signs of God in creation, in the precise laws of physics, in the miracle of life, and they attribute it all to chance and natural processes. They see the fulfilment of Quranic prophecies, the transformation of lives by faith, and the consistency of the divine message, and they find ways to dismiss it all. The verse is not saying that a piece of sky will actually fall on them as a test; it is using a hyperbolic scenario to illustrate their spiritual obstinacy. Even the most dramatic and undeniable sign would be reinterpreted through the lens of their denial. This does not mean that all disbelievers are immune to evidence; many people have come to faith through the signs in the Quran and in the universe. But there is a type of denier whose arrogance and love of falsehood have sealed their perception so tightly that no key will open it. The verse warns against becoming that person. It also comforts the Prophet by explaining that the rejection he faces is not due to a deficiency in the message; it is due to a disease in the hearts of the recipients. He is not to blame for their inability to see a falling sky as anything but clouds.

=45=

After exposing the depth of the deniers' obstinacy, the verse issues a clear instruction: leave them. This is a command to the Prophet to cease exhausting himself over those who have made it clear that they will not believe, no matter what signs they see. The verb "dharhum" means to leave them, to abandon them to their own devices, to stop worrying about them and stop engaging in endless arguments that bear no fruit. It is a release and a relief for the Prophet's heart. He had been tirelessly striving for their guidance, and their rejection weighed heavily on him. Now he is told to step back and entrust their affair to Allah. This leaving is not a cessation of the general call to Islam, which continues for others, but a specific disengagement from those who have proven themselves to be unreachable. The reason for leaving them is that they will inevitably face their Day. The Day is the Day of Judgment, but also any day on which the divine punishment descends upon them, whether in this world or the next. On that Day, they will be struck dead, overtaken by a punishment that renders them utterly helpless.

The Arabic "yus'aqun" means to be thunderstruck, to be struck down as if by a lightning bolt, a sudden and complete incapacitation. This is the same fate that befell the people of Thamud and other destroyed nations. The verse balances mercy and justice. The Prophet is shown mercy by being relieved of the burden of the obstinate. The deniers are warned that the matter is now between them and their Lord, and the meeting will be terrifying.

For the believer, this verse teaches a valuable lesson about boundaries in da'wah. One should convey the message with wisdom and patience, but one is not responsible for forcing acceptance. When people consistently mock, reject, and rationalise away every sign, it is permissible, and sometimes necessary, to withdraw and focus energy on those who are receptive.

=46=

This verse continues the description of the Day that the deniers will face. On that Day, all the plotting and planning they engaged in during their worldly life to oppose the truth and harm the Prophet will prove utterly useless. The schemes that they thought were so clever, the alliances they forged, the propaganda they spread, the conspiracies they hatched, all of it will evaporate into nothingness.

The Arabic "kayd" is similar to "makr," referring to cunning plans and strategies. In the world, these schemes seemed formidable and often caused real harm to the believers. But on the Day of Judgment, when the true balance of power is revealed, the schemes will be exposed as pathetic and futile. Not a single one of their plots will avert the punishment or lessen its severity.

The verse then adds that they will not be helped. No ally will come to their rescue. The idols they worshipped will disown them. The jinn they sought help from will abandon them. The powerful leaders they followed will be in the same predicament, unable to help themselves, let alone anyone else. This complete abandonment is the ultimate humiliation for those who lived their lives in arrogance, believing they were self-sufficient and well-protected. The verse echoes a theme that appeared in Surah Adh-Dhariyat regarding the people of Thamud who were not able to rise up nor were they helped. The message is consistent: worldly power and alliances are a mirage. The only real help is the help of Allah, and that help is exclusively for the believers. For the believers who were suffering from the schemes of the Quraysh, this verse was a promise that their oppressors' plots had an expiration date. All their scheming would end in total failure on a day that would make all worldly victories and defeats irrelevant. The verse encourages the believer to place their trust in Allah's justice rather than obsessing over the temporary successes of the unjust.

=47=

This verse reveals that the punishment of the deniers is not postponed entirely to the Day of Judgment. They will face another torment before that great Day.

The phrase "doona dhalik" indicates something besides, in addition to, or prior to the Day of Judgment. The scholars have interpreted this "torment before that" in several ways. It could refer to the worldly punishments that befell the Quraysh, such as the severe famine that struck Mecca, the defeat at the Battle of Badr where many of their leaders were killed, and other calamities that served as a prelude to their ultimate fate in the Hereafter. It could also refer to the punishment of the grave, the barzakh, which awaits every disbeliever and sinful person from the moment of death until the resurrection. The punishment of the grave is a well-established doctrine in Islam, supported by numerous hadiths. The grave is either a garden of Paradise or a pit of Hell, and the disbeliever experiences torment there while awaiting the final judgment. Another opinion includes the general suffering, anxiety, and inner torment that the deniers experience in this life as a result of their disbelief, even if they outwardly appear prosperous. The verse states that most of them do not know about this prior torment. They are heedless, unaware that the calamities they face and the darkness of the grave are just the beginning of a long chain of consequences. They think that death is the end, or that the delay in punishment means it will never come. But Allah's justice operates in stages, and the full weight of the punishment is being accumulated. This verse serves as a sobering reminder that the consequences of disbelief are not always deferred to a distant future. There is a present and intermediate punishment that is just as real, and the ignorance of it does not make it any less certain.

For the believer, this knowledge increases the urgency of repentance and the fear of dying in a state of unbelief.

=48=

The surah now delivers its concluding instructions to the Prophet, and through him to every believer. The first command is to be patient with the decree of the Lord.

The Arabic word "sabr" is profound, meaning patience, steadfastness, perseverance, and the restraint of the soul from panic and complaint. The Prophet had endured years of mockery, rejection, persecution, and the pain of watching his people turn away from the truth. He had been called a madman, a poet, a fortune-teller, and a fabricator. He had faced plots against his life. The decree of the Lord included the delay of victory, the continued existence of the deniers, and the testing of the believers. He is told to bear all of this with patience, accepting that his Lord's wisdom is perfect and that the timing of events is in the best hands.

The second part of the verse provides the deepest comfort and motivation for this patience: "for you are truly under Our watchful Eyes." The Arabic "bi a'yunina" means within Our eyes, under Our direct gaze and protection.

This is an expression of intense divine care and attention. The Prophet is not alone. He is not forgotten. He is not left to fend for himself against a hostile world. He is under the very eyes of Allah, which means he is enveloped in protection, guidance, and love. Every insult thrown at him is seen. Every tear he sheds is counted. Every effort he makes is appreciated.

This is an incredibly intimate and reassuring image. The believer who internalises this truth can withstand any hardship, knowing that the Lord of the universe is watching over them with the eyes of mercy and care.

The verse then adds a practical instruction that sustains patience: glorify the praises of your Lord when you rise. This refers to the morning prayer and the remembrances said upon waking from sleep.

The first act of the day should be the praise and glorification of Allah. Beginning the day with the remembrance of Allah fortifies the soul for whatever trials the day may bring. The connection between patience and glorification is essential. The heart that is full of Allah's praise finds it easier to be patient with His decree.

=49=

The final verse of the surah completes the instructions for worship and remembrance. After commanding glorification when rising, it now extends the command to the night and the pre-dawn hours. The Prophet is told to glorify Allah during a part of the night, which refers to the night prayer, the tahajjud, or simply the evening remembrances and the Isha prayer. The night is a time of stillness and solitude, when the world sleeps and the distractions of the day fade away. Worship in the night is a special mark of the righteous, as described in the previous surah, Adh-Dhariyat, where the believers slept only little and sought forgiveness before dawn. Here, the same pattern is encouraged.

The second time mentioned is "at the fading of the stars," which refers to the time just before dawn when the stars begin to disappear from the sky. This is the time of the fajr prayer and the pre-dawn supplications, a time when angels witness the worship and when divine mercy descends.

The mention of the fading stars is also poetically significant. The surah opened with cosmic oaths: Mount Tur, the written book, the visited house, the raised canopy, and the blazing seas. Now it closes with the image of stars fading at dawn. The entire cosmos is framed as a context for worship. The day begins with glorification, the night contains glorification, and the day ends as the stars fade with glorification. The life of the believer is thus a continuous cycle of remembrance, punctuated by the celestial signs that mark the passage of time. This final verse leaves the listener with a sense of tranquillity and purpose. The storms of opposition, the plots of the deniers, and the anxieties of the world are all stilled by the rhythm of devotion that surrounds the believer from the moment they rise to the moment the stars retreat before the dawn. The surah, having traversed the terrifying scenes of judgment, the bliss of Paradise, and the refutation of the deniers, now ends on a note of intimate, sustained worship, anchoring the soul in the One who alone deserves all praise.
