=21=

After describing the physical and social delights of Paradise, the Quran now addresses a deep emotional concern that touches every believing parent: what will happen to their children and descendants in the Hereafter? This verse brings extraordinary comfort and hope. It declares that those who believe and whose descendants follow them in faith will be joined together in Paradise, and their descendants will be elevated to the rank of their parents even if the parents attained a higher spiritual station through a lifetime of righteous deeds.

The Arabic phrasing indicates that Allah will cause the offspring to catch up, to be attached, to be reunited. This is an act of pure divine generosity. A parent who reaches the highest levels of Paradise will not have to suffer the grief of seeing their beloved child in a lower station. Instead, Allah will elevate the child to the parent’s rank so that the family can enjoy eternal bliss together, and the joy of the parent is thereby completed. Crucially, the verse immediately adds a clarification that safeguards the principle of perfect justice: "never discounting anything of the reward of their deeds. " The parent's reward is not diminished to make room for the child. The elevation of the child is an additional gift, not a transfer from the parent's account.

The second sentence reinforces this principle: "Every person will reap only what they sowed." No soul will be held accountable for another's actions, and no soul will be deprived of what it earned. This combination of mercy and justice is a hallmark of the Quran's portrayal of the Hereafter. The verse is a direct response to the anxiety of the early Muslims who worried about the spiritual fate of their children and parents. It reassures them that the bonds of faith can transcend the apparent separation of death and that family ties are honoured in the Hereafter in a way that exceeds human expectation. For the contemporary believer, this verse encourages the raising of children upon faith, with the hope that even if the children's own deeds are less than the parents', Allah’s mercy may reunite them all in the higher ranks.

=22=

Returning to the physical delights of Paradise, this verse emphasises the abundance and personalisation of the heavenly provision. The believers will be provided with whatever fruit or meat they desire.

The word "continually" captures the unending, uninterrupted nature of this provision. In the world, even the richest person must sometimes wait for a meal, or find that a desired food is out of season or unavailable. In Paradise, desire and fulfilment occur simultaneously. The moment a believer's heart inclines toward a particular fruit or a particular cut of meat, it is presented to them, fresh and perfect. The specific mention of fruit and meat together covers the two broad categories of food that were most prized in the ancient world and remain universally beloved today. Fruit represents the sweet, refreshing, and natural delights, while meat represents the rich, satisfying, and substantial fare. By mentioning both, the verse indicates that Paradise offers a complete culinary experience, satisfying every palate and every craving. The foods of Paradise are not like the foods of this world. They do not cause fatigue, illness, or satiety in a negative sense. They are consumed purely for pleasure, and their taste far surpasses anything known on earth. The Prophet described the fruits of Paradise in hadith, saying that whenever the inhabitants eat of its fruit, they will find a renewed and even more delightful flavour. This constant provision of desired food is a physical manifestation of Allah’s love and care for His servants. In the world, food is a necessity tied to labour, and the poor often go without. In Paradise, food is a pure gift, a symbol of divine hospitality, provided without any effort or cost. The verse inspires the believer to work for this reward and to remember that the fleeting pleasures of the world’s table are a mere shadow of the banquet that awaits the righteous.

=23=

After describing the solid foods of Paradise, the verse moves to the beverages that the believers will enjoy. They will pass around among themselves a drink, and this mutual passing indicates a culture of intimate friendship, sharing, and joyful social interaction. The drink is described as wine, but it is a wine utterly unlike the intoxicating beverages of this world.

The verse clarifies that this drink leads to no idle talk or sinfulness. In the world, alcohol is a major cause of social harm. It leads to drunkenness, loss of reason, foul speech, arguments, violence, and the commission of sins that a sober person would avoid. For this reason, it is forbidden in Islam. The wine of Paradise, however, is pure. It does not cloud the mind, it does not loosen the tongue in a harmful way, and it does not cause headaches or nausea. It delivers a state of euphoria and delight without any negative consequence whatsoever. The believers will drink it and feel only increased joy, clarity, and love for one another. Their conversation will remain elevated, free from the frivolous and sinful chatter that the deniers used to engage in, as described earlier in the surah. The passing around of the cup also symbolises the egalitarian nature of Paradise. In the courts of earthly kings, there are servants who serve and elites who drink. In Paradise, the believers serve one another out of love, or they are served by the youthful attendants mentioned in the next verse, but the social atmosphere is one of mutual affection and brotherhood.

For the believer in this world, this verse serves as a reminder of the wisdom behind the prohibition of alcohol. The temporary pleasure of worldly drink is not worth the spiritual and social damage it causes. The real, perfect, and eternal drink awaits the patient in the Gardens of bliss.

=24=

The picture of the believers' honour and comfort in Paradise is further enhanced by the mention of the servants who will attend to them. These servants are described as youthful, meaning they will always be in a state of freshness, vigour, and beauty. They will never age, tire, or become ill. They will circulate among the believers, offering food, drink, and whatever else is desired, with perfect grace and attentiveness. The comparison to "spotless pearls" is a powerful image of purity, beauty, and preciousness. A pearl that is spotless is rare and highly valued. The servants are not ordinary attendants; they are like hidden gems, beautiful to behold and a delight to have around. Their presence signifies that the believers are kings and queens in Paradise, attended by a retinue that befits their honoured status. There is a well-known hadith that describes these servants: when the believer in Paradise wishes for something, the servant will come to them instantly, without needing to be summoned, carrying exactly what was desired. This instantaneous, intuitive service is part of the perfection of Paradise. Some scholars have also mentioned that these youthful servants are the children of the believers who died before reaching the age of accountability, or they are a special creation of Allah for this purpose. Regardless of their origin, their role adds to the atmosphere of ease, dignity, and delight.

The verse also carries a subtle lesson about the nature of true honour. In the world, being served is often a sign of arrogance or oppression. In Paradise, it is a pure gift from Allah, untainted by any sense of exploitation, because those who serve do so willingly and joyfully as part of the perfect divine order. The believer humbles themselves in worship in this world, and in the next, they are elevated to a station where even the service of angels and pure youths is a part of their reward.

=25=

The scene shifts from the external pleasures of Paradise to the intimate social interactions of its inhabitants. They will turn to one another, facing each other with full attention and genuine interest.

The word "inquisitively" translates the Arabic "yatasa'alun," which implies mutual questioning, friendly inquiry, and the sharing of stories. This is not an interrogation but a warm and loving conversation among people who have shared the journey of faith and have now arrived at their eternal home. They will ask one another about their experiences in the world, about the trials they faced, about the moments of fear and hope, and about how they came to be in this place of unimaginable bliss. This turning to one another also reflects the arrangement described earlier, where the thrones are lined up facing each other. The architecture of Paradise facilitates community, not isolation. The believers do not sit alone, lost in their own pleasures. They form a society of love, where the joy is multiplied by sharing it with others. The conversation that follows in the next verses reveals the content of their talk. They will remember the days when they were among their people, surrounded by disbelief and mockery, feeling the fear of Allah in their hearts. Now, they will look back at those times with a sense of profound relief and gratitude. The inquisitive turning is therefore a turning toward shared memory, toward the recognition of the mercy that saved them and brought them together.

For the believer in this world, this verse offers a beautiful vision of the social life of Paradise. It is a place where friendships are perfected, where there is no backbiting, no envy, no misunderstanding, and no loneliness. The conversations are pure, meaningful, and filled with the remembrance of Allah. This should inspire the believer to cultivate relationships in this world that are based on faith and sincere love, so that they might be among those who turn to one another in Paradise with smiles and tears of joy.

=26=

The conversation in Paradise continues with the believers recounting their past life on earth. They will say that before this reward, meaning in the worldly life, they used to be in a state of awe, fear, and loving reverence of Allah while living in the midst of their families and communities.

The Arabic word "mushfiqin" conveys a tender, caring fear, the kind of anxiety a loving parent feels for a child. It is not a terror that paralyses but a deep concern that leads to caution and righteousness. The significant detail here is that they maintained this awe even while surrounded by their people, many of whom were likely heedless, sinful, or outright hostile to faith. It is one thing to be conscious of Allah in a mosque or in a gathering of the righteous. It is quite another to maintain that consciousness in a society that mocks piety and pressures one toward conformity with falsehood. These believers are recounting how they held onto their fear of Allah despite the environment.

This is a deeply comforting message for the early Muslims of Mecca, who were a tiny minority surrounded by the Quraysh polytheists. They felt the pressure of their people every day. This verse tells them that the very struggle they are undergoing will become a sweet memory in Paradise, a badge of honour that they will recount to their fellow believers with a sense of accomplishment and gratitude.

The phrase "in the midst of our people" also indicates that their faith was not an isolated, monastic withdrawal. They lived among their people, interacted with them, and faced their trials, yet they preserved the core of their faith.

This is the ideal of Islamic spirituality: to be in the world but not of it, to maintain the fear of Allah while fulfilling social responsibilities and enduring social pressures. The verse encourages the believers to remain steadfast, knowing that their private and public struggle to maintain taqwa is seen by Allah and will one day be a source of pride rather than shame.

=27=

The believers continue their conversation by stating the outcome of their earthly struggle with profound gratitude. "So Allah has graced us," they say, attributing their salvation not to their own deeds alone but to the grace and favour of Allah. This is a central theological point. The Prophet Muhammad taught that no one enters Paradise solely by their deeds. It is the mercy and grace of Allah that ultimately admits a servant into the Gardens. The believers in Paradise will fully recognise this truth. Their fear of Allah in the world did not create a sense of entitlement; it created a deeper appreciation that whatever good they did was by Allah's help, and whatever reward they receive is by His generosity. The grace they acknowledge is comprehensive. It includes the faith itself that Allah placed in their hearts, the ability to perform righteous deeds, the forgiveness of their sins, and the multiplication of their rewards. Then they mention the specific aspect of this grace that is most starkly appreciated from their vantage point in Paradise: Allah protected them from the torment of Hell's scorching heat.

The word "samum" refers to a searing, penetrating heat, a wind of fire that pierces the body. It is the same word used to describe the scorching wind in other Quranic passages. The believers are now safe in the Gardens of cool shade and flowing springs, and they look back at the Fire they have been saved from, and their gratitude deepens immeasurably. The juxtaposition of the two fates is the ultimate contrast, and the believers, fully aware of the reality of both destinations, offer this testimony of grace to one another. For the living, this verse serves as a powerful motivation. The only way to join that conversation and to utter those words of gratitude is to live now with the fear of Allah that leads to His grace and protection.

=28=

The believers complete their testimony by identifying the specific action that, by Allah's grace, led to their salvation. They say, "Indeed, we used to call upon Him alone before.

" The word "before" refers to the worldly life. The core of their faith was the exclusive devotion of their supplication and worship to Allah alone. They did not call upon idols, saints, angels, or any other intermediary. They directed their prayers, their hopes, and their pleas for help directly to the One Creator.

This is the essence of tawhid, the pure monotheism that all prophets taught. In a society filled with polytheism, maintaining this exclusive calling upon Allah was a revolutionary act and a daily act of defiance against the dominant culture. The believers in Paradise are tracing their salvation back to this single, defining characteristic: they worshipped and invoked none but Allah. Then they conclude with a statement of praise and recognition: "He is truly the Most Kind, Most Merciful.

" The Arabic names Al-Barr and Ar-Rahim are used here. Al-Barr is the One who is abounding in kindness and goodness, who is gentle and generous toward His servants, who fulfills His promises and rewards with unimaginable bounty. Ar-Rahim is the One whose mercy is vast, perpetual, and specially extended to the believers. In Paradise, the believers will see these names of Allah manifested in every aspect of their existence. The grace, the protection, the reunion with family, the eternal delights, all of it is a testament to Allah being Al-Barr and Ar-Rahim. Their statement is not a mere recitation of doctrine; it is a heart's overflowing acknowledgment of a reality they are immersed in.

This verse teaches the believer that the foundation of all hope is the sincere calling upon Allah alone, and that the reward for such devotion is a direct experience of His kindness and mercy that will be celebrated in the most beautiful conversations of Paradise.

=29=

After the lengthy and beautiful description of Paradise and its inhabitants' conversations, the surah returns to the present reality and the task of the Prophet. The verse begins with the imperative: "So continue to remind.

" The word "dhakkir" means to remind, to admonish, to cause to remember. The Prophet is not to be deterred by the accusations and mockery of the deniers. The truth he has just proclaimed about the punishment of the deniers and the bliss of the righteous must be repeated, reinforced, and brought to the hearts of the people. The reminder is a continuous, ongoing duty.

The verse then addresses the slander that the Prophet was facing. The Quraysh were calling him a fortune-teller or a madman, just as earlier nations had called their messengers. Allah directly refutes these accusations, stating that the Prophet, by the grace of his Lord, is not a soothsayer or a madman.

The mention of "by the grace of your Lord" is significant. The Prophet's character, his truthfulness, his sound mind, and the miraculous Quran he brought are all a grace from Allah. A fortune-teller is someone who claims to know the unseen through jinn and who speaks in rhymed, cryptic utterances for money. A madman is someone whose speech is irrational and confused. The Quran is neither cryptic nor irrational. It is a clear, coherent, and powerful message that calls to pure monotheism, moral excellence, and accountability before God. The grace of the Lord is manifest in the Prophet's sanity, his wisdom, and the content of the revelation itself. This verse comforts the Prophet and simultaneously challenges the deniers to look honestly at the man they are slandering. He lived among them for forty years before prophethood, known as Al-Amin, the trustworthy. His message is the opposite of the chaos of fortune-telling and madness. The command to continue reminding despite the slander is a model for all believers who call others to the truth. Opposition and name-calling should not silence the da'wah. The grace of Allah is sufficient to refute the false accusations and to strengthen the heart of the caller.

=30=

The surah now presents another one of the shifting accusations that the Quraysh would hurl against the Prophet. The previous verse mentioned fortune-teller and madman.

This verse adds another label: poet. The Arabs highly respected poetry. A skilled poet was believed to be inspired by a jinn, and his words could sway tribes and ignite wars. By calling the Prophet a poet, the deniers were trying to reduce the Quran to a work of human literary genius with jinn inspiration, stripping it of its divine authority. But they added a darker element to this accusation. They said, "for whom we eagerly await an ill-fate. " The Arabic word "rayb al-manun" refers to the calamities of time, the twists of fate, specifically death. The Quraysh were essentially saying, "He is just a poet, and like all humans, he will eventually die or suffer a misfortune. We just need to be patient, and his movement will die with him." This was a wishful thinking strategy of avoidance. Instead of confronting the message, they convinced themselves that time would erase the problem. This verse exposes that mentality.

The following verse will respond with a defiant challenge. Here, the Quran simply quotes their statement to highlight its absurdity. They called the Quran poetry, but the Quran is manifestly different in style, content, and purpose from all Arabic poetry. Poetry was about love, war, tribal pride, and lament. The Quran is about the nature of God, the Hereafter, law, morality, and cosmic signs. The accusation was a desperate attempt to pigeonhole something they could not categorise. Their hope for his death was also a sign of their moral bankruptcy. When arguments fail, the debater begins to hope for the physical elimination of the opponent. The believers are taught through this verse that the same tactics are used against the truth in every age: dismiss, label, and wait for it to fade away. But the truth is not dependent on a single human life. It is sustained by the Living God who does not die. The ill-fate the deniers awaited would come upon them, not upon the message.

=31=

This is the Quranic response to the Quraysh's statement that they are waiting for the Prophet to suffer an ill-fate. The Prophet is instructed to answer with a bold and calm challenge. "Keep waiting! I too am waiting with you.

" This is not a passive statement. It is a declaration of confidence in the ultimate outcome. The deniers are waiting for the Prophet's death or failure, and the Prophet is waiting for the fulfilment of Allah's promise: either the punishment of the deniers in this world or his own victory and the triumph of Islam.

The word "tarabbas" implies a state of anticipation and watchfulness. The verse essentially says: let us see whose expectation proves true.

This is a form of mubahala, a mutual invocation of judgment. It places the burden on the deniers to watch and wait, but it warns them that what they are waiting for is not what they think. While they wait for the message to die, the message is waiting for their own reckoning. The Prophet's waiting is not a waiting of anxiety but of serene certainty, grounded in the promises of the One who controls all fates. For the early Muslims, this verse was a source of strength. They were a small minority being told that they just had to wait, and time would prove the truth. The Prophet himself was comforted, knowing that the constant threats and hopes for his demise were being addressed directly by his Lord.

The verse also teaches a profound lesson about dealing with opposition. The believer should not be consumed by the desire for immediate retribution. Instead, they should entrust the matter to Allah and wait, knowing that Allah's timing is perfect and that the truth will ultimately prevail. The waiting of the believer is an active waiting, filled with continued worship, patience, and the spreading of the message, while the waiting of the denier is a passive, arrogant expectation that will end in shock and regret.

=32=

The surah now interrogates the source of the deniers' wild and contradictory accusations. The question is posed in a way that demands reflection. "Or do their intelligent minds prompt them to this?

" The Arabic word "ahlam" is the plural of "hilm," which means intelligence, rationality, forbearance, and sound judgment. The question is heavily ironic. Does their intelligence, their mature reasoning, lead them to call the Prophet a poet, a madman, or a fortune-teller? Does a sound mind conclude that the Quran, with its profound wisdom and miraculous eloquence, is the product of jinn or madness? The obvious answer is no. A truly intelligent person would recognise the truth of the message. So if it is not their intelligence leading them to this paradoxical and self-contradictory position, then what is the cause? The verse provides the alternative: "Or are they just a transgressing people?

" The word "taghun" refers to those who have crossed all bounds of reason and morality. Their rejection is not rooted in intellectual difficulty but in a spiritual disease of transgression. Their arrogance, their love of their ancestral ways, their material interests, and their addiction to sin have pushed them beyond the limits of honest inquiry. They are not seeking the truth; they are running from it.

This is a crucial Quranic insight into the psychology of disbelief. People do not reject faith primarily because of a lack of evidence. The evidence is all around them. They reject faith because they are in a state of rebellion, and the call to submit to God threatens their ego and their desires. The verse invites the listener to examine their own motivations. If someone finds themselves making flimsy excuses against the truth, they should ask themselves: is this my intelligence speaking, or is it my transgression?

The verse also serves as a warning to those who think they are being clever by dismissing religion. True intelligence leads to faith, not away from it.

=33=

The surah now addresses yet another accusation that the Quraysh would level, perhaps the most central one: that the Prophet fabricated the Quran himself. They claimed that he was a forger, a fabricator who invented the verses and attributed them to God. The verse quotes their claim and then delivers a devastating verdict. "In fact, they have no faith.

" This is not a logical refutation in this particular verse; it is a diagnosis. The reason they accuse him of fabrication is not that they have discovered any evidence of forgery. The reason is that they lack faith. Their hearts are empty of the belief that would allow them to recognise the Quran as divine speech. When a person's heart is devoid of faith, they will always find some label to attach to the revelation that allows them to dismiss it. For one person it is fabrication, for another it is myth, for another it is a psychological phenomenon. The common denominator is an absence of iman. The verse implies that the Quran itself is its own proof. Its content, its style, its consistency, its impact on the soul, its prophecies, and its teachings are so clearly beyond human capacity that the charge of fabrication can only arise from a heart that has willfully shut itself off from the truth.

This is a profound lesson for believers who engage in dialogue with those who attack the Quran. Often, the issue is not a lack of evidence but a lack of the spiritual receptivity that comes from sincerity and humility.

The next verse will issue the logical challenge to produce something like it, but this verse first establishes the root cause of the denial. The statement "they have no faith" is both a description and a lament. They are missing the most precious thing, and their accusation is a symptom of that absence.

For the believer, this verse fosters gratitude for the gift of faith, which allows one to see the Quran for what it truly is: the uncreated word of God.

=34=

This is the direct challenge that follows the accusation of fabrication. If they truly believe that the Quran is a human invention, then they, with all their eloquence and literary genius, should be able to produce something similar. The challenge is breathtaking in its confidence. The Quran is presented as the ultimate miracle of the Prophet Muhammad, a miracle that is not a temporary display of supernatural power but a living, readable, recitable book that remains among us. The Arabs of the time were masters of the Arabic language. They prided themselves on their poetry and their oratory. They held annual poetry competitions, and the winning poems were hung on the Kaaba. If anyone could meet this challenge, it would be them.

Yet, despite their intense motivation to discredit the Prophet and stop his message, they never managed to produce even a single surah comparable to the shortest surah of the Quran. The challenge began with asking for a whole book like it, then ten surahs, and finally a single surah. Their total failure is a historical testimony to the Quran's divine origin. The challenge is not merely about literary style. It is about producing a text with the same profound wisdom, the same comprehensive guidance, the same absolute consistency over twenty-three years of revelation, the same ability to transform individuals and societies, and the same miraculous spiritual power. No human author can write a book that is simultaneously a law code, a devotional manual, a historical narrative, a book of cosmic signs, and a psychological manual, all in the most sublime Arabic, without a single contradiction. The verse thus places a permanent burden of proof on the deniers. Their inability to meet the challenge stands as a silent but powerful proof of the Quran's truth.

For the believer, this verse strengthens the rational foundations of faith, providing a tangible argument for the veracity of the revelation.

=35=

The surah now shifts from the direct challenge to the deniers' literary abilities to a series of profound rational questions about existence itself. This verse poses two fundamental questions that strike at the heart of atheism and polytheism. "Or were they created by nothing?

" The question asks the deniers to reflect on their own existence. Did they come into being from absolute nothingness, without a cause, without a creator?

This is logically absurd. Nothingness cannot produce something. The spontaneous generation of complex, intelligent, and moral beings from a void is not only scientifically untenable but rationally impossible. The very existence of anything points to a cause that brought it into existence.

The second question is: "Or are they their own creators?" This addresses the possibility that they created themselves, which is even more absurd. A thing cannot exist before it exists in order to bring itself into existence. Self-creation is a logical contradiction. By presenting these two absurd alternatives, the verse forces the mind to the only rational conclusion: they were created by a Creator who is not themselves and who is not nothing.

This is the basic argument for the existence of God that theologians call the argument from contingency. A contingent being, one that did not always exist and does not have to exist, requires a necessary being to bring it into existence. The deniers are being cornered by simple logic. They deny the Creator, yet they cannot account for their own existence in any coherent way. This verse is a powerful tool for the believer in a secular age. It reminds us that belief in a Creator is not a leap into the dark but the most rational conclusion one can draw from the undeniable fact of one's own existence. The questions are short, but they demolish the foundations of every atheistic worldview.

=36=

The questioning continues, now moving from the microcosm of the human self to the macrocosm of the entire universe. The deniers are asked: Did they create the heavens and the earth?

The question is so obviously absurd that it needs no answer. The heavens and the earth, with all their grandeur, complexity, and order, are clearly not the product of human creation. The deniers themselves never claimed to have created the universe. They acknowledged that Allah was the Creator, as the Quran notes elsewhere. Their idolatry was not a denial of a Creator but the association of partners with Him.

However, the question serves to remind them that the One who created the heavens and the earth is the only One deserving of worship. If they did not create the universe, and their idols did not create the universe, then they should direct their worship exclusively to the One who did.

The verse then delivers a verdict similar to verse 33: "In fact, they have no firm belief." The Arabic word "yuqinun" means certainty, firm conviction. Their problem is not a lack of knowledge; it is a lack of yaqeen. They have a superficial acknowledgment of the Creator, but that acknowledgment does not penetrate their hearts and transform their behaviour. If they had true certainty, they would worship Allah alone and accept the message of His messenger. The verse diagnoses a common spiritual malady: the gap between intellectual assent and heartfelt conviction. Many people today will admit there is a God or a higher power, but this admission does not lead them to pray, to obey, or to prepare for judgment. That is not firm belief. Firm belief is when the truth of Allah's existence and lordship becomes the central organising principle of one's life, shaping every decision and action. The deniers lacked this, and therefore their recognition of the Creator was practically useless.

=37=

The series of rational questions continues with a challenge to the deniers' assumed authority and self-sufficiency. Are they the possessors of the treasuries of the Lord? The treasuries of Allah refer to the infinite stores of provision, mercy, knowledge, and power that belong to Him alone. Rain, sustenance, life, death, guidance, and honour are all within His treasuries.

The question asks whether the deniers have control over these resources. If they did, they would be able to grant themselves whatever they wished: they could make themselves rich without working, give themselves long life, protect themselves from calamities, or grant prophethood to whomever they chose. Their obvious inability to control any of these things demonstrates that they are not in charge of the universe.

The second part of the verse asks: "or are they in control of everything? " The Arabic word "musaytirun" refers to someone who is an absolute controller, an overseer who manages all affairs. Do the deniers hold the reins of destiny? Do they determine who lives and who dies, who is guided and who is misguided? Clearly they do not. Their denial of the Prophet and their rejection of the message do not stem from any actual authority or power. They are not the gatekeepers of divine favour. They have no treasury from which to reward the Prophet if they wanted, and no power to harm him beyond what Allah permits. This verse is deeply humbling for any human being who walks the earth with arrogance. It reminds the believer that all power and all resources belong to Allah alone, so one should ask from Him, fear Him, and rely on Him. It also exposes the emptiness of the deniers' threats and their false sense of control. They do not hold the keys to anything that truly matters. The One who does hold those keys is the Lord who sent the message they are mocking.

=38=

This verse targets a specific claim of the pre-Islamic Arabs. Some of them believed that soothsayers and fortune-tellers could ascend to the heavens by some means, perhaps through jinn, to eavesdrop on the divine decrees and then bring information back to earth. The Quran elsewhere describes how the jinn used to try to steal a hearing from the heavens but were chased away by flaming meteors. This verse challenges the deniers: do they have a stairway, a ladder, a means of ascending to the heavenly realm to listen in on the conversations of the angels? If they claim to have access to the unseen through such means, then let them produce a clear, compelling proof, a sultan mubin, to back up their claim. The challenge exposes the baselessness of their superstitions. They have no stairway. They have no access. Their soothsayers are frauds who mix a grain of truth with a mountain of lies, and whatever truth they might have obtained through jinn has been cut off. The verse is also a response to the deniers' demand for a sign or their claim that they would believe if they had direct access to the unseen. It tells them that they do not have such access, and they cannot demand it as a condition for faith. The unseen belongs to Allah alone, and He reveals it to whomever He chooses from among His messengers. The challenge to produce a compelling proof is similar to the earlier challenge to produce a surah like the Quran. It places the deniers in a position where they must either put up evidence or remain silent. Their silence is an admission that their claims are empty. For the contemporary believer, this verse reinforces the principle that matters of the unseen are known only through authentic revelation. All forms of fortune-telling, astrology that claims to predict specific events, and any attempt to access the unseen through jinn or spirits are either delusion or forbidden practices. The only reliable source of knowledge about the unseen is the Quran and the authentic Sunnah.

=39=

This verse takes aim at one of the most peculiar and offensive beliefs of the pagan Arabs: that the angels were the daughters of Allah. The Quraysh and other Arab tribes worshipped angels, considering them to be female, and claimed that they were the offspring of God. This belief was riddled with contradictions and moral absurdities, which the Quran repeatedly exposes. Here, the verse questions them: does Allah have daughters, as you claim, while you yourselves prefer sons? The Arabs of that era considered daughters to be a source of shame and dishonour. They would bury infant girls alive and would mourn the birth of a daughter.

Yet they had the audacity to ascribe daughters to Allah while desiring sons for themselves. This was a profound insult to the divine majesty. It implied that they were assigning to God what they themselves considered inferior and undesirable. The verse exposes their hypocrisy and their intellectual inconsistency. If daughters were good enough for God, why were they a disgrace for humans? The logic of the Quran is that God is far above having children of any kind, male or female. He is the Self-Sufficient, the Eternal.

The concept of divine offspring is a pagan corruption that reduces the Creator to the level of His creation. This verse is part of a broader Quranic campaign against shirk in all its forms, including the attribution of progeny to Allah. It also contains a subtle social critique. By highlighting their preference for sons over daughters, the Quran indirectly challenges the deeply ingrained misogyny of the society. The birth of a daughter should not be a source of grief, and the ascription of daughters to Allah should be recognised as a monstrous falsehood. The verse is a powerful example of how the Quran uses rational argumentation and moral sensibility to dismantle the idols of the mind and the society.

=40=

The series of rhetorical questions now turns to a possible motive that the deniers might have for their rejection. The verse asks whether the Prophet is demanding some financial reward or compensation for delivering the message, such that the deniers feel burdened by a heavy debt they cannot pay. The implied answer is absolutely not. The Prophet, like all messengers before him, asked for no worldly compensation. He did not seek wealth, status, or power. He lived a life of simplicity and poverty, often going without food. The Quraysh knew this perfectly well. They could not accuse him of preaching for personal gain, because they saw him give away everything and ask for nothing. This verse refutes any notion that their rejection was due to some unreasonable demand made upon them. The message of Islam is free. It requires no payment, no subscription, and no donation to the messenger. All it requires is the submission of the heart to the Creator. If the message were expensive or demanding in a material way, they might have had an excuse. But it is not. Their rejection, therefore, is utterly unjustified.

The verse also carries a deeper spiritual lesson. The true caller to Allah never seeks material compensation from the people. His reward is with Allah alone. This preserves the purity of the message and ensures that no one can claim that the religion is a scheme for the preacher's enrichment. For the believers, this verse is a reminder to check their own intentions. When they give da'wah or do any act of service for the religion, they should ask themselves whether they are secretly seeking a reward from people or from Allah. The deniers might not be able to claim a financial burden as an excuse, but the question also challenges them to reflect on what other burdens they imagine they are being asked to bear. The truth is light, not a burden. The only thing it requires them to abandon is falsehood, and falsehood is the real heavy debt upon their souls.
