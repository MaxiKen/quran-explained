=1=

The surah opens with three separate letters: Alif, Lām, Mīm. These are among the mysterious disjointed letters that appear at the beginning of twenty-nine chapters of the Quran. No prophet or companion ever gave a definitive explanation of their exact meaning, and the most authentic position is that their full knowledge rests with Allah alone.

However, the very presence of these letters carries profound significance. The Quran is placing before its listeners the same letters that make up their everyday speech, the very alphabet they use to compose their own words, and then immediately challenging them to produce anything like this Book. The Arabs of that time prided themselves on their eloquence and mastery of language.

Yet when the Quran came and began with simple letters, it was as though the Creator was saying: this revelation is constructed from the same tools you possess, yet you cannot imitate it. This highlights the miraculous nature of the Quran.

The placement of Alif-Lām-Mīm here also serves as an attention-grabbing opening. The audience would have been used to orators beginning with strange sounds or letters to make people listen intently. Once their attention was captured, the message that followed would land on alert hearts and minds.

In the context of this particular surah, the sudden mention of these letters, followed by a bold prophecy about the Romans, establishes that what is coming is a decisive word from the Lord of all creation. The disjointed letters remind the believer that the Quran is not the product of human intellect but is revealed wisdom. They also connect this surah to others that begin with the same letters, such as Surah Al-Baqarah and Surah Āl-ʿImrān, creating a subtle thematic link. The miraculous prediction that follows about a distant geopolitical conflict is thus anchored in a divine source, and the opening letters function as the stamp of that divine origin. The listener is being told: pay attention, for the One who revealed these letters to the unlettered Prophet is about to inform you of a future event that no human calculation could have foretold. In our own lives, we can reflect that God often begins guidance with small, seemingly simple signs that lead to deeper understanding for those who are willing to ponder. The disjointed letters, in their mysterious simplicity, invite us into a posture of humility, acknowledging that there are dimensions of divine knowledge beyond our immediate grasp.

=2=

The verse declares a historical fact that was fresh in the minds of the early listeners: the Romans, meaning the Byzantine Christian Empire, had just suffered a crushing military defeat. Around 614 to 615 CE, the Persian Sassanid forces overwhelmed the Byzantines, capturing Jerusalem, seizing the True Cross according to historical reports, and pushing deep into Roman territory. The loss was devastating, and the Roman state appeared to be on the verge of collapse. To the Arab pagans of Mecca, this event carried a particular significance. They saw the Romans as fellow people of a scripture, while the Persians were, in their view, closer to their own idolatrous outlook because they worshipped fire and maintained dualistic beliefs. The pagan Quraysh therefore rejoiced at Roman misfortune and saw in it an omen that the followers of a revealed book could be humiliated. In this atmosphere of gloating, the Quran stepped in with a statement of fact that would become the foundation for a stunning prophecy. It is crucial to note the Arabic word used for “defeated” is ghulibati, which implies a decisive and humiliating overpowering. The verse does not merely mention a skirmish but a major turning point in world history. The Quran acknowledges the reality of the defeat without sugar-coating it, because the truthfulness of revelation does not require ignoring facts. By stating openly that the Romans have been defeated, the Quran demonstrates that its message engages with real-world events and human history. It addresses the concerns of the people who are wondering about the fate of empires and the struggle between great powers. The choice of the Romans as the subject also shows that God’s knowledge encompasses all nations, not just the immediate Arabian context. The believers at the time were themselves a small, persecuted minority, much like the Romans after their loss seemed broken and without hope. By mentioning the Romans, the verse subtly draws a parallel that will later be made explicit: just as the Romans will rebound, so too will the believers taste victory, because the affairs of all nations are in God’s hands. This verse thus sets the stage for a powerful message of hope, reminding every generation that no defeat is final if God wills a different outcome, and that what looks like the end can be the beginning of a divine turnaround.

=3=

The location of the Roman defeat is described as “in a nearby land.” The Arabic expression adnā al-arḍ can mean the nearest land or the lowest land. Geographically, the battles took place in the region of Palestine and Syria, specifically near the Dead Sea area, which is indeed the lowest point on the earth’s surface.

This fact was not known to the Arabs of the seventh century with any precision, yet the Quran’s choice of words aligns perfectly with what later scientific discovery would confirm. The phrase tells us that the event was not remote; it was close enough for the Arabs to know about it, to feel its repercussions, and to form opinions on the outcome.

The verse then pivots dramatically with the words “Yet following their defeat, they will triumph.” This is the core of the prophecy. At a time when the Romans were at their weakest, when no political analyst or military strategist could foresee a Roman recovery, the Quran pronounced a definitive future victory.

The Arabic uses the word sayaghlibūn, a future emphatic form, conveying absolute certainty. This was not a guess or a hopeful wish; it was a statement of fact about what would inevitably happen. The prophecy was so bold that it became a test of the Quran’s truthfulness. The Meccan pagans mocked it. According to some reports, Abu Bakr even placed a wager with the disbelievers on this prophecy, which initially had a longer time frame, until the Prophet advised him to revise the terms and the timing, resulting in the exact fulfillment within the specified period.

The verse teaches us that God’s knowledge encompasses the future just as it does the past, and that He can grant victory to whom He wills regardless of present appearances. In a broader sense, the verse instills in the believer the principle that defeat is a temporary phase in the divine plan. No matter how dire the circumstances, triumph may be just around the corner. The neighboring land where the battle took place also symbolizes that God’s signs are not far from human observation; we only need to look closely at history to see His hand at work. This lesson is timeless: the believer never loses hope because the One who controls the outcome has already spoken the final word.

=4=

The Quran now specifies a precise time frame: the victory will occur within “three to nine years.” The Arabic phrase fī biḍʿi sinīn uses the word biḍʿ, which classically denotes a number between three and nine or, as some scholars say, three to nine inclusive. This precision is extraordinary. A general claim about a future victory might be dismissed as wishful thinking, but a time-bound prediction leaves no room for ambiguity. About eight years after this revelation, the Byzantine Emperor Heraclius launched a counteroffensive that culminated in a decisive victory over the Persians at the Battle of Nineveh in 627 CE. Remarkably, historical sources note that this Roman triumph coincided with the Muslim victory at the Battle of Badr.

The verse then directs attention away from purely material causes: “The whole matter rests with Allah before and after victory.” Human beings tend to attribute success to military strategy, alliances, and resources, but the Quran reminds us that all affairs, including defeat and victory, are under divine control. Before the triumph, during the long years of difficulty, the matter belongs to Allah, and after the triumph, when people might become arrogant, the matter still belongs to Him.

This is a profound corrective for both despair and pride. Then comes the statement: “And on that day the believers will rejoice.” The joy of the believers would be twofold. They would rejoice because God’s promise had come true and the Quran’s credibility was demonstrated in front of the disbelievers. They would also rejoice because on the very day the Romans achieved their victory, the Muslims at Badr were granted a miraculous triumph against overwhelming odds. The parallel between the two victories is deliberate. It shows that God’s support is not limited to one nation or one group; He aids those who strive in His cause according to His wisdom. For us today, the lesson is that the timelines of worldly events are set by the Most Wise. We may want immediate relief or instant success, but God’s decree operates on a schedule that perfectly combines wisdom, mercy, and testing. The believer’s role is to trust the timing and to know that the final joy will be all the sweeter for having been preceded by patient perseverance.

=5=

The joy of the believers is emphatically linked to the source of the triumph: “at the victory willed by Allah.” This clarifies that the victory is not a random geopolitical event but a deliberate act of divine help.

The Arabic uses the word bi-naṣri-llāh, the victory of Allah, indicating that the victory belongs to Him and comes from Him. It is His will that determines the rise and fall of nations.

The verse continues, “He gives victory to whoever He wills.” This immediately broadens the principle beyond the Romans. God’s assistance is not based on lineage, wealth, or outward religiosity, but on His absolute will and wisdom. At the time, the Romans were Christians whose beliefs had, according to Islamic teaching, deviated from pure monotheism, yet God granted them victory over the pagan Persians as a sign for the Muslim believers and as a punishment for the arrogance of the Meccan idolaters. This demonstrates that divine victory in this world is not always a direct reward for correct belief; it may serve a greater purpose in the overall plan of guiding humanity.

The verse then grounds this attribute of giving victory in two divine names: “For He is the Almighty, Most Merciful.” The name Almighty, al-ʿAzīz, means that He possesses irresistible power. No force in the universe can prevent Him from granting victory to whomever He decides.

The pairing with Most Merciful, ar-Raḥīm, is deeply significant. God’s exercise of power is not arbitrary or cruel; it flows from His mercy. Even when He gives victory to one group over another, there is mercy in it for those who reflect and turn back to Him. The defeated may learn humility and return to the truth, while the victors should recognize the source of their success and be grateful. In our personal struggles, this combination of divine power and mercy gives immense comfort. The Almighty can change any situation, no matter how entrenched, and the Most Merciful intends only what is ultimately good for those who turn to Him. The verse thus transforms a historical event into a living theology of hope and reliance. Whenever we witness global events or face personal trials, we must remember that victory, when it comes, is a gift from God, not merely the product of our efforts, and that the timing and the beneficiary of that victory are chosen by the One who perfectly combines might and mercy.

=6=

After detailing the prophecy, the Quran anchors it with a powerful statement: “This is the promise of Allah.” The word waʿd carries the weight of a binding commitment from the Creator. When Allah promises something, it is not like a human promise that may be broken due to inability or change of circumstances. Divine promise is an extension of His perfect attributes; He is truthful and all-powerful, so whatever He wills to occur must occur. To reinforce this, the verse adds, “And Allah never fails in His promise.

” The Arabic phrasing yukhlifu-llāhu waʿdahū uses the word yukhlif, meaning to go back on one’s word or to breach a promise. The verse negates this entirely: God does not and cannot break His word. This assurance was crucial for the early Muslims who were facing intense persecution and needed something firm to hold onto. If the promise of a distant Roman victory was so certain, then the promises of divine support for the believers, of the ultimate triumph of truth, and of the Hereafter are equally certain.

The verse then ends with a sobering observation: “But most people do not know.” This ignorance is not a lack of intelligence but a lack of deep, faith-rooted knowledge. Many people live on the surface of life, perceiving only immediate material causes. They see a powerful empire like Persia crushing the Romans and assume that is the final reality. They fail to grasp that behind the visible world operates the unseen hand of God, whose promises are unfolding with absolute precision. This phrase appears repeatedly in the Quran to highlight a recurring human flaw: the tendency to judge by appearances and to doubt the fulfillment of divine warnings and glad tidings. The believers are taught to be among the few who know: to know that God’s word is true, that His timing is perfect, and that patience will always be rewarded. In our modern context, the same pattern persists. People trust economic forecasts and political analyses but often dismiss or forget the promises of God regarding the consequences of moral action, the power of sincere prayer, and the reality of the afterlife. This verse calls us to elevate our knowledge from the shallow information of the world to the deep certainty that comes from recognizing who is making the promise. When we internalize that God never fails in His promise, our anxieties about the future dissolve, and we are empowered to live with steadfast conviction.

=7=

This verse diagnoses the root cause of why most people fail to grasp the certainty of God’s promises. “They only know the worldly affairs of this life” refers to a superficial knowledge that is limited to what can be seen, touched, and measured in the immediate world. This includes politics, trade, agriculture, and the outward aspects of civilization.

The Arabic word ẓāhiran min al-ḥayāti-d-dunyā literally means what is apparent of the life of this world. People become experts in their trades and masters of social dynamics, yet they remain blind to the deeper reality that gives this world its meaning.

The verse then states, “but are totally oblivious to the Hereafter.” The word ghāfilūn implies a heedlessness that is willful and persistent. It is not that they lack information about the afterlife; rather, they push it out of their conscious concern and live as if this world is all that exists. The danger of this state is that it leads a person to measure everything by short-term material gain or loss. The Meccan disbelievers saw the Roman defeat and assumed the pagans were right, just as they saw the small numbers and poverty of the Muslims and considered them deluded. Their error was forgetting that this world is a testing ground and that the ultimate outcome is determined in the eternal life to come. This verse serves as a wake-up call for every generation. We may be highly educated in science, technology, and finance, but if our understanding does not extend to the reality of the Hereafter—the judgment, the scales, Paradise, and Hellfire—then according to the Quran we are among those who do not truly know. The cure for this shallowness is to constantly connect the events of daily life to their eternal consequences. When we read the news, we should ask what it teaches us about the fleeting nature of power. When we succeed, we should thank God and use the success to invest in the Hereafter. When we fail, we should remember that the final success belongs to those who keep faith.

The verse also implies a criticism of a purely secular worldview. A society that restricts its knowledge to the outward life becomes spiritually blind and morally adrift. The Quran is calling humanity to a holistic knowledge where the visible and the invisible, the temporary and the eternal, are all integrated under the lordship of the One who created both.

=8=

The surah now shifts from the external sign of the Roman victory to the internal sign within every human being. The question “Have they not reflected upon their own being?” is a direct appeal to introspection.

The Arabic word anfusihim means their own selves. The human soul and body are complex miracles. From the intricate design of the eye to the unfathomable workings of the mind, every person carries within themselves clear evidence of a wise and purposeful Creator.

The verse then expands the call to reflection to the entire cosmos: “Allah only created the heavens and the earth and everything in between for a purpose and an appointed term.” The words bi-l-ḥaqq indicate that creation is based on truth and for a just, meaningful purpose. It is not a game or a product of random chance.

The phrase “appointed term” (ajalin musamman) points to the fact that this universe is not eternal; it has a divinely set lifespan after which it will be dismantled and the Hereafter will commence. This truth has a direct implication: if the universe has a beginning, a purpose, and an end, then human life must also be accountable to the One who set that purpose.

Yet the verse laments that “most people are truly in denial of the meeting with their Lord.” Despite the clarity of the signs within themselves and in the heavens and earth, they persist in denying the ultimate meeting with God.

The use of la-liqāʾi rabbihim la-kāfirūn is emphatic; it is a willful rejection born out of arrogance or love of worldly desires. This denial is not based on intellectual proof but on a desire to avoid moral responsibility. The verse is a powerful reminder that atheism or agnosticism is often a spiritual ailment, not a logical conclusion. If a person sincerely reflects on the order, beauty, and purpose in creation, they will inevitably be led to the Creator and to the reality of the afterlife. For the modern reader, this verse challenges the prevailing materialist narrative that sees the universe as a meaningless accident. It invites the scientist, the philosopher, and the ordinary person to look inward and upward, and to recognize that the most profound knowledge is the knowledge that leads to God. Our own existence is the nearest sign, yet it is often the most overlooked.

=9=

The Quran now invites the deniers to learn from history. “Have they not travelled throughout the land” is a call to observe the ruins and remnants of past civilizations that littered the Arabian landscape and the wider region. The Quraysh were a trading people; their caravans traveled to Syria and Yemen, passing by the remains of the Thamūd, the people of Lot, and other destroyed nations. The verse points out that those ancient peoples were far superior in might. They had greater physical strength, more advanced technology, impressive architecture, and more abundant agriculture. “They cultivated the land and developed it more than these Meccans ever have” is a direct contrast. The Quraysh had achieved some prosperity, but they had never reached the level of the civilizations of old.

Yet this material superiority could not save them from destruction when they rejected the truth. The reason for their downfall is then given: “Their messengers came to them with clear proofs.” God did not destroy them arbitrarily; He sent messengers with evident signs, miracles, and rational arguments. The nations had every opportunity to recognize the truth and reform themselves.

The final judgment was a consequence of their persistent rejection. The verse then articulates a fundamental principle of divine justice: “Allah would have never wronged them, but it was they who wronged themselves.” God’s punishment is never an act of injustice. He gives life, provides sustenance, sends guidance, and allows ample time for repentance. When destruction comes, it is because people have filled the scale of their deeds with oppression, corruption, and denial. They wrong themselves by choosing a path that leads to their own ruin.

This principle applies both to nations in history and to individuals on the Day of Judgment. In a modern context, the call to travel and observe the fate of past empires remains relevant. Archaeological discoveries repeatedly confirm the sudden collapse of sophisticated societies that had no external reason to fall. The moral and spiritual decay preceded the physical one. For us, the lesson is clear: no amount of technological or economic progress can protect a society that abandons the guidance of its Creator.

The verse teaches that self-destruction is the inevitable outcome of rejecting truth and committing injustice. It encourages believers to be students of history, seeing in it the patterns that confirm God’s unchanging laws.

=10=

This verse draws the conclusion from the historical survey. “Then most evil was the end of the evildoers” summarizes the fate of those past nations.

The Arabic word as-sūʾā here intensifies the horror of their final state: a terrible end in this world through catastrophic punishment, followed by an even worse punishment in the Hereafter. The verse identifies the specific cause of this evil end: “for denying and mocking the signs of Allah. ” The word kadhdhabū means they denied the truth of the signs, treating them as lies. More than that, they yastahziʾūn, meaning they actively mocked and ridiculed the messages and the messengers. This combination of denial and mockery represents the height of arrogance. It is not merely that they disbelieved intellectually; they also scorned the idea that God’s words could have any claim over their lives. When a people reaches this stage, their hearts become sealed, and punishment becomes inevitable. The verse acts as a direct warning to the Meccans who were doing exactly the same thing: they were denying the Quran and mocking the Prophet. The historical examples were presented to show that the same behavior led to the destruction of far mightier nations, so the Quraysh should not feel secure.

For the believer, this verse highlights the seriousness of how one responds to divine guidance. It is possible to be exposed to the truth and yet treat it lightly, making jokes about religious teachings, or dismissing moral commands as outdated. Such an attitude is spiritually lethal. The “signs of Allah” include both the verses of scripture and the signs in the natural world. Denying them is to close the door to all healing and guidance. Mocking them adds a layer of insolence that invites a stern response from God.

However, the verse also contains hope by implication: if the evil end is for those who deny and mock, then a good end awaits those who accept, honor, and live by the signs of Allah. This fits the pattern of the surah, which constantly moves between warning and glad tidings. The lesson for our times is to guard our speech and our attitude toward sacred things. In an age of cynicism and irreverence, the believer is called to a posture of deep respect, reflection, and sincere acceptance of God’s signs wherever they appear.

=11=

Having established that the end of the deniers is most evil, the surah now provides the logical foundation for the resurrection and final judgment. The verse begins with a statement of God’s power: “It is Allah Who originates the creation.

” The word yabdaʾu means He initiates, He begins. Every living thing, every soul, and the entire universe came into existence by His will without any prior model.

This is the first creation. Then comes the second part: “and will resurrect it.

” The Arabic yubdiʾu wa yuʿīd uses the same root for repetition, indicating that He will bring creation back after death. The argument is clear and compelling: the One who created something from nothing is perfectly capable of recreating it after its dissolution.

In fact, re-creation is logically easier, as the Quran states elsewhere. This refutes the materialist objection that dead and decayed bodies cannot be brought back.

The verse concludes, “And then to Him you will all be returned.” The final return is not just a physical resurrection but a presentation before the divine court for judgment. The entire cycle of human life—origin, death, and resurrection—is placed under God’s absolute authority. This verse serves as a bridge from the historical punishments in the previous passage to the detailed description of the Day of Judgment that follows. For the believers, this is a source of comfort and accountability. They know that their life has a purpose and that their deeds will not be lost. For the deniers, it is a sobering warning that their denial does not erase the reality.

In the context of the surah, the prediction about the Romans’ victory already demonstrated that God controls the events of this world. Now the focus turns to the afterlife, where a far greater distinction between success and failure will take place. In our daily lives, this verse invites constant awareness of the ultimate return. Every action, every intention, every choice is made with the knowledge that we will stand before the One who originated us. This awareness transforms mundane activities into acts of worship and restrains us from injustice. The verse is a miniature creedal statement that encapsulates the entire Islamic worldview: tawḥīd in creation, belief in resurrection, and certainty in final accountability.

=12=

This verse zooms into the moment of the Hour, the Day of Resurrection. “On the Day the Hour will arrive” points to the sudden, inevitable coming of that decisive moment. The Hour, as-Sāʿah, is a Quranic term for the end of the world and the beginning of the afterlife, so called because it comes upon humanity suddenly, within a single moment.

The verse then describes the state of the wicked: “the wicked will be dumbstruck. ” The Arabic word al-mujrimūn refers to the criminals, those who have earned guilt through their persistent denial and evil deeds. The word yublisūn comes from the root ib-lā-s, which conveys the meaning of being silenced by despair and shock, utterly confounded.

This is the state of someone who sees the unimaginable terror of that Day and realizes that all their worldly arguments, excuses, and arrogance have evaporated. Their tongues are tied; they cannot formulate a defense. This silence is a stark contrast to their boastfulness and mockery in the world. They used to demand proofs, ridicule the messengers, and confidently deny the resurrection. Now, faced with the overwhelming reality, all that bravado collapses into mute despair. The description is brief but incredibly powerful, painting a picture of utter hopelessness. The verse implicitly contrasts the wicked with the believers, who will be given light and will be guided to speak and intercede by God’s permission. For the listener, this is intended to provoke a moment of self-examination. Before the Hour arrives when it is too late, every person has the opportunity to break the silence of their soul by speaking words of faith and repentance now. In a wider sense, the verse warns against the false security that comes from worldly knowledge and power. The criminals of various nations were often the elite who wielded influence and eloquence, but on that Day, their status will mean nothing, and their voices will be extinguished. For the modern person who relies on rhetoric, social status, or intellectual arguments to dismiss the afterlife, this verse says: there will come a time when all of that will vanish, and you will stand alone and silent before your Creator. The verse calls us to find our voice in sincere submission before that day of enforced silence arrives.

=13=

The scene of judgment continues. The criminals are silent, and the verse now strips away one of the false hopes they entertained in the world. Many idolaters did not deny God entirely; they believed that their idols, saints, angels, or other beings could act as intercessors on their behalf before Allah. They would say, as the Quran records elsewhere, “We only worship them so that they may bring us nearer to Allah. ” On the Day of Judgment, this hope will be utterly shattered. The verse states bluntly: “There will be no intercessors for them from among their associate-gods.

” The word shurakāʾihim refers to those partners they set up alongside God. None of them will be able to speak, plead, or mediate. They will be utterly powerless.

In fact, the situation will become even more devastating: “and they will totally deny their associate-gods.” The criminals, in their desperation, will renounce the very beings they worshipped. The Quran describes in other passages how they will turn on their false gods and say that they were misled, and the false gods themselves will disown their worshipers. This mutual disavowal is a scene of complete spiritual bankruptcy.

The verse teaches the core Islamic doctrine of tawḥīd: all intercession belongs to Allah alone, and He grants it only to whom He wills and for whom He pleases. No created being has the inherent right or power to intercede without God’s permission. The denial of their associate-gods by the criminals themselves is the ultimate proof that shirk, or associating partners with God, is based on a lie that even its adherents will eventually confess is false. The lesson for humanity is profound. We often pin our hopes on intermediaries—whether they be influential people, religious figures, or even abstract forces like money and status—thinking they will save us in times of ultimate crisis. On the Day of Judgment, every such false reliance will crumble. The only hope lies in a direct relationship with Allah, built on pure monotheism and righteous deeds, and in seeking the intercession of the Prophet Muhammad, which God has promised for the believers. The verse, therefore, is a call to immediate reform: examine what you rely on besides God, and be aware that those things will abandon you when you need them most. True security is found only in the One who never abandons His sincere servants.

=14=

The progression moves from the specific fate of the wicked to the universal division of all humanity. The repetition of “And on the Day the Hour will arrive” emphasizes the centrality of that event.

This is not a distant, abstract concept but the pivotal moment toward which all human history is moving. The verse then states: “the people will then be split into two groups. ” The Arabic yawmaʾidhin yatafarraqūn uses the verb which means they will separate, disperse, and be sorted into distinct categories. This division is final and absolute. On earth, people are mixed: believers and disbelievers, righteous and corrupt, sincere and hypocritical live side by side. In many cases, it is difficult to distinguish them by outward appearance. But on that Day, God will issue His judgment, and the inner reality of every soul will become manifest. The two groups are then elaborated in the following two verses. This verse functions as a heading, a stark announcement that the time of testing is over and the time of sorting has come. There is no middle ground, no third category. Every human being will belong to one of two destinations based on their faith and deeds. This binary outcome underscores the seriousness of the choices we make in this life.

In the context of the surah, the previous discussion about the Romans and the Meccans was about temporary worldly divisions. Now the eternal division is laid out. The worldly victory of the Romans was a sign for believers, but the ultimate victory is this final separation into the saved and the damned. For the contemporary reader, this verse challenges the relativism that suggests all paths lead to the same end or that judgment is a myth. The Quran asserts that reality is profoundly moral and that our lives are heading toward a decisive sorting. The verse calls us to live with the awareness that we are being prepared for one of two eternal homes. It is a call to actively seek to be among the people of the right hand, the successful group, by cultivating faith and righteousness. The clarity of this division is a mercy because it removes ambiguity and motivates the believer to strive.

=15=

The first of the two groups is now described. “As for those who believed and did good” combines the two foundational pillars of salvation: sound faith and righteous action. Belief alone, without corresponding deeds, is not the path to this joyful end, just as deeds without faith lack a foundation.

The Arabic āmanū wa ʿamilū-ṣ-ṣāliḥāti is a phrase that recurs throughout the Quran, binding the internal conviction of the heart with the external manifestation of virtue. Their reward is stated simply yet profoundly: “they will be rejoicing in a Garden.

” The word fī rawḍatin yuḥbarūn uses rawḍah, a lush, well-watered garden, a place of beauty and pleasure. The verb yuḥbarūn comes from ḥabara, meaning to delight, to gladden, to make someone so happy that their joy is visible on their faces.

This is not a passive state of mere contentment; it is active, overflowing happiness. The Garden is the perfect environment, and their inner state matches it perfectly. They will be honored and made to rejoice. This verse, placed immediately after the division, gives the believer a powerful motivation. The contrast with the previous silence and despair of the wicked could not be starker. The believers will be in a garden, a place of life and growth, while the others are in a state of barren loss.

Furthermore, the verb is in the passive form, indicating that they are made to rejoice by a force outside themselves; God and His angels will be the cause of their joy. This implies that the delights of Paradise are not just self-generated but are gifts continuously bestowed. For the Muslims who were facing persecution in Mecca, this verse was a balm for their wounded hearts. It assured them that their present suffering was not the final word. In every era, believers who endure hardship, isolation, or mockery for their faith can draw strength from this promise. Their faith and good deeds are not in vain. They are moving toward a reality where joy will be their permanent state, with no fear of it ever ending. The verse invites us to check our own spiritual account: do we have the faith that leads to action, and are our deeds aligned with what is pleasing to God?

This is the path to the garden of rejoicing.

=16=

The second group is now described with a correspondingly sobering outcome. The verse lists three interrelated crimes: “those who disbelieved,” meaning they rejected the core truth of monotheism and the messengership; “and denied Our signs,” meaning the verses of the Quran, the miracles of the prophets, and the signs in the universe; “and the meeting with Allah in the Hereafter,” meaning they specifically denied the resurrection and final judgment. The accumulation of these charges shows that their disbelief was not a passive ignorance but an active rejection of clear evidence. The punishment they face is expressed in the words “they will be confined in punishment. ” The Arabic fa-ulāʾika fī-l-ʿadhābi muḥḍarūn uses the passive participle muḥḍarūn, which stems from the root meaning to be brought, to be made present, or to be mustered. It implies that they are not just placed in punishment but are forcibly brought and permanently detained there. There is an element of compulsion and a lack of escape.

The word muḥḍarūn can also connote being made to attend, as one is summoned to court. They will have no choice but to face the consequences of their denial. This confinement is the opposite of the freedom and joy in the garden. In this world, they moved freely, denying God’s signs without immediate visible penalty, but in the Hereafter, their movement is restricted, and they are surrounded by the punishment they used to mock. The verse serves as a powerful warning.

The mention of denying the “meeting with Allah” specifically highlights the core issue: much of human sin stems from forgetting or denying that we will return to God and give account. If that meeting were constantly in our consciousness, our behavior would change dramatically.

The verse also balances the previous promise. The Quran always presents the two outcomes together to give a complete picture of divine justice. Mercy and punishment are both real, and human beings choose their destination through their response to God’s signs. In the modern context, where the concept of hell is often dismissed as archaic or unloving, this verse reaffirms that a just God must ultimately separate the unrepentant evildoers from the righteous and that the denial of truth after it has been clearly presented has eternal consequences. It is a call to take belief seriously, to honor the signs of Allah, and to live in preparation for the meeting with Him.

=17=

After detailing the two final destinations, the surah transitions into guidance for the believers on how to remain connected to God and avoid the fate of the deniers. The verse begins with the particle fa, which means “so” or “therefore,” linking the command to the preceding truth. Because the Day of Judgment and the division of humanity are certain, you should glorify Allah. The command is “glorify Allah,” from the Arabic sabbiḥū, which means to declare Him far above any imperfection, to exalt Him, and to worship Him with this conscious declaration. The specific times mentioned are “in the evening and in the morning.” The evening, al-masāʾ, covers the time from the declining of the sun until night, and the morning, aṣ-ṣabāḥ, covers the early part of the day. These two times bookend the daily cycle. By mentioning them, the verse establishes the principle that a believer’s day should be framed by the remembrance of God. The evening glorification corresponds particularly to the Maghrib and Isha prayers, and the morning glorification to the Fajr prayer.

This is the majority opinion of the scholars. However, the command also encompasses voluntary dhikr and supplication. Beginning and ending the day with God’s glorification protects a person from heedlessness and provides spiritual strength to face the challenges in between. The psychological and spiritual impact of this practice is profound. In the quiet of the early morning, glorifying Allah sets the tone of purpose and submission before the world’s noise intrudes. In the evening, it serves as a cleansing of the day’s mistakes and a peaceful closure. The verse is placed here to remind the believers that the knowledge of the Hereafter should translate into a disciplined life of worship. The disbelievers are oblivious to the Hereafter, as mentioned earlier, but the believers must be in constant remembrance. The specific mention of these two times also alludes to the signs of God in the alternation of night and day, which is a theme that will be developed later in the surah. The verse is concise but establishes a rhythm of life centered on the Creator. For us today, it is a clear call to never let a day begin or end without intentionally turning to God in glorification and prayer.

This is the practical safeguard against slipping into the heedlessness that characterizes those who only know the surface of worldly life.

=18=

This verse continues the command to glorify and praise Allah by expanding both the spatial and temporal dimensions of worship. The opening phrase “all praise is for Him in the heavens and the earth” declares that praise, al-ḥamd, belongs to Allah from every corner of existence. The heavens and the earth and all that they contain—angels, stars, mountains, trees, animals—are in a constant state of praising their Creator, whether we perceive it or not. The Quran elsewhere says, “There is not a thing except that it exalts [Allah] by His praise. ” By inserting this statement in the middle of the commands about prayer times, the verse lifts the believer’s vision. When we stand for prayer, we are not performing a solitary act; we are joining the universal chorus of worship that encompasses the entire cosmos. This awareness dignifies our worship and fills it with a sense of belonging to the greater order of creation. Then the verse returns to specific times: “as well as in the afternoon, and at noon. ” The afternoon, al-ʿashīy, refers to the time from the zenith’s decline until sunset, which includes the ʿAṣr prayer. The noon, aẓ-ẓuhr, is when the sun passes its meridian, the time of the Ẓuhr prayer. So this verse, together with the previous one, covers the five daily prayers according to many classical commentators. The evening and morning point to Maghrib, Isha, and Fajr, while the afternoon and noon point to ʿAṣr and Ẓuhr.

Thus, within two short verses, the entire daily prayer schedule is beautifully referenced. This demonstrates that the obligation of the five daily prayers, though not yet fully detailed in its timings during the Meccan period, was already being instilled in principle.

The mention of the heavens and earth in between reminds us that prayer is not a burden but an elevation. We are invited to align our limited time with the eternal praise that fills the universe. For contemporary believers, this verse transforms the understanding of daily prayers. Instead of seeing them as interruptions to our worldly activities, we can see them as our participation in the continuous worship of all creation. The times of prayer are sacred pauses where we join the angels and the very elements of the earth in acknowledging the praise due to the Almighty. This perspective helps cultivate khushūʿ, or mindful presence, in prayer, and reinforces that a life structured around these moments is a life lived in harmony with the cosmos.

=19=

After instructing believers in prayer, the surah returns to the proof of resurrection, drawing on observable natural phenomena. The verse begins: “He brings forth the living from the dead and the dead from the living.

” This is a profound statement of God’s creative power. The living from the dead can be seen in numerous ways: a chick emerging from an apparently lifeless egg, a plant sprouting from a dry seed, a righteous child born to sinful parents, or a believer emerging from a community of disbelievers. Conversely, the dead from the living is equally visible: a lifeless egg from a living bird, a dry seed from a thriving plant, a disbelieving offspring from believing parents. The cycle of life and death is entirely in God’s control. This constant exchange demonstrates that the boundary between life and death is not a barrier for the Creator.

The verse then narrows the focus to a specific sign that was immensely relevant to the Arabs: “And He gives life to the earth after its death. ” The barren, cracked, lifeless desert land would, after a season of rain, burst into vibrant green life. The people of Mecca witnessed this annually.

The Arabic verb yuḥyī and the noun arḍa are central; the earth is dead and God brings it to life. This observable miracle is then directly linked to human resurrection: “And so will you be brought forth from the grave. ” Just as the rain revives the dead earth, God’s command will bring the dead human bodies out of their graves alive. The logic is unassailable for anyone who contemplates. The verse thus ties together the earlier command to worship with the doctrine of resurrection. The one who prays to Allah is the same One who controls life and death. The daily prayers, which mark the passage of time, are set within a world that constantly exhibits signs of resurrection.

This verse also contains an ecological awareness: the earth’s revival is an act of divine mercy that mirrors the spiritual revival of souls through revelation. For modern readers, the scientific explanation of life cycles does not diminish the sign; it only deepens our awe at the precision and consistency of God’s laws. The verse challenges us to see the ordinary processes of nature as continuous reminders of our own eventual return to life. Every spring, every blooming flower, every birth is a small-scale rehearsal of the great resurrection that we must all face.

=20=

The surah now begins a beautiful sequence of verses detailing the signs of God in creation and human life. The phrase “One of His signs” introduces a list of evidences that point to God’s power, wisdom, and mercy.

The first sign is the very origin of humanity: “that He created you from dust.” The Arabic min turābin refers to the soil, the humble, lifeless substance of the earth.

The first human, Adam, was created directly from dust, and every subsequent human being is created from the nutrients that ultimately come from the earth. Our physical bodies are composed of the same elements found in the soil. This origin is a powerful reminder of our lowly beginnings and our complete dependence on the Creator.

The verse then immediately follows with the word “then—behold!—you are human beings spreading over the earth. ” The transition is dramatic. From inert dust, God produced a living, intelligent, speaking, and multiplying race that fills the globe.

The word basharan refers to human beings in their full physical and social reality, with skin, features, intellect, and emotion. The phrase tantashirūn means you spread, disperse, and propagate throughout the earth. This transformation from dust to a global civilization is one of the most obvious yet overlooked miracles. We walk, talk, build, invent, and yet our substance is from the same soil we tread upon. The verse calls upon the listener to reflect on their own personal existence. Every individual began as a drop of fluid, yet grew into a fully formed human with a unique personality and role. This sign demolishes any argument that resurrection is impossible. If God could initiate human life from dust, then recreating it after death is even easier.

The verse also carries an ethical lesson. Knowing that we come from dust should keep us humble. No matter how advanced we become, our origin is the same lowly material. This humbling reflection counters arrogance and reminds us of our equality as human beings. For the contemporary audience, the science of embryology and the study of the elements that compose the human body only reinforce this verse’s message. The more we learn about our biological origins, the more we should be amazed at the Creator who fashioned this stunningly complex being from the simple substance of the earth. The sign is both a proof and a call to gratitude.
