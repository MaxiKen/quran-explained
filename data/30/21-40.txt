=21=

The sequence of signs continues by turning from the origin of humanity to one of the most intimate and powerful relationships in human life: marriage. The verse opens, “And one of His signs is that He created for you spouses from among yourselves.

” The word azwāj, translated as spouses, means pairs, mates, or partners. The phrase “from among yourselves” is profoundly significant. It means that the spouse is of the same human nature, the same essence, not a completely alien being. This shared nature creates a foundation of mutual understanding. The purpose is then stated: “so that you may find comfort in them.

” The Arabic taskunū ilayhā carries the meaning of finding stillness, peace, and tranquility. A spouse is meant to be a source of rest for the soul in a world full of turmoil. This comfort is not merely physical; it is emotional, psychological, and spiritual. The home becomes a sanctuary where the heart settles.

The verse then declares, “And He has placed between you compassion and mercy. ” The word mawaddah is a deep, affectionate love that is visible in action, while raḥmah is a merciful tenderness that cares for the other even when they are weak or difficult. The combination covers the active, passionate aspect of love and the forgiving, nurturing dimension. These qualities are not simply human achievements; God has placed them, meaning they are a divine gift implanted in the hearts of spouses. This explains why even in cultures where love is not articulated, the bond can be incredibly strong.

The verse concludes, “Surely in this are signs for people who reflect.” Reflection, tafakkur, is required to see beyond the physical attraction and social institution to the Hand of the Creator. The harmony and love between two individuals, who were strangers, is a miracle that points to God’s wisdom. For contemporary life, this verse elevates marriage from a mere contract to a sacred sign. It teaches that marital love is a divine blessing, not just a chemical reaction. When spouses realize this, their relationship transforms into worship. They see each other’s strengths and weaknesses through the lens of God’s plan, cultivating patience and gratitude.

This verse also counters modern individualism that often isolates people; it reminds us that we are created in pairs and that our completion lies partly in these God-given bonds. Reflecting on this sign helps a person appreciate their spouse and work through difficulties by recalling that the very compassion they feel is a deposit from the Most Merciful.

=22=

The next sign expands outward from the domestic sphere to the vast cosmos and the rich tapestry of human diversity. The verse begins with the creation of the heavens and the earth.

This is the macro-level sign: the staggering order, complexity, and beauty of the universe. The heavens with their stars, galaxies, and precise laws, and the earth with its perfect conditions for life, all point to a Creator of infinite power and wisdom. Then the verse shifts to a micro-level social sign: “and the diversity of your languages and colours.

” The word alwān refers to skin colours, races, and ethnicities, while alsinah refers to languages, dialects, and forms of speech. The fact that humanity is divided into countless ethnicities, each with its own unique language, is not a flaw or an accident. It is a sign. If God had willed, He could have made all human beings identical, speaking one tongue. Instead, He created this rich diversity as an expression of His creative power. The study of linguistics and anthropology only deepens the sense of wonder. Every language is a complex system, capable of poetry, logic, and emotional expression. The colours of skin, from the darkest to the lightest, are a testament to the artist’s palette.

The verse then specifies the audience for this sign: “for those of sound knowledge.” The word al-ʿālimīn refers to those who possess deep, informed understanding. It takes a certain level of knowledge and reflection to appreciate these signs fully. The ignorant may see diversity as a cause for division or racism, but the knowledgeable see in it the handiwork of God, which leads to respect and wonder. The verse implicitly condemns any form of racial or linguistic prejudice. All colours and languages are deliberately created by Allah, and no one has the right to claim superiority based on these innate differences.

This is beautifully articulated by the Prophet when he said in his farewell sermon that no Arab has superiority over a non-Arab, nor a non-Arab over an Arab, nor a white over a black, nor a black over a white, except by piety. This verse encourages believers to travel, learn about other cultures, and see God’s signs in the global human family. In a world torn by ethnic conflicts and identity politics, this Quranic sign is a healing message. It urges us to celebrate diversity as divine art and to use our differences for mutual enrichment, not enmity. The sign of languages and colours is a standing invitation to humility and awe.

=23=

Moving to a sign that every human experiences daily, the verse focuses on sleep and work. “And one of His signs is your sleep by night and by day for rest.” Sleep is a universal phenomenon, yet it is a profound mystery. It is a state of temporary death, where consciousness ceases and the body undergoes repair and rejuvenation. The fact that God made the night primarily for sleep and the day for activity is part of a balanced design, but the verse also acknowledges that people may sleep during the day for rest, as the phrase “by night and by day” indicates.

The word subāt in Arabic for deep sleep carries the connotation of cutting off, as if the soul is being gently severed from the world to be restored. This mercy is often taken for granted. Without sleep, human life would be impossible.

The verse then pairs this with “as well as your seeking His bounty in both.” This refers to the effort to earn a livelihood, which occurs both by day and, for some, by night.

The phrase “His bounty” is a reminder that all provisions come from God, and our work is merely a means to access them. The alternation between rest and activity is a rhythmic sign of God’s care. The body and mind are designed to work and then rest, to strive and then recuperate. If one tries to invert this pattern completely or ignore the need for rest, the system breaks down.

The verse ends with “Surely in this are signs for people who listen.” Listening here implies deep attentiveness, the kind that leads to understanding and gratitude. Those who merely sleep and wake without reflecting are missing the sign. The believer hears the call in the daily routine: the night is a divine covering of mercy, the morning a resurrection of life. For the modern world, where sleep deprivation is a common ailment and work-life balance is a constant struggle, this verse offers divine wisdom. It validates the need for rest and frames economic activity as a pursuit of God’s bounty, not just a material rat race. It also connects to the earlier theme of resurrection; sleep is the minor death, and waking is the daily rehearsal of the Day of Judgment. The sign invites us to transform our most ordinary activities into conscious acts of worship. When we go to sleep with the remembrance of God and wake up with gratitude, we are living the sign and truly listening to what it proclaims.

=24=

The natural world continues to provide signs. Here, the sign is meteorological: lightning and rain. “He shows you lightning, inspiring you with hope and fear.” Lightning is a dramatic, awe-inspiring phenomenon. The flash in the sky simultaneously causes hope for rain and fear of thunderbolts or destructive storms. This dual emotion reflects the human condition before God: the believer balances between hope in His mercy and fear of His punishment. The lightning becomes a visual parable.

The verse then proceeds to the life-giving water: “And He sends down rain from the sky, reviving the earth after its death. ” This is the culmination of the process. The lightning heralds the rain, and the rain transforms the dead, barren earth into a lush, living carpet. This reviving of the earth is a recurring Quranic proof for the resurrection. The power that can bring a lifeless desert to life can certainly bring human bones back to life. The verse is directed at “people who understand.” Understanding, yaʿqilūn, comes from the same root as intellect, ʿaql. This sign is not just for emotional appreciation but for intellectual deduction. The one who uses their reason will connect the life cycle of plants to the ultimate destiny of humanity. Lightning also reminds us of the power of God that can be both merciful and severe. The same water that gives life can become a flood if God wills. This balance of hope and fear keeps the believer on a straight path, neither complacent nor despairing. For contemporary society, where rain is often explained purely through meteorological models, this verse does not deny the science but adds the spiritual dimension. The process is a sign; the science is simply a description of how God consistently operates. Reflecting on the sign leads to shukr, gratitude. Every time we see rain, we should remember that we are witnessing a life-giving miracle, and that our own spiritual dryness can be revived by the water of divine revelation just as the earth is revived by rain. The verse therefore connects the natural with the supernatural, training the mind to see the Creator behind every physical cause. It is a call to a deeper literacy of the world around us, where each natural event is an ayah, a sign, pointing to the One who made it.

=25=

This sign shifts to the very stability of the cosmos and the immediacy of the resurrection. The first part, “that the heavens and the earth persist by His command,” declares that the entire universe is sustained moment by moment by God’s command.

The Arabic taqūmu bi-amrihī means they stand, hold firm, and remain established by His order. The heavens, with all their massive celestial bodies, and the earth, with its intricate systems, do not run on independent, self-sustaining laws. They are upheld by the continuous will and command of Allah. If He withdrew His sustaining order for a moment, everything would collapse. This understanding completely transforms the way a believer looks at the universe. It is not a clockwork mechanism wound up and left to run; it is a living act of divine sustenance.

The second part of the verse then pivots to the ultimate act of command: “Then when He calls you out of the earth just once, you will instantly come forth.” The resurrection is portrayed as a single divine call.

The word daʿwah, a call, is a summons. When that call comes, there will be no delay.

The phrase “just once” emphasizes the ease and the suddenness. No lengthy process of reassembly is needed from God’s perspective. The dead will simply be and come forth from their graves, responding to the call of their Creator. The connection between the first and second parts is profound. The same divine command that upholds the massive heavens and earth without effort can just as easily bring forth humanity from the earth. The power required is identical. In a world where people see the universe as stable and thus consider resurrection far-fetched, the Quran reframes the perception. The stability of the universe is itself a continuous miracle of divine command. What we see as “laws of nature” are simply God’s habitual way of commanding things.

Therefore, a new command for resurrection is not a departure from some autonomous system but another instance of the same divine authority. This verse invites believers to trust in the absolute power of God. It instills a sense of security: the universe is not a chaotic, random place; it is in the hands of its Creator. And it instills a sense of urgency: the call will come, and when it does, our response will be instantaneous. We are living on a planet that is held in existence by a command; our own existence is equally fragile and dependent. The only appropriate response is submission to the One whose command sustains all.

=26=

The surah reinforces the absolute dominion of Allah over all creation, connecting to the previous verse’s theme of His sustaining command. The verse states simply and profoundly, “And to Him belong all those in the heavens and the earth.

” The word man here refers to all beings, particularly intelligent beings such as angels, humans, jinn, and any other creatures that inhabit the cosmos. Ownership here is not like human possession, which can be transferred or lost. Divine ownership is absolute, originating in creation, sustenance, and final judgment. Everything and everyone belongs to Allah, which means we are all, in our very essence, His servants. There is no room for pride or a sense of self-sufficiency.

The second part of the verse, “all are subject to His Will,” translates the Arabic qānitūn. This word carries meanings of being obedient, devout, humble, and continuously submissive. It implies that the entire universe, willingly or unwillingly, conforms to God’s will. The angels are in constant willing obedience. The celestial bodies follow their ordained paths without deviation. Even those humans and jinn who rebel against God’s commandments cannot escape their ontological state of submission; they exist by His will, they are subject to His physical laws, they will die by His decree, and they will be resurrected by His command. Their rebellion is limited to the moral sphere and is only permitted for a time as a test.

Ultimately, their very rejection of God occurs within the framework of His permission and will. This verse serves as a humbling reminder. When a person recognizes that they belong to God and are inherently subject to Him, the only rational posture is voluntary submission, Islam. Resisting God is resisting one’s own fundamental nature and the reality of the entire cosmos. For the believers in Mecca who were being oppressed, this verse was a source of strength. The oppressors might think they have power, but they are owned by God and entirely subject to His will. Their apparent freedom is temporary.

The verse also connects beautifully to the signs series. After presenting multiple signs of God’s power and wisdom, it draws the conclusion: all these signs point to the fact that everything and everyone belongs to Him and is under His will. In our daily lives, recalling this verse can immediately dispel arrogance and anxiety. If we belong to God, we are under the care of the Most Merciful. If we are subject to Him, our ultimate destiny is safe as long as we remain willingly obedient. The verse is a pure distillation of monotheistic belief and a direct refutation of any notion of independent power.

=27=

This verse restates the core argument for resurrection with an added rational layer. “And He is the One Who originates the creation then will resurrect it.

” The pairing of origination and resurrection is repeated throughout the Quran to establish that denying the second is illogical for anyone who accepts the first. The verse then adds the parenthetical phrase, “which is even easier for Him. ” The Arabic huwā ahwanu ʿalayh means it is easier or more insignificant in relation to Him.

This is based on a simple logical principle: creating something without any prior model is a greater demonstration of power than recreating it from existing materials. For God, however, nothing is difficult; the word “easier” is used to reason with the human mind according to its own categories of understanding. The argument effectively demolishes the disbelievers’ objection that decomposed bodies cannot be brought back. It concedes for the sake of argument that origination might be considered a greater feat, and since God did that, He can certainly do what is less.

The verse then elevates the discourse to a statement of God’s transcendent attributes: “To Him belong the finest attributes in the heavens and the earth.” The Arabic al-mathal al-aʿlā refers to the highest, most sublime description. Everything praiseworthy, every perfection, every quality of beauty and majesty belongs to God in its absolute form. Whatever virtuous qualities creatures possess—knowledge, power, mercy—are but faint, borrowed reflections of His attributes.

Finally, the verse closes with two divine names: “And He is the Almighty, All-Wise.” The Almighty, al-ʿAzīz, the One whose power cannot be resisted, who can originate and resurrect at will. The All-Wise, al-Ḥakīm, whose every action is placed in perfect wisdom. Resurrection is an act of both power and wisdom; it is easy for His power and necessary for His justice. In the flow of the surah, this verse serves as a summary of the theological argument before moving into a parable about shirk. It calls on the listener to reflect that the One with such sublime attributes has no partners, and the act of creation and resurrection belongs exclusively to Him. For modern skeptics who accept the Big Bang or a first cause but deny an afterlife, the same logic applies: if God could initiate existence, why is its renewal considered impossible? The verse invites us to internalize God’s attributes so that our faith becomes rooted in both intellect and awe.

=28=

After presenting cosmic and natural signs, the Quran now employs a powerful rational and social argument against shirk, idolatry. The verse begins, “He sets forth for you an example drawn from your own lives.” This makes the argument deeply personal and immediately accessible. The example is framed as a question: “would you allow some of those bondspeople in your possession to be your equal partners in whatever wealth We have provided you, keeping them in mind as you are mindful of your peers? ” The scenario involves a master and their slaves or servants. The wealth that the master possesses is ultimately from God, but the master manages it. Would a master willingly make their slave a full and equal partner in that wealth, such that they share ownership and decision-making equally, and the master fears the slave’s opinion and treats the slave with the same regard they treat a free peer? The expected answer from any sensible human is a resounding no. A master would never tolerate a slave sharing equally in their property and having the same standing. Now the Quran turns this logic against the idolaters. If you human beings, with your limited sovereignty, refuse to share your wealth equally with your slaves, how can you conceive that Allah, the Almighty Creator, would share His dominion and authority with any of His creation? The idols, saints, or angels that people worship are, after all, God’s creation, utterly dependent on Him. To set them up as partners in worship is far more absurd than the scenario the human master rejects.

The verse concludes, “This is how We make the signs clear for people who understand.” Understanding here is the key. The argument is not based on emotion or blind faith, but on a rational analogy that exposes the inconsistency in the idolater’s position.

The verse also carries a subtle social message about the dignity of human beings and a critique of slavery as an institution that is incompatible with the dignity of free persons, though its primary purpose is theological. In a contemporary context, this verse can be used to dismantle any form of shirk, whether it is the worship of celebrities, ideologies, or one’s own ego. No one would willingly make their subordinate an equal partner in their most intimate affairs, so how can we make created things partners with the Creator in our ultimate devotion? It is a brilliant, time-transcending argument for pure monotheism, grounded in innate human psychology.

=29=

After presenting the logical argument, the Quran identifies the real root of idolatry: it is not based on reason but on unchecked desire. The verse begins with “In fact,” drawing a contrast with the rational clarity that was just presented. “The wrongdoers merely follow their desires with no knowledge.

” The word aẓ-ẓālimūn, the wrongdoers, are those who place things in the wrong place, the most egregious being the act of shirk. Their motivation is not evidence or sound reasoning; it is the blind following of hawā, whims and caprices. This desire could be for social conformity, for the preservation of a comfortable tradition, for the worship of lusts, or for the pride of self-sufficiency.

The phrase “with no knowledge” underscores that they lack true knowledge, ʿilm. Knowledge in the Quran is revelation-based, grounded in truth. So their desires are untethered from divine guidance and lead them into misguidance.

The verse then poses a rhetorical question: “Who then can guide those Allah has left to stray?” The verb aḍalla here means that Allah has caused them to stray as a consequence of their persistent, deliberate choice to follow desires over clear signs. It is a divine law: when a person repeatedly rejects the truth and prefers their desires, God seals their heart, and they are left to stray further. Once God leaves them astray, no human, no false god, no argument can bring them back unless God wills. This does not mean they were originally forced to disbelieve; it is the outcome of their own decision.

The final part, “They will have no helpers,” brings back the theme that on the Day of Judgment, those who relied on false partners will find no assistance. In this life, they might have allies and support, but in the ultimate reckoning, they are alone and defenseless.

For believers, this verse is a stark warning about the danger of prioritizing desires over revelation. It calls for constant self-examination: is my practice of religion based on knowledge from the Quran and Sunnah, or is it driven by cultural habits, emotional whims, or intellectual fashion?

The verse also teaches us not to be overconfident in our own rationality. Left to our own devices, desire can masquerade as logic. We need the anchor of revelation to truly know and be guided. It fosters a humility where we constantly ask God to keep us guided and not to leave us to our own desires even for a moment. This verse, therefore, is both a diagnosis of misguidance and a prescription for seeking divine protection.

=30=

This verse is a profound command to the Prophet and, by extension, to all believers, to adhere to the pure, primordial faith. It begins, “So be steadfast in faith in all uprightness.

” The Arabic fa-aqim wajhaka li-d-dīni ḥanīfā means to set your face firmly and unwaveringly toward the religion, being an upright, pure monotheist. Ḥanīf is the one who turns away from all falsehood and deviates toward the truth. It describes the state of Abraham, who rejected idolatry and submitted wholly to God.

The verse then defines this faith: “the natural Way of Allah which He has instilled in all people.” The word fiṭrah refers to the innate, original disposition with which every human being is created. It is the inborn recognition that there is a Creator and that He alone deserves worship. The Prophet is reported to have said that every child is born upon the fiṭrah, and it is their parents who make them into Jews, Christians, or Magians. This means that monotheism is not an artificial construct; it is the default state of the human soul. The environment and choices then either nurture or corrupt it.

The command continues, “Let there be no change in this creation of Allah.” This phrase can be interpreted in two interconnected ways. First, it is a prohibition against altering the fiṭrah, the natural way, through idolatry and false beliefs. Second, it refers to the creation of Allah, meaning do not alter the religion of God by deviating from it.

The verse emphasizes that this upright faith is the Dīn al-Qayyim, the Straight Way, the path that leads directly to God. “But most people do not know” laments that despite this innate knowledge, the majority are ignorant of it due to their neglect and the veils of social conditioning. This verse has deep contemporary relevance. In a world of competing ideologies, spiritual confusion, and a crisis of identity, the concept of fiṭrah offers a powerful anchor. It teaches that the human being is not a blank slate. The moral and spiritual compass is already installed. Our task is to remove the rust of heedlessness and return to that original state. It also implies that the message of Islam is universal, addressing the deepest nature of every human being, regardless of culture or time. The command to be steadfast in this faith is a call to resist the pressures that pull us away from our primordial truth and to live a life of intentional, pure devotion.

=31=

After establishing the principle of the upright natural faith, the surah outlines the practical behaviors that sustain and express that faith. The verse opens with a direct address to the believers: “Always turn to Him in repentance.

” The Arabic wa-anībū ilayh comes from the root inābah, which implies a continuous, repeated turning back to God. It is more than a one-time repentance; it is a lifestyle of returning. Every time a believer slips, they turn back. Every time they are distracted by the world, they return their focus. This constant return is the mark of a living faith.

The second command, “be mindful of Him,” translates wat-taqūhu, have taqwā, God-consciousness. This is the inner awareness that God is watching, that His commands are to be followed, and His prohibitions are to be avoided. It is the internal fence that protects a person from sin.

The third command, “and establish prayers,” wā aqīmu-ṣ-ṣalāh, brings the inner state of turning and mindfulness into external, disciplined action. The prayer is the physical and spiritual manifestation of the connection with God, performed at fixed times as a constant reminder. These three commands are then sealed with a stern prohibition: “And do not be polytheists.

” The Arabic wa-lā takūnū mina-l-mushrikīn is a clear line in the sand. All the goodness of repentance, taqwā, and prayer is negated if one falls into shirk, associating partners with God.

The sequence is instructive. Turning to God in repentance purifies the past. Taqwā safeguards the present. Prayer sustains the future. And shirk destroys all of it. This verse connects to the previous one by showing that the natural faith, the fiṭrah, must be actively maintained through these deeds. It is not a passive state but a dynamic engagement. For the modern believer, the verse provides a concise formula for spiritual survival in a world that constantly tries to pull us into materialism and distractions. Continuous repentance counters the accumulation of sins. Mindfulness of God provides a filter for the countless decisions of daily life. Establishing prayer creates islands of sacred time and space in a secular environment. And the warning against shirk is a reminder that even subtle forms of idolatry—such as the worship of money, status, ego, or ideologies—can creep in and corrupt pure faith. The verse is a comprehensive guide for building a resilient spiritual life grounded in pure monotheism.

=32=

This verse completes the warning against polytheism by describing a specific type of corruption: religious division. The verse begins with the particle “like,” connecting it to the previous prohibition: do not be like those. The description is “those who have divided their faith and split into sects.

” The Arabic farraqū dīnahum wa kānū shiyaʿan uses two powerful verbs. Farraqū means they tore apart, they divided into fragments, what was meant to be a unified whole. Kānū shiyaʿan means they became sects, groups, parties, each with its own distinct identity, often in conflict with others. The “faith” here is the pristine religion that God revealed, which was originally one. The act of dividing it refers to introducing innovations, altering doctrines, and making divisions over secondary matters until the unity of the believers is shattered.

The verse then adds a chilling detail: “each rejoicing in what they have.” Every sect is pleased with its own fragment, convinced that it alone possesses the truth, celebrating its distinctiveness while condemning others. This self-satisfaction is a barrier to seeking the original, undivided truth. The joy in division is a sign of deep-seated sectarian arrogance. This verse directly addresses the People of the Book, the Jews and Christians, who had split into numerous denominations, but it also serves as a universal warning for the Muslim Ummah. The Prophet warned that the Muslims would also divide into sects, and that only the one following his way and the way of his companions would be saved. The verse thus commands believers to hold fast to the original, unified faith and to avoid the sectarian mindset that fragments the community. In contemporary times, this verse is incredibly relevant. The Muslim world is riven by sects, movements, and groups that often see each other as rivals rather than brothers and sisters in faith. The verse calls for a return to the fundamentals that unite: the Quran, the authentic Sunnah, and the understanding of the early righteous community. It warns against the psychology of partisanship where loyalty is to a group rather than to God and His Messenger. The rejoicing in one’s own group can lead to a blindness that prevents sincere self-criticism. True adherence to the Straight Way requires humility, a constant willingness to align with the truth regardless of which group it comes from, and a strong commitment to the unity of believers.

=33=

The surah now exposes a recurring pattern of human behavior that contradicts pure monotheism. The verse begins by describing a universal human response to distress: “When people are touched with hardship, they cry out to their Lord, turning to Him alone.

” The word ḍurr covers any kind of harm, illness, poverty, danger, or distress. In such moments, the deepest fitrah surfaces. All the false layers of idolatry, arrogance, and self-reliance fall away. A person in a sinking ship, or facing a terminal diagnosis, or in the grip of a natural disaster, instinctively calls out to the One true Creator. They become mukhliṣīn, sincere and devoted exclusively to Him. Their prayers are raw, unfiltered, and purely monotheistic.

This is because, in the depth of crisis, they realize that no idol, no saint, no human being, no material resource can help them. Only the Lord of the universe can save.

The verse then reveals the tragedy: “But as soon as He gives them a taste of His mercy, a group of them associates others with their Lord in worship.” When the crisis passes and God shows them mercy—safety, healing, prosperity—they forget the sincere devotion they had in the moment of need. A group returns to shirk, attributing their deliverance to other causes or resuming the worship of false gods alongside God.

This is the height of ingratitude. The mercy was a direct gift from Allah, yet they use it as an occasion to turn away and share their devotion with what cannot harm or benefit. This verse is not speaking about a hypothetical scenario; it is a recurring historical and personal reality. In our own lives, we may recall moments of intense fear or desperation when our prayers were uniquely sincere.

Yet when life becomes comfortable, the intensity fades, and other “gods”—career, entertainment, ego—creep back into the central place of our concern. The verse serves as a mirror, asking us to examine whether our devotion remains constant or fluctuates based on our material circumstances.

The use of “a group of them” indicates that not all people revert; some remain steadfast in gratitude. But the warning is for the many. It teaches that true faith is proven in times of ease, not just in times of hardship. To worship God during a crisis is natural; to worship Him with the same sincerity when life is good is the mark of a true believer.

=34=

The description of the human failing continues. “Becoming ungrateful for whatever favours We have given them.

” The Arabic li-yakfurū bimā ātaynāhum uses the verb kafarū, which means to cover up, to be ungrateful, to disbelieve. It is the very same root as disbelief. This reveals the deep link between ingratitude and kufr. When God delivers a person from hardship and showers them with favors, the only appropriate response is shukr, gratitude. To respond with ingratitude by associating partners with God is to cover up and deny the favor. They enjoy the blessings but fail to acknowledge the Giver. Instead, they attribute their comfort to their own efforts, luck, or to false deities. This spiritual blindness is an injustice against their own souls.

The verse then delivers a stern, threatening statement: “So enjoy yourselves, for soon you will know.” This is not a permissive command but a severe warning.

The word tamattaʿū means to take enjoyment temporarily. They are being given a short respite.

The phrase “for soon you will know” is an ominous foreshadowing of the consequences they will face, either in this world through a reversal of fortune, or definitively in the Hereafter when they stand before God in judgment. The enjoyment of worldly pleasures is fleeting, and the awakening will be painful. This pattern of a threat following a description of heedlessness is common in the Quran to instill a sense of urgency. The believer is warned not to interpret God’s respite and the continuation of blessings as a sign of approval for ingratitude and sin. In a contemporary setting, many people live in perpetual cycles of crisis and comfort. They pray earnestly during exams, illness, or financial trouble, only to forget God entirely when life is smooth. The enjoyment of wealth, health, and leisure becomes a barrier rather than a means to thankfulness. This verse cautions that such enjoyment is a test, and the end will be known soon enough. The “soon” may refer to the nearness of death, which puts everything into perspective. The verse encourages believers to constantly examine their state: are our blessings leading us to greater gratitude and humility, or to spiritual complacency and subtle shirk? The cultivation of gratitude, shukr, is the antidote to the disease described, and it keeps the heart anchored in monotheism through all conditions of life.

=35=

After describing the irrational behavior of the idolaters, the Quran poses a direct challenge. The verse asks, “Or have We sent down to them an authority which attests to what they associate with Him?

” The word sulṭān, translated as authority, refers to a clear proof, a compelling evidence, or a divinely revealed sanction. The question is rhetorical and sarcastic. It demands: do you have any revelation, any scripture, any authentic communication from God that explicitly permits or commands the worship of idols or the association of partners with Him? The obvious answer is no. God has never sent any book or messenger that validates shirk.

In fact, every single prophet came with the same essential message: worship God alone and avoid false deities. The very scriptures that some of them might claim to follow—such as the Torah or the Gospel—condemn idolatry in the strongest terms. So their polytheism is entirely manufactured, without any heavenly warrant. It is a tradition of their forefathers or a product of their own desires, as the previous verses established.

This verse establishes a fundamental principle in Islam: worship must be based on divine authority. No act of devotion, no belief about God, can be accepted unless it is supported by what He has revealed. Humans do not have the right to invent ways of worship or to declare that certain beings can intercede or share in divinity without proof from God. The challenge reinforces that the burden of proof lies on those who make claims about God. If no such authority exists, their claims are null and void. For the modern context, this verse is a call to question all religious practices and beliefs that have no basis in authentic revelation. It encourages believers to be people of evidence. Innovation in religion, no matter how sincerely practiced, is an act of making claims about God without His permission.

The question also serves as a powerful tool for dawah. When discussing faith with others, one can ask: has God given you a clear revelation that permits what you believe or practice? If not, then it is time to return to the pure monotheism that all prophets taught. The verse leaves no room for baseless religious authority. It places the final say with God and His revealed word, insisting that the only acceptable religion is that which He has ordained and sent down.

=36=

This verse sketches a concise psychological portrait of a certain type of human being who lacks stable faith. It begins, “If We give people a taste of mercy, they become prideful because of it.” The “taste of mercy” can be any blessing: health, wealth, success, knowledge, or relief from hardship. The response described is farḥ, which is an exultant, boastful joy that leads to pride and a sense of self-sufficiency. They attribute the blessing to themselves and become arrogant, forgetting the Giver entirely. Their hearts are not softened by gratitude but hardened by entitlement.

This is a profound observation of how prosperity can corrupt character when God is absent from the equation. The verse then contrasts this with the response to adversity: “But if they are afflicted with an evil for what their hands have done, they instantly fall into despair. ” The “evil” is a hardship, a loss, or a punishment. Significantly, the verse notes that this affliction comes “for what their hands have done,” linking suffering to their own misdeeds.

However, instead of turning to repentance, they fall into qunūṭ, deep despair. They lose all hope in God’s mercy. Their worldview oscillates between two extremes: arrogant joy in good times and hopeless despair in bad times. The believer, by contrast, maintains a balanced state. In blessing, they are grateful and humble, recognizing that it is a test. In hardship, they are patient and hopeful, seeing it as a means of expiation and a call to return to God. The verse highlights that this instability is a natural consequence of being attached to the material world rather than being anchored in God.

The phrase “for what their hands have done” also implies that the despair itself is a spiritual sickness; they cannot see beyond the immediate cause to the wisdom and mercy of God. In modern life, this verse describes what we now call the hedonic treadmill and the prevalence of anxiety and depression in materially prosperous societies. People ride the highs of success only to crash into despair at setbacks. The Quran offers the cure: faith in God’s plan, gratitude in ease, and patience with hope in difficulty. The verse is a reminder to believers to guard against these human tendencies and to cultivate a constant, even-keeled devotion that is not shaken by the changing winds of worldly fortune.

=37=

After exposing the psychological instability of those who lack faith, the verse shifts attention to the broader distribution of provisions as a divine sign. “Have they not seen that Allah gives abundant or limited provisions to whoever He wills?

” The word yabsuṭu means He extends, expands, and gives in abundance. The word yaqdiru means He measures, restricts, and gives in a limited amount. Both are under His will. This simple observation of life’s economic inequalities is transformed into a theological sign. The fact that some people are wealthy while others struggle is not a random outcome of blind forces; it is a deliberate divine apportionment based on wisdom. This system serves multiple purposes: it tests the rich in gratitude and charity, and the poor in patience and trust. It weaves the social fabric so that human beings are interdependent. If everyone were wealthy, the virtues of generosity and sacrifice would have no arena. If everyone were poor, the virtues of patience and reliance would dominate, but other tests would be missing.

The verse then concludes, “Surely in this are signs for people who believe. ” It takes faith to see the wisdom in this distribution. A materialist sees only injustice or luck. A believer sees the hand of a Wise Provider. They understand that wealth is not a sign of God’s pleasure, nor poverty a sign of His displeasure. The true measure is taqwā, not net worth. This perspective liberates believers from envy, frustration, and the rat race mentality. They strive for lawful provision, but their hearts are content with what they receive, knowing it is God’s precise decree. In a world driven by consumerism and visible wealth disparities amplified by social media, this verse is a crucial antidote. It teaches that our rizq, provision, is already apportioned. The abundance of others is a test for us just as our own limited means is a test. The signs are for believers because only faith can turn economic observation into spiritual insight.

The verse also implicitly encourages believers to be agents of God’s mercy by redistributing wealth through charity, thus participating in the divine plan of providing for those whose provision has been constricted. It ties the economic sign to the spiritual reality, affirming that the entire economic system of the world is under God’s control and serves His moral purpose.

=38=

Following the sign of provision, the surah immediately issues a practical command to implement the principle of redistribution and care. The particle fa, “so,” connects the sign to the action: because Allah provides variably, you must give. “Give your close relatives their due.

” The word dha-l-qurbā refers to relatives, especially those who are near in kinship. Their due includes maintaining family ties, providing financial support if they are in need, and general kindness. Islam places enormous emphasis on family bonds. After close relatives, the verse mentions “the poor,” al-miskīn, those who have insufficient means. Then “the needy traveller,” ibn as-sabīl, the wayfarer who is stranded and cut off from resources. This trio represents concentric circles of responsibility: starting from the family, then the local poor, then the transient stranger. The comprehensive nature of this charity is a hallmark of Quranic ethics. The verse states, “That is best for those who seek the pleasure of Allah.

” The word wajh Allah, the face or pleasure of God, is the ultimate motive. The giving is not for show, for reciprocity, or for a tax benefit. It is purely for God’s sake. This sincerity elevates a material transaction into an act of worship.

The verse then declares, “and it is they who will be successful. ” Success, falāḥ, is the ultimate attainment in both this life and the next. Those who give with the right intention will find their wealth blessed, their hearts purified, and their Hereafter secured. In the historical context, the Meccan surahs placed heavy emphasis on social justice and the care of the marginalized. The Muslim community was being shaped into a society of compassion. For contemporary believers, this verse is a direct call to action. It challenges the selfish accumulation of wealth and reminds us that our money has a social function. Giving to family strengthens the primary social unit. Giving to the poor and the traveler addresses systemic inequality and individual crisis. In a world where the gap between rich and poor is ever-widening, the Quranic model of charity is a powerful tool for social healing.

The verse also implies that financial success is redefined: true success is not in hoarding but in giving. Those who give for God’s pleasure are the truly successful, regardless of their balance sheets.

=39=

This verse draws a sharp contrast between two economic behaviors: usury and charity. It begins, “Whatever loans you give, only seeking interest at the expense of people’s wealth will not increase with Allah.

” The word ribā, translated as interest or usury, refers to any excess charged on a loan. The phrase “at the expense of people’s wealth” highlights the exploitative nature of ribā. It is wealth that grows by consuming the resources of others, causing the rich to become richer and the poor to become poorer. The Quran declares that such wealth, even if it appears to multiply numerically, has no blessing and no increase in the sight of Allah. It is devoid of barakah, divine blessing. It may grow in the bank account, but its spiritual value is zero, and its ultimate consequence is loss in the Hereafter unless repented from.

The verse then offers the alternative: “But whatever charity you give, only seeking the pleasure of Allah—it is they whose reward will be multiplied.” Zakāh, here translated as charity, is from the root zakā, meaning to purify and to grow. Charity given sincerely for God’s pleasure purifies the giver’s soul and wealth, and Allah multiplies its reward. The multiplication is both spiritual and, sometimes, material. The hadith states that charity never decreases wealth. This verse lays down a fundamental economic principle in Islam: real growth is not in ribā but in charity. One is a transaction of exploitation, the other a transaction of purification and compassion. The internal motive is crucial: charity must be given seeking the pleasure of Allah, not for show or worldly return.

In the context of the surah, after discussing provision and giving, this verse clarifies the ethics of financial dealings. It was revealed in Mecca before the formal prohibition of ribā, planting the seeds for the later comprehensive ban in Medina. For modern Muslims, living in a global financial system largely built on interest, this verse is a powerful reminder to detest ribā in their hearts, to avoid it to the best of their ability, and to gravitate toward charitable, ethical investments. The verse encourages a mindset shift: true increase is measured by God’s pleasure, not by compound interest. The one who gives charity is promised multiplied reward, an increase that no bank can match. It is a call to trust in divine economy over worldly financial systems.

=40=

The surah reaches a climactic restatement of God’s exclusive power over the entire human lifecycle, directly challenging the idolaters. The verse begins, “It is Allah Who created you.

” This is the starting point: existence itself is a gift from God, an act of creation from nothing. “Then gives you provisions. ” After creating, He sustains. Every breath, every morsel of food, every ability is a continuous provision from Him. “Then will cause you to die.” He is the One who decrees the term of life and brings it to an end. Death is not a random event; it is under His command. “And then will bring you back to life.

” The sequence completes with resurrection. God holds the keys to the entire journey of the soul. After stating these four exclusive divine acts, the verse poses a devastating rhetorical question: “Can any of your associate-gods do any of this?” The challenge is absolute. The idols and false gods cannot create a fly, let alone a human. They cannot provide sustenance; they are themselves sustained. They have no control over death, and they certainly cannot bring anyone back to life.

The question demands a confession of the truth from the idolaters’ own consciences. The verse then concludes with a declaration of tanzīh, transcendence: “Glorified and Exalted is He above what they associate with Him in worship!” Subḥānahū wa taʿālā ʿammā yushrikūn is the formula of declaring God far above any partners.

This is the essence of tawḥīd, pure monotheism. The verse perfectly summarizes the entire argument of the surah so far. It calls on humans to recognize that the One who does all these things for them is the only One worthy of worship. In our daily lives, reciting or reflecting on this verse fortifies faith. It reminds us that our entire existence, from beginning to end, is in God’s hands. It challenges us to direct all our worship, love, and reliance to Him alone. Anything we turn to besides God is utterly incapable of providing the most essential things. This verse is a powerful tool for introspection: if Allah is the one doing all of this for us, how can we ever justify giving our ultimate devotion to anything else? The verse, and indeed the section it closes, anchors the heart in the sublime reality of God’s oneness and complete sufficiency.
