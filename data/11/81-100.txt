=81=

Lot’s moment of despair is met with immediate divine relief. The angels, who had remained silent while Lot faced the mob, now reveal their true identity and the purpose of their visit. The address, “O Lot! We are the messengers of your Lord,” transforms the situation completely. The strangers he feared for are not helpless humans but angels sent by the Lord of the worlds, the very “strong supporter” he had wished for. Their first reassurance, “They will never reach you,” is an absolute promise of protection. The mob, no matter how determined or how numerous, is utterly powerless against the emissaries of divine punishment. This instantly calms Lot’s anxiety and redirects his focus from the immediate danger to the urgent need for escape. The angels then issue a precise command: “So travel with your family in the dark of night.” The timing, “in the dark of night,” is strategic. Under cover of darkness, the believers could leave the city unnoticed by the wicked mob, avoiding a confrontation that might delay or prevent their departure. The instruction is further detailed: “and do not let any of you look back. ” This command was both a physical and a spiritual directive. Physically, looking back might slow their escape or cause them to witness the terrifying descent of the punishment, overwhelming them with fear.

Spiritually, it symbolized a complete severance from the land of sin, a forward-focused departure toward salvation without any lingering attachment to the doomed city. Then comes a painful exception: “except your wife. She will certainly suffer the fate of the others.” Lot’s wife was not a believer; her heart was with the people of the city, and she would betray the plan. The angels explicitly exclude her from the group of the saved, revealing that family ties cannot override the bonds of faith. She is not commanded to come, and her fate is sealed. The angels then give the exact timing of the punishment: “Their appointed time is the morning. Is the morning not near?” This conveys the imminent and unstoppable nature of the decree. The time between the night’s escape and the morning’s destruction was short, a final respite that made the angels’ instruction all the more urgent. For the Prophet Muhammad and the believers, this was a vivid lesson: when Allah’s help arrives, it is decisive, and the moment of separation from the disbelievers is often swift and final, demanding complete obedience without looking back.

=82=

With the believers safely departed, the execution of the divine decree begins. The phrase “When Our command came” signals the arrival of the moment of judgment, the same pattern seen with Noah, ‘Âd, and Thamûd. The nature of the punishment of Lot’s people is uniquely tailored to their unique crimes. “We turned the cities upside down” describes a catastrophic geological upheaval. The entire urban area, possibly the cities of Sodom and Gomorrah, was literally flipped, so that the top became the bottom and the foundations were hurled into the air. This inversion is a profound symbol: they had inverted their natural human dispositions by pursuing men instead of women and by perverting the lawful order of life. So their very land was physically inverted as a reflection of their moral inversion. The punishment did not end with the upheaval.

The verse continues: “and rained down on them clustered stones of baked clay.” “Sijjīl” refers to stones of baked, hardened clay, formed specifically for this purpose. The description “clustered” indicates that these stones came down in groups, dense and relentless. They were not ordinary stones from the earth; they were missiles of divine wrath, each one perhaps marked and designated. This dual punishment—the earth turning beneath them and the sky raining destruction upon them—meant there was no escape. The combined forces from above and below obliterated the entire civilization. The specific mention of baked clay stones carries a significant irony. The people of Lot lived in a region with clay, and they built with it; now their own material becomes the instrument of their ruin, transformed by divine power. This verse demonstrates the completeness of Allah’s retribution: not a single person among the criminals survived. It also serves as a historical precedent for the Quraysh, who lived in similar stone-built houses and passed by the ruins of Lot’s people on their trade routes. The punishment is not a myth; it is a geographic reality, and its testimony remains. The believer learns that no amount of material advancement or urban development can protect a people when they collectively and openly embrace the most corrupt of practices.

=83=

The verse adds a chilling detail about the stones that rained upon the people of Lot. “Marked by your Lord O Prophet” indicates that each stone was specifically designated, carrying a sign from the Lord.

The Arabic word “musawwamah” can mean marked, indicating that these stones were prepared in advance, each assigned for a specific criminal, or that they bore distinguishing marks visible to anyone who saw them. This demonstrates the precision of divine justice; the punishment was not a random rain of debris but a targeted strike, possibly with the name of the individual sinner written upon the stone that was to destroy them. Such a detail emphasizes Allah’s individual accountability; no one was punished for another’s crime, and no one escaped their own due recompense. Some commentators also say the marks indicated that these stones were unlike any earthly rocks; they were unique creations of divine wrath.

The verse then makes a startling pivot, directly addressing the contemporary Meccan audience: “And these stones are not far from the pagan wrongdoers!” The location where the punishment occurred—the Dead Sea area, the route to Syria—was well known to the Quraysh. They passed by the ruined cities during their trade journeys and could see the evidence of the destruction.

The phrase “not far” means both spatially close and temporally relevant; the threat is not some distant, abstract possibility but an imminent danger. The very stones that destroyed the people of Lot are, in their nature and the One who sent them, close to the wrongdoers of Quraysh. If the pagans of Mecca continued their mockery, rejection, and oppression, what happened to the people of Lot could just as easily happen to them.

This is a powerful rhetorical device: turning a historical narrative into a present-day warning. It directly links the stories of the surah to the situation of the Prophet and his opponents, making the lesson immediate and personal. For the believers, it was a reassurance that the same Lord who delivered justice against the worst of sinners is fully capable of delivering it against their own oppressors, and that the respite given to the Quraysh was temporary. The verse shatters any complacency and forces the listener to see the connection between ancient ruins and their own life choices.

=84=

The surah now introduces the fourth prophetic narrative in this sequence, that of Shu’aib and the people of Midian. Midian was a community known for trade and commerce, situated in the region of northwestern Arabia along the trade routes. “Their brother Shu’aib” indicates he was of their lineage, known for his integrity. Shu’aib is often called “the orator of the prophets” due to his eloquent and powerful preaching. He begins with the universal call: “O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him.” Tawḥīd is again the foundation.

However, the specific societal illness of Midian was not only idolatry but also widespread economic corruption. Hence, Shu’aib immediately addresses their distinctive sin: “And do not give short measure and weight.” They were cheating in their commercial transactions, giving less than what was due when they sold and perhaps demanding full measure when they bought. This economic injustice was a violation of the rights of people and a symptom of their lack of faith in the Hereafter. Shu’aib contrasts their current worldly prosperity with the danger of what is to come: “I do see you in prosperity now.

” The word “khayr” means good things, wealth, well-being. They were materially flourishing.

Yet this prosperity was not a sign of divine approval but a test and a passing phase. Shu’aib’s vision pierces beyond the present: “but I truly fear for you the torment of an overwhelming Day.

” The word “muḥīṭ” means encompassing, all-surrounding, a punishment that would encircle and destroy them completely, leaving no escape. This fear was born of love and sincere concern.

The verse establishes that true religion encompasses not only correct belief but also social and economic justice. Cheating in business is not a minor matter; it is a sin that, when systemic, can lead to national destruction. For the Meccans, who were also a trading people, this message was particularly relevant. The surah is teaching that the rejection of the Prophet could manifest in specific societal ills, and those ills, if unrepented, invite the same divine judgment that fell on Midian.

=85=

Shu’aib elaborates on the ethical demands of faith, moving from the negative prohibition to the positive command. “Give full measure and weigh with justice” is a direct call to integrity in business.

The word “awfū” means to fulfill completely, to give in full without any deduction. The phrase “with justice” using “qisṭ” emphasizes that the standard of fairness must be objective and unwavering, not dependent on the customer’s status or one’s own desire for profit.

This command covers all forms of measurement: length, volume, weight, and by extension, any standard of exchange. The believer is to be scrupulously honest, even if it means a smaller profit margin.

The verse then broadens the command: “Do not defraud people of their property.” The word “tabkhasū” means to diminish, to cheat, to deprive people of what is rightfully theirs. This applies not only to commercial transactions but to any form of injustice that causes a person to lose their wealth unjustly: theft, fraud, bribery, monopoly, and exploitation. The economic well-being of a society depends on mutual trust, and when that trust is destroyed by widespread cheating, the social fabric unravels.

The next phrase, “nor go about spreading corruption in the land,” extends the prohibition beyond economic crimes. “Ta‘thaw” implies actively roaming and striving to cause corruption. This includes banditry, violence, moral decay, and any act that disrupts the peace and security of the community. The “land,” arḍ, is the shared home of humanity; it is an amānah, a trust, and those who corrupt it are at war with the order Allah has established. Shu’aib’s message thus integrates worship, economic justice, and social peace into a single comprehensive call. A society that worships Allah alone will naturally uphold justice and shun corruption. A society that abandons tawḥīd will inevitably descend into cheating and chaos. For the Quraysh, this was a mirror: their trade practices and their treatment of the weak, including the early Muslims, would be scrutinized under this same divine standard. The verse is a timeless charter for an ethical economy rooted in faith.

=86=

Shu’aib continues his argument with a profound insight into the psychology of greed and the rewards of honesty. “What is left as a lawful gain by Allah” refers to the profit that remains after giving full measure and dealing justly. The dishonest merchant might think that by cheating he gains extra, but Shu’aib corrects this perception. The wealth that is acquired through lawful, ethical means, even if it appears smaller in quantity, is infused with barakah, divine blessing. It is “far better” because it brings peace of mind, the pleasure of Allah, and eternal reward, while dishonest gains bring only a fleeting material increase that ultimately leads to destruction in this world and the next.

The phrase “by Allah” reminds them that it is Allah who apportions the profit; no amount of cheating can increase what Allah has decreed for a person. Cheating simply turns a lawful provision into an unlawful one, stripping it of blessing. The conditional clause, “if you are truly believers,” is crucial. It suggests that true faith is not merely a claim but a reality that transforms one’s business ethics. If their belief in Allah was real, they would trust His promise and prefer His leftover lawful gain over the tainted gains of fraud.

This is a profound test of iman: can you walk away from an illicit profit because you believe Allah’s reward is better? Shu’aib then concludes with a statement that underscores his role: “And I am not a keeper over you. ” The word “ḥafīẓ” means a guardian or a watcher who controls and records. Shu’aib clarifies that he is not appointed to force them into righteousness or to police their every transaction. His task is to convey the message clearly. The real Keeper over them is Allah, who sees every deceitful act.

This statement is a blend of warning and humility. He places the responsibility squarely on their shoulders: they have heard the truth, and now the choice and its consequences are theirs. For the believers, this teaches that while we must enjoin good and forbid evil, we cannot compel people to be honest. The ultimate accountability belongs to Allah.

The verse also reflects the profound pastoral wisdom of Shu’aib: he appeals to their faith, their reason, and their fear of Allah, then steps back to let the message work in their hearts.

=87=

The response of the people of Midian is dripping with sarcasm and mockery, much like the reactions of the previous nations. They address Shu’aib with feigned politeness that barely conceals their contempt. “Does your prayer command you that we should abandon what our forefathers worshipped” is a deliberate attempt to belittle his message.

The word “ṣalātuka,” your prayer, is used derisively, as if to say: “Is this what your praying has led you to—demanding we overturn our entire ancestral religion? ” They saw his devotion as a source of fanaticism. The demand to abandon the forefathers’ gods struck at the core of their cultural identity, the very argument used by every disbelieving nation in the surah. They then connect the religious demand to an economic one: “or give up managing our wealth as we please?” This exposes the heart of their objection. They saw the prohibition of cheating and corruption as an infringement on their personal freedom to do whatever they wished with their property. They viewed wealth as an absolute right, with no moral strings attached.

The phrase “as we please” reveals their worldview: they were the ultimate arbiters of right and wrong, and any restriction from a prophet was an unacceptable intrusion. Their sarcasm peaks with the final line: “Indeed, you are such a tolerant, sensible man! ” The words “ḥalīm” and “rashīd” are used mockingly. Shu’aib was known before prophethood for these very qualities—patience and wisdom—and now they throw his own noble traits back at him as insults. They imply that he has lost his mind, that someone so formerly sensible could not possibly believe what he is now preaching unless he had become deluded. This mockery is a defense mechanism; by ridiculing the messenger, they avoid engaging with the substance of his message.

The verse teaches that when the truth threatens people’s cherished desires—whether ancestral traditions or economic freedom—the response is often mockery. It also prepares the believer to expect such treatment, as the most respected person can become the most ridiculed when they stand for truth. Shu’aib’s response in the next verses will demonstrate patience and steadfastness.

=88=

Shu’aib’s reply is a model of prophetic character: calm, logical, and deeply sincere. He ignores their sarcasm and appeals to their reason with the same phrasing used by Noah, Hûd, and Ṣâliḥ: “Consider if I stand on a clear proof from my Lord.” He invites them to objectively consider the possibility that he is a true prophet with a divine commission, and that his teaching is not his own invention but a bayyinah, a clear evidence from Allah. He then adds a personal note: “and He has blessed me with a good provision from Him. ” Shu’aib acknowledges that Allah has given him rizq ḥasan, a beautiful and lawful provision.

This is significant because it refutes any suspicion that he was preaching against economic injustice out of envy or a desire to seize their wealth. He himself had a good, divinely-given provision, so his call was not motivated by greed. He then makes a crucial statement of integrity: “I do not want to do what I am forbidding you from. ” He is not a hypocrite who preaches one thing and practices another. His own hands are clean; he does not cheat in his own dealings.

This is an essential quality of a true caller to righteousness: the message must be embodied in the messenger first. Next, he clarifies his goal: “I only intend reform to the best of my ability. ” His ambition is not power or control but iṣlāḥ, the sincere desire to set things right in society.

The phrase “to the best of my ability” acknowledges human limitations; he will do everything within his capacity, but the results are not solely in his hands. He then attributes all potential success to Allah: “My success comes only through Allah.” Tawfīq, the divine facilitation and success, is not from one’s own cleverness or effort; it is a gift from Allah.

This is followed by the twin pillars of reliance: “In Him I trust and to Him I turn.” Tawakkul, trust, for the future, and inābah, returning, for the past and present. This speech is a comprehensive manifesto of prophetic preaching: grounded in clear proof, accompanied by personal integrity, aimed at reform, dependent on Allah alone for success, and maintained through trust and repentance. It is a template for every Muslim who seeks to call others to goodness in any capacity.

=89=

After establishing the sincerity of his mission, Shu’aib delivers a direct and terrifying historical warning. He calls his people to learn from the ruins of the past rather than repeat their mistakes. “O my people! Do not let your opposition to me lead you to a fate similar to that of the people of Noah, or Hûd, or Ṣâliḥ. ” The word “shiqāq” for opposition implies deep enmity and schism, a deliberate setting of oneself against the truth. Shu’aib warns that their hostile stance toward him, if continued, will bring upon them the same catastrophic punishments that wiped out those earlier nations. The list of names is not random; the surah has just recounted these stories in vivid detail. Noah’s people were drowned, ‘Âd were destroyed by the wind, Thamûd by the blast, and the Midianites were geographically and temporally positioned to be familiar with these histories. Shu’aib is essentially saying: you have seen the ruins, you have heard the tales; will you not take heed before you become another tale of destruction? Then he adds a particularly potent reference: “And the people of Lot are not far from you.” This could mean they were not distant in time; the destruction of Lot’s people was relatively recent in historical memory. Or it could mean they were not far in location; the Dead Sea region was within their trade route area, and they could pass by the overturned cities. Or it could mean they were not far in terms of morality; the corruption of Midian, though different in specifics, was rooted in the same rejection of Allah and His messenger.

The phrase “not far” resonates with the earlier verse that said the stones were “not far” from the Quraysh. It creates a chain of warnings stretching from the ancient past to the immediate present. The lesson is that divine punishment is not a remote legend; it is a near reality that can strike those who persist in the same patterns of sin. For the Quraysh listening to this recitation, the connection was inescapable: Shu’aib’s warning to Midian was also Muhammad’s warning to Mecca.

=90=

Despite the severe warning, Shu’aib immediately opens the door of mercy, following the consistent prophetic pattern of balancing threat with invitation. “So seek your Lord’s forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance.

” This is the same call given by Noah, Hûd, Ṣâliḥ, and earlier in the surah to the Meccans. It demonstrates that no matter how grave the sins or how persistent the rejection, the path back to Allah remains open as long as the punishment has not yet arrived. Istighfār and tawbah are the lifeboats in the storm of divine wrath. Shu’aib’s people, like all others, are urged to seize this opportunity before it is too late.

The verse then concludes with a beautiful and particularly intimate description of Allah: “Surely my Lord is Most Merciful, All-Loving.” The name “Raḥīm” indicates an ongoing, continuous mercy.

The name “Wadūd” is especially striking. It means the One who loves His righteous servants and is beloved to them, the One whose affection is constant and who places love in the hearts of the believers.

This is the only time in the surah that this attribute is mentioned, and it serves to soften the harshness of the warnings that dominate the chapter. Shu’aib is telling his people that the Lord to whom he invites them is not a tyrant eager to punish but a loving, merciful being who wishes to forgive and embrace them. The presence of Wadūd at this point in the narrative is profoundly significant. It tells us that behind the thunder of the warnings, the heart of the divine message is love. The Lord who destroys the unrepentant criminals is the same Lord who showers His love on those who turn to Him. For the Meccans, this verse was a gentle whisper after the loud roars of the preceding stories. It invited them to experience the love of Allah if they would only repent. For all believers, it is a reminder that no sin is too great for the Most Merciful, All-Loving Lord, as long as one returns with a sincere heart.

=91=

The response of the people of Midian to this merciful invitation is a chilling escalation from sarcasm to direct threats and social intimidation. They begin by dismissing his message with deliberate obtuseness: “We do not comprehend much of what you say.

” This is not an admission of intellectual difficulty but a way of saying, “Your words are nonsensical to us; we do not consider them worth understanding.” It is a willful refusal to engage, a closing of the mind. Then they move to belittlement: “and surely we see you powerless among us.

” The word “ḍa‘īf” means physically weak or socially insignificant. Shu’aib likely had no large armed following and was not a wealthy chief, so they judged him by worldly standards and found him lacking. In their eyes, a true messenger should have been a mighty king, not a humble, gentle man. This judgment based on worldly power mirrors exactly the arguments of the Quraysh against the Prophet. Next comes the explicit threat: “Were it not for your clan, we would have certainly stoned you.” Shu’aib’s clan, though perhaps not as powerful as some, still provided him with tribal protection according to the customs of the time. The Midianites admitted that the only thing holding them back from murdering him was the fear of clan retaliation. This exposes the complete failure of the message to soften their hearts; they would kill him if they could get away with it.

Finally, they deliver the ultimate insult: “for you are nothing to us.” They reduce his entire prophetic mission, his wisdom, and his righteousness to zero.

This is the peak of arrogance: a prophet of Allah standing before them, offering them eternal salvation, and they declare that he holds absolutely no value in their sight. This verse reveals the dark depths of human pride when confronted with a truth that demands the sacrifice of wealth, tradition, and ego. The believers were learning that such rejection, however painful, was the predictable path of the prophets.

The mention of clan protection also prefigures the role of Abu Talib’s protection for the Prophet Muhammad.

=92=

Shu’aib’s response cuts through their threat with a profound moral and theological question. He seizes upon their own admission that they only refrained from stoning him due to respect for his clan. He asks: “O my people! Do you have more regard for my clan than for Allah? ” This is a devastating exposure of their scale of values. They fear the retaliation of a human tribe, yet they do not fear the punishment of the Almighty Creator. They show respect, caution, and restraint out of concern for worldly consequences, but they show no respect, caution, or restraint toward the Lord of all worlds.

This is an irrational inversion of priorities that reveals the fundamental flaw in their worldview: the seen and the temporal overrides the unseen and the eternal. Shu’aib’s question is designed to wake them up to this absurdity.

The phrase “turning your back on Him entirely” adds another layer. Not only do they fear the clan more, but their entire posture toward Allah is one of complete disregard and abandonment. They have cast Allah behind their backs as if He is irrelevant to their daily lives and decisions. Shu’aib then grounds his argument in a statement of divine surveillance: “Surely my Lord is Fully Aware of what you do.

” The word “muḥīṭ” means encompassing, having complete knowledge and control. While they think their threats and their secret intentions are hidden, Allah surrounds their actions with perfect awareness.

This is a warning and a comfort: a warning to them that their evil is recorded and will be recompensed, and a comfort to Shu’aib that his Lord sees his steadfastness and will deal with the oppressors. This verse teaches the believer to always recalibrate their fears: the fear of Allah should always outweigh the fear of human beings. When we make decisions based on what people might do rather than what Allah has commanded, we are essentially treating the clan, the boss, the society, as more significant than Allah. Shu’aib’s courageous stance is a timeless example of prioritizing divine awareness over human power.

=93=

After exhausting every gentle argument and facing only threats and mockery, Shu’aib now adopts a tone of finality and resolve. “O my people! Persist in your ways, for I too will persist in mine.

” This is not a surrender or an endorsement of their ways, but a declaration that the time for debate has ended. He is saying: if you are determined to continue your path of disbelief, cheating, and corruption, then go ahead—but know that I am equally determined to continue my path of worship, integrity, and warning.

The word “persist” implies a steady, ongoing action. The division between truth and falsehood is now absolute, and each party will see the outcome of their chosen path. He then introduces the test of time: “You will soon come to know who will be visited by a humiliating torment and is a liar! ” This is a direct challenge. The truth will be revealed by the outcome. Either Shu’aib is a true messenger, in which case the torment will descend upon the disbelievers, or he is a liar, in which case he will face the consequences of his fabrication. The description “humiliating torment” underscores the disgrace that accompanies the punishment, a humiliation that is the opposite of the honor they think they hold.

The word “liar” echoes their earlier mockery of him; he throws the word back, saying that the future will prove who the true liar is. Finally, he says: “And watch! I too am watching with you.

” This is a mutual waiting. They will watch for his ruin, and he will watch for the descent of divine judgment. This phrase is similar to what the Prophet was commanded to say later: “Say to those who disbelieve, ‘Persist in your ways; we will certainly persist in ours. And wait! Surely we too are waiting.’” It marks the transition from active dawah to the stage where the messenger leaves the people to their choice and awaits the divine decree. For the believers, it is a lesson that there comes a point when further argument is fruitless, and the matter must be entrusted entirely to Allah, whose judgment will soon make the truth unmistakably clear.

=94=

The pattern that has dominated the surah now reaches its conclusion for the people of Midian. “When Our command came” echoes the identical phrasing used for Noah, ‘Âd, Thamûd, and Lot. The divine decree of punishment, which Shu’aib had warned about for so long, finally arrived. The verse immediately pivots to the salvation of the believers: “We saved Shu’aib and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us. ” The rescue is not attributed to their own strength or cleverness but solely to a raḥmah, a mercy from Allah. This underscores that the distinction between the saved and the destroyed is not ethnic or economic but purely a matter of faith and divine grace. The believers were a minority, likely poor and powerless like the followers of other prophets, but they were the recipients of the mercy that mattered most. The nature of the punishment is then specified: “And the mighty blast overtook the wrongdoers. ” The word “ṣayḥah” is the same used for Thamûd—a thunderous, terrifying cry or explosion that shattered them. It “overtook” them, meaning it seized them suddenly, giving them no time to flee or repent. The description “so they fell lifeless in their homes” is a repeated refrain, creating a powerful rhythmic connection between the fates of Thamûd and Midian.

The term “jāthimīn” describes them as fallen on their knees or faces, motionless, as if struck down in an instant. Their homes, the centers of their economic schemes, their cheating, and their mocking conversations, became their graves. There is a profound irony: they were so attached to their wealth and property, yet those very homes could not protect them from a single divine blast. For the Quraysh, another trading community, this was a terrifying preview. Their prosperity did not insulate Midian, and it would not insulate Mecca. The verse reinforces that Allah’s punishment is consistent in its pattern, and that the righteous are always safely separated before the doom descends. It is a reminder that the believer’s ultimate security lies not in their surroundings but in their connection to the Lord who controls the blast.

=95=

The aftermath of the punishment is described in language identical to the conclusion of Thamûd’s story, deliberately linking the two. “As if they had never lived there” conveys the total erasure of their civilization. The bustling markets, the weighing scales, the fine homes, the confident merchants—all of it vanished as if it had been a fleeting dream. No trace of their daily life remained except the empty shells of their dwellings. This phrase is profoundly moving; it reduces all human pride and achievement to nothing in a single moment.

The verse then delivers the divine verdict: “So away with Midian as it was with Thamûd!” The word “bu‘dan,” away with them, is a curse of distance and expulsion from mercy. The explicit comparison “as it was with Thamûd” ties the two stories together, showing that the same crime—denial of the messenger and persistence in corruption—leads to the same outcome. It also creates a powerful cumulative effect in the surah: the listener has now witnessed the destruction of Noah’s people, ‘Âd, Thamûd, Lot’s people, and Midian, all within a few dozen verses.

The repetition of the same pattern with slight variations in the method of punishment drives home the certainty of divine justice. For the Quraysh, this explicit coupling of Midian and Thamûd was a warning: the ruins of Thamûd were known to them in the north, and Midian was also in their trade route memory. These were not distant legends but local, verifiable histories.

The verse also teaches that nations are not destroyed arbitrarily; there is a divine law, a sunnah, that governs the rise and fall of civilizations. When economic corruption, mockery of prophets, and idolatry become entrenched and are met with final rejection, the society reaches a point of no return. The curse “away with” signifies not just physical destruction but a spiritual and historical infamy that continues to be recited in the Quran until the Day of Judgment.

For believers, it reinforces that the consequences of rejecting reform can be absolute, and that the only true permanence is in the Hereafter.

=96=

The surah now transitions to a more condensed account of Moses, perhaps the most frequently mentioned prophet in the Quran. “Indeed, We sent Moses with Our signs and compelling proof” introduces the mission of Mūsā.

The word “signs,” āyāt, refers to the numerous miracles that Moses was granted: the staff turning into a serpent, the shining hand, the splitting of the sea, and the other nine signs mentioned in other surahs. These were clear and undeniable supernatural events that demonstrated his prophethood.

The phrase “compelling proof,” sulṭān mubīn, indicates an authority or a manifest power that left no room for reasonable doubt. Unlike some previous prophets who had fewer tangible miracles, Moses was equipped with a powerful array of visible signs that directly challenged the magicians and the entire system of Pharaoh.

The mention of Moses here, after the series of Arabian prophets, broadens the historical scope of the surah to include the greatest confrontation between a prophet and a worldly empire. It also connects the Arabian experience to the Israelite experience, showing that the struggle between monotheism and tyranny is universal. For the Quraysh, the story of Moses and Pharaoh was particularly relevant. The Quraysh, like Pharaoh, were a powerful elite who oppressed a weaker community of believers. The believers in Mecca, like the Children of Israel, were a minority suffering persecution. The message is clear: just as Moses was sent with signs, and Pharaoh’s rejection led to his destruction, so too Muhammad is sent with the greatest sign—the Quran—and the rejection of the Quraysh will lead to their downfall. This verse is brief because Moses’ story is told extensively elsewhere, but its placement here is strategic. It follows the stories of the destroyed Arab nations to show that the pattern extends to the great empires. The signs given to Moses were tailored to the Egyptian context, just as the signs given to Muhammad were tailored to the Arabian context. The principle is the same: divine messengers come with undeniable proof, but the tyrants choose arrogance over submission.

=97=

The verse specifies the target audience of Moses’ mission: “to Pharaoh and his chiefs.” Pharaoh, Fir‘awn, is the archetypal tyrant in the Quran, a ruler who claimed divinity and enslaved an entire people. The “chiefs” were his court, the elite class that supported his regime and benefited from the oppression. They were the equivalent of the mala’ of the earlier stories—the powerful few who held sway over the masses. The verse highlights a critical sociological phenomenon: “but they followed the command of Pharaoh.” The chiefs and the people under them chose to obey the tyrant rather than the messenger with clear proofs.

This is the recurring tragedy of nations; they submit to the arrogance of a human ruler whose power seems immediate and tangible, and they reject the call of a prophet whose authority is unseen but true. The phrase “followed the command of Pharaoh” is loaded with meaning; it is not that they were forced, but they actively aligned their will with his, becoming complicit in his tyranny. Then the verse delivers a devastating assessment of Pharaoh’s leadership: “and Pharaoh’s command was not well guided.

” The word “rashīd” means guided, sensible, leading to right results. Pharaoh’s entire system of rule, his policies, his decrees, were based on falsehood, delusion, and ego. His command was not just a little misguided; it was utterly devoid of any true guidance.

This is the exact opposite of the prophetic path, which is always “well guided” by divine revelation. By following Pharaoh, the chiefs were following a path leading straight to ruin in this world and the next. For the Meccan chiefs like Abu Jahl, this was a prophetic mirror. They were the mala’ being called by Muhammad, and they had a choice: follow the guidance of the messenger or follow their own arrogant commands, which were the equivalent of Pharaoh’s misguided leadership.

The verse teaches that the quality of leadership is measured not by worldly power but by whether it aligns with divine guidance. A leader who is not guided by revelation is, by definition, not “well guided,” and those who follow him share in his fate.

=98=

This verse shifts from the worldly destruction of Pharaoh to his role on the Day of Resurrection, adding an eschatological dimension that deepens the tragedy of his followers. “He will be before his people on the Day of Judgment” means that Pharaoh will walk at the head of his nation as they are driven toward their final destination.

The word “yaqdumuhum” suggests he will be their leader and frontrunner, just as he led them in this world, but now he is leading them to a place of unimaginable horror. This is a devastating irony: they followed his command in the world, and now they will follow him into the Fire. The leadership they trusted will become the cause of their eternal doom.

The verse then declares: “and will lead them into the Fire.” He will not just point the way; he will enter with them and bring them in.

This is the ultimate consequence of blind following without discernment. Those who surrender their moral responsibility to a tyrant will share his punishment.

The Quran then pauses to comment: “What an evil place to be led into!” The word “wird” refers to a watering place, a destination that one arrives at seeking refreshment. Here, the destination is not a cool spring but the scorching Fire. The exclamation is a divine expression of disgust and warning. It jolts the listener to consider the final outcome of submitting to misguided leadership. For the Meccans, this verse served as a stark warning about following Abu Jahl and the other chiefs who were leading the opposition to the Prophet. Those leaders were the Pharaohs of Mecca, and their followers would one day be led by them into a punishment far worse than any worldly defeat.

The verse also teaches the principle that on the Day of Judgment, the leaders of misguidance will not be able to protect their followers; they will only lead them deeper into destruction. It is a call to every individual to examine whom they are following and to ensure that their leader is one who follows divine guidance, not one who issues misguided commands. The verse beautifully encapsulates the surah’s theme of accountability, connecting the historical narrative directly to the eternal consequences.

=99=

The final verdict on Pharaoh and his followers is summarized with a phrase that resonates with the earlier fates of ‘Âd and Thamûd. “They were followed by a curse in this life” means that a la‘nah, a state of being expelled from divine mercy, attached itself to them in the world. This worldly curse manifests in several ways: they were drowned in the sea, their bodies were preserved as a sign for later generations, and their memory is eternally associated with tyranny and disbelief. Whenever the story of Pharaoh is recited, he is cursed and condemned.

This is a living curse that continues as long as the Quran is read. The verse then adds: “and will receive another on the Day of Judgment.” The curse is renewed and intensified in the Hereafter, where they will face the full consequences of their crimes—the Fire, the humiliation, and the eternal separation from Allah’s mercy. This doubling of the curse ensures that they are punished both in the transient world and in the permanent world.

The verse concludes with the exclamation: “What an evil gift to receive!” The word “rifd” refers to a gift or a provision, often something given to a traveler. The irony is biting: they are presented with a “gift,” but it is the most horrific gift imaginable. The curse is their provision, their appointed share. This echoes the earlier verse about the brief enjoyment followed by painful punishment. The Quran frequently uses such ironic contrasts to emphasize the deceptive nature of worldly success. The Quraysh, who were enjoying a “gift” of prosperity and power, were being warned that if they continued on Pharaoh’s path, their ultimate gift would be a curse in this life and the next. This verse serves as a powerful rhetorical conclusion to the series of prophetic stories, encapsulating the surah’s message: the temporary gains of tyranny and disbelief are nothing compared to the eternal curse that follows.

For the believer, it is a reminder to value the gifts that truly matter—the mercy and blessings of Allah—rather than the “evil gifts” of this fleeting world.

=100=

After concluding the condensed reference to Moses and Pharaoh, the surah pauses to reflect on the entire sequence of narratives that have been presented. Allah addresses the Prophet directly: “These are accounts, We relate to you O Prophet, of the destroyed cities.

” The word “anbā’,” accounts or news, refers to the detailed stories of Noah, ‘Âd, Thamûd, Lot, Midian, and Pharaoh. These are not fictional tales but historical reports of real communities that were wiped out due to their rejection of truth.

The phrase “We relate to you” emphasizes that the Prophet’s knowledge of these events comes solely from divine revelation, as he was an unlettered man with no access to such histories. This confirms his prophethood and the Quran’s divine origin.

The verse then categorizes the physical remains of these cities: “Some are still standing barren, while others have been mowed down.” The word “qā’im” means standing, erect, but with the connotation of being barren, lifeless, like a standing skeleton. This describes cities whose structures, walls, or pillars are still partially visible, such as the ruins of Thamûd’s rock-cut dwellings or the remnants of Pharaoh’s monuments. Their empty shells stand as mute witnesses to the destruction.

The word “ḥaṣīd,” mowed down, evokes the image of a harvested crop, cut down and leveled to the ground, leaving no trace. This describes cities that were completely obliterated, like the overturned cities of Lot, submerged or reduced to indistinguishable debris. This variation in the physical remnants serves a purpose: regardless of whether the ruins stand as a visible warning or have been erased, the lesson remains accessible through the Quranic recitation. For the Quraysh, this was a direct address. They passed by some of these ruins on their journeys, and now they were hearing the authentic version of what happened. The verse invites them to reflect on these physical and recited signs before it is too late. It also tells the Prophet that these stories are a source of strength and confirmation; he is not alone in his struggle, but is part of a long divine tradition.
