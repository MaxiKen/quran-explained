=21=

The surah continues to unfold the final outcome of those who fabricated lies against Allah and hindered others from His path. The phrase “It is they who have ruined themselves” uses a profound Quranic concept of self-loss, khusrān.

This is not merely a material loss but the eternal bankruptcy of the soul. Imagine a trader who starts with the priceless capital of a pure soul, a lifetime of opportunities, and clear guidance, yet ends with nothing but wrath because he traded faith for fleeting desires. This ruination is entirely self-inflicted; Allah did not wrong them, but they wronged their own selves by rejecting truth after it became clear. They bartered away Paradise for a few moments of pride or a few coins of worldly gain.

The second part of the verse, “and whatever gods they fabricated will fail them,” exposes the utter helplessness of the false deities they invented and worshipped. These fabricated gods include idols of stone, human leaders followed in disobedience to Allah, or even personal whims elevated to the status of a deity. On the Day of Judgment, these gods will distance themselves from their worshippers, as stated in other Quranic passages. The idols will be utterly absent, unable to hear, see, or intercede. The tyrants who demanded worship will disown their followers. Even the desires people obeyed will vanish, leaving them with nothing but regret.

This is a direct development from the earlier verse that spoke of multiplied punishment; here the focus is on the internal devastation—the soul’s realization that it has destroyed its own chance at eternal bliss. The word “fabricated” suggests that these gods were never real; they were merely imaginations and lies projected onto wood, stone, or human figures. So the failure is guaranteed because falsehood has no power. For the believers hearing this, it provides immense comfort. The gods of the Quraysh—the idols around the Kaaba—would utterly fail their worshippers, and the believers would witness the collapse of the very system that persecuted them. This verse therefore ties the doom of the individual wrongdoer to the collapse of the whole apparatus of false worship, preparing the way for the clear contrast that follows in the next few verses.

=22=

After establishing that the fabricators of lies have ruined themselves, this concise verse drives the point home with an absolute and terrifying certainty. “Without a doubt” removes any ambiguity.

The Arabic construction is an emphatic affirmation, leaving no room for the listener to imagine a different outcome. The phrase “they will be the worst losers” translates “al-akhsarūna a‘mālan,” indicating that they are the most severe in terms of loss, not just among human beings but in the entire scale of possible losses. Why are they the worst losers? Because they lost the greatest possible gain—eternal happiness in Paradise and the vision of their Lord—and in exchange received the worst possible outcome—the unending punishment of the Fire. A person who loses money in a business might recover; a person who loses health may still have hope; but a person who loses his eternal soul has suffered a loss from which there is no recovery and no compensation. The loss is measured against the tremendous opportunity that life represented. Allah gave them senses, intellect, a revealed Book, and messengers to guide them, yet they misused all of it. The addition “in the Hereafter” specifies the arena of this loss. In the worldly life they might have appeared as winners, accumulating wealth, followers, and power. The disbelieving chiefs mocked Noah and the believers, and the Quraysh leaders enjoyed high social standing while the Prophet and his companions faced boycott and hardship. But the real scale of profit and loss is only visible on the Day of Resurrection. There, the apparent winners will be seen as utterly bankrupt, and the apparent losers will be the truly successful. This verse acts as a corrective lens for the believer, training the heart to evaluate everything by its eternal consequences rather than its immediate appearance. It also serves as the final word on the fate of those described in verses 18 and 19, connecting all the previous warnings to this ultimate declaration of ruin, before the surah turns to the contrasting portrait of the successful.

=23=

Having painted the dark picture of the worst losers, the surah now balances it with the radiant portrait of the ultimate winners. The verse opens with “Surely those who believe,” grounding success immediately in iman, the sincere faith that resides in the heart and is testified by the tongue. This faith is not a vague sentiment; it is the firm belief in Allah, His messengers, His books, His angels, the Last Day, and the divine decree. Then the description adds “do good,” combining inner belief with outward action. Righteous deeds are the necessary fruit of genuine faith, encompassing prayers, charity, honesty, kindness to others, and all forms of obedience. A faith that does not produce good deeds is, as the scholars say, like a tree without fruit.

The third and particularly beautiful quality mentioned is “and humble themselves before their Lord.” The Arabic word used is “akhbatū,” which comes from a root meaning a low, soft, easy plain of land. Just as that land is subdued and receptive to water, the believing heart is subdued, soft, and fully surrendered before its Creator. This humility, khushū‘ and ikhbāt, is the essence of worship. It means the heart is not arrogant, does not rebel against commands, and accepts the truth with tranquillity. These three qualities—faith, righteous action, and heartfelt humility—are a complete description of the ideal believer. The reward is exactly opposite to the loss of the previous group: “will be the residents of Paradise. They will be there forever.” They are not merely visitors; they are permanent residents, asḥāb al-jannah, its companions and rightful inhabitants. The eternity of their stay matches the eternity of the losers’ punishment, but the difference in quality is beyond imagination.

This verse also subtly connects to the stories of the prophets that follow. Noah and the few believers with him embodied these exact qualities: they believed in the face of mockery, they did good by boarding the ark in obedience, and they humbled themselves before their Lord.

Thus, the surah establishes the universal rule of success and failure before illustrating it through the lives of the messengers, making the listener eager to hear how these principles played out in human history.

=24=

To make the contrast between the losers and the winners vivid and unforgettable, Allah presents a powerful parable. “The example of these two parties” refers directly to the disbelieving wrongdoers on one side and the believing, humble doers of good on the other. They are likened to “the blind and the deaf, compared to the seeing and the hearing.” The blind person is one who cannot perceive light, and the deaf person cannot perceive sound.

Spiritually, the disbelievers have rendered themselves incapable of seeing the signs of Allah in creation and in the Quran, and incapable of hearing the truth with acceptance. They have eyes, but they do not look with reflection; they have ears, but they do not listen with the intent to be guided.

This is entirely in line with the earlier verse that stated their punishment is multiplied because “they failed to hear or see the truth.” The blindness and deafness here are not an original condition that Allah forced upon them; they are the result of their own persistent turning away, which led to a seal upon their faculties.

In contrast, the believers are the seeing and the hearing. Their hearts’ eyes are open to the signs of Allah in the horizon and within themselves. Their ears are receptive to the recitation of the Quran, which increases their faith and humility. They perceive the reality of the Hereafter as clearly as a healthy eye sees the sun. The rhetorical question, “Can the two be equal?” expects an emphatic negation. No reasonable person would equate a blind, deaf man with a seeing, hearing man, just as no one should equate a disbeliever destined for the Fire with a believer destined for Paradise.

The verse ends with an appeal: “Will you not then be mindful?” This invitation to tadhakkur, to remembrance and reflection, is a call to awaken from heedlessness. The parables of the Quran are meant to be bridges that carry abstract truths into concrete understanding. This parable serves as a perfect summary before the surah launches into the detailed story of Noah, where the seeing and hearing believers and the blind and deaf disbelievers will play out their roles in real time. The stage is set for the historical narratives that follow.

=25=

With this verse, the surah transitions from the general principles of guidance and misguidance into the first of the detailed prophetic narratives that form its core. The opening phrase “Surely We sent Noah to his people” uses the plural of majesty, “We,” emphasizing the divine authority behind the mission. Noah, Nūḥ, is one of the greatest messengers, the first sent to a people who had fallen into widespread idolatry after generations of monotheism following Adam. His story is foundational, and the Quran recounts it repeatedly, each time highlighting different aspects suitable to the context. Here, in Surah Hud, the emphasis is on the clear warning and the bitter rejection he faced, which mirrors the experience of the Prophet Muhammad in Mecca.

The phrase “to his people” reminds us that Noah was one of them; he was not a stranger but their brother in lineage, known for his truthfulness for centuries before prophethood. This made their rejection even more inexcusable. Noah announces his mission with clarity: “Indeed, I am sent to you with a clear warning. ” The word “indeed” adds emphasis and seriousness. The warning, nadhār, is clear, unambiguous, and leaves no room for confusion. It is a warning about the consequences of their idolatry and immorality, a warning that this life is not the end and that a painful punishment awaits if they do not return to the worship of Allah alone. The clarity of the warning is a recurring theme; Allah never punishes a people without sending a messenger who explains the truth in their own language, removing any excuse. Noah’s long life, some reports say 950 years of calling, was a continuous delivery of this clear warning. The verse thus introduces the pattern that will be repeated for Hûd, Ṣâliḥ, Lot, and Shu‘aib: a messenger from among the people, bringing a clear divine warning, and meeting with arrogant rejection from the elite. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a consolation; he was not the first to be rejected, and the path of Noah was his path. The believers too could see their own small community reflected in the few followers of Noah, strengthening their patience.

=26=

The core content of Noah’s clear warning is presented here in its purest form. “That you should worship none but Allah” is the uncompromising message of tawḥīd, the absolute oneness of God. Noah’s people had originally been upon the worship of Allah alone, but over time, as the hadith of Ibn Abbas explains, righteous men died, and Satan inspired the people to erect statues in their memory, which later generations began to worship. So the central call was to abandon those idols and return to the exclusive worship of the One who created them.

This is exactly the same call that the Prophet Muhammad was commanded to deliver at the start of this surah: “Worship none but Allah.” The continuity of the prophetic message across millennia is a powerful proof of its divine origin. Noah then adds the emotional and compelling element: “I truly fear for you the torment of a painful Day.

” The word “truly” shows that his fear is not feigned or exaggerated; it is a deep, sincere concern for their wellbeing. A warner who does not genuinely fear for his people would be a cold transmitter of information, but Noah’s heart ached for them. He feared a “painful Day,” which in the immediate context referred to the punishment that would come if they persisted—both the great flood in this world and the eternal torment of the Hereafter. His fear was not for his own safety but for theirs. This reveals the immense mercy and compassion of the prophets. They were not seeking power or revenge; they were like a man shouting to warn a caravan heading toward a cliff. The fact that his people responded with mockery and threats did not diminish his love for them.

This verse teaches that the starting point of all true reform is the correction of belief about God. Societal ills, moral decay, and injustice all stem from a heart that worships something other than Allah. Noah’s call to worship Allah alone was the most urgent and merciful message he could bring, and his fear of the painful Day was a reflection of his profound care for their eternal destiny, a model for all who call others to the truth.

=27=

The response of the elite to Noah’s sincere warning reveals the typical arguments of arrogance. The verse specifies that it was “the disbelieving chiefs,” the mala’, the wealthy and powerful leaders, who spoke. They felt their status and traditions threatened by a message that declared all humans equal before Allah. Their first objection is: “We see you only as a human being like ourselves. ” This was their fundamental stumbling block. They could not accept that a mere man, without angelic attendants or visible treasures, could be a messenger from God. They wanted a messenger who matched their false notions of grandeur, not one who ate, walked in the markets, and shared their humanity.

This is exactly what the Quraysh said to the Prophet, as mentioned earlier in the surah. Their second objection is a social argument: “We see that no one follows you except the lowliest among us, who do so hastily without thinking.” They looked down upon the poor, the slaves, and the socially weak who were the first to believe, much like the early followers of Muhammad. Their claim that these followers accepted the message hastily without reflection was a projection of their own arrogance. In reality, the poor often accept truth more readily because they have less worldly pride to overcome. Their third statement, “We do not see anything that makes all of you any better than us,” was a judgment based purely on material and social standing. They measured virtue by wealth and lineage, not by faith and character.

Finally, they concluded with the blunt accusation: “In fact, we think you are liars.” This direct charge of lying shows that they had exhausted any intellectual argument and resorted to slander. Noah’s response in the following verses will dismantle each of these objections with wisdom, humility, and reliance on Allah, demonstrating the profound difference between the truth-bearer and the arrogant elite.

=28=

Noah answers with remarkable gentleness and logic. He begins with “O my people!” showing that despite their insults, he still regards them with affection and a sense of responsibility. He invites them to “consider,” to use their reason rather than their pride. His argument is: “if I stand on a clear proof from my Lord and He has blessed me with a mercy from Himself, which you fail to see. ” The clear proof, bayyinah, is the revelation and the signs of his prophethood. The mercy from Allah is the gift of prophethood itself and the guidance it brings. Noah is not offering a theory; he possesses a direct, divine insight that his people are blind to. Their inability to see it does not negate its reality; it only proves their spiritual blindness, matching the earlier parable of the blind and the deaf. Then Noah asks a rhetorical question that contains a profound principle of Islamic da‘wah: “Should we then force it on you against your will?” The truth is so self-evident to Noah and the believers that they might wish to compel people to accept it out of sheer desire for their salvation. But Noah immediately recognizes that forced faith is neither possible nor accepted by Allah. Religion must enter the heart through conviction and free choice. This verse is a clear declaration that compulsion in religion is contrary to the prophetic way. The mission is to convey, not to coerce. This gentle, respectful approach contrasts sharply with the arrogance of the chiefs. Noah does not threaten them with force, even though his truth is absolutely certain to him. He presents the reality and leaves their response to their own will, while warning of the consequences of a wrong choice.

This verse also contains a subtle lesson for callers to Islam: no matter how clear the truth is to you, and no matter how stubborn the rejection, your role is not to force hearts open. Guidance belongs to Allah alone. This verse will resonate again when we reach the story of Shu‘aib and the final advice to the Prophet at the end of the surah.

=29=

Noah continues his heartfelt appeal, addressing another unspoken suspicion. The chiefs might have thought that Noah was seeking wealth, leadership, or some worldly gain through his preaching. So he declares his complete disinterest in material compensation: “I do not ask you for a payment for this message. My reward is only from Allah. ” This is a hallmark of all true prophets. They never seek financial gain from the people they are sent to guide. Their sustenance and their reward are entrusted entirely to Allah, which guarantees their sincerity and frees them from any accusation of self-interest. The Prophet Muhammad was likewise commanded to say, “I do not ask you for any reward for it; my reward is only from the Lord of the worlds. ” Noah then addresses the specific demand that had been implied by the chiefs: that he should expel the “lowliest” followers they despised. He states firmly: “And I will never dismiss the believers.

” The word “never” shows absolute resolve. He would not sacrifice the dignity of sincere believers to please the arrogant elite. His reason is deeply theological: “for they will surely meet their Lord.” The believers, regardless of their worldly poverty or social status, have the supreme honor of an appointment with their Creator. On that Day, their worldly station will mean nothing, and their faith will be everything. How could Noah then drive them away to placate people who were blind to this reality? He ends with a sad observation: “But I can see that you are a people acting ignorantly.” Their ignorance is not a lack of intelligence but a spiritual folly that values worldly status over eternal standing. This verse directly parallels the incidents in Mecca where the Quraysh leaders asked the Prophet to expel the weak Muslims like Bilal and ‘Ammar so they might sit with him. The Quran’s firm response upheld the honor of those believers. Noah’s stand becomes an eternal principle: the community of faith is based on taqwa, not on wealth or lineage, and a true leader of faith will never humiliate sincere believers for the sake of worldly approval.

=30=

Noah adds a powerful and fear-filled reason for his refusal to dismiss the believers. He asks his people to consider his own position before his Lord. “Who would protect me from Allah if I were to dismiss them?” This rhetorical question reveals his profound awareness of divine accountability. Noah understands that if he wrongs those believers—treating them with contempt because of their poverty and driving them away from the community of faith—he would be committing a grave injustice that Allah would surely punish. No one among his people, no chief, no idol, could shield him from that divine punishment. His fear of Allah is far greater than his fear of the chiefs’ displeasure.

This is the mark of a true prophet: he does not bend the truth to please the powerful because he knows the power of Allah is absolute and His justice is inevitable. The chiefs were asking Noah to commit an act of oppression to win their favor, but Noah’s conscience was bound to his Lord. He then appeals to them one more time: “Will you not then be mindful?

” The word “tadhakkarūn” implies a recalling of something they already know deep inside. They know that justice requires honoring sincere people, and they know that Allah is the ultimate protector. Noah is inviting them to wake up from their arrogance and see the simple truth: that dismissing the poor and sincere is an act of oppression that even their own conscience, if they would only consult it, would reject. This verse, together with the previous one, establishes a fundamental social principle in Islam: no believer should ever be looked down upon for their worldly status, and leaders must protect the dignity of the weak. It also reveals the deep pastoral care of Noah; he is not defending the believers as a political tactic but because he genuinely honors their faith and fears for his own soul should he mistreat them. It is a model of prophetic integrity that the surah places before the Quraysh, challenging their social elitism directly.

=31=

Noah’s humility reaches its peak in this comprehensive disavowal of any pretension. The chiefs had implicitly criticized him for being an ordinary man, so he clarifies exactly what he does and does not claim. “I do not say to you that I possess Allah’s treasuries”: he does not claim to control the treasures of provision, rain, or wealth that would have made him appear powerful in their eyes. His inability to shower them with riches was not a sign of falsehood, but a sign of his honest humanity. “Or know the unseen”: he does not claim to have independent knowledge of hidden matters, future events, or the secrets of hearts unless Allah reveals something to him. This acknowledges that all prophetic knowledge is a gift, not an inherent power. “Nor do I claim to be an angel”: he directly addresses their obsession with an angelic messenger. He is a man, eating and drinking, and this is exactly what a messenger to humans should be—someone they can relate to and emulate. Then he adds a remark that goes to the heart of their demand to expel the weak believers: “nor do I say that Allah will never grant goodness to those you look down upon.” He refuses to pass judgment on the inner worth or future destiny of the believers they despised. Perhaps, in Allah’s sight, those poor believers are far nobler than the chiefs. “Allah knows best what is hidden within them. ” The secrets of sincerity, the depths of faith, the hidden tears of repentance—these are known only to Allah. Noah humbly defers all judgment of hearts to the only true Judge.

The verse concludes: “If I did, then I would truly be one of the wrongdoers.” Were he to claim any of these things—possession of divine treasures, knowledge of the unseen, angelic nature, or the right to judge the inner worth of believers—he would be transgressing the boundaries of his servanthood and joining the ranks of the unjust.

This verse teaches profound lessons about the proper attitude of a servant of Allah: acknowledging one’s limitations, attributing all power and knowledge to Allah, and never looking down upon any believer. It is a powerful antidote to the arrogance of the religious elite in any age.

=32=

The response of the chiefs to Noah’s clear, humble, and logical arguments is not reflection but frustration and defiance. The phrase “You have argued with us far too much” reveals their state of mind. Noah had spent centuries—950 years according to the Quran—calling them, reasoning with them, debating them gently. His persistence was a mercy, but they perceived it as an annoying, tedious debate.

The word “jādaltanā” implies a long, drawn-out dispute. They were tired of hearing the same call to monotheism, the same warnings, the same challenges to their ancestral ways. In their arrogance, they refused to engage with the substance of his message. Instead, they resorted to the ultimate challenge: “so bring upon us what you threaten us with, if what you say is true. ” This is an open demand for the punishment. They dared Noah to make good on his warnings, believing that the absence of immediate doom proved his falsehood. This attitude mirrors the Quraysh’s earlier demand in this surah, when they asked, “What is holding it back?” and previously when they asked for stones to rain from the sky. It is the challenge of a people who have grown complacent and feel secure in their worldly power. They do not realize that Allah’s delay is a respite and a test, not an indication of powerlessness. Their demand for the punishment is essentially a mockery, a way of saying, “We have heard enough of your threats; show us something tangible or be silent.” This moment in the narrative is the turning point. When a people reach the stage where they mockingly demand the punishment, the door of repentance is closing.

The next verses will show that the decree has been sealed, and the patience of Noah is about to give way to the execution of divine justice. The verse serves as a warning to all who treat the warnings of the messengers with contempt and delay, thinking that life will continue as usual forever.

=33=

Noah’s reply is immediate, firm, and theologically precise. He does not claim to control the punishment; he defers all power to its true possessor. “It is Allah Who can bring it upon you” corrects their misunderstanding. They thought Noah was personally threatening them, as if he had some magical power to bring disaster. Noah clarifies that the punishment is not in his hands at all. He is merely a warner, not an executor. The punishment belongs to Allah alone, and it will come solely by His will and command. This removes any pretense that Noah is acting out of personal vengeance.

The phrase “if He wills” adds a crucial condition. The timing and occurrence of the punishment are subject to divine wisdom. Allah may delay it, bring it soon, or even forgive if people repent. Noah does not presume to dictate what Allah will do. He simply states the fact that Allah is fully capable of sending it, and if He wills, it will come. Then Noah delivers the chilling consequence: “and then you will have no escape.

” The word “mu‘jizīn” comes from a root meaning to render someone helpless or to escape from someone’s power. Noah tells them that once Allah’s punishment arrives, they will not be able to outrun it, hide from it, or prevent it. No mountain, no fortress, no ally will save them.

This is exactly the same truth stated earlier in the surah about the punishment overtaking the disbelievers and not being averted from them. The chiefs had challenged Noah as if they could handle whatever might come, but Noah reminds them of the absolute sovereignty of Allah. Their mocking tone will soon be silenced. For the believers in Mecca hearing this story, it was a reminder that the Quraysh’s similar challenges would meet the same fate. The ultimate power is not with the arrogant mockers but with the Lord of the worlds, and His punishment, when it comes, is inescapable. This verse is a lesson in attributing all power to Allah and never speaking on His behalf without knowledge.

=34=

Noah now reaches a profound and sorrowful conclusion about the limits of his mission. After centuries of ceaseless effort, he acknowledges a divine reality that every believer must understand: guidance ultimately rests in the hands of Allah. “My advice will not benefit you—no matter how hard I try—if Allah wills for you to stray.

” The word “advice” translates nuṣḥ, which means sincere, pure, well-wishing counsel. Noah has given them exactly that—advice untainted by any selfish motive.

Yet he admits that even the most sincere human effort cannot override the divine decree. If Allah, based on His perfect knowledge and wisdom, has willed that they remain astray because of their persistent, stubborn rejection and their hearts’ sealed state, then no amount of human preaching can change that. This does not mean Allah forced them to stray unjustly; rather, it means their repeated choice of disbelief led to Allah’s decree that they would remain in misguidance.

The phrase “no matter how hard I try” emphasizes that Noah’s effort is not lacking; the obstacle is their own spiritual condition and the consequent divine will. He then concludes: “He is your Lord, and to Him you will all be returned.

” This statement brings the focus back to accountability. They belong to Allah, and they will return to Him, not to their idols, not to their chiefs. On that Day, the truth of Noah’s warning will become utterly clear. For the Prophet Muhammad, this verse was a profound comfort. It taught him that his grief over the rejection of the Quraysh, mentioned earlier in the surah, should not overwhelm him. Even the most patient and long-suffering of messengers, Noah, reached a point where he recognized that some hearts are beyond the reach of human effort. The return is to Allah, and He will judge with perfect justice. This verse balances the duty of continuous preaching with the humble acceptance of Allah’s ultimate decree, a balance that every caller to Islam must strive to maintain.

=35=

In a striking shift, the narrative of Noah is briefly paused to directly address the Meccan disbelievers and their accusation against the Prophet. The Quran often weaves the stories of the past with direct addresses to the present audience, showing that the same patterns of rejection are repeating. The verse asks: “Or do they say, ‘He has fabricated this Quran!’?

” This is the same accusation that was already challenged earlier in the surah with the demand to produce ten similar chapters. Here, a different angle is taken. The Prophet is instructed to respond with a statement of personal accountability and dissociation: “If I have done so, then I bear the burden of that sin! But I am free from your sinful accusation.” The logic is profound and disarming. The Prophet is not claiming an impossible power of mind-reading; he simply states the consequences. If—and the “if” is purely hypothetical—he had indeed fabricated it, the sin and its heavy penalty would fall on him alone. He would bear the burden on a day when no bearer can carry the burden of another.

This statement underscores his absolute confidence in his truthfulness and his fear of Allah’s punishment for lying about Him. He is not asking them to believe him blindly, but he is making clear that he knows the stakes.

The second part, “But I am free from your sinful accusation,” is a declaration of innocence. The word “barī’” means completely disassociated and free. He does not share in their crime of falsely accusing a messenger of fabrication. In the larger context of the surah, this verse bridges the story of Noah, who was also accused of falsehood, and the upcoming continuation of Noah’s story. It reminds the listener that the same lie is being hurled at Muhammad, and his response is an echo of the innocence and sincerity of all the prophets. It also teaches an important principle: when faced with false accusations, a believer can confidently entrust the matter to Allah, declare his innocence, and leave the judgment to the One who knows all.

=36=

The focus returns to Noah, and the verse marks a decisive turning point in his mission. After centuries of tireless preaching, Allah sent down a revelation that sealed the fate of his people. “None of your people will believe except those who already have.” This was a divine decree based on eternal knowledge. Allah knew that the hearts of the remaining disbelievers were so hardened by persistent rejection, mockery, and injustice that they would never accept faith. The door of repentance for them, as a people, was closed.

The word “your people” emphasizes that even his own community, his relatives, his neighbors, had reached a terminal state of disbelief. This revelation was not a statement about the deficiency of Noah’s efforts but about the spiritual death of his audience. The few who had already believed—said to be about eighty souls after all those centuries—were all that would be saved. Immediately after this heavy news, Allah consoles His prophet: “So do not be distressed by what they have been doing.” Noah had watched his people mock, reject, and plot against the believers for hundreds of years. His heart carried an immense burden of grief for them. Allah now relieves him of that burden. The command not to grieve echoes the earlier comfort given to the Prophet Muhammad in this surah, where he was told not to be distressed by their mockery. The “what they have been doing” includes their idol worship, their mockery, their oppression of the weak believers, and their calls to bring the punishment. Noah is freed from the emotional weight of hoping for their change. His mission now shifts from public calling to the practical preparation for the divine judgment. This verse is a lesson in the reality of guidance: a preacher’s duty is to deliver the message with patience and sincerity, but the results are in Allah’s hands. When a community collectively and persistently rejects the truth, there may come a point of no return. For the believers in Mecca, this was a sobering message, reminding them that the respite given to Quraysh would eventually expire.

=37=

With the decree sealed, Allah gives Noah a command that signals the imminent punishment. “And build the Ark” is a sudden shift from preaching to construction. The Ark, al-fulk, was a massive ship, unlike anything Noah’s people had probably seen, to be built in an inland location far from the sea, adding to the strangeness and the mockery that would follow. The order to build comes with a deeply reassuring phrase: “under Our watchful Eyes and directions. ” The plural “Eyes” for Allah is understood in Islamic theology as an attribute befitting His majesty, indicating His special care, protection, and oversight. Noah was not building alone; he was under divine supervision. Every plank, every nail, every measurement was guided by revelation.

The word “waḥyinā” means “Our inspiration” or “Our directions,” meaning the technical knowledge of shipbuilding was taught directly by Allah to a man who had never built a ship. This miraculous guidance is part of the divine support for His messenger.

The second part of the command is both a prohibition and a prediction: “and do not plead with Me for those who have done wrong, for they will surely be drowned.” Allah knows that Noah’s merciful heart, later demonstrated when he pleaded for his son, would be inclined to intercede for his people. So He forbids it in advance. The fate is sealed; the wrongdoers are doomed to the flood.

This is not divine cruelty but divine justice after centuries of clear warning and persistent rebellion. The specific mention of drowning prepares Noah and the audience for the method of destruction. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a parallel to the eventual command to migrate from Mecca—a physical separation from a doomed elite. The ark became the vessel of salvation, a physical divider between the believers and the disbelievers, just as the hijra later separated the community of faith from the community of shirk.

The verse emphasizes that salvation follows obedience to divine instruction, however strange it may seem.

=38=

Noah obeyed the command without hesitation. “So he began to build the Ark” reflects his immediate and unwavering compliance, despite the immense challenges. He was an old man, far from any sea, constructing a colossal wooden ship. The scene is vivid: a prophet laboring with his hands under the hot sun, joined by the small band of believers, building a vessel that seemed absurd to the onlookers. The verse describes the reaction of the disbelieving elite: “whenever some of the chiefs of his people passed by, they mocked him.” The chiefs, who had previously argued with him and demanded the punishment, now found fresh material for ridicule. They saw a madman building a boat on dry land, talking about a flood. They likely made sarcastic remarks: “Have you become a carpenter after being a prophet?” or “Will your ship sail on sand? ” This mockery was a test of Noah’s patience, but he was fortified by the knowledge that the divine command was true. His response is recorded with calm confidence: “If you laugh at us, we will soon laugh at you similarly.

” This is not a petty, vengeful statement. It is a prophetic declaration of the coming reversal. The laughter of the believers would come when they saw the punishment descend and realized the truth of the warning. It is the laughter of the vindicated, not the spiteful. This mirrors the tone set earlier in the surah: the punishment overtakes them and they are overwhelmed by what they ridiculed. For the early Muslims in Mecca, this was a direct parallel. The Quraysh mocked the Prophet’s message and the few believers daily. This verse assured them that a day would come when truth would be manifest and falsehood would be humiliated. It taught them to absorb ridicule with patience, knowing that the final chapter of the story had not yet been written. The ark, the object of mockery, would soon become the only means of survival.

=39=

Noah continues his response to the mockers, expanding on the nature of the coming punishment to emphasize that his warning is not merely about a worldly flood but about something far greater. “You will soon come to know” is a phrase that conveys certainty and nearness. The knowledge he speaks of is experiential; they will no longer just hear about the punishment, they will taste it.

The word “soon” is relative; for an eternal decree, the remaining years until the flood were indeed short. The punishment he describes has two layers. First, “a humiliating torment in this life,” which refers to the drowning in the flood.

The word “humiliating,” mukhzi, is crucial. The chiefs prided themselves on their honor and social standing, but the flood would bring them a death of absolute disgrace, swept away like refuse, their power and arrogance made utterly meaningless. They would be humiliated in the very world where they mocked the believers. Second, “and overwhelmed by an everlasting punishment in the next.” This clarifies that physical death in the flood is not the end. The disbelievers will face the eternal punishment of the Hereafter, which is far more severe and endless.

The word “overwhelmed” suggests that the punishment will surround them from all sides, with no escape, as they are engulfed by Hellfire. This double punishment for Noah’s people is unique in that they were completely destroyed in this world and then face the Fire in the next. It serves as the ultimate warning. For the Quraysh, who had similarly mocked and challenged the Prophet, this verse was a clear message: your mockery today will turn into regret, and if you persist, you too will face humiliation in this life—as they later did at Badr—and everlasting punishment in the Hereafter. The verse connects the two realms, showing that the consequences of rejecting the messengers span both worlds.

=40=

The moment of divine judgment finally arrives. “And when Our command came” signals the execution of the decree. “The oven burst with water” translates the Arabic “al-tannūr,” which most commentators explain as the oven, possibly a stone oven in Noah’s house, from which water miraculously gushed, serving as the sign that the flood had begun. This was a clear signal that there was no more time for deliberation. Allah’s command to Noah was specific and urgent: “Take into the Ark a pair from every species.” This refers to male and female of every kind of animal, to ensure the survival of animal life after the flood.

The phrase “along with your family” includes his believing household members, but it is immediately qualified: “except those against whom the decree to drown has already been passed.” This exception refers to his wife and his son, as will be detailed in the subsequent verses. Divine justice does not spare a disbeliever merely because of family ties.

The command also includes “and those who believe,” the small community of faithful followers. The verse ends with a sad and telling remark: “But none believed with him except for a few.” After a preaching career that spanned nearly a millennium, only a handful of people entered the Ark. This small number—often cited as around eighty in some traditions—is a stark reminder that truth is not measured by majority. The fewness of the believers does not indicate falsehood; it indicates the depth of human resistance to the truth. For the Prophet Muhammad and the handful of Muslims in Mecca, this was a profound comfort. Their small numbers and the mockery they endured were not signs of failure but a replication of the exact pattern of Noah’s mission. The ark became the sanctuary, the dividing line between salvation and destruction, prefiguring the hijra and the eventual victory of the believers. The verse encapsulates the theme of the surah: the patient, the few, and the faithful are ultimately saved, while the arrogant majority are drowned.
