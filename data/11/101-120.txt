=101=

After recounting the fates of the destroyed cities, the Quran now establishes a fundamental principle of divine justice. “We did not wrong them, rather they wronged themselves” is a clear declaration that removes any notion of injustice from Allah’s actions. The destruction that befell those nations was not an act of tyranny from the Almighty but the natural consequence of their own choices. They were given messengers, clear warnings, ample time for reflection, and the free will to choose belief or disbelief. When they persistently chose oppression, shirk, and corruption, they essentially wronged their own souls.

The word “ẓalamū” implies that they placed something out of its proper place; they misused their God-given faculties, corrupted their societies, and sealed their own doom. This statement is profoundly comforting for the believers and terrifying for the wrongdoers. It tells us that Allah’s punishment is never arbitrary or cruel; it is always the result of human transgression.

The second part of the verse exposes the utter futility of the false gods: “The gods they invoked beside Allah were of no help at all when the command of your Lord came.” In their moments of crisis, when the floodwaters rose, the winds screamed, or the blast struck, the idols they worshipped could do nothing. They were absent, powerless, incapable of hearing or responding.

The phrase “of no help at all” emphasizes absolute zero—not even a partial assistance or a delay. The word “invoked” points to their earnest pleas, which went completely unanswered.

The verse then delivers the final blow: “and only contributed to their ruin.” The Arabic “wa mā zādūhum ghayra tatbīb” means that the false gods actually increased them in nothing but destruction and loss. Their worship of these gods was not neutral; it actively compounded their ruin by being the very cause of their punishment.

This is a powerful lesson for any who place their trust in anything other than Allah—whether idols, wealth, power, or human connections. On the Day of Judgment, those things will not only fail to help but will become a source of regret and an added reason for punishment. For the Quraysh, this was a direct attack on the idols around the Kaaba; they would not save them when the divine command arrived.

=102=

Having established that the destruction was a result of the people’s own wrongdoing, this verse describes the nature of that divine seizure. “Such is the crushing grip of your Lord” uses the word “akhdh,” which means a seizing or a taking hold, and it is qualified as the grip of the Lord, indicating an act of irresistible power.

The word “crushing” or “painful” reflects the severity and the completeness of the seizure. It is a grip from which there is no escape and no possibility of resistance.

The phrase “when He seizes the societies entrenched in wrongdoing” specifies that this is not a random act of destruction but a targeted response to systematic, deep-rooted injustice. The word “qurā” means towns or societies, and they are described as “ẓālimah,” meaning persisting in wrongdoing. The grip comes when the wrongdoing has become the dominant characteristic of the society, when reform is no longer hoped for, and when the final warning has been ignored.

The verse then emphasizes the qualitative nature of this grip: “Indeed, His grip is terribly painful and severe. ” The word “alīm” means intensely painful, and “shadīd” means severe, powerful, and harsh. The combination gives a picture of an experience that is both physically devastating and psychologically shattering. The destruction is not just an end of life but a profoundly painful process that carries a taste of the greater punishment to come in the Hereafter. This verse serves as a culmination of the historical narratives that preceded it. The listener has heard about the flood, the wind, the blast, the shower of stones, and the drowning. Each was a manifestation of this crushing grip. The verse is both a warning and a reassurance: a warning to all societies that if they become entrenched in wrongdoing, they are never beyond the reach of divine justice; and a reassurance to the oppressed believers that the Lord will eventually seize their oppressors, and when He does, it will be decisive and final.

The mention of “your Lord” personalizes the message for the Prophet and his community, reminding them that this same Lord is their protector and the judge of their enemies. It also instills a healthy fear in the heart of every believer, knowing that Allah’s punishment is not to be taken lightly.

=103=

The Quran now draws the moral from the entire sequence of destruction narratives. “Surely in this is a sign” refers to the detailed accounts of the destroyed cities and the crushing grip of the Lord. A “sign,” āyah, is not just a piece of information but a guiding pointer to a deeper truth. This sign, however, is only effective for a specific group: “for those who fear the torment of the Hereafter. ” This qualification is crucial. People who are entirely absorbed in this world and do not believe in the afterlife will view these stories as mere ancient legends or natural disasters, not as divine warnings. But those who already possess a reverent fear of the eternal punishment will see these stories as urgent reminders that the same fate awaits any individual or community that follows the same path of rebellion.

The verse then shifts the focus from the worldly punishments to the ultimate gathering: “That is a Day for which humanity will be gathered. ” The “Day” refers to the Day of Judgment. The gathering, jam‘, is comprehensive; every human being from the first to the last will be assembled, with no exceptions. This gathering is not a local event but a universal one, contrasting with the localized destructions in the world.

The verse adds another description: “and a Day that will be witnessed by all. ” The word “mashhūd” means witnessed, attended by all creation—angels, jinn, and humans. No one will be absent, and the events of that Day will be openly seen, with no concealment. For the believers who were a small, marginalized community in Mecca, this was a profound perspective shift. The worldly destructions were only a preview; the real, all-encompassing event is yet to come. Their present suffering was temporary, and their vindication would be on a stage witnessed by all of creation. This verse connects the historical accounts directly to eschatology, ensuring that the listener does not think the punishment ended with death. The worst is yet to come for the disbelievers, and the best is yet to come for the believers. The sign in the historical narratives is thus only fully appreciated by those whose hearts are tuned to the reality of the Hereafter.

=104=

This short verse addresses the apparent delay of the Day of Judgment, a frequent objection of the disbelievers who mockingly asked, “When will this promise be fulfilled?” The pronoun “it” refers to the Day of Gathering and Witnessing mentioned in the previous verse. “We only delay it” indicates that the timing is entirely under divine control. The delay is not due to forgetfulness, inability, or a change of plan; it is a deliberate postponement by the All-Wise.

The phrase “for a fixed term” translates “li-ajalin ma‘dūd,” meaning a precisely counted, predetermined term. This term is known only to Allah, and it is fixed in the Preserved Tablet. It cannot be advanced or delayed even by a single moment. This verse serves multiple purposes. First, it answers the mockery of the pagans by affirming that the Day is certain, and the delay is part of a divine plan, not a sign of its non-existence. Second, it provides comfort to the believers who were yearning for justice; the appointed term will arrive exactly when Allah’s wisdom dictates, and not a moment late. The delay is actually a mercy because it allows time for more people to repent and believe. Third, it warns that the term is fixed and limited; the delay should not be mistaken for perpetual reprieve. The counting down of that term is happening, and no one knows how much remains. For the Meccans, this verse challenged their false sense of security; they thought the lack of immediate punishment meant the Prophet was lying, but in reality, the clock was ticking.

The verse also instills a sense of urgency in the believer: since the term is fixed but unknown, one must always be prepared for that Day. The shortness of the verse reflects the certainty and precision of the divine decree, requiring no lengthy explanation. It is a statement of absolute fact that anchors the preceding warning and the following description of the Day’s outcomes.

The phrase “for a fixed term” appears elsewhere in the Quran and consistently teaches that divine decisions operate on a perfect timetable that human impatience cannot alter.

=105=

The verse now transports us to the scene of the Day itself, describing its overwhelming authority and the division of humanity. “When that Day arrives” uses the word “ya’ti” to indicate its coming, as if it is a visitor that will inevitably show up. Upon its arrival, the first characteristic mentioned is the absolute silence of creation: “no one will dare speak except with His permission.” The court of the Almighty is not a place of casual chatter, arguments, or defense statements spoken out of turn. Everyone will be struck with awe, their tongues stilled by the majesty of the occasion.

The Arabic word “takallama” implies any speech, and the negation is comprehensive. No prophet, no angel, no human, no jinn will utter a word until Allah grants permission.

This is a direct refutation of the disbelievers’ assumption that they would argue and justify themselves before Allah as they did in the world. In that court, even the most eloquent will be mute. The only speech allowed will be that which Allah permits—perhaps the testimony of the prophets, the shahādah of the limbs, or the intercession granted to those He approves. This creates a picture of absolute divine sovereignty.

The verse then introduces the great division: “Some of them will be miserable, others joyful. ” The Arabic words “shaqī” and “sa‘īd” denote two fixed, eternal conditions. The miserable are those whose record is given in their left hand, whose faces are darkened, and who are destined for the Fire. The joyful are those whose faces are bright, who receive their book in their right hand, and who enter Paradise. This division is final and based on the faith and deeds of the worldly life. There is no third category, no neutral zone. The verse is a powerful reminder that the entire human drama reduces to these two ultimate outcomes. For the believers, it was a source of hope: despite their current misery in Mecca, they could be among the joyful.

For the disbelievers, it was a chilling warning that their current laughter and mockery would turn into eternal misery. The structure of the verse—first the silence, then the division—emphasizes that the judgment is conducted in an atmosphere of profound solemnity.

=106=

The surah now elaborates on the fate of the miserable group. “As for those bound for misery” translates the word “shaqū,” which indicates that misery is their appointed destiny, a direct result of their own choices in the world. They are described as being “in the Fire,” al-nār, the ultimate abode of punishment.

The verse then describes their condition within the Fire with two vivid words: “sighing and gasping. ” “Zafīr” refers to a deep, heavy sigh, like the exhaling of a person in extreme anguish, a sound that comes from the depth of the chest. “Shahīq” is a sharp, labored inhalation, like the gasping of a donkey, indicating an inability to breathe normally amidst the torment. Together, these two sounds paint an audible picture of ceaseless agony. They are not simply burning; they are in a state of constant respiratory distress, their every breath a cry of pain and a desperate gasp for relief that never comes. This auditory description makes the punishment intensely real and psychologically impactful. The disbelievers of Mecca, who prided themselves on their eloquence and their joyful gatherings, are being told that their final state will be one of inarticulate moaning and gasping.

The verse also connects back to earlier descriptions of arrogance; those who walked with pride and spoke with scorn will be reduced to these primal sounds of suffering. The brevity of the verse adds to its power; it does not need a lengthy description because these two words carry a universe of horror. For the believers, this was a stark reminder of the seriousness of the Hereafter, encouraging them to endure their own hardships with patience.

The verse also serves as a counterpoint to the following description of the joyful, creating a vivid contrast between the two destinies. This suffering is not temporary but, as the next verse will clarify, eternal, underscoring the absolute finality of the judgment.

=107=

The eternality of the punishment for the miserable is now emphasized in a striking way. “Staying there forever” translates “khālidīna fīhā,” meaning they will abide in the Fire perpetually, never to exit, never to die, never to find relief.

The phrase “as long as the heavens and the earth will endure” is an Arabic idiom for a duration that is, from a human perspective, eternal. It does not mean that the punishment will end if the heavens and the earth end; rather, it emphasizes a span of time so vast that it is comparable to the lifespan of the cosmos. The heavens and the earth of the Hereafter are different and everlasting, so the phrase indicates an unending duration. The clause “except what your Lord wills” has been the subject of careful scholarly reflection. The majority of Sunni scholars interpret this exception as referring not to an end of the punishment but to the possibility that Allah’s will may encompass matters that we cannot fully comprehend, such as the punishment of certain monotheistic sinners who might eventually be taken out of the Fire by intercession or divine mercy. For the outright disbelievers, the exception does not imply a release but rather underscores that the entire matter is subject to Allah’s will, and whatever He decrees will happen. It preserves the absolute sovereignty of Allah even over the decree of eternal punishment.

The verse then concludes, “Surely your Lord does what He intends,” affirming that Allah is not constrained by any external necessity; He acts according to His perfect wisdom and justice. If He wills something, it is done, and no one can question it. This verse is a powerful statement of the seriousness of dying upon disbelief. It also reassures the believers that Allah’s will is always just and good, even when it involves the eternal punishment of others. For the Meccans, it was a final warning: the Fire is real, it is eternal, and their mockery of it would not alter their fate.

The verse teaches the believer to never take the mercy of Allah for granted and to strive until the end to be among the joyful rather than the miserable.

=108=

The parallel description of the joyful mirrors the previous verse but with a crucial and uplifting addition. “As for those destined to joy” refers to the “su‘idū,” the companions of happiness and success. Their abode is “Paradise,” al-jannah, the garden of eternal bliss. Like the inhabitants of the Fire, they will abide there “forever, as long as the heavens and the earth will endure, except what your Lord wills. ” The inclusion of the same exception clause here, for Paradise, has been explained by scholars as emphasizing that even the eternal reward is subject to Allah’s will, not an obligation upon Him.

However, the dominant interpretation is that the exception does not imply an eventual end to Paradise but rather indicates that the blessings of Paradise are constantly renewed according to Allah’s will, or that it refers to the period before the final judgment when the believers might be in an intermediate state. The verse then adds a phrase that is absent from the description of the Fire: “a generous giving, without end.

” The word “‘aṭā’” means a gift, a grant, emphasizing that Paradise is not a wage that is owed but a pure favor from Allah. It is described as “ghayra majdhūdh,” meaning not cut off, uninterrupted, ceaseless.

This is the definitive answer to any possible interpretation that the exception might imply an eventual end. The gift of Paradise is explicitly stated to be without end. While the people of the Fire face an eternal punishment, the people of Paradise enjoy an eternal reward that is not merely a continuation of the same pleasures but a continuously renewed, ever-increasing bestowal of Allah’s generosity.

This contrast is central to the surah’s message: the ultimate outcomes are not just opposites in quality—joy versus misery—but also in their very nature. The punishment is a consequence of justice, while the reward is an overflow of divine generosity. For the believers who were poor and persecuted, the promise of an endless, generous gift from the Lord of all worlds was the ultimate motivation to remain steadfast. It shifted their focus from what they were losing in this world to what they would gain in the next—a gain that has no expiry, no reduction, and no end.

=109=

After the extensive narratives and the vivid description of the Hereafter, the surah returns to directly address the Prophet and, through him, the believers. “So do not be in doubt O Prophet about what those pagans worship.” The command “lā taku fī miryatin” is a reassurance that forbids any lingering uncertainty about the falsehood of the idols. The Prophet’s heart, burdened by the persistence of his people, might have been tempted to wonder if there was any truth in their ancestral religion. Allah tells him to have absolutely no doubt: their worship is entirely baseless.

The verse then explains why: “They worship nothing except what their forefathers worshipped before them.” Their religion is not based on reason, evidence, or divine revelation but purely on blind imitation of ancestors.

This is a devastating critique that exposes the intellectual emptiness of shirk. They have inherited a tradition without ever examining its validity.

The verse then delivers a promise of justice: “And We will certainly give them their share of punishment in full, without any reduction.” The word “naṣībahum” implies a portion that is allocated and due to them. Their punishment is a debt that will be paid in full, with no discount and no mitigation.

The word “manqūṣ” means reduced or diminished. This assures the believers that no matter how much the disbelievers enjoy worldly life, their full share of punishment is reserved and will be delivered. It also carries a subtle warning: the fact that they are not punished immediately does not mean they have escaped; their portion is being preserved. For the Quraysh, this verse was both a diagnosis of their spiritual sickness—blind traditionalism—and a pronouncement of their inevitable doom unless they broke free from that imitation. For the believers, it was a call to root their faith in certainty and evidence, not in family or cultural heritage. It also provided patience: the worldly prosperity of the pagans was not an injustice, because their full punishment was being stored up, and the believers’ full reward was also guaranteed. The verse connects the end of the narratives back to the ongoing struggle in Mecca, ensuring that the lessons of history are applied to the immediate context.

=110=

The surah now introduces a new dimension to the discussion of rejection and doubt, linking the experience of Moses to that of the Prophet Muhammad. “Indeed, We had given Moses the Scripture” refers to the Torah, a revealed book containing guidance and light. Despite this clear revelation, “differences arose regarding it.” The people to whom Moses was sent, and the later generations of Israelites, fell into disputes over the meaning, authenticity, and interpretation of the Scripture. Some believed, some disbelieved, some distorted the text, and some rejected parts of it.

This is exactly what was happening with the Quran in Mecca and would continue to happen. The verse then addresses why Allah does not immediately settle these differences with a decisive, punishing judgment: “Had it not been for a prior decree from your Lord, their differences would have been settled at once.” The “prior decree” is the divine wisdom that has determined a fixed term for judgment and has allowed for human free will and testing. If Allah had willed, He could have sent a punishment that would have forced everyone into belief or destroyed the disbelievers on the spot, removing all disagreement. But His decree established that life is a test, and the final settlement is deferred to the Day of Judgment. This teaches that the existence of religious differences is not a sign of Allah’s inability or neglect but a part of His wise plan.

The verse concludes, “They are truly in alarming doubt about it. ” Despite having the Scripture, many among them are engulfed in “miryatin,” a disturbing, unsettling doubt. This doubt is not an honest intellectual quest but a state of confusion that arises from their own stubbornness and refusal to submit. For the Prophet, this verse was a source of comfort. If Moses, with his clear Scripture, faced the same divisions and doubts, then the Quran’s reception was not a failure on his part. The believers learned that the presence of doubters and disputants is a normal part of the human response to revelation, and patience is required until the prior decree of final judgment arrives. It also warned the believers not to be shaken by the doubts of others, as those doubts are rooted in their own spiritual sickness.

=111=

This verse provides the ultimate assurance of divine justice that undergirds the entire discussion of differences and doubt. “And surely your Lord will fully pay all for their deeds.

” The word “yuwaffiyannahum” means to give them their due in full and complete measure. Every single individual, regardless of whether they believed or disbelieved, whether they were among those who spread doubt or those who held fast to faith, will receive the exact recompense for everything they did. No good deed will be lost, no evil deed will be overlooked.

This promise of full payment is a source of immense comfort for the believers who were being wronged and whose good deeds seemed unrewarded in the world. It is also a stark warning to the wrongdoers who seemed to be getting away with their crimes.

The verse then anchors this promise in Allah’s perfect knowledge: “He is certainly All-Aware of what they do. ” The word “Khabīr” means that Allah is intimately acquainted with every action, every intention, every hidden thought. Nothing escapes His knowledge.

This is crucial because full recompense requires perfect knowledge of the deeds. Human courts can judge only by what is apparent and often miss the truth; divine judgment is based on complete awareness. The verse connects back to the theme of divine awareness that has been woven throughout the surah: the disbelievers who tried to hide their hearts, the oppressors who plotted in secret, all will find that their deeds are fully recorded and will be fully repaid. For the Prophet and his followers, this was the ultimate reassurance. The differences and disputes over the Scripture, the mockery and the rejection, would all be settled by the One who is fully aware. There was no need to take revenge or despair; the final accounting was in the best hands. This verse serves as a perfect conclusion to the discussion of Moses and the divisions over scripture, shifting the focus from the historical fact of disagreement to the future fact of judgment. It teaches the believer to leave the judging to Allah and to focus on his own deeds, knowing that the Lord who is All-Aware will never allow a single atom’s weight of good or evil to be forgotten. It is a verse that inspires both hope and fear in perfect balance.

=112=

After the long journey through the stories of the prophets and the reflections on divine justice, the surah turns to the practical conclusion: a direct command to the Prophet and the believers. “So be steadfast as you are commanded O Prophet” is a command of istiqāmah, uprightness and firmness.

The phrase “as you are commanded” indicates that the standard of steadfastness is not self-determined but defined by divine instruction. The Prophet is to remain exactly on the path that Allah has prescribed, without deviation to the right or left, regardless of the pressures and temptations.

This command, though addressed to the Prophet, includes the entire community, as indicated by “along with those who turn in submission to Allah with you.” The word “tābū” means those who have turned in repentance and submission. The believers who have joined the Prophet in faith are to accompany him in this steadfastness.

This is a call for communal solidarity in righteousness; the journey of faith is not walked alone but alongside fellow believers who strengthen one another. The command is then immediately followed by a warning: “And do not transgress.

” The word “taṭghaw” means to overstep the bounds, to go beyond the limits set by Allah. Even in the pursuit of steadfastness, believers might be tempted to exceed the divine limits—perhaps through excessive zeal, harshness toward others, or inventing new practices not commanded. The command restricts the believers to the balanced path of the Shariah, not the extremes of human innovation.

The verse concludes, “Surely He is All-Seeing of what you believers do.” Allah’s sight, Baṣīr, encompasses all actions.

This is both a comfort and a check: the believers’ efforts to be steadfast are seen and will be rewarded, and any transgressions are also seen and will be accounted for. This verse encapsulates the essence of the Islamic life: constant, balanced, communal steadfastness within the divine commands, under the watchful eye of Allah. For the Prophet, who felt the weight of the entire mission, this command was a source of clarity and strength. The verse is deeply practical; it tells the believer that the response to the stories of destruction and the debates with disbelievers is not anxiety or extremism but steady, obedient perseverance, trusting that Allah sees all.

=113=

This verse delivers a crucial warning that complements the command to be steadfast. “And do not be inclined to the wrongdoers” uses the word “tarkanū,” which means to lean toward, to rely upon, or to show favor and sympathy. The prohibition is not limited to committing the same wrongdoing but extends to any form of inclination, whether it be affection, reliance, or a softening of one’s stance against their falsehood. The wrongdoers, ẓālimīn, are those who persist in oppression and disbelief. The believers are commanded to maintain a clear, firm distance from them in terms of loyalty, love, and moral compromise. The consequence of violating this command is severe: “or you will be touched by the Fire.

” The word “tamassakum” suggests a direct contact with the Fire, a punishment that would affect those who incline toward wrongdoing. This indicates that associating with wrongdoers in a way that compromises one’s faith can lead to a share of their punishment, either in this world or the next.

The verse then strips away any illusion of worldly protection: “For then you would have no protectors other than Allah, nor would you be helped.” If the believers were to align themselves with the wrongdoers thinking that this would bring them safety, influence, or material benefit, they would be gravely mistaken. Those wrongdoers would not protect them from Allah’s punishment, and Allah Himself would not help them because they had betrayed the principle of loyalty to truth. This verse was particularly relevant for the early Muslims in Mecca, some of whom might have been tempted to soften their opposition to the Quraysh to ease persecution or gain social acceptance. It drew a clear line: the community of faith must be distinct from the community of disbelief. The verse does not command rudeness or severing family ties that do not involve religious compromise, but it forbids any inclination that blurs the boundaries of faith and morality. For all believers, it is a perpetual reminder to guard their hearts and their alliances, ensuring that their primary loyalty is to Allah and His messenger, and that no fear of worldly loss justifies leaning toward those who have chosen the path of wrongdoing.

=114=

With the command for steadfastness and the warning against inclination toward wrongdoers established, the surah now prescribes the most powerful practical tool for maintaining that steadfastness: prayer. “Establish prayer O Prophet at both ends of the day and in the early part of the night.” The two ends of the day refer to the morning (Fajr) and the combination of noon and afternoon (Zuhr and Asr). The early part of the night refers to the sunset and evening prayers (Maghrib and Isha). This verse, according to many scholars, encompasses the five daily prayers, which were made obligatory later during the Night Journey, but were being practiced in some form. The command to “establish” prayer, aqimi al-ṣalāh, means to perform it with all its physical, spiritual, and mental components, not merely to go through the motions. Prayer is the central act of worship that repeatedly reconnects the believer with Allah, providing the strength to remain upright and the purification needed to resist the pull of wrongdoing.

The verse then states a profound principle: “Surely good deeds wipe out evil deeds. ” This is a direct divine promise. The “ḥasanāt,” good deeds, specifically including prayer, have the power to expiate and erase the “sayyi’āt,” the minor sins that inevitably occur in a person’s life.

This is supported by a well-known hadith in which a man confessed to touching a woman and the Prophet recited this verse to him, indicating that the five daily prayers and other good actions serve as continuous purification. This principle offers immense hope; the believer is not expected to be sinless but is expected to constantly follow up mistakes with righteous actions.

The verse concludes, “That is a reminder for the mindful.” The “dhikrā” is the Quran itself and this specific teaching. It benefits those who are “dhākirīn,” those who remember Allah and are mindful of their duties. For the Meccan believers under immense pressure and temptation, this verse was a lifeline. It told them that their daily struggles and occasional slips could be cleansed through the consistent performance of prayer and good deeds. It also emphasized that prayer is not just an obligation but a divine gift—a means of staying clean and strong on the path of steadfastness.

The placement of this verse after the command to be upright and the prohibition of inclination is perfect: prayer is the very engine that enables both.

=115=

After prescribing prayer as the spiritual sustenance, the surah adds a second, indispensable quality: patience. “And be patient!” The command “iṣbir” is singular, addressed to the Prophet but applying to every believer. Patience, ṣabr, is the comprehensive virtue that holds a person back from despair, from anger, from hasty action, and from abandoning the path. It is the strength to endure hardship, to persist in obedience, and to resist sin.

In the context of Mecca, where the persecution was intense and the wait for divine victory was long, patience was not a passive waiting but an active, difficult struggle against the soul’s impulse to give up or retaliate. The command is brief but loaded with the entire experience of the preceding narratives: Noah’s centuries of patience, Hûd’s patience in the face of mockery, Ṣâliḥ’s patience with his people’s betrayal, Shu’aib’s patience under threats, and all the prophets’ steadfastness. The Prophet and the believers are now called to embody that same virtue. The verse immediately follows the command with a powerful motivation: “Certainly Allah does not discount the reward of the good-doers.

” The word “yuḍī‘u” means to waste, to lose, to let perish. Allah’s assurance is that the reward of the muḥsinīn—those who excel in doing good with excellence—will never be lost, no matter how long it is delayed. The world may not reward patience; indeed, the patient may be mocked as weak or foolish. But in the divine economy, not a single moment of patient endurance or a single act of goodness is forgotten or devalued. The reward is preserved, multiplied, and will be delivered in the most perfect form. This verse is a cornerstone of psychological resilience for the believer. It reorients the expectation of reward from the immediate and the worldly to the eternal and the divine. When a person truly internalizes that Allah never wastes the reward of the good-doers, patience becomes not just bearable but meaningful and even joyful.

The verse also connects patience with iḥsān, excellence, teaching that the highest level of patience is that which is accompanied by beautiful conduct. It prepares the believers for the next verses, which reflect on the scarcity of righteous people in past nations, reminding them that they are the rare few who must embody these virtues.

=116=

This verse offers a reflective lament over the state of past nations, serving as both a warning and an encouragement for the contemporary believers. “If only there had been among the destroyed peoples before you, O believers, virtuous individuals who forbade corruption in the land.

” The word “ulū baqiyyatin” refers to people of remnant, meaning individuals possessing a deep, residual quality of goodness, wisdom, and virtue. The verse expresses that the absence of such people, who would actively speak out against evil and work to reform society, was the tragedy of those destroyed nations. There were no strong, righteous voices rising to challenge the widespread corruption, except for a tiny minority—the prophets and their few followers—who were saved. This implies that the general populace was complicit in the evil, either by participation or by silent acceptance.

The verse then contrasts these absent reformers with the reality: “But the wrongdoers only pursued their worldly pleasures, becoming wicked.” The word “utrifū” means they indulged in luxury and abundance, living a life of ease and pleasure, which made them arrogant, heedless, and deeply entrenched in sin. They “became wicked,” committing crimes with full knowledge and persistence. This analysis reveals that societal decay is often fueled by a combination of wealth, pleasure-seeking, and the failure of the morally upright to actively oppose evil. For the small Muslim community in Mecca, this verse was a direct call to action. They were the “remnant of good” in their society. They were not to remain silent or merely seek personal salvation; they had a duty to forbid corruption, even if they were few.

The verse also explains why those past nations were destroyed: the evil was widespread, the good were either silent or absent, and the tyrants ruled unchallenged. It reminded the believers that their very presence as a community that enjoins good and forbids evil was a mercy that might hold back punishment. It is a timeless lesson: a society that lacks a critical mass of courageous, virtuous individuals who actively oppose corruption is on a path to destruction, no matter how advanced or prosperous it appears.

=117=

This verse establishes a fundamental principle of divine justice that clarifies the condition for destruction. “And your Lord O Prophet would never destroy a society unjustly” negates any possibility of arbitrary or tyrannical punishment from Allah.

The word “ẓulm” means injustice, and Allah is completely free from it. He does not destroy a community that is innocent of the crimes that warrant such punishment.

The verse then specifies the condition that protects a society from destruction: “while its people were acting rightly.” The word “muṣliḥūn” comes from iṣlāḥ, meaning those who set things right, who engage in reform, who promote goodness and justice in the land. If a society contains a body of people who are actively working to maintain righteousness, forbid evil, and uphold the truth, Allah will not destroy that society. The destruction comes when the muṣliḥūn are absent, silenced, or reduced to a tiny minority that no longer influences the society, and when the general populace has embraced wrongdoing. This verse serves as a powerful reassurance to the believers and a warning to the disbelievers. The Quraysh might have pointed to the prosperity of Mecca and assumed it was a sign of divine favor, but the verse indicates that true safety lies in the presence of righteousness and reform, not in wealth. Conversely, the Muslims, though small and persecuted, were the muṣliḥūn, and their presence was a source of protection and hope.

The verse also clarifies the discussions of the previous verse about the destroyed nations; they were not destroyed unjustly but precisely because they lacked this reforming element. It teaches that a society’s security is not guaranteed by material strength but by its moral and spiritual health. For all times, this is a call for believers to be the agents of iṣlāḥ in their communities, to stand for justice, and to actively correct wrongs. Their very presence and their efforts are, by Allah’s grace, a shield against widespread calamity. The verse therefore links social responsibility with divine protection, making reform not just a noble act but a communal necessity.

=118=

This verse addresses a profound theological question: why didn’t Allah simply compel all of humanity to believe, thereby eliminating all the conflict, destruction, and division? “Had your Lord so willed, He would have certainly made humanity one single community of believers.” The power of Allah is absolute; He could have created all humans with a uniform nature that only obeyed, like angels. He could have forced belief upon them. But He did not. His will was to create beings with free will, capable of choosing belief or disbelief, and thus capable of a moral and spiritual journey that would be tested.

The second part of the verse explains the consequence of this divine will: “but they will always choose to differ.” The word “yazālūna” indicates a continuous state. Human beings, exercising their free will, will persistently disagree and differ in their beliefs, opinions, and religions. This diversity is part of the human condition, a result of the gift of choice. This verse does not celebrate disagreement as something good in itself, but it acknowledges it as an inevitable reality of the world Allah created. It serves to manage the expectations of the Prophet and the believers. They should not be overly distressed that people do not all accept Islam; even with the clearest proofs and the most sincere efforts, differences will remain because of human free will and stubbornness.

This is not a failure of the message but a feature of the test. The verse also subtly refutes the disbelievers who argued that if Allah truly wanted them to worship Him alone, He would have forced them. The response is that Allah’s will is not to force belief but to test. The differences that arise are a platform for the exercise of patience, justice, and the ultimate divine judgment. The verse thus prepares the believers to accept the reality of pluralism in beliefs, while remaining steadfast in their own truth, leaving the judgment of the differing parties to Allah on the Day of Gathering.

=119=

The previous verse’s statement that humanity will always differ is now qualified by a crucial exception. “Except those shown mercy by your Lord” refers to the believers, those whom Allah has guided to the truth and granted the mercy of faith and unity. They are the exception to the general rule of human discord. While the majority of people differ and dispute, a specific group, by Allah’s mercy, is united upon the truth. This mercy is not random; it is granted to those who sincerely seek the truth and submit to it.

The phrase “and so He created them” has been interpreted in two complementary ways by scholars. Some say it means that Allah created the people of mercy specifically for mercy and Paradise. Others say it refers to the whole of humanity, meaning that Allah created them with the capacity to differ and to choose, and this very creation is the arena for the manifestation of His mercy and justice.

The verse then reveals the inevitable outcome of this design: “And so the Word of your Lord will be fulfilled: ‘I will surely fill up Hell with jinn and humans all together.’” This is the decree that Allah pronounced at the beginning of creation. Hell will be populated by those jinn and humans who chose to follow Satan and reject the divine guidance.

The mention of “jinn and humans” together emphasizes that the test applies to both species of accountable beings, and the punishment is not for one without the other. This verse is stark and serves as a sobering conclusion to the discussion of free will and difference. It explains the ultimate consequence of the choice to differ from the truth: Hell will be filled. For the believers, this verse inspires immense gratitude for the mercy that saved them from being among the fuel of Hell. It also instills a reverent fear, reminding them that they are only saved by divine mercy, not by their own merit.

For the disbelievers, it is a final warning that their choice to persist in difference will lead them to that very Fire that has been promised to be filled.

=120=

The surah approaches its concluding section by reflecting on the very purpose of the narratives that have filled its central chapters. “And We relate to you O Prophet the stories of the messengers” refers to the detailed accounts of Noah, Hûd, Ṣâliḥ, Abraham, Lot, Shu’aib, and Moses that have been recited.

The word “naquṣṣu” means to narrate in sequence with care and precision. The stories are not mere history; they are a divine communication designed for a specific purpose: “to reassure your heart. ” The Arabic word “thabbita” means to make firm, to strengthen, to steady. The Prophet’s heart, facing constant rejection, mockery, and the burden of conveying the message, needed this firmness. By seeing that all the previous messengers faced the same trials—the same accusations of lying, madness, and sorcery; the same demands for punishment; the same small following of the weak and poor—his heart was reassured that he was on the right path, that the struggle was universal, and that the final victory belonged to the righteous.

The verse then states that this surah contains more than just stories: “And there has come to you in this surah the truth, a warning to the disbelievers, and a reminder to the believers. ” The word “al-ḥaqq” is the absolute truth: the reality of tawḥīd, the certainty of resurrection, the justice of divine judgment. The surah delivers a “warning,” maw‘iẓah, a severe admonition that should shake the disbelievers from their heedlessness. And it delivers a “reminder,” dhikrā, a beneficial recollection for the believers that strengthens their faith and guides their actions. This three-fold description captures the comprehensive nature of the Quranic message: it is truth that confronts falsehood, warning that alarms the negligent, and reminder that comforts and guides the faithful. For the believers in Mecca, this verse was a validation of their faith; they were the recipients of a divine reminder, while their opponents were the targets of a divine warning. It also elevated the status of the surah, marking it as a self-contained treasure of guidance. The verse beautifully transitions the surah from its narrative core to its final practical commands and declarations, grounding the entire message in the steadfastness and reassurance of the Prophet’s heart.
