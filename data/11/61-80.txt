=61=

The surah now presents the third major narrative of a destroyed nation, the people of Thamûd, continuing the pattern established with Noah and ‘Âd. Thamûd were the successors to ‘Âd in the land, dwelling in the region of al-Ḥijr in northwestern Arabia, carving magnificent homes out of the mountains. They inherited a great civilization but also the arrogance that came with it. The verse opens, “And to the people of Thamûd We sent their brother Ṣâliḥ. ” The designation “their brother” stresses that he was one of them, known for his truthfulness and noble character, just as Hûd and Noah were. This commonality removes any excuse of unfamiliarity. Ṣâliḥ begins his call with exactly the same uncompromising message as every messenger: “O my people! Worship Allah. You have no god other than Him. ” The declaration is absolute. There is no room for intermediaries, no sharing of worship, no negotiation about the fundamental principle of tawḥīd. The idols they had carved from the very mountains they lived in were utterly false. Then Ṣâliḥ draws their attention to a specific blessing that should have been obvious to them: “He is the One Who produced you from the earth and settled you on it. ” This is a reminder that their very origin is from the earth, their bodies created from its elements, and that Allah caused them to emerge as a human community. He then “settled” them on the earth, making it a habitable home, giving them mastery over its resources and the ability to build those very mountains into dwellings. The logical conclusion is that the One who originated them and established them is the One who deserves their worship. Ṣâliḥ then extends the same merciful invitation that was given earlier: “So seek His forgiveness and turn to Him in repentance.” Istighfār and tawbah are the gateway to every good. He immediately follows this with an encouragement that highlights Allah’s closeness: “Surely my Lord is Ever Near, All-Responsive to prayers.” Allah is qarīb, near, not distant and uninvolved. He is mujīb, the One who answers the call of those who call upon Him.

This is a beautiful combination: the Lord who created them from the earth is intimately near and ready to forgive and respond. For the people of Thamûd, this was a powerful invitation to abandon their idols and turn directly to a merciful, attentive God.

This verse also teaches believers today that Allah is not a remote, indifferent deity; He is near and responsive, a truth that makes sincere repentance a hopeful and joyful act rather than a despairing one.

=62=

The response of Thamûd reflects the specific disappointment of a people who had expected their righteous man to conform. They address him with a tone of disillusionment: “O Ṣâliḥ! We truly had high hopes in you before this.” Before his prophethood, Ṣâliḥ was highly respected and trusted. They had anticipated he would become a prominent leader, a wise elder who would reinforce their traditions and perhaps lead them to greater worldly glory. His sudden call to abandon their ancestral religion shattered those expectations.

The phrase “before this” refers to the message of tawḥīd that he now brought. This is a common human reaction: when someone known for goodness departs from a community’s accepted norms, the disappointment can be sharper than the reaction to an obvious enemy. They then articulate their core objection: “How dare you forbid us to worship what our forefathers had worshipped?

” The word “forbid” shows they viewed his message not as an invitation but as a restriction, a deprivation of their inherited rights. The worship of forefathers’ gods was a deeply ingrained cultural identity; to abandon it seemed like a betrayal of their ancestors.

This is the exact same argument used by the people of Noah and by the Quraysh. Tradition becomes a fortress against truth.

The verse concludes with their confession of doubt: “We are certainly in alarming doubt about what you are inviting us to.” The word “alarming,” murīb, describes a doubt that disturbs and unsettles. Their doubt, however, was not an honest intellectual quest; it was a veil for their unwillingness to sacrifice pride and social conformity. They could not bring themselves to accept that their entire civilization was built on falsehood. This verse exposes a recurring human weakness: preferring familiar falsehood over disruptive truth. It challenges believers to examine whether they cling to inherited customs or opinions simply because they are familiar, even when clear truth calls for change. It also consoles those who face rejection from people who once respected them; the truth can turn admirers into opponents, but this is the path of the prophets.

=63=

Ṣâliḥ’s reply mirrors the responses of Noah and Hûd, grounding his mission in divine authority rather than personal opinion. He begins with the gentle and appealing address, “O my people,” and invites them to use their reason: “Consider if I stand on a clear proof from my Lord.” The “clear proof,” bayyinah, is the revelation and the signs of his prophethood, including the she-camel that will be mentioned shortly. He is not speculating; he is standing on a firm foundation of certainty from Allah.

The phrase “and He has blessed me with a mercy from Himself” refers to the gift of prophethood and divine guidance. This mercy is a direct favor from Allah, something that cannot be earned but is granted. Ṣâliḥ is not claiming personal superiority; he is acknowledging that any truth he brings is a blessing from his Lord. He then asks a rhetorical question that reveals his fear of Allah: “Who could help me against Allah if I were to disobey Him?” If he were to abandon the clear proof, hide the truth, or compromise the message to please his people, he would be guilty of disobedience to the Almighty. In that case, no one—not his people, not their idols, not any worldly power—could protect him from divine punishment. This shows that his unwavering stance is not stubbornness but a profound sense of accountability. He knows the stakes are eternal, and he will not trade his soul for their approval. He ends with a sober assessment: “You would only contribute to my doom. ” If he listened to them and neglected his duty, they would not save him on the Day of Judgment; they would only be the cause of his destruction.

This verse teaches that the true caller to Allah never compromises the message out of fear of people or desire to please them, because the ultimate help and protection come only from Allah. The believers in Mecca learned from this that the disapproval of the Quraysh could not compare to the danger of disobeying their Lord.

=64=

Ṣâliḥ now presents the specific sign that would be the test for his people. “And O my people! This she-camel of Allah is a sign for you.” According to exegetical reports, the people of Thamûd had challenged Ṣâliḥ to produce a miracle, specifically asking for a pregnant she-camel to emerge from a rock. By Allah’s permission, a huge, unique she-camel miraculously emerged from the mountain, a clear sign that Ṣâliḥ was a true messenger. She is called “the she-camel of Allah,” nāqat Allāh, a phrase that elevates her status as a direct creation and a test from the Lord. The possessive “of Allah” indicates she is under His special protection and her presence is a divine trust. The command is clear: “So leave her to graze freely on Allah’s earth and do her no harm. ” The she-camel was to roam and drink from the available water, and she had a designated drinking day while the people had another. This arrangement was a test of their obedience and their willingness to share resources according to divine command rather than their own whims. The earth is described as “Allah’s earth,” reminding them that all resources ultimately belong to Him, not to them. The warning that follows is stark: “or a swift punishment will overtake you! ” The word “swift” indicates that the punishment would be immediate and without delay. This was not an idle threat. The entire community was placed under a probation; harming the camel would trigger a collective doom. This miracle was a manifestation of Allah’s mercy—giving them a tangible sign—and also His justice—attaching a severe consequence to rebellion. The lesson for the Quraysh was clear: they were demanding visible miracles, but such signs, if given, become a binding test with disastrous consequences if rejected.

The story teaches that signs are given according to divine wisdom, and when they come, they must be respected. Any violation of a sacred trust from Allah invites swift and decisive retribution.

=65=

The verse records the tragic and defiant act that sealed their doom. “But they killed her” is a short, devastating statement. Despite the clear command, the clear sign, and the clear warning, the people of Thamûd, led by their most wretched members, hamstrung and slaughtered the miraculous she-camel. This was not a casual slip; it was a deliberate, collective rebellion against the divine command, an act of open mockery and defiance. They had crossed the line. Immediately, Ṣâliḥ pronounced the consequence: “You have only three more days to enjoy life in your homes—this is an unfailing promise!” The specification of “three days” is precise and absolute. It was not an estimate but a divine revelation informing him of the exact timeframe before the punishment.

The phrase “enjoy life in your homes” is deeply ironic. The homes they had so proudly carved from the mountains, symbols of their skill and security, would become their graves. Their enjoyment was now limited to a mere three days, a fleeting respite that would end in utter destruction.

The word “unfailing promise” emphasizes that this was a decree from Allah, not a threat from Ṣâliḥ, and it would come to pass without any possibility of delay or error. Some narrations mention that they even plotted to kill Ṣâliḥ during those three days, adding to their criminal obstinacy. The three days gave them a chance to reflect and perhaps repent, but their hearts were too hardened. For the Meccans who were persecuting the Prophet, this verse was a clear warning: their mockery and their demands for punishment could lead to a similar fixed term after which no escape is possible. It also teaches that while Allah is Forbearing and gives many chances, there is a point of no return. When a society collectively violates a sacred command with impunity, the clock of divine judgment begins to tick. The apparent delay should not be mistaken for neglect.

=66=

The execution of the decree unfolds exactly according to the established pattern. “When Our command came” signals the arrival of the promised punishment after the three days expired. The nature of that punishment will be detailed in the next verse, but here the emphasis is on the salvation of the righteous. “We saved Ṣâliḥ and those who believed with him by a mercy from Us. ” Just as Noah and Hûd were rescued, Ṣâliḥ and his believing community were saved from the destruction that engulfed their people. The rescue is attributed to “a mercy from Us,” underscoring that salvation, even for the prophets, is not a matter of personal right but of divine grace. The believers were not saved because of their own power or because they happened to be in a safe location; Allah directly intervened to protect them.

The verse adds, “and spared them the disgrace of that Day. ” The “Day” here refers to the day of the blast that destroyed Thamûd. The believers were not only saved from death but also from the humiliation and terror that accompanied it. They witnessed the destruction of their people from a position of safety and divine honor, not shame.

This is a powerful reassurance: the believer may be mocked and marginalized in this life, but when judgment comes, they will be spared the disgrace that falls upon the rejecters. The verse then concludes with a divine affirmation: “Surely your Lord alone is the All-Powerful, Almighty.” The two names, ‘Azīz (All-Powerful) and Dhū Intiqām (Almighty or Possessor of Retribution), emphasize that Allah’s power is unchallenged and that He enacts justice upon those who rebel. The Meccan believers, who felt powerless against the Quraysh, were reminded that their Lord is the truly Powerful, and His retribution is certain. This verse connects the salvation of the believers directly to Allah’s power, not to worldly means, strengthening their reliance on Him alone.

=67=

The method of destruction is now specified, and it is terrifyingly sudden and complete. “And the mighty blast overtook the wrongdoers.

” The Arabic word “ṣayḥah” refers to a terrifying, thunderous cry or blast. Some reports say it was an angelic shout, others a cosmic sound that shattered everything. It was a single, overwhelming noise that came with the divine command.

The word “overtook” suggests that it seized them unexpectedly, giving them no time to flee or react. It came in the early morning, according to other Quranic verses, perhaps after their three nights of defiant plotting. The victims are identified as “the wrongdoers,” al-ẓālimīn, those who committed the ultimate injustice of shirk and the killing of the she-camel. This specific designation shows that the punishment was not a random natural disaster but a targeted divine justice. The result of the blast is described in stark terms: “so they fell lifeless in their homes.

” The word “jāthimīn” paints a picture of bodies collapsed on the ground, motionless, as if struck down where they stood. Their homes, the magnificent palaces carved from the rock, became their tombs. The very dwellings that symbolized their strength and permanence could not shield them from a single sound from their Lord. This demonstrates the absolute fragility of human constructions before divine power. For the Quraysh, who lived in their own strong houses and felt secure in Mecca, this was a sobering parallel. Their homes would not protect them if they persisted in wrongdoing. The verse strips away any illusion of safety attached to material structures or worldly strength. It reinforces that the ultimate security is only with Allah and that His punishment, when it comes, can penetrate any fortress and reduce any power to instant silence. The brevity and vividness of the verse make it a powerful reminder of the end of arrogance.

=68=

The aftermath of the blast is described with a haunting image. “As if they had never lived there” emphasizes the totality of their eradication. The homes were still standing, the buildings and carvings were still there, but the people were gone as though they had never inhabited those spaces. There was no lingering trace of their bustling life, their laughter, their pride—only silence and ruins. This phrase conveys the idea that a civilization, no matter how deeply rooted it appears, can be wiped away so completely that it leaves behind no living memory except in the stories of warning.

The verse then delivers a summary judgment similar to the one given for ‘Âd: “Surely Thamûd denied their Lord.” The root crime is identified once again. It was not merely that they killed the she-camel or rejected Ṣâliḥ; they denied their Lord, the One who created and sustained them. Kufr, denial, is the source from which all their other sins flowed. The declaration “so away with Thamûd!” is a divine verdict of rejection.

The word “bu‘dan,” away with them, is a curse of distance and expulsion from mercy. It is as if Allah Himself pronounces their eternal banishment.

The phrase echoes the ending of the ‘Âd narrative and creates a rhythmic warning in the surah: ‘Âd, then Thamûd, each met the same fate because of the same crime. For the Quraysh, who were listening to these stories being recited, the implication was unavoidable. They were the next in line if they did not repent.

The verse also serves as a timeless reminder that the ruins of destroyed civilizations are not just archaeological curiosities; they are signs from Allah, lessons etched in stone and sand. A believer walking through the world sees these reminders and understands that the denial of the Lord leads to utter ruin, both in this world and in the Hereafter. The story of Thamûd, thus concluded, transitions the surah toward the story of Abraham, where mercy and glad tidings will take center stage after the recurring themes of warning and destruction.

=69=

The surah shifts its tone from the relentless destruction of the defiant nations to a narrative filled with mercy, hospitality, and intimate divine communication. The story of Abraham, Ibrāhīm, the father of prophets, now begins. “And surely Our messenger-angels came to Abraham with good news of a son.” These angels were the same beings who had been sent to execute the punishment on the people of Lot, but before that mission, they visited Abraham with a blessed announcement. The “good news” was the prophecy of a son, specifically Isaac, as will be clarified later, and also the continuation of prophethood through his lineage. The angels appeared in human form, and they greeted him with the word “Salāman,” Peace! Abraham, the epitome of hospitality, responded with a better greeting: “Peace be upon you.” This demonstrates his refined character and his immediate, warm reception of strangers. The verse highlights his swift and generous hospitality: “Then it was not long before he brought them a fat, roasted calf.

” The phrase “it was not long” indicates that Abraham did not hesitate or wait to see if the guests would stay; he immediately went to prepare a meal, choosing a fat, high-quality calf and roasting it for them. He did not yet know they were angels; he assumed they were human travelers. This act of serving the best food to unknown guests is a model of prophetic generosity. It also shows that hospitality is a hallmark of true faith. Abraham’s action is so exemplary that it is emulated in the sunnah of the Prophet Muhammad, who also emphasized honoring guests. The scene sets a warm, familial tone that contrasts with the harshness of the previous stories. The surah is about to reveal profound truths through Abraham’s household: the joy of a promised child, the intercession for a sinful people, and the limits of divine mercy when decree is issued. This verse thus serves as a beautiful opening, teaching that before the test of faith comes, sometimes there is the blessing of unexpected guests bearing divine tidings, and the proper response is generosity, peace, and swift service.

=70=

The atmosphere of warm hospitality suddenly shifts to tension. Abraham, having presented the roasted calf, noticed something unusual: “when he saw that their hands did not reach for the food, he became suspicious and fearful of them.” In ancient Arab culture, if a guest refused to eat, it was a sign that they did not come in peace; they might be enemies or harbingers of ill intent. The angels, being spiritual beings, did not consume physical food, and their abstention immediately signaled to Abraham’s keen perception that these were no ordinary travelers.

The word “nākira” translated as “suspicious” means an inner sense of unease, a feeling that something is wrong. This quickly turned to “khīfah,” fear. He suspected that they might have come with some evil purpose, and his fear was instinctive, given the sudden strangeness of the situation. The angels, seeing his distress, responded immediately: “They reassured him, ‘Do not be afraid!’” Their first words were to calm his heart. Then they revealed their identity and mission: “We are angels sent only against the people of Lot.

” The word “only” is crucial; their mission was specific and targeted. They were not sent to harm Abraham or his household. This disclosure transformed Abraham’s fear into understanding, though it would soon be replaced by a different kind of concern—not for himself but for his nephew Lot and his people. This verse illustrates the human side of prophethood; even the intimate friend of Allah, Khalīl Allāh, experienced fear and needed reassurance. It also shows the angels’ respect for Abraham; they did not leave him in a state of anxiety. For the believers, this is a comfort: the angels of mercy and punishment are under Allah’s command and do not overstep their bounds.

The verse also sets the stage for the next part of the narrative: Abraham will soon begin to plead for Lot’s people, demonstrating his immense mercy and compassion, but he will also learn the limits of intercession when the divine decree is final.

=71=

The scene now includes Sarah, Abraham’s wife, who was standing nearby, perhaps serving or observing the conversation. The verse says, “And his wife was standing by, so she laughed.” There are various interpretations of her laughter. Some say she laughed out of joy and relief when she understood the angels were not a threat and that her husband’s fear was unfounded. Others say she laughed in astonishment at the news she was about to receive.

The Arabic word “ḍaḥikat” can also be read as “she menstruated,” according to some non-standard readings, but the majority of scholars affirm the meaning of laughter as consistent with her reaction of surprise. Immediately, the verse continues: “then We gave her good news of the birth of Isaac, and, after him, Jacob.” The good news, bushra, is now delivered directly to Sarah, indicating her central role in the miracle. She was old and barren, and Abraham was an old man. The promise of a child under such circumstances was a supernatural announcement. The angels specified Isaac, and not only Isaac, but that Isaac would survive, grow, and have a son of his own—Jacob—thus ensuring the continuation of the prophetic lineage.

This promise of a son and a grandson was immense. It gave Sarah the assurance that her line would be blessed with prophethood. This moment is one of the most tender in the Quran: an elderly, previously barren woman being told she will be a mother and a grandmother of prophets.

The sequence of the narrative teaches that after fear comes glad tidings for the righteous. Abraham’s initial fear turned into the joy of a promised family. It also connects to the earlier pattern of mercy following hardship; Sarah’s lifelong barrenness was now ending with a gift beyond imagination. For the believers, especially those who suffer from the delay of answered prayers, this verse is a reminder that Allah’s gifts can arrive in the most unexpected ways and at the most unexpected times, and His blessings can extend to future generations.

=72=

Sarah’s immediate reaction to the glad tidings is a mixture of astonishment and incredulity, but not disbelief in Allah’s power. The exclamation “Oh, my!” translates “Yā waylatā,” an expression of great surprise used by the Arabs when something extraordinary occurs. Her rhetorical question, “How can I have a child in this old age, and my husband here is an old man?” is a natural human reaction. She mentions both her own old age and Abraham’s, emphasizing that from every observable physical standpoint, such an event was impossible. She was barren, past the age of childbearing, and Abraham was also advanced in years. Her question is similar to that of Abraham himself on another occasion, or of Zechariah when he received news of John. It is not a rejection of the news but an expression of amazement at the way Allah’s decree operates outside the normal laws of nature. She then sums up the situation: “This is truly an astonishing thing! ” The word “‘ajīb” indicates something that goes beyond the ordinary, something that inspires wonder. It is the recognition of a miracle in the making.

This verse reveals the authenticity of human emotion in the Quran; the prophets and their families were not immune to shock or the feeling of improbability. Their faith was tested by circumstances that seemed to contradict the promise, yet their astonishment was directed at the wonder of the act, not at the ability of the Giver. For the believers, this is a profound lesson in how to receive what seems impossible from a worldly perspective: we can acknowledge the outward impossibility while fully trusting in Allah’s absolute power.

The verse also highlights the beauty of marital partnership in receiving divine blessings; Sarah’s reaction includes her husband, “my husband here is an old man,” showing they are together in this extraordinary event, sharing in the astonishment and the joy that will follow. The angels will now respond to her wonder with a lesson about Allah’s decree.

=73=

The angels address Sarah’s wonder with a gentle but profound correction that elevates her perspective from the physical to the divine. “Are you astonished by Allah’s decree?

” The word “amr Allāh,” the command or decree of Allah, refers to His will and creative power. The angels are reminding her that Allah’s decrees are not bound by human limitations. When Allah decrees a thing, He simply says “Be,” and it is. Old age and barrenness are no obstacle to the One who created the heavens and the earth. This gentle rhetorical question redirects awe from the miracle itself to the One who performed it. Astonishment should not be at the impossibility of the event but at the majesty and power of the decree. Immediately following this teaching, they bestow upon the household a beautiful blessing: “May Allah’s mercy and blessings be upon you, O people of this house.” The address, “Ahl al-Bayt,” the people of this house, encompasses Abraham, Sarah, and their future prophetic lineage. The angels invoke upon them raḥmah (mercy) and barakāt (blessings), a comprehensive prayer that covers all spiritual and material good.

This is a greeting of honor and a prophecy of the barakah that will flow through their descendants, including the final prophet Muhammad. The verse closes with a statement of praise: “Indeed, He is Praiseworthy, All-Glorious.” Ḥamīd means that Allah is inherently and perpetually praised, regardless of whether His creation praises Him. Majīd means He is full of glory, majesty, and honor. The connection is clear: the One who is so praised and glorious is certainly capable of granting such a miraculous gift, and His acts are always worthy of praise. This verse is a treasure for believers facing seemingly impossible situations. It teaches us to shift our gaze from the obstacle to the decree of Allah, and to remember that His mercy and blessings are the ultimate provision for any household that remains faithful.

The mention of “the people of this house” also connects to the later Islamic veneration of the Prophet’s household, showing the honored place of a family dedicated to Allah’s service.

=74=

The narrative returns to Abraham after the interlude of Sarah’s glad tidings. The emotional state of Abraham is described: “after the fear had left Abraham, and the good news had reached him.” The fear he initially felt upon seeing the angels not eat has now dissipated, replaced by the joy of the promised son. His heart is settled, his mind is clear, and now the angels’ stated mission against the people of Lot comes to the forefront of his attention. Abraham immediately shifts from personal joy to concern for others. He “began to plead with Us for the people of Lot.

” The word “yujādilunā,” translated as plead, implies an earnest, back-and-forth argument or dispute, though done with reverence. Abraham knew the angels were sent with a punishment, and his heart ached for his nephew Lot and his people. Despite their notorious wickedness, Abraham’s mercy and compassion compelled him to intercede. He hoped to avert the punishment or to lessen its severity. This pleading is an expression of his profound humanitarian concern. Some scholars mention that he argued that there might be believers among them, or that Lot was among them, or that perhaps they could repent. Abraham’s intercession shows that a believer should never be indifferent to the fate of others, even those who are sinful. He is a model of caring deeply for humanity.

However, this intercession also sets the stage for an important lesson: there are limits to intercession when the divine decree of destruction has been issued. The angels will soon gently rebuke him. This moment also highlights the stark contrast between the intimate blessings on Abraham’s household and the impending doom on Lot’s people. The two realities exist side by side, and a prophet lives in the tension between divine mercy and divine justice. For the Prophet Muhammad, who similarly wished to save his people from punishment, this was a relatable scene. He too pleaded for his ummah, and he too was told that certain decrees are final.

The verse teaches that intercession is a noble act, but it must ultimately submit to Allah’s perfect wisdom.

=75=

In a beautiful parenthetical note, the Quran explains why Abraham engaged in such pleading. “Truly, Abraham was forbearing, tender-hearted, and ever turning to his Lord.” These three qualities form a portrait of his inner character.

The first, “forbearing,” translates ḥalīm, which means he was extremely patient, slow to anger, and able to bear the ignorance and provocation of others without harshness. This forbearance is what made him able to argue gently on behalf of a wicked people rather than simply demand their destruction.

The second quality, “tender-hearted,” translates awwāh, a word rich in meaning. It implies a heart that is deeply sensitive, often sighing or expressing sorrow out of compassion and fear of Allah. An awwāh is one who feels the pain of others intensely and is moved to pray for them. Some commentators also include the meaning of being frequent in supplication and humble before Allah.

The third quality, “ever turning to his Lord,” translates munīb, meaning he was constantly returning to Allah in repentance and devotion, orienting his entire being toward his Creator. These three traits together made Abraham uniquely suited to plead for mercy. His forbearance prevented him from judging hastily; his tender-heartedness made him grieve for the sinners; his constant turning to Allah meant his intercession was rooted in humility, not in challenging divine justice. This verse is a profound insight into the prophetic personality. It shows that the best of believers are not harsh, judgmental, or indifferent to the suffering of others, even wrongdoers. Rather, they are patient, deeply empathetic, and always connected to Allah. For the believers, it provides a model of character: to be forbearing with people’s faults, to have a soft heart that feels the spiritual danger of others, and to constantly turn to Allah. It also consoles the Prophet Muhammad, whose similar tenderness for his people caused him grief; he is following in the footsteps of his father Abraham, whose heart ached even for Lot’s doomed community. The verse is a perfect interlude that enriches the narrative with psychological and spiritual depth.

=76=

The angels now respond to Abraham’s intercession with firmness, but also with respect and clarification. They address him by name: “O Abraham! Plead no more!

” The phrase “a‘riḍ ‘an hādhā” literally means “turn away from this,” indicating that he should stop his pleading. This is not a harsh reprimand but a merciful instruction. Abraham’s compassionate nature was leading him to continue arguing, but the angels inform him that the matter has passed beyond the stage where intercession could alter the outcome. The reason is given: “Your Lord’s decree has already come.” The divine word, the qaḍā’, has been issued, and the punishment is inevitable. Allah’s decree is based on His infinite knowledge and wisdom, and it has now reached the point of execution. The angels are messengers of that decree; they cannot change it, nor can Abraham’s pleading reverse it.

The second part of their statement emphasizes the certainty and nature of the coming torment: “and they will certainly be afflicted with a punishment that cannot be averted!” The word “ātiyā” emphasizes that the punishment is coming without a doubt, and “lā maradda lahu” means there is no turning it back, no repelling it. It is irreversible.

This verse teaches a profound truth about the balance between divine mercy and divine justice. Allah’s mercy is vast, and He welcomes intercession in many matters.

However, when a people’s rebellion reaches a terminal point and the decree of destruction is finalized, even the most beloved prophet cannot avert it. This is not because Allah is unmerciful, but because His justice requires that persistent, unrepentant evil be punished. For Abraham, the lesson was to trust his Lord’s decree even when his own tender heart wished otherwise. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a reminder that some rejecters, like Abu Jahl, would not be guided, and he should not exhaust himself in grief over them.

The verse also teaches believers to accept the limits of their ability to help others. We should plead and pray, but when Allah’s decree manifests, we must submit, knowing that His decision is based on knowledge we do not fully possess. It is a lesson in the surrender that underlies true faith.

=77=

The scene shifts from the tent of Abraham to the city of Lot, where the angels, now in the form of exceptionally handsome young men, arrive. “When Our messenger-angels came to Lot, he was distressed and worried by their arrival.

” The word “sī’a” indicates that he felt immediate distress, and “ḍāqa bihim dhar‘an” expresses a sense of being severely troubled and constrained, as if his capacity to handle the situation was overwhelmed. Lot knew his people’s depravity; they would openly assault male travelers. He saw the extraordinary beauty of the visitors and immediately understood the grave danger they were in. He also felt the weight of his responsibility as a host, obligated to protect his guests in a city where no one would help him. The angels, still in human form, did not immediately reveal their identity to him, which added to his anxiety. His first utterance, “This is a terrible day,” reflects his inner turmoil. He sensed that a major crisis was unfolding. This was not an ordinary challenge; it was a confrontation with the worst evil of his community, and he feared he would be unable to shield his guests from harm.

This verse reveals the human vulnerability of the prophets. Lot was a righteous man living among the wicked, and his distress shows that he was not desensitized to evil. He felt its weight acutely. It also highlights the isolation of a believer in a corrupt society; he had no strong clan to back him, and he felt the crushing pressure of standing alone against a mob. For the Prophet Muhammad, this was a mirrored image: he was one man in Mecca facing the threats of the Quraysh, his own people, who were morally corrupt in their idolatry and oppression.

The verse teaches that feeling distressed by evil is a sign of living faith, and that even prophets faced moments of overwhelming anxiety. However, Lot’s distress will soon be met with divine assistance, showing that Allah’s help arrives at the precise moment of need, often from unseen sources.

=78=

The crisis escalates rapidly. The verse describes the approach of the mob: “And the men of his people—who were used to shameful deeds—came to him rushing.

” The phrase “who were used to shameful deeds” establishes their habitual corruption. Their sin was not a rare slip but a deeply ingrained practice, specifically the act of sodomy with men, which they had normalized and openly practiced. They “came to him rushing,” indicating an urgent, lustful stampede. They had heard about the beautiful guests and converged on Lot’s house without shame. Lot, in a desperate attempt to protect his guests and reform his people, makes a plea that must be understood in its historical and linguistic context. He says, “O my people! Here are my daughters for marriage—they are pure for you.” By “my daughters,” most commentators explain that he referred to the women of the community, as a prophet is like a father to his people. He was offering them the lawful, pure path of marriage with women, instead of their perverted, unlawful desires.

The word “pure” emphasizes the moral and legal cleanliness of that option. This was not, as some mistakenly think, offering his own biological daughters for illicit purposes; it was an invitation to the natural, God-given institution of marriage. He then pleads: “So fear Allah, and do not humiliate me by disrespecting my guests.” He appeals to their sense of taqwa, the fear of God, and to their sense of honor regarding guests. In Arab culture, the host’s honor was bound up with the safety of his guests. To harm a guest was the height of disgrace. His final desperate question, “Is there not even a single right-minded man among you?” is a cry of despair. “Rashīd” means a man of sound mind and moral compass. Lot is saying, in effect: has your evil so consumed you that not one man among you has any sense of decency left? This verse exposes the depths of societal corruption, where evil becomes so prevalent that it extinguishes even basic human decency. It also shows a prophet striving with every possible argument: a lawful alternative, fear of Allah, cultural honor, and appeal to reason. For the believers, it is a lesson in using multiple approaches when calling people to righteousness, even in the face of overwhelming opposition.

The mob’s response is brazen, shameless, and utterly dismissive of Lot’s pleas. The word “argued” translates “qālū,” which in this context is a defiant reply. They say, “You certainly know that we have no need for your daughters.

” This is a shocking declaration. They openly admit that they have no interest in the lawful, pure relationships Lot offered.

The phrase “no need” means they have no desire whatsoever for what is right and natural. Their lust has completely inverted their fitrah, their innate human disposition. They are not ignorant of the lawful option; they simply reject it with full knowledge.

The second sentence, “You already know what we desire,” is even more explicit and audacious. They are telling Lot that he is fully aware of their perverted lust for male intercourse. There is no attempt to hide it, no shame, no pretense. Their sin has become their public identity.

This is the hallmark of a society that has passed the point of moral recovery: sin is not committed secretly with guilt but openly with pride. The phrase shows that they expected Lot to simply stand aside and let them have their way. The verse serves as a chilling example of how desire, when followed unchecked, can corrupt human nature to the point where evil is not only pursued but celebrated. For the Quraysh, who did not engage in this specific sin, the lesson was broader: persistent denial of the messenger’s call and open mockery of the truth is a similar kind of brazen defiance that leads to destruction.

The verse also teaches that there is a limit to argumentation. When people openly declare their preference for what is forbidden, the time for debate is over, and only divine judgment remains. Lot now realizes that human effort cannot stop this mob, and his attention turns inward to his own helplessness, which will be addressed in the next verse.

Lot’s final words to the mob, before the angels intervene, express his complete sense of powerlessness and isolation. “If only I had the strength to resist you” translates his wish for physical power, for a force that could physically repel the mob and protect his household and guests. He was an outsider in the city, having migrated there with his family, and he had no clan of his own to back him up. In the tribal society of the time, a man’s safety depended largely on the might of his clan, and Lot lacked that social protection.

The word “quwwah” indicates any form of strength, whether personal or military, and he felt utterly devoid of it. The second part, “or could rely on a strong supporter,” adds another dimension. “Rukn” means a pillar or support, and “shadīd” means strong. He wished he had a powerful ally, a tribe or group, who could shelter him and stand against the onslaught. This cry is not a complaint against Allah but an honest admission of his human limits. He is laying his vulnerability bare. What Lot did not yet realize was that the “strong supporter” he yearned for was already present in his home. The angels, who had not yet revealed their true nature or their power, were the very support that would make any earthly clan irrelevant. This moment of profound weakness becomes the perfect setup for the divine intervention that follows in the next verse. It is a universal human experience: the believer reaches the end of his own resources, and then Allah’s help descends. Lot’s feeling of helplessness was not a failure of faith; it was the soil in which divine aid would be planted. For the Prophet Muhammad, besieged in Mecca and later in the early days of Medina, this sentiment was deeply relatable.

The verse teaches that acknowledging one’s weakness and wishing for support is not incompatible with faith; it is the recognition of need that turns the heart fully to the only true Support. The answer to Lot’s unspoken prayer was about to be given by his heavenly guests.
