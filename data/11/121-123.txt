The surah now draws to its close with a powerful declaration of separation and resolve, addressed directly to the disbelievers of Mecca. The Prophet is commanded to “Say to those who disbelieve,” making this a public pronouncement. After all the arguments, proofs, and stories of the messengers that have filled this surah, the time for further debate has reached its limit. The disbelievers have heard the message, they have been warned about the fates of ‘Âd, Thamûd, Noah’s people, Lot’s people, Midian, and Pharaoh, and they have persistently rejected. Now the Prophet is to declare a clear parting of ways: “Persist in your ways; we will certainly persist in ours.

” The word “i‘malū” comes from ‘amal, meaning action or deed; it is translated here as “persist in your ways,” implying “continue acting as you have been.” This is not an endorsement of their disbelief but a recognition that they are determined in their path and will not change. The Prophet is to say: if you are set on your path of idolatry, denial, and opposition, then so be it. As for us, “we will certainly persist in ours,” the path of monotheism, obedience, and steadfastness.

This statement echoes the words of Shu’aib earlier in the surah: “O my people! Persist in your ways, for I too will persist in mine.” It carries the same tone of finality and mutual waiting. There is no compromise, no blending of the two ways. This declaration is both a warning and a release. It warns them that their actions will have consequences, and it releases the Prophet from any further obligation to convince them after he has conveyed the message clearly. It also protects the believers from the emotional drain of endless arguments; they can now focus on their own path rather than being consumed by the rejection of others. For the believers today, this verse teaches that there is a point where continued debate with obstinate deniers becomes fruitless, and one must turn one’s energy toward building and strengthening the community of faith. The separation is not born of hatred but of clarity, and it entrusts the outcome to Allah, which the next verse will emphasize.

=122=

This verse completes the mutual declaration of the previous one with an even sharper edge. “And wait!” translates the command “wantazirū,” meaning to wait and watch expectantly. The disbelievers had been challenging the Prophet, demanding the punishment, and mocking the warnings. They were, in their own way, “waiting” for some calamity to befall the believers or for the Prophet’s movement to fail. They thought time was on their side. The Prophet is commanded to tell them to wait—but then immediately adds, “Surely we too are waiting.” The believers are also in a state of anticipation, but their waiting is entirely different in nature. They are waiting for one of two beautiful outcomes: either the divine punishment will descend upon the disbelievers in this world, vindicating the truth of the message, or the believers will be granted victory and establishment, or if neither occurs in their lifetime, they await the ultimate judgment in the Hereafter where they will be rewarded and the disbelievers punished.

This is a classic prophetic stance: the messenger leaves the matter to Allah and waits for the divine decree, confident that it will be just and favorable for the righteous. The disbelievers wait in mockery and doubt; the believers wait with certainty and hope.

The word “muntazirūn” is in the active participle form, indicating a continuous state; the believers live in a perpetual state of watchful hope, not passive idleness. This verse also serves as a psychological weapon: it turns the tables on the mockers. They thought they were the ones waiting for the Prophet’s downfall, but the Prophet declares that he and his followers are also waiting for the fulfillment of Allah’s promise. The waiting period is a test for both sides. For the believers, it is a test of patience and steadfastness.

For the disbelievers, it is a period of respite that they mistake for security, until the sudden arrival of what they were warned about. The verse beautifully caps the long series of prophetic stories, because every messenger in the surah reached exactly this point—the moment of mutual waiting before the divine command descended. Noah waited, Hûd waited, Ṣâliḥ waited, Shu’aib waited, and in each case the wait ended with the salvation of the believers and the destruction of the rejecters.

This is the sunnah of Allah that will not change.

The final verse of Surah Hud is a majestic conclusion that ties together all the major themes of the surah into a powerful closing statement. The verse opens with a declaration of Allah’s exclusive knowledge: “To Allah alone belongs the knowledge of what is hidden in the heavens and the earth.

” The word “ghayb” encompasses everything that is absent from human perception: the future, the past events of the prophets, the secrets of hearts, the timing of the Day of Judgment, the decrees written in the Preserved Tablet. Throughout the surah, the Prophet has been given knowledge of unseen stories, and the disbelievers have been told that they do not know when the punishment will come. This opening reaffirms that all such knowledge is solely with Allah. No human, no soothsayer, no idol possesses it. Then the verse states the inevitable direction of all existence: “And to Him all matters are returned.” Every affair, every soul, every deed, every dispute, and every story ultimately goes back to Allah.

This is the eschatological anchor of the entire surah. The destroyed nations returned to Him, the prophets returned to Him, and the current believers and disbelievers will all return to Him for judgment.

This statement provides the ultimate reason for the two commands that follow: “So worship Him and put your trust in Him.” The logical sequence is profound. Since Allah alone knows the unseen and to Him is the final return, the only rational response is to dedicate worship exclusively to Him and to place complete trust in Him. Worship, ‘ibādah, is the ultimate expression of submission, and trust, tawakkul, is the ultimate expression of reliance on His wisdom and plan. These two commands are the twin pillars of the believer’s response to the entire message of the surah. The verse—and the surah—ends with a final, comforting, and cautioning note: “And your Lord is never unaware of what you do. ” The Arabic “bi-ghāfilin” negates any notion of heedlessness or neglect. Allah is fully aware of every action, every struggle, every injustice, and every act of patience. For the believers, this is an assurance that their efforts are seen and will be rewarded.

For the disbelievers, it is a warning that their deeds are recorded and will be recompensed. The surah thus closes by leaving the listener in the presence of the All-Knowing, All-Aware Lord, with a call to worship and trust, and the certainty that nothing escapes His sight. The entire passage of Surah Hud has been completed.
