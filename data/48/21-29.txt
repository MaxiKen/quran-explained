=21=

After promising the believers the immediate spoils of Khaybar, the Qur’an now opens their eyes to a far wider horizon. The phrase “other gains which are beyond your reach” refers to the future conquests that at that moment seemed impossible. The small community of Medina could not imagine that Persia and Byzantium would crumble before them, that the treasures of the great empires would flow into their hands, or that Islam would spread far beyond Arabia.

Yet Allah was already keeping these gains in store, safely reserved by His decree. The believers were not able to reach them by their own strength, but Allah, who is Most Capable of everything, would deliver them when the time was right. This verse is a profound lesson in the divine management of the Muslim community. It taught the companions not to measure their future by their present circumstances. The truce of Ḥudaibiyah had just shown them that apparent weakness was the seed of strength. The future conquests would prove that every promise of Allah comes true in ways they could never have engineered.

The phrase “Allah is Most Capable of everything” anchors this hope in the absolute power of the Almighty. No obstacle, no opposing empire, no vast distance can frustrate His will. For later generations, this verse instils a confident outlook. When the global position of Muslims seems weak, and the restoration of dignity appears out of reach, believers recall that the gains that seem “beyond your reach” are not beyond Allah’s power. He keeps them in store, and they will unfold according to His plan, provided the believers hold fast to the faith and the way of the Prophet.

This promise is not a call to passive waiting, but a motivation to work righteously, knowing that the results are guaranteed by the One for whom nothing is impossible.

=22=

This verse continues the theme of reassurance by addressing a lingering fear in the hearts of some believers: what if the Quraysh had broken the treaty immediately and attacked? The answer is definitive. Allah declares that if the disbelievers had chosen to fight, they would not have stood their ground.

The word “certainly flee” leaves no room for doubt. Their show of strength was hollow. The Quraysh, despite their pride and their superior numbers and weapons, would have turned their backs and run away.

The verse then adds that after fleeing, “they would never find any protector or helper.” This means that no ally, no idol, no tribal pact could have saved them from the consequences of their aggression. The protection they thought they had was an illusion; true protection comes only from Allah, and He had already decided to grant victory to His Messenger.

Historically, this prediction came true in multiple ways. When the Quraysh later broke the treaty, the conquest of Mecca happened almost without bloodshed, and the disbelievers surrendered. Their supposed protectors melted away.

The verse also serves a psychological purpose. It tells the Muslims that the peace they accepted at Ḥudaibiyah was not a sign of weakness. If battle had been the divine will, they would have triumphed. The fact that Allah chose peace was a matter of wisdom, not necessity. This teaches an enduring principle: the believer’s apparent restraint is often not a sign of powerlessness but of obedience to a higher strategy.

For Muslims today, the verse is a reminder that the enemies of faith, no matter how intimidating, are ultimately without real support. Their bluster will fail, and they will find no one to help them when Allah’s decree unfolds. It also nurtures an inner calm, freeing believers from the fear of worldly powers, because victory is not dependent on numbers but on the divine decision.

=23=

After stating that the disbelievers would flee if they fought, Allah grounds that promise in an unchangeable law. “Allah’s way” refers to the consistent pattern that He has decreed for the conflict between truth and falsehood. Throughout history, the forces of disbelief have appeared formidable, but when the moment of decisive confrontation arrives and the believers are sincere, the false forces collapse.

This is not random; it is a long-established sunnah that has operated since the time of earlier prophets. The verse then underscores its permanence: “And you will find no change in Allah’s way.” This means the law will not alter for anyone. It gives the believers a basis for certainty that does not depend on shifting circumstances.

In the context of Ḥudaibiyah, this verse answered those who thought the Muslims had suffered a setback. The battle did not happen because Allah, in His wisdom, delayed it, but if it had happened, the established way would have brought victory. The fact that the way includes moments of peace, truce, and testing is also part of the divine pattern.

This verse also connects to earlier Quranic declarations that “Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves.” The unchangeable way works both in victory and in defeat, depending on the state of the believers. For the companions, this was a powerful injection of confidence. For us, it teaches that we should study the patterns of Allah’s help throughout history to strengthen our faith. When believers uphold their covenant, strive sincerely, and trust in Allah, the outcome is guaranteed according to a fixed law, whether the victory appears as a peace treaty, a conquest, or spiritual dominance. No power on earth can alter that law.

=24=

This verse provides the specific historical detail of what occurred at Ḥudaibiyah. The tension had escalated to the brink of fighting. A group of Quraysh fighters had attacked the Muslim camp, but they were captured. The Prophet, however, released them, demonstrating a desire for peace. Allah says He is the One who held back the hands of the Quraysh from slaughtering the believers, and also held back the hands of the believers from the Quraysh. This mutual restraint was not a coincidence of human diplomacy; it was a direct act of divine intervention.

The phrase “after giving you the upper hand over a group of them” refers to the incident where around eighty armed men from Mecca came down from Mount Tan‘im intending a surprise attack, but the Muslims captured them. This brief upper hand was a clear sign that if battle had commenced, the believers would have prevailed.

Yet Allah chose to stop the bloodshed, and the prisoners were freed as a gesture of goodwill, paving the way for the treaty. The verse highlights that this restraint was all within Allah’s knowledge and plan. “And Allah is All-Seeing of what you do” reminds both sides that every intention, every movement, every secret thought was under His watch. For the believers, it was a testimony that their obedience and patience were seen, and for the disbelievers, that their aggression was not hidden. The practical lesson is profound: sometimes Allah withholds a conflict even when one side has the upper hand, because the resulting peace serves a greater purpose. It teaches believers not to rush into confrontation when restraint can bring about a lasting good. The wisdom of the Prophet in choosing peace over a skirmish was inspired and protected by Allah, and this model of preferring the long-term benefit over immediate satisfaction remains central to Islamic ethics.

=25=

This verse reveals the hidden divine wisdom behind the treaty that prevented the Muslims from entering Mecca that year. It identifies the Quraysh as the ones who “persisted in disbelief” and “hindered you from the Sacred Mosque,” preventing the sacrificial animals from being slaughtered at the Ka‘bah. This was a grievous act, and the believers felt deep pain.

The verse then discloses a merciful reason known only to Allah. In Mecca, there were believing men and women who had secretly embraced Islam and could not emigrate. The Muslims from Medina did not know their identities. If fighting had erupted or even if the Muslims had entered with force, “you might have trampled them underfoot,” meaning some of those hidden believers could have been unintentionally killed or harmed. The blood of a believer, even if shed unknowingly, would have brought a heavy burden and guilt. To preserve those unknown believers, Allah prevented the entry.

The phrase “That was so Allah may admit into His mercy whoever He wills” means that this preservation was a means to keep those believers safe so they could continue to grow in faith and eventually be admitted to His mercy. The verse then states a decisive principle: “Had those unknown believers stood apart,” meaning had they been clearly distinct and separate from the disbelievers, “We would have certainly inflicted a painful punishment on the disbelievers.” The punishment would have been the believers’ victorious entry or even military action. This teaches that Allah’s delay of punishment on a wrongdoing people can sometimes be due to the presence of righteous individuals among them, just as earlier nations were destroyed only after the good people were separated. The mercy towards the hidden believers outweighed the immediate desire for retaliation. This verse profoundly instructs believers that Allah’s decisions are always governed by a wider knowledge and a deeper mercy, and that preserving the lives and faith of even a small group of unknown believers is of immense importance in His sight.

=26=

This verse contrasts two internal states. On one side, the Quraysh negotiators displayed arrogant pride, described as “the pride of pre-Islamic ignorance.” This was the tribal haughtiness that refused to acknowledge the Prophet’s title “Messenger of Allah” in the treaty document, that looked down on the Muslims as inferior, and that stubbornly clung to ancestral ways. Their hearts were swollen with a sense of false superiority. On the other side, at that very tense moment, “Allah sent down His serenity upon His Messenger and the believers.” While the Quraysh boiled with arrogance, the believers were gifted with calmness and composure. This divine gift kept them from responding to insults with rage or breaking off negotiations. The serenity also “inspired them to uphold the declaration of faith. ” The declaration of faith here is the kalimah of taqwa, the word of piety, which includes the testimony that there is no god but Allah. They were reminded to cling to it, to let it define their response, and to trust that the honour they seemed to lose on paper was preserved by Allah. The verse says “for they were better entitled and more worthy of it.” The believers, by their very submission and faith, had the true right to this declaration; the Quraysh’s pride disqualified them from it.

The closing phrase, “And Allah has perfect knowledge of all things,” assures that He knew the sincerity of the believers and the insincerity of the disbelievers. Historically, this refers to the moment when the Quraysh objected to the phrase “In the name of Allah, the Most Compassionate, Most Merciful” and to the title “Muhammad, the Messenger of Allah.” The Prophet calmly instructed Ali to change the wording, a supreme act of restraint.

This verse teaches that when facing arrogant opposition, true dignity lies not in angry outbursts but in the serenity that Allah sends down to those who uphold their faith. The pride of ignorance leads to ruin, while the calm confidence of faith leads to lasting victory.

=27=

This verse brings tremendous comfort by confirming the truth of a dream the Prophet had seen before setting out from Medina. He had seen himself and his companions entering the Sacred Mosque in safety, with their heads shaved or hair shortened, performing the rites of ‘umrah without any fear. The treaty of Ḥudaibiyah seemed to shatter that dream, and the hypocrites even taunted the believers about it. But Allah here issues a powerful guarantee: “Allah will fulfil His Messenger’s vision in all truth. ” The insertion of “Allah willing” is a teaching to always tie future matters to the will of Allah, and it also showed that the fulfilment was certain because Allah Himself had willed it. The detailed description “some with heads shaved and others with hair shortened” mirrors exactly the practices of ‘umrah completion, leaving no ambiguity that the vision would be materially realised. “Without fear” confirms the state of security that would accompany that entry.

The verse then explains the divine wisdom behind the delay: “He knew what you did not know. ” This refers to the hidden believers in Mecca, the benefits of the treaty, and the timing that would make the entry peaceful and honourable. “So He first granted you the triumph at hand” links back to the opening of the surah. The immediate triumph at Ḥudaibiyah was the necessary precursor to the peaceful entry into Mecca the following year.

This verse teaches that when a divine promise seems delayed, it is never abandoned. The believers learned that Allah’s timing is perfect and that what appears as a denial is often a preparation for a more beautiful fulfilment. The vision was fulfilled in the seventh year of Hijrah during the ‘umrah of Qada’, exactly as described, and this strengthened the faith of the entire community in the truthfulness of the Prophet and the certainty of Allah’s words.

=28=

After describing the fulfilment of the vision and the victory, the surah places the entire mission in its cosmic context. The triumph at Ḥudaibiyah and the subsequent spread of Islam are not random historical events. They are the unfolding of a divine plan set in motion when Allah sent His Messenger with “right guidance and the religion of truth.” Right guidance is the Qur’an, the Sunnah, and the clear path that leads to Allah. The religion of truth is Islam itself, a faith built on pure monotheism, justice, and a complete way of life. The purpose of sending the Messenger with this guidance is that it should “prevail over all others.” The verb implies dominance and clear superiority, not necessarily by the sword at all times, but by the evident truth of its message, its moral and spiritual power, and ultimately its political and social manifestation. The treaty of Ḥudaibiyah was a critical step in this prevailing. By the time the Prophet passed away, Islam was the dominant force in Arabia, and within decades it reached far beyond. This verse is a promise that the religion of truth will ultimately triumph over every false religion and ideology. It does not promise that every individual will accept it, but that as a divinely revealed path, it will be established and recognised in its completeness.

The verse concludes, “And sufficient is Allah as a Witness. ” He testifies to the truth of His Messenger and His religion. When Allah is one’s witness, no other validation is needed.

For believers, this is a source of unshakable confidence. The success of the message does not depend on human approval or numerical strength. Allah, who is the Witness, guarantees its ultimate victory.

This verse also implies that those who reject the Prophet are not rejecting a mere man but are opposing a mission that Allah Himself bears witness to, a thought that gives pause to any sincere seeker of truth.

=29=

The final verse of the surah is a magnificent portrait of the Prophet and his community. It begins with the foundational testimony: “Muḥammad is the Messenger of Allah.” This simple statement is the core of faith. Then it describes the companions: “those with him are firm with the disbelievers and compassionate with one another. ” This beautiful balance of sternness against disbelief and softness among themselves is the hallmark of a community shaped by revelation. Their strength is not tyrannical; their mercy is not weak.

The verse then describes their worship: “You see them bowing and prostrating in prayer, seeking Allah’s bounty and pleasure.” Their entire orientation is toward Allah, their bodies bent in humility, their hearts yearning for His grace. This inward devotion leaves an outward trace: “The sign of brightness can be seen on their faces from the trace of prostrating in prayer.” This refers to a spiritual radiance, a light of purity and devotion that illuminates the face, a mark that is not a physical scar but a luminosity that others can sense. Then comes a stunning revelation: “This is their description in the Torah.

” The earlier scripture already contained a portrait of the followers of the final prophet, described with these very traits. The verse then gives “their parable in the Gospel.” In the teachings of Jesus, the community of believers is likened to a seed that puts forth its sprout, then strengthens, becomes thick, and stands firmly on its stem, filling the sowers with delight. This agricultural imagery captures the gradual growth of the Muslim community from its tiny, vulnerable beginning in Mecca to its eventual strength and stability. The sowers are the prophets and the righteous, who rejoice at the fulfilment of the divine promise. The purpose of this growth is revealed: “in this way Allah makes the believers a source of dismay for the disbelievers.” The strength and unity of the believers become a cause of anger and frustration for those who oppose the faith, because it shows that despite all efforts to crush it, the seed of Islam has become a mighty tree.

The surah then closes with the ultimate promise: “To those of them who believe and do good, Allah has promised forgiveness and a great reward.” The mention of both faith and righteous deeds reaffirms that the noble description applies fully to those whose actions match their belief, and their end is eternal bliss. This verse ties together the entire message of the surah, showing that the triumph, the serenity, and the future victories are all part of a divine plan for a community that embodies strength, worship, and mercy, a community foretold in ancient scriptures, and destined for forgiveness and a great reward.
