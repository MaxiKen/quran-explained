=41=

After the comforting and beautiful descriptions of the foremost and the people of the right, the surah now turns to the third and most tragic group: the people of the left. The exclamation “how miserable will they be” is a direct echo of the earlier introduction in verse nine, but now it is repeated with even greater weight because the listener has just heard in detail about the bliss of the saved. The contrast is intentional and devastating. To understand the depth of misery, one must first contemplate the height of joy, and that is exactly what the Quran has done. The people of the left are those who will receive their record in their left hand or from behind their backs, a sign of their complete loss and humiliation. The rhetorical question embedded in “how miserable” invites the listener to imagine the worst possible state of suffering, and then realize that the reality will be far worse. In Arabic, the word for miserable conveys wretchedness, ruin, and a condition of being utterly undone.

This is not a mild discomfort; it is a complete and total catastrophe for those who fall into this group. The verse also carries a profound warning. Every human being listening to these words is still in the phase of choice. The division into groups has not yet happened. By emphasizing the misery of the left, the Quran is sounding an alarm, urging people to examine their lives and ensure that they are not on a path that leads to this destination.

The earlier descriptions of Paradise were an invitation of hope; this description of misery is a warning of fear, and both are necessary for a balanced spiritual psychology.

=42=

The surah begins to detail the punishment of the people of the left by describing the environment they will inhabit. They will be in “scorching heat” and “boiling water.

” This is a direct and horrifying contrast to the extended shade and flowing cool water that the people of the right enjoy. The scorching heat refers to the overwhelming, penetrating fire of Hell, a heat that is not like anything on earth. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, described the fire of this world as being one-seventieth of the fire of Hell, having been washed in water seventy times to cool it down to its present state. The boiling water is another form of torment: water, which in Paradise is a source of life and refreshment, becomes an instrument of agony. It is not for drinking in a pleasurable sense, but for burning the skin and entrails. The combination of heat from outside and boiling liquid inside creates an inescapable, all-encompassing suffering. The people of the left had sought comfort and pleasure in this world, often through means that were forbidden or at the expense of their duties to Allah.

In the Hereafter, they are surrounded by the opposite of comfort: a furnace-like atmosphere with nothing to cool them down. The verse uses sensory language that would resonate deeply with the desert Arabs, who knew the terror of extreme heat and the desperate need for cool water. The punishment is not random but is a fitting recompense for their rejection of Allah’s signs and their ingratitude for His blessings.

=43=

This verse introduces a terrible irony. In Paradise, the believers enjoy an extended, pleasant shade. Here, the people of the left also have something called shade, but it is the “shade of black smoke.

” The Arabic term used for smoke is “yahmum,” which describes a thick, dark, suffocating smoke that billows from the flames of Hell. This is not a shade that provides relief from the heat; rather, it is itself a part of the punishment. It is oppressive, dark, and choking. In this world, when a person is in the desert sun, any shade, even from a cloud of smoke, might provide some respite. But the shade of Hell is deceitful; it offers no coolness and no protection. It blocks any possible light of mercy and instead adds the torment of polluted, stifling air. The blackness of the smoke symbolizes utter despair and hopelessness. The people of the left used to live in the light of the worldly sun, but they ignored the light of guidance. As a result, they are plunged into darkness. The three elements—scorching heat, boiling water, and black smoke—combine to form a hellish environment where every physical avenue of relief is cut off. The shade, which might have been a place of rest, becomes yet another layer of suffering, demonstrating that in Hell, every expectation of comfort is inverted. The things that might normally be good become sources of pain, reflecting how the disbelievers inverted their own nature by using their faculties for sin rather than obedience.

=44=

Completing the description of the infernal shade, the verse states that it is “neither cool nor refreshing.” Shade in the normal world serves two primary purposes: it provides a lower temperature, offering coolness, and it gives a feeling of relief and refreshment to the person who escapes the direct sun. The shade of Hell offers neither of these things. It is not cool; it may even be superheated with the very smoke that composes it. And it is not refreshing; it does not bring any pleasure, relief, or restoration.

The Arabic word “karim” used for refreshing also carries the connotation of something beneficial, generous, and honorable. So this shade is the opposite of generous; it is stingy, mean, and worthless. The verse shuts the door on any possible hope that there might be some small mercy hidden in the darkness. It is not just that the punishment is great; it is that there is absolutely no admixture of ease. In this worldly life, even in times of great suffering, moments of relief or distraction might occur. In Hell, as the Quran says elsewhere, the punishment is never lightened, and the damned are never given a break.

The phrase “neither cool nor refreshing” is short but devastatingly final. It emphasizes the absolute negative quality of the abode of the people of the left. Every aspect of their environment, including the shade, is perfectly calibrated for maximum distress, a direct consequence of their having denied the Day of Reckoning and lived as if they would never be held accountable.

=45=

The surah now shifts from describing the punishment itself to explaining the reasons that led the people of the left to this terrible fate. The verse begins with “Indeed,” emphasizing certainty, and states that before this torment, in their worldly lives, they were “spoiled by luxury.

” The Arabic word “mutrafeen” refers to those who lived a life of ease, abundance, and self-indulgence. They were the wealthy, the comfortable, those who had access to every pleasure and who allowed that pleasure to distract them completely from the purpose of life. They became accustomed to having their desires fulfilled instantly, and this led them to arrogance, heedlessness, and a feeling of self-sufficiency that made them reject the idea of resurrection and judgment. They were so immersed in the luxuries of the world that they could not bear the thought of a life beyond it, and they mocked those who warned them.

This is a universal principle: luxury and ease often lead to spiritual corruption unless they are accompanied by gratitude and a sense of responsibility. The Quraysh elite, with their trade caravans and their comfortable lives in Mecca, were a direct example of this. Their wealth made them feel secure, and they saw no need for a God who would hold them accountable. The verse serves as a warning to all believers that material comfort, while not inherently evil, is a dangerous test that can lead to a hardened heart if one is not vigilant.

=46=

Alongside their life of luxury, the people of the left are guilty of persisting in “the worst of sin.” The Arabic term “al-hinth al-‘azim” refers to a great, grave, and atrocious sin. Many commentators have identified this as shirk, the sin of associating partners with Allah. This was the cardinal sin of the Meccan pagans, who worshipped idols alongside Allah and refused to accept pure monotheism. Shirk is the worst of sins because it corrupts the very foundation of a person’s relationship with their Creator, directing worship and gratitude to false gods and depriving the soul of its true purpose. But the phrase can also encompass all forms of major sin that a person commits openly and persistently without repentance.

The word “persisted” is crucial; it indicates that this was not a momentary lapse or a sin committed in weakness followed by regret, but a deliberate, continuous, and stubborn adherence to wrongdoing. They clung to their sins, defended them, and refused to abandon them. This persistence is what makes sin so destructive. Allah’s mercy is vast and open to those who repent, but those who persist until death close the door of mercy upon themselves. The combination of luxury and persistence in the worst sin paints the picture of a person who had every opportunity to recognize the truth, who was given blessings that should have inspired gratitude, but who instead used those blessings to fuel their rebellion against their Lord. The punishment they now face is the direct and just consequence of that chosen path.

=47=

The surah now cites a specific example of the sinful talk and denial that characterized the people of the left in their worldly life. They used to ask, mockingly, about the resurrection. Their question, “When we are dead and reduced to dust and bones, will we really be resurrected?” was not a sincere inquiry but a sarcastic dismissal. They framed the concept of bodily resurrection as absurd by reducing it to its most seemingly impossible elements: dust and dry bones. From a purely materialistic viewpoint, once the body decomposes into dust and the bones crumble, there appears to be no mechanism by which a person could be reconstituted. The disbelievers prided themselves on this rational objection, believing that it conclusively disproved the prophets’ message.

However, their error lay in measuring the power of Allah by human standards. They could not imagine a power that transcends the laws of nature as they understood them. Their mockery was a defense mechanism; by ridiculing the idea of judgment, they could continue their lives of sin without guilt.

The question itself reveals their worldview: they saw human life as purely material, ending with death, with no soul, no accountability, and no afterlife. The Quran records this question to expose the bankruptcy of their reasoning and to remind the believers that the same arguments are recycled by every generation of deniers. It also sets up the definitive response that the following verses will deliver, which is that the One who created them from nothing the first time can certainly recreate them.

=48=

The mockery of the disbelievers continues with an extension of their question: they ask not only about their own resurrection but also about the resurrection of their forefathers. This addition was meant to make the idea seem even more ludicrous. Their forefathers had died long ago, their bodies having completely vanished into the earth, with no trace left. If it was impossible for recent corpses to be resurrected, how much more impossible for those who had been dead for centuries?

The mention of forefathers also reveals a psychological attachment to ancestral traditions. The Meccan polytheists were deeply tied to the religion and customs of their forefathers. By asking whether their ancestors would be resurrected, they were implicitly saying, “We follow the way of our forefathers, who never believed in any resurrection, and if your claim is true, then they must all be in trouble, and we find that unacceptable.” It was a form of tribal loyalty blinding them to the truth.

This verse shows that false beliefs often persist not because of evidence but because of sentimental attachment to heritage. The Quran challenges this mindset repeatedly, urging people to use their reason and not blindly follow the ways of their ancestors when those ways conflict with the truth.

The question about forefathers also serves a rhetorical function: the disbelievers thought it made the resurrection seem more far-fetched, but in reality, for the Creator, bringing back one person or a billion is exactly the same. The scale of the task does not increase the difficulty for the One whose power is infinite.

=49=

After quoting the mockery of the deniers, Allah instructs the Prophet to give a decisive and unequivocal answer. The command “Say, O Prophet” appears throughout the Quran and empowers the Messenger to speak with divine authority, even when surrounded by opponents.

The answer begins with “Most certainly,” leaving no room for doubt or ambiguity. The substance of the response is that both the earlier and later generations will indeed be gathered. This directly addresses the two-part mockery. Their own generation, the later generation in their view, and the generations of their forefathers, the earlier ones, are all included in the divine decree. No one is lost in the dust of history. Every human being who ever lived is within the scope of Allah’s knowledge and power.

This statement also reasserts the theme of the surah, which has repeatedly mentioned “a multitude from earlier generations and a multitude from later generations.” The disbelievers thought that the passage of time made resurrection impossible, as if Allah might forget the dead or lose track of them.

The answer is that time is irrelevant to the Creator; He is the Lord of all generations, and His command encompasses the first and the last. The certainty expressed in this verse is meant to shake the believer’s heart with conviction and to silence the mockery of the deniers with an undeniable truth.

=50=

The verse completes the divine answer by stating that all these earlier and later generations “will surely be gathered together for the appointed Day.” The phrase “surely be gathered” uses a verb that emphasizes the inevitability and comprehensive nature of the assembly. It is not a possibility or a conditional event; it is a fixed, certain occurrence in the future. The gathering is described as being “together,” meaning that all of humanity, across all times and places, will be brought to a single location for judgment. This mass assembly is one of the most awesome scenes described in the Quran. Every prophet, every tyrant, every saint, every sinner, from Adam to the last child born before the Hour, will stand together on the same plain. The social distinctions of the world will vanish; king and slave will be shoulder to shoulder, awaiting their reckoning. The gathering is for “the appointed Day,” a term that underscores the precision of Allah’s decree. The Day of Judgment is not a random event; it is set for a specific time in Allah’s knowledge, a time that no one can hasten or delay. This appointment was made before the creation of the heavens and the earth, and it will be kept without fail. For the believers, this gathering is a reunion with the righteous of all ages.

For the disbelievers, it is a terrifying convocation where they will have to answer for every deed. The verse serves as a powerful conclusion to the argument, leaving the mockers with nothing to say, for the Creator who set the appointment is the One who will certainly fulfill it.

=51=

After establishing the certainty of the gathering, the surah turns to directly address the deniers with a tone of confrontation and warning. The phrase “Then you” singles out a specific audience, those who are misguided and in denial.

The word “misguided” indicates that they have strayed from the path of truth; they are not on a neutral journey but are actively lost, heading in the wrong direction. The word “deniers” describes their active rejection of the truth despite it having been made clear to them. This direct address creates a powerful rhetorical effect. Up to this point, the surah has been describing various groups and their fates, but now it turns to the present listener and says, in effect, “As for you who are among these deniers, here is what awaits you specifically.” It personalizes the warning and makes it impossible to dismiss as applying only to others. The address is in the second person plural, speaking to the Meccan pagans who were the immediate audience but also to all who share their characteristics until the end of time. The believers who hear this verse also benefit, because it reinforces their gratitude for having been guided away from this path and increases their fear of falling into misguidance.

The word “then” connects the doom of the deniers directly to the preceding verses: since the gathering is certain and you are among the deniers of that gathering, then your fate is sealed unless you repent.

=52=

The punishment for the deniers is now described in graphic detail, beginning with the statement that they will certainly eat from the tree of Zaqqum. Zaqqum is a tree that grows in the depths of Hellfire, and its nature is described in various places in the Quran as being utterly vile and repulsive. Its fruit is shaped like the heads of devils, a horrifying appearance, and its taste is indescribably bitter and disgusting. The deniers will be forced to eat from this tree, not as an optional meal but as a compulsory part of their torment.

The word “certainly” emphasizes that this is not a threat that might be averted; it is a definite consequence of their denial. In the worldly life, the disbelievers enjoyed delicious foods and fine dining, using the blessings of Allah in a state of ingratitude. In Hell, their food is the fruit of Zaqqum, an inversion of the luxurious eating they enjoyed.

The mention of Zaqqum served as a specific answer to the mockery of the Quraysh. When the Quran first mentioned the tree of Zaqqum, some of the disbelievers mocked it, saying that a tree could not grow in fire. They thought this was a logical contradiction and ridiculed the Prophet. But this verse and others make clear that the laws of nature in Hell are different; Allah creates whatever He wills, including a tree that thrives in the flames and produces fruit of torment. The eating of Zaqqum is a physical manifestation of their spiritual devouring of falsehood in the world, and it causes them intense suffering.

=53=

The torment of Zaqqum is intensified by the statement that the deniers will be “filling up their bellies with it.” They will not just taste a morsel; they will be forced to gorge themselves until their stomachs are completely full of this abominable fruit.

This detail adds a dimension of excess and overconsumption to the punishment. In the world, the people of luxury were accustomed to eating to their fill, indulging their appetites without restraint. Their sin was characterized by a lack of moderation and a focus on satisfying the belly. In Hell, the same belly becomes an instrument of torment. The feeling of being overstuffed with something foul and painful is a terrible suffering in itself.

Furthermore, the eating is not done willingly; they are compelled to eat, and the food does not satisfy or nourish; it only causes pain. The stomachs will be stretched and filled with this hot, repulsive substance, which will then cause internal burning and turmoil.

The verse also indicates the completeness of the punishment: it is not a token or a small sample but a full measure. Just as their sins were full and their rejection was complete, their portion of the tree of Zaqqum will be full. The imagery is designed to evoke a visceral sense of disgust and horror, to make the listener recoil from the idea and ask themselves what actions lead to such a fate, thus motivating them to avoid those actions at all costs.

=54=

The punishment does not stop at eating the foul fruit; it is compounded. “Then on top of that” indicates a layering of torments. After their bellies are filled with Zaqqum, the deniers will drink boiling water. In a normal meal, one drinks water to aid digestion, to cleanse the palate, and to find refreshment. In Hell, the drink is a further punishment. The boiling water will be so intensely hot that it scalds and burns everything it touches. It mixes with the Zaqqum in their stomachs, creating a churning, boiling mixture that causes indescribable internal agony.

The Arabic word “hamim” for boiling water appears frequently in descriptions of Hell, emphasizing its role as a primary element of torment. The combination of foul solid food and scalding liquid creates a complete and inescapable suffering of the digestive system.

The verse also highlights the sequence of punishment; there is no break, no relief. They eat, then they drink, and the drinking only makes things worse. In the world, the disbelievers used to enjoy elaborate meals followed by fine drinks, often intoxicating themselves. In Hell, they are served a meal that is the exact opposite of every pleasure they ever knew. This aspect of the Quranic warning is meant to break the heart’s attachment to worldly food and drink, reminding believers that these temporary pleasures are not worth an eternity of such torment.

=55=

The manner in which the deniers will drink the boiling water is described with a powerful and degrading simile: they will drink it “like thirsty camels do.” Camels, when they are extremely thirsty, drink with a desperate, greedy gulping, consuming vast amounts of water rapidly. This comparison carries several layers of meaning. First, it emphasizes the intensity of the thirst that the inhabitants of Hell will experience, a thirst so severe that they will gulp down boiling water despite the agony it causes, just as a parched camel desperate for water would. Their thirst overrides the pain, forcing them to drink what should be avoided. Second, the comparison to camels, animals that are known for their large water intake and sometimes seen as gluttonous in their drinking, adds an element of humiliation. In the world, the deniers were proud and arrogant, considering themselves the elite of society. On the Day of Judgment, they are reduced to the level of frantic, thirsty beasts, losing all dignity and composure. Third, for the Arab audience, the camel was a familiar creature, and the image of a herd of camels rushing to a water trough and noisily gulping water was a common sight. The verse uses that familiar image to paint a vivid picture of the degradation of the damned. It also serves as a stark contrast to the people of Paradise, who drink from pure streams with elegant cups, served by youths, in a state of perfect contentment and dignity. The people of the left, however, drink like animals, desperate and degraded, a fitting end for those who lived like animals, following their lusts without regard for divine guidance.

=56=

This verse serves as a concluding sentence for the section on the people of the left, summarizing everything that has been described as “their accommodation.” The Arabic word “nuzul” originally refers to the hospitality offered to a guest, the lodging and food prepared for someone who arrives.

The use of this word here is deeply ironic. In Arab culture, honoring a guest with a generous nuzul was a matter of great pride and a sacred duty. The Quraysh in particular took pride in their hospitality to pilgrims. Here, Allah, the Host of the Day of Judgment, informs the deniers what kind of accommodation He has prepared for them. Instead of a warm welcome, they receive scorching heat, boiling water, black smoke, and the tree of Zaqqum. Their nuzul is a punishment, not a gift. This irony drives home the point that they have utterly failed to be proper guests in the world, a world that was Allah’s provision for them. They enjoyed His blessings and then denied Him, so their accommodation in the next life reflects their ingratitude.

The phrase “on the Day of Judgment” anchors this entire description in a specific time and place, reminding the listener that this is not a metaphor or a myth but an actual future event. The Day of Judgment is the day when all accounts are settled, and the accommodation each person receives is the direct result of the life they lived. The verse brings the first major section of the surah to a close and prepares for a transition. The surah will now move from describing the fates of the three groups to presenting rational proofs for the resurrection and the power of Allah.

=57=

With this verse, the surah begins a new section that presents logical arguments for the reality of resurrection, shifting from description to persuasion. Allah declares, “It is We Who created you,” reminding human beings of the most fundamental fact of their existence. Their very being is the result of a divine creative act. They did not create themselves, nor did they come into existence by random chance. This initial creation is presented as evidence that the One who brought them into being from nothing is fully capable of bringing them back to life after death. The argument is simple yet profound: if you acknowledge that you were created the first time, what rational basis do you have for doubting the possibility of a second creation?

In fact, the second creation is easier, because the first creation was from non-existence, while the second creation involves reconstituting materials that already existed. The verse then poses a rhetorical question: “Will you not then believe in resurrection? ” The word “then” links the premise to the conclusion. Given that Allah created you initially, the logical response is to believe that He can and will resurrect you.

The question is phrased as an appeal to reason and conscience, encouraging the listener to overcome their irrational denial. This verse also marks a shift in tone; Allah is no longer merely describing the consequences of denial but is now actively engaging the mind of the skeptic, dismantling the intellectual foundations of their disbelief.

=58=

The argument for resurrection is developed by directing human attention to a very personal and intimate aspect of their existence: the reproductive fluid. Allah asks, “Have you considered what you ejaculate?

” This is a powerful rhetorical question that forces the listener to think about something they take for granted. The emission of semen is an everyday biological reality for men, and from this seemingly insignificant fluid, human beings are formed.

The question is not merely about biology; it is about the mystery and miracle of conception. A tiny drop of fluid, which a man discharges without any control over its inner nature, contains the blueprint for a complete human being. The development of that drop into a fetus with a heart, brain, limbs, eyes, and all the complex systems of the human body is an undeniable sign of a Creator. By asking this question, Allah is inviting people to reflect on the process of their own creation and to recognize the divine power that operates within it. The verse uses a direct and slightly shocking word to grab attention and to break through the heedlessness that typically surrounds such topics. It makes the familiar unfamiliar, turning a mundane act into a sign of Allah’s power. If a human being can be created from a drop of fluid, then the reassembly of a decayed body is hardly beyond the same power. This argument is deeply personal; every human is the product of this process, so no one can claim that it is irrelevant to them.

The question awaits an answer: you did not create this; so who did?

=59=

The question from the previous verse is now made explicit: “Is it you who create a child out of it, or is it We Who do so?” There are only two options, and the answer is obvious. No human being can claim to create life from sperm. The most advanced science can describe the process of embryogenesis, but it cannot initiate or sustain the creation of a soul and a living, conscious being from that fluid. The parents provide the material, but the formation of the child in the womb is a miracle that happens by the will and power of Allah. The verse forces the listener to concede that it is Allah Who creates. Once this concession is made, the argument for resurrection becomes unavoidable. The One who knits a perfect human baby together in the darkness of the womb, stage by stage, from a drop of fluid, can undoubtedly recompose the same human being from scattered bones and dust. The rhetorical strategy here is masterful: instead of launching into abstract philosophical discussions about resurrection, the Quran points to the most common, observable, and undeniable miracle of creation that each person has some direct connection to. If you believe you were created from a drop of sperm, you must believe in the power of the Creator to bring you back.

The question also carries a moral implication: the Creator who brought you into existence with such care and precision deserves your gratitude and worship. To use the body He created to sin against Him is the height of ingratitude.

=60=

The argument continues by linking the beginning of life to its end. Allah states, “We have ordained death for all of you.” Death is not an accident or a random event; it is a divine decree. Every human being has a lifespan that is appointed by Allah, and when that time comes, death arrives and cannot be delayed or advanced by a moment. The universality of death is emphasized by “all of you,” reminding every listener that no one escapes this fate. The wealthy, the powerful, the poor, the weak, all are equal before the decree of death. This universality itself points to a higher power that governs human existence.

The verse then adds, “and We cannot be prevented. ” This means that no one can thwart Allah’s will regarding death. If a person tries to run from death, they will meet it. If a nation tries to conquer death through medicine or technology, they only delay it within the bounds Allah permits. The power of Allah is absolute, and His decree of death is invincible.

This statement is also an implicit answer to those who might think that the concept of resurrection is too fantastic. If Allah’s power cannot be prevented from causing the death of every living being, then His power to resurrect them after death is equally undeniable. The progression of this argument is beautiful: from creation from a drop, to the inevitability of death, the mind is being prepared to accept the next step, which is the resurrection and recreation in new forms. The verse leaves the listener with a sense of humility and dependence, realizing that they are completely within the grip of divine power from their origin to their end, and so their return to Him is perfectly logical and expected.
