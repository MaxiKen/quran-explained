=81=

After establishing the divine origin and supreme nobility of the Quran, the surah turns to the deniers with a rebuke that is as logical as it is emotional. The question “How can you then take this message lightly?” challenges the absurdity of their attitude. The “message” refers to the Quran itself, the revelation from the Lord of all worlds, the Book that the angels in their purity can scarcely touch, the truth sworn to by the positions of the stars. Given this reality, the only reasonable response would be to receive it with awe, to listen with full attention, and to hasten to belief and obedience. Instead, the disbelievers treated it as something trivial. They joked about its verses, they called it old fables, they turned away when it was recited, and they busied themselves with worldly amusements as if this life was all there is.

The Arabic expression conveys the idea of making something one’s portion of frivolity, treating serious matters as a joke. This is a profound psychological insight: when people lack sincerity, they deflect truth with mockery and indifference to avoid confronting its implications. The verse is not just a historical comment on the Quraysh; it is a mirror for every reader. Do we approach the Quran with the gravity it deserves? Do we rush through our recitation without reflection? Do we treat its commands as optional suggestions? The verse asks every believer to examine their own heart.

The question also implies a severe consequence: those who take the message lightly in this world will find the reality of what they dismissed heavy upon them on the Day of Judgment. The tone is one of sorrowful astonishment, as if the heavens and earth are bewildered by the foolishness of a creature who plays with the rope thrown to rescue him from drowning.

=82=

The rebuke deepens by linking the disbelievers’ attitude toward the Quran with their attitude toward the provisions they receive daily from Allah. The verse asks, “and repay Allah for your provisions with denial?

” The word “provisions” refers to the sustenance, the rizq, that Allah bestows on every human being: the air they breathe, the water they drink, the food they eat, the health they enjoy, the faculties they use, the life itself. These provisions are pure gifts, given without any prior merit on the part of the recipient. The natural human response to a generous gift is gratitude. If a person is constantly showered with kindness by a benefactor, and then they respond by rejecting that benefactor’s message, mocking his messengers, and denying his very existence, that is the height of moral perversity.

The word “repay” here is deeply ironic; normally one repays a kindness with thanks, but these people repay the greatest kindness with the greatest ingratitude, which is kufr, denial. The verse thus exposes the fundamental injustice of disbelief. It is not merely an intellectual error; it is a moral failure, a violation of the basic contract of gratitude that binds the creature to the Creator. Every bite of food that a disbeliever eats is a testimony against them, for it comes from Allah, yet they use the energy it provides to sin and to reject. This argument is intensely personal. It asks each human being to look at their own life, to count the blessings they enjoy moment by moment, and to ask themselves whether their response is thanks or denial. The connection to the previous verse is seamless: taking the Quran lightly is one form of repaying divine provision with denial, because the Quran itself is the greatest provision after life itself—a guide to eternal happiness—and to dismiss it is the most catastrophic ingratitude imaginable.

=83=

The surah now moves to the final and most undeniable proof of human powerlessness: the moment of death. The question “Why then are you helpless when the soul of a dying person reaches their throat?” places the disbelievers in a scene that every human being will one day face. When death arrives, the soul begins its final departure from the body, rising up through the chest until it reaches the throat. At that point, the dying person is in the throes of their last moments, gasping, their eyes fixed, their loved ones standing around. The verse challenges those who deny divine power and the afterlife: if you are truly independent and powerful as your arrogance suggests, why can you do nothing at this critical juncture? You cannot stop death. You cannot push the soul back into the body. You cannot even alleviate the dying person’s suffering. All your wealth, your medicine, your status, and your supposed gods are utterly useless.

The word “helpless” translates a state of complete inability and bewilderment. The family members look on, wringing their hands, but they are powerless. This scene is a stark reminder that despite all human pretensions, we are not in control. Death is the ultimate proof that there is a power above us that governs our fate.

The verse also prepares the ground for the next point: while humans are helpless, Allah is not. The rhetorical question is intended to break the shell of pride and force the listener to admit their own weakness, which is the first step toward true faith. It is a moment of existential confrontation that no person can evade, and the Quran uses it masterfully to bring the heart to submission.

=84=

This verse completes the scene of the deathbed, adding a crucial detail: “while you are looking on.” The family and friends of the dying person are present, watching the process unfold before their very eyes. They see the chest heaving, the breathing becoming labored, the eyes glazing over, the sweat on the brow. They are fully aware that the soul is departing, that the person they love is slipping away from them.

Yet, despite being witnesses, they are completely incapable of intervening. The phrase emphasizes the frustration and sorrow of the onlookers. They have eyes that see the tragedy, but no hands that can stop it. They possess love that wishes to hold the person back, but no power to do so. This helpless observation is a profound humiliation for a species that prides itself on its agency and control. It also serves as a reminder that the same fate awaits the onlookers themselves. One day, each of them will be the one on the bed, and others will be looking on, equally helpless. The verse thus universalizes the experience of death, making it a mirror in which every listener sees their own inevitable end. The presence of onlookers also establishes a dramatic contrast with the next verse, where Allah describes His own nearness. The humans are near in body but far in power; Allah is unseen but infinitely nearer and in complete control. The detail “while you are looking on” is not a neutral observation; it is an indictment of human arrogance. You see with your own eyes that you are not the masters of life and death, and yet you continue to deny the One who is the true Master. This visible proof should be enough to break any rational person’s stubbornness.

=85=

This is one of the most powerful and awe-inspiring verses in the surah. Allah declares, “And We are nearer to such a person than you, but you cannot see.” The “such a person” refers to the dying individual whose soul is reaching the throat. The family and friends are physically present, perhaps holding the dying person’s hand, leaning over the bed, their eyes fixed on the face. They feel they are the closest ones to their loved one at that moment. But Allah reveals a reality that shatters this perception: He, through His knowledge, power, and the angels of death, is nearer to the dying person than even the closest human companion. This nearness is not physical in a spatial sense; it is a nearness of complete encompassment, authority, and awareness. The angels appointed to take the soul are carrying out Allah’s command right there, closer than the jugular vein, closer than any family member could ever be.

Yet, “you cannot see.” The human eye is veiled from the unseen realm. The angels are present, the gates of the next world are opening, the dying person begins to perceive what the onlookers cannot, but the living stand by in ignorance of the cosmic event unfolding before them. This verse serves multiple purposes. It demonstrates the vast gulf between human perception and divine reality. It humbles those who think their physical presence gives them any control or significance. It also speaks to the experience of the dying believer, who is not alone in their final moments. The angels of mercy are near, bringing glad tidings, nearer than the grieving family.

For the disbeliever, the angels of punishment are near, and that nearness is a terror. The verse ultimately redirects the focus from the human scene to the divine truth that governs it, reinforcing the message that Allah’s power and presence encompass every moment of existence, especially its most critical juncture.

=86=

This verse presents a conditional statement that challenges the very foundation of the disbelievers’ arrogance. The phrase “Now, if you are not subject to Our Will as you claim” sets up a logical test. Throughout their lives, the deniers behaved as if they were independent beings, not subject to any divine judgment or decree. They rejected the idea of resurrection and accountability, living as if they would never be returned to their Lord. Some even claimed that their gods could intercede for them, or that their wealth and power proved that they were favored by fate and not under any higher authority.

The verse now calls their bluff. If you truly are not under Our command, if you genuinely are independent and not bound by the divine will that governs life and death, then here is the moment to prove it.

The word “claim” is significant; it exposes that their stance is a baseless assertion, not a reality. They may have convinced themselves of their self-sufficiency, but the moment of death will reveal the truth. The verse is like a direct challenge thrown down in a debate: you say one thing, but your helplessness at death proves the opposite.

The use of “now” brings the argument into the immediate present of the deathbed scene, making it vivid and urgent. It also carries a warning: you have lived your whole life under the delusion of independence, but now the veil is lifting. This conditional statement sets up the command in the next verse, which will complete the challenge. It appeals to the intellect and to the sense of fair play: if you make a claim, be prepared to back it up when the test comes. The fact that they cannot do so is the final, undeniable proof that their claim is false.

=87=

This verse delivers the ultimate, unanswerable challenge. “Bring that soul back, if what you say is true.” The dying person’s soul has reached their throat; they are on the very brink of departure. The family is looking on helplessly, and Allah has declared that He and His angels are nearer than them. Now, in a tone of absolute authority, the Creator says: if you are truly independent of My will, if you are not going to be resurrected and judged, if your denial of My power is justified, then perform this one simple task—return the soul to the body. Pull it back from the edge of departure. Restore life to the dying person. The challenge is devastatingly effective because everyone present knows it cannot be done. No human being in history, regardless of their power, wealth, or knowledge, has ever been able to reverse death once the soul has reached the throat. The mightiest kings and the most brilliant doctors are all equally powerless before this reality. The silence that follows the challenge is itself the answer: the claim of the deniers is false.

This verse also connects back to the earlier arguments about resurrection. The One who can challenge humanity to bring back a departing soul is the One who has absolute power over life and death. And if no human can prevent a single soul from departing, how can they deny that the same God who commands the soul to leave can also command it to return on the Day of Resurrection? The logic is airtight.

For the believer, this verse is a source of profound trust in Allah. Even in the most heartbreaking moment of losing a loved one, there is a comfort in knowing that the soul is in Allah’s hands, that He is nearer than we are, and that His decree is perfect. The challenge also serves as a reminder that this life is not the end, and the moment of death is a transition into the true and everlasting life.

=88=

Having taken the listener through the moment of death and exposed human powerlessness, the surah now returns to the three groups with which it began, but this time describing their fates from the perspective of the very moment of departure. “So, if the deceased is one of those brought near to Us,” refers to the foremost, the as-sabiqun, who were described in the opening section as the ones nearest to Allah.

The verse begins with “so,” linking the deathbed scene to the judgment that immediately follows. The moment the soul departs, its destination is determined. There is no intermediate period of uncertainty; the dying person’s category among the three groups is already known to Allah, and the treatment they receive reflects that category instantly. “Those brought near” are the elite, the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs, and the righteous who excelled in faith and good deeds. Their station is nearness to Allah, as mentioned in verse 11. This verse confirms that the status they attained in this life through their striving will be honored at the very first stage of the afterlife.

The phrase “if the deceased is” indicates that the Prophet and the believers are being informed about the unseen reality that awaits different categories of people. It serves as both a comfort to the righteous and a warning to the wicked.

For those who love Allah and long to be among the muqarrabun, this verse fills the heart with hope. It personalizes the earlier general descriptions and says: when your moment comes, if you are among this blessed group, this is what will happen. The verse leads naturally into the beautiful description of their immediate reward.

=89=

The reward for the one who is among those brought near is described in three exquisite elements: serenity, fragrance, and a Garden of Bliss. “Serenity” translates the Arabic word “rawh,” which conveys rest, ease, relief, and the soothing mercy of Allah. After the intense struggle of death, the believer who has lived a life of obedience is met not with terror but with a profound calm and peace. The angels greet them with reassurance, telling them not to fear or grieve. This serenity envelops the soul as it leaves the body and continues into the grave and beyond. “Fragrance” refers to the beautiful scent that accompanies the believing soul. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, described how when a believer dies, angels with white faces and perfume from Paradise come down. The soul is extracted gently and placed in a shroud of fragrance, and its sweet smell is perceptible in the heavenly realms.

This is a physical manifestation of the spiritual purity of the deceased. “And a Garden of Bliss” is their ultimate destination, the Jannat an-Na’im that was described earlier. But notably, it is mentioned here in the immediate context of death, suggesting that the believer begins to experience the joy and comfort of Paradise from the very moment they leave this world. The grave becomes a garden of Paradise for them, and their soul rejoices in the pleasures of the Barzakh until the Day of Resurrection, when they will enter the fullness of the Garden. This three-part reward addresses the soul’s deepest needs: peace from fear, honor and purity symbolized by fragrance, and the certainty of eternal bliss. It is a beautiful culmination of a life lived in nearness to Allah.

=90=

The surah now describes the fate of the second group at the moment of death. “And if the deceased is one of the people of the right,” refers to the general body of the believers, those who received their records in their right hands. Their station is lower than the foremost, but they are still among the saved and the successful. This verse demonstrates that the categorization presented earlier in the surah is not merely a theoretical division for the Day of Judgment; it has immediate consequences at the time of death. The treatment of the soul at its departure reflects its standing with Allah. The people of the right are the righteous, the obedient, those who fulfilled their obligations and avoided major sins. At their death, they are not met with the punishment that awaits the deniers, but neither do they receive the instant, intimate nearness of the foremost. Their experience is one of safety, honor, and good tidings. The verse introduces what they will be told, linking their death to the greetings they will receive from their fellow believers. It is important to note that the moment of death is described differently for each group, confirming that the afterlife is not a single uniform experience but a profoundly individual one, based on one’s faith and deeds. The inclusion of the people of the right here also provides a broad hope for the Muslim community. Many believers may not have reached the spiritual heights of the sabiqun, but they can still die in a state of faith and receive the glad tidings that belong to the companions of the right. The verse thus encourages those who may feel that the highest ranks are beyond them to persist in righteousness, knowing that their Lord has prepared a generous reception for them as well.

=91=

The specific reward for the person of the right at death is a greeting of peace. “Then they will be told, ‘Greetings to you from the people of the right.’” The angels receive the soul of the deceased believer and convey salutations of peace. The greeting “Salam” is one of the most beautiful words in the Islamic vocabulary, meaning peace, safety, and freedom from all harm. The full phrase “Salamun laka” means “Peace be upon you,” and it carries a reassurance that the deceased is now safe, their trials are over, and they are welcomed into the company of the righteous. The source of the greeting is identified as “from the people of the right,” which can mean that the angels convey the greetings of the other believers who have already passed away, or that the greeting is on behalf of the community of the right. Either way, it signifies that the dying believer is being immediately integrated into the blessed society of the saved. They are not entering a lonely darkness; they are being greeted and welcomed as a member of a great and honored community.

This is a stark contrast to the fate of the deniers, who will receive no greeting of peace but only the message of punishment. The verse echoes the earlier description of Paradise, where the inhabitants will hear only good and virtuous speech. Here, the first words the believer hears after death are words of pure peace. This greeting is a source of immense comfort for the living as well. When a righteous person passes away, the believers can have hope that they are being welcomed with such greetings by the angels and the believing souls who preceded them. It transforms the sorrow of death into a moment of anticipated joy for the deceased.

=92=

The final case is that of the disbeliever, the one who belongs to the people of the left. “But if such person is one of the misguided deniers,” combines two descriptive terms that we have seen before. “Misguided” refers to those who have gone astray from the path of truth, who have lost their way in the darkness of shirk and sin. “Deniers” refers to those who actively rejected the truth, who belied the messengers and the Day of Judgment. By coupling these two terms, the verse indicates that their state is both intellectual and volitional; they are lost, and they persist in rejecting the guidance that could save them. This person, at the moment of death, is identified as belonging to the people of the left, the ashab al-mash’amah, whose miserable fate was described in terrifying detail earlier.

The word “if” here is not one of doubt but of conditional certainty, meaning that this fate is sealed for anyone who meets this description. The verse serves as the starkest of warnings. After the soothing descriptions of the fates of the two believing groups, the surah plunges the listener back into the reality of the punishment that awaits those who turn away. It is impossible to read these passages without feeling a deep sense of urgency to ensure one is not counted among this third group.

The verse also creates a powerful dichotomy: the near ones receive serenity and fragrance, the right receive greetings of peace, and the misguided deniers receive what will now be described. The structure of the surah, with its repeated cycles of mercy and warning, is designed to move the heart between hope and fear until it settles firmly on the path of faith.

=93=

The punishment for the misguided denier at the moment of death and beyond is summarized with the phrase “then their accommodation will be boiling water to drink.” The word “accommodation” here, as earlier in the surah with the people of the left, is deeply ironic. It refers to the hospitality, the nuzul, that is prepared for a guest upon arrival.

For the disbeliever, the arrival is into the afterlife, and the welcoming drink is boiling, scalding water that tears at the insides and causes unimaginable pain. This is not a refreshment; it is a torment. The boiling water is the first thing they are given, a foretaste of the Hellfire that awaits them fully on the Day of Judgment. The hadith literature describes how the angels of punishment come to the dying disbeliever with faces like night, bringing rough cloth from Hell. The soul is ripped violently from the body, and it is immediately wrapped in that cloth of punishment. The grave constricts upon them, and they are shown their seat in the Fire. The boiling water is part of the punishment in the Barzakh, the intermediate life of the grave, and it will be their drink on the Day of Resurrection as well. The specific mention of “to drink” emphasizes that they are forced to consume this liquid, just as the people of the left were forced to eat from Zaqqum and drink boiling water. It is a physical torment that engages the very faculties they used to enjoy the pleasures of this world in sin. The contrast with the fragrance and serenity of the believer could not be starker. Life is a journey, and the hospitality one receives at its end is determined by the path one walked.

=94=

To the boiling water is added “and burning in Hellfire.” This describes the ultimate abode and the comprehensive nature of the punishment. The boiling water is the drink, but the environment itself is one of blazing, engulfing flame. The disbeliever will burn in the Fire, their skin roasted and continually replaced so that the torment is perpetually renewed.

The word “burning” indicates an active, ongoing process, not a single event. They will be in a state of perpetual combustion, surrounded by the heat and smoke described earlier.

The phrase also confirms that the punishment is both internal and external; the boiling water torments from within, and the Hellfire torments from without. There is no escape, no shelter, no respite. This verse, together with the previous one, summarizes in a few words the entire horrific destiny of the people of the left that was detailed at length earlier in the surah. The brevity here at the end of the surah serves a different purpose. After the lengthy and detailed descriptions, these short, sharp sentences hit the heart like hammer blows. They are like a final summary, a last warning before the surah closes. The listener is left with the image of the two paths: one leading to the Garden of Bliss with serenity and fragrance, the other leading to boiling water and Hellfire. Every soul must choose.

The verse also reinforces the reality that Hell is not a myth or a temporary state but a tangible, physical reality of unimaginable suffering, prepared for those who die in a state of denial and misguidance.

=95=

After the long journey through the descriptions of the three groups, the cosmic signs, and the proofs of resurrection, the surah now places a seal of absolute certainty upon everything that has been revealed. “Indeed, this is the absolute truth.

” The phrase “this is” refers to the entire message of the surah: the reality of the Day of Judgment, the division of humanity into three groups, the rewards of Paradise, the punishments of Hell, the power of Allah demonstrated in creation, and the divine origin of the Quran. All of it is “the absolute truth. ” The Arabic word used is “haqq al-yaqin,” which is one of the strongest possible expressions for certainty. Yaqin is certainty itself, and haqq is truth; combined, they convey something that is true beyond any shadow of doubt, a reality so established that denying it does not affect it in the slightest.

This is the truth of certainty, not the truth of speculation or conjecture. Human beings deal mostly in probabilities and opinions; they argue about what might be true. But the Quran announces that what it has communicated is not in that category. It is the truth that comes from the Lord of all worlds, witnessed by the angels, recorded in the Preserved Tablet, and confirmed by the signs in the universe and within the human soul. This verse is an anchor for the believer. In a world of shifting opinions and constant confusion, the believer holds onto the “absolute truth” that can never change. It is also a final warning to the skeptic. There is no room left for doubt. The stakes are eternal, and the message has been delivered with the utmost clarity. The truth demands a response; it can be accepted with faith or rejected with loss, but it cannot be ignored.

=96=

The surah ends as it effectively began, with a command to glorify Allah. “So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Greatest.

” The word “so” ties this command directly to the absolute truth that has just been affirmed. Because all of this—the Event, the three groups, the rewards and punishments, the proofs of power—is the absolute truth, the only fitting response is to exalt the One who revealed it. “Glorify” means to declare Allah’s perfection, to praise Him, to acknowledge His supreme majesty, and to worship Him with humility and devotion. The “Name of your Lord” encompasses all of His beautiful names and attributes. To glorify His name is to engage in tasbih, saying “Subhana Rabbiyal A’la” and similar phrases, but also to live a life that reflects His greatness through obedience and avoidance of sin. The description “the Greatest” emphasizes the attribute of supreme majesty, Al-A’la. This echoes the command in verse 74, creating a beautiful envelope structure for the surah. The entire surah is thus framed by the glorification of Allah.

The first audience, the Prophet and the believers, and all subsequent readers, are brought to a point where their hearts, having been filled with fear of punishment, hope for reward, awe of creation, and conviction in the truth, naturally pour out in praise. It is not a commandment that feels external; it is the logical conclusion of the spiritual journey the surah has taken them on. This final verse leaves the believer in a state of worship, their lips moist with remembrance, their heart attached to their Lord, the Greatest, as they go forth to live the message they have just absorbed. The supplied passage, Surah Al-Waqi’ah, has been fully completed.
