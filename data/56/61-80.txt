=61=

This verse continues directly from the previous one, completing the thought that Allah cannot be prevented from transforming and recreating human beings in forms unknown to them. The phrase “from transforming” indicates that the divine power is not limited to causing death; it extends to the complete alteration and reconstitution of the human form.

This is a direct refutation of the disbelievers who mocked the idea of bodily resurrection. They thought that once bones decay and turn to dust, reconstitution is impossible. Allah responds by saying He can recreate them in new forms that they cannot even conceive of. The “forms unknown to you” might refer to the resurrection bodies that will be different from our earthly bodies, adapted for eternal life in the Hereafter. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, informed us that people will be resurrected barefoot, naked, and uncircumcised, and that the believers will have their physical defects healed and their beauty perfected. The verse could also include the possibility of transformation in this life, as a warning; Allah could change people’s forms if He willed, as He transformed some earlier nations into apes and pigs for their disobedience.

However, the primary meaning is the recreation on the Day of Judgment. The argument flows logically from the earlier verses: Allah created you initially from a drop of fluid, He ordained death upon you, and He is fully capable of bringing you back in a new creation. The disbelievers’ difficulty in imagining this stems from their limited perspective, but the Creator who designed the first form from nothing can certainly design a second form.

This verse also humbles human beings, reminding us that we do not control our own bodies. Our forms were given by Allah, and He can alter them as He pleases. The idea of being recreated in unknown forms should inspire awe and reflection on the majesty of the Creator, whose power is not bound by the physical laws we observe. It also serves as a motive for believers to look forward to the perfected state they will enjoy in Paradise, free from all the frailties and limitations of the earthly body.

=62=

After presenting the arguments from creation and death, the surah now appeals directly to the intellect and memory of the listener. “You already know how you were first created” points to the undeniable reality that every human being is aware of their origin. Even a person who denies God intellectually knows that they were once non-existent, then they came into being through a process they did not control. The verse is saying: you have this knowledge, you recognize that your first creation was a deliberate act, so why do you not use that knowledge to reason about the resurrection?

The word translated as “mindful” comes from the Arabic root tadhakkaru, which means to remember, to reflect, to take heed. It is an invitation to connect the dots. The logic is straightforward: the One who did the harder thing—creating you from nothing—is surely able to do the easier thing—bringing you back from dust. The disbelievers of Mecca acknowledged that Allah created them, yet they simultaneously denied the resurrection. This inconsistency is what the verse exposes. They failed to let this knowledge penetrate their hearts and shape their beliefs. The verse is a call to intellectual honesty and spiritual awareness. A person who truly reflects on their own creation, on the miracle of their body, their mind, their faculties, will naturally be mindful of the Creator’s power and the purpose of their existence. Mindfulness here means more than just casual awareness; it means a conscious, continuous remembrance that leads to worship, obedience, and preparation for the Hereafter.

The question “Will you not then be mindful?” is phrased as a gentle rebuke, encouraging the listener to overcome their heedlessness. It also serves as a reminder for believers to renew their faith by contemplating the signs within themselves. The verse thus functions as a bridge between the rational proofs and the moral responsibility that follows from accepting them.

=63=

With this verse, the surah shifts to a new set of signs drawn from agriculture, a domain of human experience that was intimately familiar to the Meccans. The question “Have you considered what you sow?” invites human beings to reflect on the act of planting seeds. A farmer takes grains of wheat or barley, places them into the dark, heavy earth, and then waits. From those seemingly lifeless, dry seeds, green shoots emerge, grow into plants, and yield a harvest. This process is an everyday miracle that is so common it is often overlooked. The verse is asking people to pause and truly consider this phenomenon. What is it about that tiny seed that contains the potential for a whole plant, with its stalks, leaves, and grain? The seed, once buried, appears to be dead and lost, yet it bursts with life.

This is a direct parallel to the resurrection. Human bodies are buried in the earth, and people think they are gone forever. But just as Allah brings a living plant from a dead seed, He can bring a living human being from scattered remains. The agricultural cycle was a powerful sign for an agrarian society, and the Quran frequently uses it as a proof of divine power.

The question also shifts the focus from the mere fact of creation to the process of growth and renewal, teaching that the earth itself is a witness to Allah’s ability to give life to the dead. Every harvest season is a physical demonstration of resurrection. The believers are thus reminded that their eventual rising from the graves is no stranger than the sprouting of a seed they plant with their own hands each year. The verse personalizes the argument, asking each individual to reflect on their own activity, making the lesson immediate and undeniable.

=64=

Following the pattern of the earlier questions about human creation and water, the verse now poses the crucial binary question regarding agriculture. “Is it you who cause it to grow, or is it We Who do so?” A farmer engages in many activities: ploughing the soil, sowing the seeds, irrigating the land, and protecting the crop. But none of these actions actually cause the seed to germinate, the cells to divide, the stalk to emerge, the leaves to form, and the grain to ripen. All of that is the work of Allah. The farmer’s role is to create favorable conditions, but the life force itself is from the Creator.

This distinction is vital for a correct understanding of reliance on Allah. Human beings easily fall into the illusion of self-sufficiency. They work hard, see results, and begin to attribute the outcome to their own effort and skill. The Quran corrects this by reminding people that their efforts are only a means, and the true cause is divine. If Allah did not will the seed to grow, all human labor would be fruitless. This does not negate the importance of effort; Islam encourages work and planning. But it places the results in the hands of Allah, teaching believers to combine action with trust in God.

The verse also continues the argument for resurrection. The One who causes the seed to grow—splitting the hard earth with a delicate green shoot—can just as easily cause a human body to rise from the grave. The process of growth is a constant, visible sign that life can emerge from what appears dead.

The question demands an honest answer from the listener, leading them to acknowledge that it is Allah alone who gives life, a truth that logically extends to the revival of the dead on the Day of Judgment.

=65=

Having established that Allah is the true cause of growth, the verse now presents a frightening possibility that demonstrates His complete control over the harvest. “If We willed, We could simply reduce this harvest to chaff.” After the seeds have germinated, after the plants have grown tall, after the ears of grain have formed, right before the harvest is gathered, Allah could destroy the entire crop, turning it into dry, broken fragments that are useless.

The word “chaff” refers to the brittle, fragmented remains of plants that are good for nothing. The farmers would be “left to lament.” They had invested their labor, their time, their hope, and at the moment of expected reward, they find nothing but ruin. This scenario is a powerful reminder that all provision is contingent on Allah’s mercy. A crop is not guaranteed just because the seeds were sown and the rains came. A sudden disease, a plague of locusts, a fire, or any other calamity could wipe out the entire season’s work. This should instill profound humility in human beings. It also serves as a warning to those who are arrogant in their wealth and who feel secure in their material abundance. In a broader sense, the verse is also a metaphor for human deeds. A person may work hard in this life, accumulating what they think is a harvest of good, but if their deeds are not rooted in faith and sincerity, they may be reduced to worthless chaff on the Day of Judgment. The lament described in the following verses is the cry of those who realize their life’s work has come to nothing. The possibility of total loss should drive a believer to constant gratitude for what they have and reliance on Allah for its preservation.

=66=

This verse quotes the exact words that the ruined farmers would say in their shock and despair: “We have truly suffered a great loss.” The phrase captures the moment of realization when they see their fields barren, their efforts wasted, and their hopes dashed.

The word “truly” adds emphasis to the depth of their conviction about the loss. It is not a small setback but a comprehensive catastrophe.

The word “great” magnifies the scale; this is not a partial loss where some crops survived, but a total annihilation of their livelihood. In the context of the surah, this lament is not just about agriculture. It prefigures the lament of the disbelievers on the Day of Judgment when they see their deeds turned to scattered dust and their place in Paradise completely lost. The Quran in other places describes that the deeds of the disbelievers are like ashes scattered by the wind on a stormy day, leaving them with nothing. The cry “We have truly suffered a great loss” will be the cry of those who realized, too late, that their life in this world was squandered in denial and sin. The verse personalizes the warning by putting the words of lament in the listener’s own mouth, as if to say: this is what you will say if you persist in your heedlessness. It is a literary technique that creates empathy and fear, making the abstract threat of loss feel immediate and real.

For the believer, it is a reminder to constantly evaluate their deeds and ensure that they are not laboring for a harvest that will eventually turn to chaff. The fear of such a loss should motivate a vigilant guarding of one’s faith and actions.

=67=

The lament of the ruined farmers continues and deepens with this statement. “In fact, we have been deprived of our livelihood.

” The word “deprived” here translates an Arabic term that can also mean forbidden or cut off. It expresses a sense of being completely shut out from the provision they expected. It is not just that the crop failed; they feel as if the very means of life has been withheld from them by some overwhelming force. There is a note of finality and hopelessness in their words. They recognize that all their efforts are now useless and there is no way to recover what was lost.

This is the state of the disbeliever on the Day of Judgment, when they will be deprived of Allah’s mercy, barred from Paradise, and cut off from all good. The provision that sustains life will be replaced with the torment of Hell. The verse implicitly asks the listener: do you wish to be among those who are eternally deprived? The agricultural example brings the message down to an everyday level. Every farmer knows the fear of crop failure, and every person depends on the success of the harvest for food. The threat of being deprived is therefore tangible and frightening. It underscores the reality that sustenance is a gift that can be withdrawn at any moment. Gratitude and obedience are the appropriate responses to the One who provides, while ingratitude and denial risk incurring the ultimate deprivation.

The verse concludes the agricultural argument with a sobering image of hopelessness, preparing the way for the next sign—water—which, like the harvest, is a blessing that can be taken away, leaving humans in utter desolation.

=68=

The surah now moves to the third major sign in this series of arguments for divine power and resurrection: water. “Have you considered the water you drink?” Water is the most basic necessity of life. A human being can survive for weeks without food, but only a few days without water. It is so common, so readily available to many, that people rarely stop to think about its miraculous nature.

The question invites contemplation of something utterly ordinary and yet utterly essential. Water has no color, no taste, no smell, yet it is the medium of life, the solvent for countless biological processes, and a source of refreshment and cleansing. The ancient Arabs, living in a parched desert, knew the value of water more acutely than most. A drink of cool, fresh water was one of the greatest pleasures and reliefs they could imagine. By asking them to consider what they drink, Allah is pointing to a blessing they enjoyed daily but perhaps never truly reflected upon.

This is a recurring method in the Quran: to take a familiar experience and turn it into a sign that points to the Creator. The phrase “what you drink” makes it personal; it is not water in the abstract, but the specific water that enters each person’s mouth and sustains their individual life. The verse implicitly prepares the listener for the question that follows: where does this water come from? It also reminds the believers that the very act of drinking, which seems so automatic, is a divine gift that deserves conscious gratitude.

The mention of water also ties back to the earlier descriptions of Paradise, where flowing water is a central delight, contrasting with the boiling water of Hell.

=69=

This verse presents the same binary question that has been used for the previous signs, now applied to the origin of water. “Is it you who bring it down from the clouds, or is it We Who do so?” Rainwater, which fills the wells, springs, and rivers that people drink from, descends from the clouds. No human being has the power to create a cloud, to cause it to release its moisture, or to direct the rain to a specific location. The entire hydrological cycle, from evaporation over the oceans to condensation into clouds to precipitation over land, is a system designed and operated by Allah. In our modern age, we can explain the science of the water cycle, but explaining a mechanism is not the same as causing it to function. The laws of physics themselves are creations of Allah, and He sustains them moment by moment. The verse challenges any claim of human self-sufficiency by pointing to the sky. Could anyone prevent a drought? Could anyone make rain fall on demand, independent of Allah’s will? The disbelievers, who relied on their trade and their idols, are reminded that the very water they drink is under the exclusive control of the Almighty.

The question also reinforces the theme of resurrection. The One who sends down water from the sky, giving life to a dead land, is the One who will send down the rain of resurrection, giving life to dead bodies. The descent of water is a sign of mercy, but also a sign of power. Each raindrop is a testimony to the Creator’s ongoing involvement with His creation, and a promise that He can bring life out of death as easily as He brings water from the clouds.

=70=

After affirming that it is Allah who sends down the fresh water, the verse now points out that even the quality of the water is a gift. “If We willed, We could make it salty.” The natural state of most of the earth’s water is saline, undrinkable seawater. The process of evaporation and precipitation is what desalinates the water and delivers it as fresh rain. If Allah willed, He could alter this process so that the rain itself fell as saltwater, or He could turn all the freshwater sources salty. If that happened, life on land would become impossible. The fact that the water we drink is sweet and palatable is a deliberate mercy from Allah, not a random coincidence of nature.

The verse then asks, “Will you not then give thanks? ” This question is the natural conclusion of the reflection. When a person realizes that their very survival depends on a specific, non-necessary quality of water—its freshness—the only appropriate response is profound gratitude. Ingratitude is not just a failure of manners; it is a failure of perception, an inability to see the kindness of the Creator in the details of daily life. The specific mention of saltiness also connects to the theme of punishment. In the descriptions of Hell, the deniers are given boiling, foul water to drink, a stark contrast to the fresh water they enjoyed in the world without gratitude.

This verse also teaches believers to cultivate a constant awareness of blessings. Even the glass of water they drink without thought is a sign and a gift. Saying “Alhamdulillah” after drinking is a small act that acknowledges this profound truth.

The question “Will you not then give thanks?” is an open invitation to shift from heedlessness to a life of conscious appreciation and worship of the Provider.

=71=

The fourth and final sign in this series is fire. “Have you considered the fire you kindle?” The ancient Arabs would produce fire by rubbing or striking together two pieces of wood from specific trees, notably the markh and afar trees, which even when green and moist could produce sparks. Fire is a dramatic element: it is bright, hot, consuming, and dangerous, yet it is also essential for cooking, warmth, and light.

The question asks people to reflect on this everyday miracle. A piece of green wood, full of moisture, contains within it the potential for a blazing flame. The very idea of fire being hidden within a living tree is a sign that contradicts ordinary expectations. Fire and water are opposites, yet Allah brings one from the other.

This is a powerful metaphor for the resurrection, where life emerges from death, a body emerges from dust. The verse encourages the listener to look beyond the surface of things and recognize the hidden capacities that Allah has placed in creation. For the Meccans, who used fire daily for their meals and for warmth during the cold desert nights, the question was an invitation to see the familiar as extraordinary.

The word “kindle” refers to human action; they start the fire. But the next verse will ask the deeper question: who created the material that makes the fire possible?

The sequence of signs—from human creation, to agriculture, to water, to fire—covers the basic elements of human life: origin, food, drink, and energy. Together, they form a comprehensive case for the recognition of Allah’s power and the certainty of resurrection.

=72=

The familiar pattern reappears, questioning the source of the trees that yield the fire. “Is it you who produce its trees, or is it We Who do so?” A person might kindle a fire by rubbing two branches together, but they did not create the wood with its flammable properties. They did not design the chemical composition that allows a green tree to burn. They did not cause the tree to grow from the earth with the specific characteristic of producing sparks. All of this is the work of Allah. The verse thus strips away the illusion of human production. Humans are at best discoverers and users of what Allah has already created. The green tree that gives fire is a striking paradox. Greenery and moisture are typically associated with cooling and quenching, yet from this very material, fire is extracted. The Creator who embedded this contrasting property in the tree is capable of embedding life in what appears dead.

The question exposes the arrogance of the polytheists who attributed the provision of their needs to their idols or to their own efforts, while ignoring the true Provider. It also deepens the argument for resurrection: if Allah can bring fire from a wet, green tree—an element completely opposite to its apparent nature—then He can certainly bring a living, conscious human being from dry bones and dust. The logic is undeniable and appeals to the direct experience of the listeners.

The verse concludes with an implicit call to recognize Allah’s sole creative power, paving the way for the dual purpose of fire mentioned in the next verse.

=73=

After establishing that Allah produces the trees for fire, the verse explains the wisdom behind this creation. Fire serves two important functions. First, it is “a reminder of the Hellfire.” Every time a human being sees a fire in this world, it should cause them to remember the blazing punishment of the Hereafter. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, said that the fire of this world is only one-seventieth of the fire of Hell, having been cooled by water seventy times.

Thus, the small fires we kindle are dim samples of a far greater and more terrifying reality. When a person feels the heat of a flame or sees its destructive power, they should reflect on what awaits those who earn Allah’s wrath.

This reminder is a mercy, a warning signal built into the fabric of daily life to steer people away from sin and toward repentance. Second, fire is “a provision for the travellers.” This refers to its practical benefits: it provides warmth on cold nights, it cooks food, it gives light in the darkness, and it keeps wild animals away. For the desert traveler, fire is a lifeline, a source of comfort and safety in a harsh environment. The dual description—a reminder of punishment and a provision for travelers—perfectly captures the balance of divine attributes between warning and mercy. Allah uses the same element both to caution and to care.

This verse teaches believers to see the world with dual vision: recognizing the blessings that call for gratitude and the warnings that call for fear and reform. The fire, therefore, is not a neutral object; it is a sign laden with spiritual meaning, inviting us to ponder the consequences of our choices.

=74=

The series of signs—from the creation of humans, the growth of plants, the sending of water, and the production of fire—concludes with a command: “So glorify the Name of your Lord, the Greatest.” The word “so” indicates that this act of glorification is the logical and necessary response to the contemplation of all these divine signs. When a person reflects deeply on the evidence of Allah’s power, wisdom, and mercy that fills the world around them, the natural reaction of a sound heart is to praise and exalt the Creator. “Glorify” means to declare Allah free from all imperfections, to magnify Him, to acknowledge His supreme greatness.

The Name mentioned here is specifically “the Greatest,” emphasizing the attribute of supreme majesty. This command is directed first to the Prophet, peace be upon him, but it extends to all believers. The act of glorification, known as tasbih, is a central practice of worship in Islam. It is performed through words like “Subhanallah,” through prayer, and through a life lived in obedience. The verse suggests that true glorification arises from knowledge and reflection, not merely from mechanical repetition. After the mind has been convinced by the evidence of the resurrection, the heart moves to adoration.

This verse also prepares for the next section of the surah, which will present the oath regarding the noble nature of the Quran. There is a seamless transition: first, the physical signs of creation are presented as proof; now, the revealed word itself will be presented as a sign. The command to glorify is repeated at the very end of the surah in verse 96, creating a beautiful envelope structure that frames the entire message in praise of the Almighty.

=75=

The surah now transitions from the physical signs of creation to a solemn oath regarding the divine origin of the Quran. Allah says, “So I do swear by the positions of the stars.

” The use of an oath by the Creator is a powerful rhetorical device in the Quran. When Allah swears by something in creation, He is drawing attention to its immense significance, its status as a sign, and the truth of the statement that follows. The “positions of the stars” refers to their locations in the sky, their settings and risings, their orbits and constellations. The stars are a breathtaking spectacle, pinpoints of light in the vast darkness, arranged in patterns that have guided travelers for millennia. Their positions are so precise that they can be used for navigation and for calculating time and seasons. This orderliness is a testament to the Creator’s meticulous design. For the Arabs, the stars were deeply familiar; they knew their courses and relied on them for travel across the trackless deserts. By swearing by the positions of the stars, Allah invokes something that the listeners recognized as majestic and dependable. This oath also serves as a rebuttal to the astrological superstitions of the pagans, who sometimes worshipped stars or used them for divination. The Quran redirects attention from false beliefs about stars to their true role as signs of Allah. The oath prepares the mind for a monumental declaration, creating a sense of awe and anticipation. The listener knows that when Allah swears an oath, what follows is a matter of absolute truth and immense importance.

The phrase “I do swear” emphasizes the personal commitment of the Divine Speaker to the truth being conveyed, leaving no room for doubt about what will be revealed regarding the Quran.

=76=

Before completing the oath with its subject, the surah inserts a parenthetical but powerful comment: “and this, if only you knew, is indeed a great oath.” Allah pauses to emphasize the gravity of the oath He has just taken. The stars are not a trivial matter; they are among the most magnificent creations. Their positions, their movements, their distances, and their number are things that human knowledge has only begun to grasp in recent centuries.

The phrase “if only you knew” is a gentle yet profound rebuke. It implies that human beings, in their limited understanding, do not fully appreciate the majesty of what is being sworn by. If they truly understood the nature of the stars, their colossal size, their precise orbits, the forces that govern them, and the wisdom behind their arrangement, they would be overwhelmed by the greatness of this oath. This phrase also creates a sense of mystery and humility. It reminds the listener that their knowledge is incomplete and that there are realities far beyond their everyday perception. The comment also serves to heighten the importance of the statement that is about to follow. By saying the oath is great, Allah is signaling that the subject of the oath is correspondingly immense. The listener is now fully attentive, aware that they are about to hear something of the utmost significance, something that is affirmed by a divine oath of the highest order. The rhetorical effect is to silence any doubt or irreverence and to fill the heart with reverence for what is to be proclaimed.

=77=

Now the subject of the great oath is revealed: “that this is truly a noble Quran.” The word “this” refers to the revelation being recited by the Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him. The oath by the stars confirms that the Quran is truly what it claims to be: a Book of divine origin. The description “noble” translates the Arabic word “karim,” which is rich in meaning. A karim Quran is generous in its guidance, honorable in its origin, elevated in its style, pure in its content, and full of benefits for humanity. It is noble in the sense that it is free from any fault, contradiction, or falsehood. It is karim because it teaches the noblest character and leads to the noblest destination.

The word also implies that the Quran is to be treated with the utmost respect. It is not an ordinary book; it is a treasure from the Lord of all worlds. This declaration directly answers the accusations of the Meccan disbelievers, who called the Quran poetry, magic, or the ravings of a madman. By swearing a mighty oath, Allah establishes beyond any doubt that the Quran is a revelation of truth, worthy of reverence and obedience. The nobility of the Quran is also reflected in its impact on the hearts of those who listen to it sincerely. It transforms individuals and societies, elevating them from the depths of ignorance to the heights of faith and civilization. The verse serves as a profound reassurance to the believers and a definitive challenge to the deniers, who are forced to confront the fact that they are rejecting something declared by the Creator of the stars to be supremely noble.

=78=

The nobility of the Quran is further explained by describing its source and location: it is “in a well-preserved Record.” This refers to the Preserved Tablet, al-Lawh al-Mahfuz, which is with Allah. The Quran existed in this divine Record before it was sent down to the Prophet.

The word for “well-preserved” is “maknun,” which means something that is hidden, protected, and kept safe from any change, corruption, or access by unauthorized beings. The Record is in the presence of Allah, far above the reach of devils or any malicious forces. This means that the Quran we have on earth is a true and accurate transmission of the original, untouched by any alteration.

The concept of a heavenly book is central to Islamic belief about revelation. The Quran was sent down in stages from this protected source, ensuring its perfect preservation.

This verse also refutes the claim that the Prophet invented the Quran. How could a man living in the desert of Arabia produce a book whose original is in a hidden, protected record with the Lord of creation? The preservation of the Quran on earth, which has been a miracle in itself, is a reflection of its heavenly preservation. No other scripture has been memorized by millions, recited daily, and transmitted with such rigorous accuracy across centuries.

The phrase “well-preserved Record” assures the believers that they are holding a piece of divine eternity in their hands, a book that is linked directly to the unchangeable knowledge of Allah. This should fill the heart with reverence and motivate a deep commitment to its study and implementation.

=79=

Continuing the description of the well-preserved Record, the verse states that it is “touched by none except the purified angels.” In the heavenly realm, the original Quranic text in the Preserved Tablet is not accessed by just any being. Only the angels who are purified—free from sin, impurity, and any defect—are permitted to touch it. This emphasizes the absolute sanctity and exalted status of the Quran. The angels who carry the revelation to the prophets are among these purified ones, particularly Jibreel (Gabriel), peace be upon him, who was entrusted with bringing the Quran to the Prophet Muhammad. The fact that even in the heavens only the purest beings can touch the Quran teaches a lesson for human beings on earth. Islamic scholars have derived from this verse the legal ruling that a person in a state of major ritual impurity—such as junub (after sexual relations) or a menstruating woman—should not physically touch the written mushaf (the physical copy of the Quran) until they have purified themselves through ghusl (full bath). While the primary meaning of the verse refers to the angels in heaven, the implication for the believers is clear: the Quran is a Book of immense purity, and those who approach it should do so in a state of physical and spiritual cleanliness.

This is not a restriction meant to make the Quran inaccessible, but rather to cultivate a deep respect for the Word of Allah. The believer should make wudu (ablution) before touching the Quran, and should have a heart that is purified from shirk, arrogance, and major sins. The verse elevates the Quran above any ordinary object, investing it with a sacred aura that commands reverence. The purification of the angels is a model for the purification of the human soul that seeks to truly benefit from the divine message.

=80=

The final verse of this section provides the ultimate summation of the Quran’s origin: it is a revelation, a tanzil, from the Lord of all worlds. The word “revelation” indicates that the Quran was sent down gradually, in stages, from the higher realm to the lower, through the trusted angel to the heart of the Prophet. This process is an act of immense mercy, as the divine wisdom is made accessible to human understanding.

The phrase “from the Lord of all worlds” defines the source. This is not a book of human philosophy, not the invention of a poet, not a collection of myths. It comes from the Rabb, the Creator, Sustainer, and Master of every world, every dimension, every creature. This title is majestic and all-encompassing. It reminds the listener that the Author of the Quran is the One who owns the stars by which He swore, the One who creates humans from a drop, the One who sends rain and brings forth plants, the One who controls life, death, and resurrection. The entire universe is His domain, and this Quran is His communication to humanity. This verse is both a declaration and an invitation. It declares the absolute authority of the Quran, leaving no excuse for those who reject it. And it invites all people to recognize their Lord, to listen to His words, and to enter into a relationship of worship and obedience. It also serves as a contrast to the limited, worldly lords that people take—lords of wealth, power, tribe, and desire. The Quran is from the One true Lord who deserves all worship. This majestic conclusion to the section on the Quran prepares the way for the following verses, which will challenge the deniers about their heedlessness of this noble revelation and its divine source.
