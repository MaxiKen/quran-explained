=21=

After the mention of fruits, the surah adds that the believers will also be served meat from any bird they desire. This further expands the picture of culinary abundance in Paradise, showing that the dwellers will not be limited to fruits alone but will have access to every kind of wholesome food that their souls might crave.

The phrase “any bird they desire” is especially significant because it combines two ideas: complete freedom of choice and immediate fulfillment of that choice. In this world, even the most sumptuous banquet requires one to accept the menu that has been prepared, but in Paradise the menu is entirely subject to the wishes of the believer. If a person thinks of a specific bird, that very meat is brought to them without delay, prepared in the most delicious manner.

The mention of bird meat also reflects a delicacy that was highly prized in Arabian society. Poultry and game birds were considered fine foods, not everyday fare. By promising this, Allah shows that Paradise contains not just basic sustenance but the finest luxuries. Importantly, the verse says “any bird,” indicating that there is no restriction of species or type. Whatever one might desire from the vast variety of birds, it is available. And since this is Paradise, the consumption of this meat does not involve any killing or suffering; it is a creation of Allah brought forth specifically for the enjoyment of the believers. On a deeper level, this verse and the preceding one about fruit reveal that Allah does not ask human beings to abandon all the good things of life, but only to enjoy them within the lawful limits in this world, and He will reward their restraint with unlimited access in the next. The believer who ate halal and avoided haram in this life will find in Paradise every gastronomic delight, purified and perfected, as an eternal reward.

=22=

The surah now describes another blessing awaiting the foremost in Paradise: they will have maidens with gorgeous eyes. The Arabic term used here is hûr ‘în, which is a compound description. Hûr refers to beings of intense whiteness and purity, with eyes of deep black and intense whiteness, creating a striking and beautiful contrast. ‘În describes wide, beautiful eyes that captivate the beholder. These maidens, often referred to as the hûr al-‘ayn, are a special creation of Paradise, not human women from the earthly life. They are described throughout the Quran and hadith as beings of extraordinary beauty, purity, and refinement, created specifically as companions for the believing men and women in Paradise. It is important to note that the blessings of Paradise are described in terms that resonate with human understanding of beauty and desire, but the reality is far beyond what words can convey. The gorgeous eyes symbolize not just physical attractiveness but also a state of perpetual delight, sincerity, and devotion, for the eyes are the windows to the soul. In Paradise, these companions will have eyes that reflect only love, contentment, and complete fidelity.

The mention of this blessing serves multiple purposes. For the early Muslim men, many of whom were unmarried or had left worldly attachments for the sake of faith, it was a promise of pure companionship in the Hereafter. It also demonstrates that Allah’s rewards encompass every dimension of human nature, including the social and emotional need for intimate companionship, and He fulfills it in the most perfect and beautiful way possible.

=23=

This verse provides a simile to help the human mind grasp the beauty of the maidens of Paradise: they are like pristine pearls. The image of a pearl immediately evokes several qualities. A pearl is formed in the depths of the sea, hidden away from dust and contamination, emerging with a flawless luster and smoothness. It is precious, rare, and highly valued. By comparing the companions of Paradise to pristine pearls, the Quran conveys that they are untouched by any impurity, perfectly preserved, and possess a radiant, gentle beauty.

The word “pristine” adds the quality of being unblemished and in their original, perfect state. In pre-Islamic Arabia, pearls were among the most treasured luxuries, obtained through dangerous diving expeditions in the Persian Gulf. The comparison would have been instantly understood as signifying something of immense worth and delicate splendor.

Moreover, a pearl is an organic gem, growing slowly in a protective shell, which carries a sense of mystery and hidden beauty. The hûr, like pearls, are hidden from the eyes of this world, awaiting the believers in the treasuries of Paradise.

This description also emphasizes their purity, modesty, and untouched nature. The simile helps the believer understand that the joys of Paradise are not coarse or vulgar but are characterized by refinement, elegance, and untainted perfection.

The use of simile is also a merciful act from Allah, who translates the ineffable realities of the unseen into images drawn from human experience, so that we might be motivated to strive for that which our minds can at least partially conceive.

=24=

After describing the various delights of the foremost, the surah pauses to explain the principle behind these gifts: all of this is given as a reward for what they used to do. This is a crucial verse because it establishes the justice and logic of the reward. Paradise is not an arbitrary gift distributed without reason, nor is it attained merely by wishful thinking or claims of faith. It is the direct compensation for a life of righteous deeds, sincere worship, and patient struggle in the path of Allah.

The phrase “what they used to do” encompasses every act of obedience, every good deed, every instance of resisting temptation, every sacrifice made for the sake of truth. The use of the past tense indicates that the deeds have been completed in the worldly life, and now the time for action is over and the time for recompense has arrived.

This verse also addresses a misconception that might arise from the luxurious descriptions. Someone might wonder how such extravagant rewards could be granted, but Allah clarifies that they are not disproportionate; they are earned. Every prostration in prayer, every penny given in charity, every moment of patience in hardship, every stand taken for justice has been recorded and is now being repaid in a currency of eternal bliss. The believers are not receiving something for nothing. This provides a sense of dignity to the reward. The inhabitants of Paradise are not charity cases; they are honored guests who are receiving what their own hands put forward. It also encourages the believers to do more, knowing that no good deed is ever lost, and every small effort in this world will be met with an unimaginable recompense in the Hereafter.

=25=

The surah now shifts from the physical pleasures of Paradise to the spiritual and moral purity of its environment. The believers are promised that in Paradise they will never hear any idle or sinful talk. “Idle talk” refers to speech that is vain, useless, and devoid of benefit. It includes gossip, frivolous chatter, empty entertainment, and words that waste time without bringing anyone closer to truth or goodness. “Sinful talk” refers to speech that is outright harmful: lies, backbiting, slander, cursing, blasphemy, and words that promote evil. In this world, the human ear is constantly bombarded with such speech, whether in private conversations, media, or public discourse. Much of what people say to each other serves no higher purpose, and much of it actually causes harm. Paradise is purified from all of this. The atmosphere there is one of complete verbal purity. No one will say anything to offend, distress, or mislead another. No one will engage in meaningless babbling.

This is a profound blessing that many might overlook. The psychological torment caused by hurtful words, the mental clutter generated by constant trivial talk, and the spiritual degradation that comes from exposure to sinful speech are all aspects of worldly life that weigh heavily on the human soul. In Paradise, this burden is lifted. The silence is filled only with what is beautiful and uplifting. The absence of idle and sinful talk also reflects the perfected character of the inhabitants of Paradise. They have been purified of the base impulses that lead to such speech, and their company is a source of unending peace and intellectual delight.

=26=

This verse completes the thought of the previous one by describing what will be heard in Paradise instead of idle or sinful talk: only good and virtuous speech. The Arabic phrase “qilan salama” conveys a greeting of peace, but in its broader sense it encompasses all speech that is pure, wholesome, and harmonious. The inhabitants of Paradise will exchange greetings of peace with one another and with the angels. Their conversations will be filled with words of love, gratitude, praise of Allah, and sharing of the joy they are experiencing. “Good and virtuous speech” means that every word spoken in Paradise will be positive, constructive, and uplifting. There will be no arguments, no disagreements, no harsh words, no sarcasm, no complaints. The believers will enjoy the most pleasant conversations, filled with wisdom, beauty, and mutual affection.

This is the natural result of hearts that have been purified from envy, anger, and all spiritual diseases. The verbal environment of Paradise reflects the inner state of its people.

This verse also serves as a reminder to believers in this world to strive to purify their own speech. The Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him, said that whoever believes in Allah and the Last Day should speak good or remain silent. The speech of a believer in this life is training for the speech of Paradise. By avoiding idle and sinful talk here, one prepares oneself for the pure discourse of the Hereafter. The promise of a place where only peace and goodness are heard is a powerful motivation to guard the tongue and to seek the company of the righteous even in this world.

=27=

With this verse, the surah transitions from the description of the foremost to the second group: the people of the right. The structure mirrors exactly what was said about them back in verse eight, creating a literary and emotional rhythm. The exclamation “how blessed will they be” is repeated, reigniting the feeling of wonder and admiration. The people of the right, as explained earlier, are the generality of the believers who will receive their records in their right hands. Their station, while below that of the foremost nearest to Allah, is still one of immense happiness and salvation. The rhetorical device used here is designed to make the listener stop and reflect. After hearing the magnificent rewards of the foremost, one might think that anything less would be a disappointment. But the Quran quickly dispels that notion by emphasizing that the people of the right are also in a state of tremendous blessedness that defies description. Their fate is not a consolation prize; it is a glorious and joyful destiny in its own right.

The repetition of the exclamation also serves to divide the surah into clear sections. The listener is being guided through the three groups one by one, with each section introduced by this powerful, emotion-laden phrase. It builds anticipation and makes the subsequent description of their rewards more impactful. For the believers listening, it provides reassurance that even if they have not reached the heights of the foremost, their place in Paradise is still beyond imagination, filled with delights that will make them forget every hardship they ever endured in the world.

=28=

The description of the rewards for the people of the right begins with an image of natural beauty: they will be amid thornless lote trees. The lote tree, known in Arabic as sidr, was a familiar tree to the Arabs. In the desert, the sidr tree was valued for its shade and its fruit, but the wild varieties were often covered with thorns, making them difficult to approach and enjoy. By specifying that the lote trees of Paradise are thornless, Allah removes the one defect associated with this tree in the earthly experience and makes it perfect. This imagery operates on multiple levels. First, it speaks of a landscape that is both beautiful and comfortable, free from the irritations and dangers that characterize the natural world after the fall. Thorns are a symbol of the curse of toil and hardship; in Paradise, nature itself cooperates fully with human happiness. Second, the lote tree has a special significance in Islamic tradition, as the Sidrat al-Muntaha, the Lote Tree of the Utmost Boundary, is the celestial tree that marks the highest point of creation visited by the Prophet during his ascension. While the trees mentioned here are not necessarily that specific tree, the association elevates the status of the tree in the minds of believers. To be amid such trees is to be in a garden of exceptional sanctity and beauty. The shade, fragrance, and fruit of the thornless lote trees contribute to an environment of complete serenity, where the believers can relax and enjoy without any hindrance or discomfort.

=29=

Continuing the portrait of the gardens for the people of the right, the surah mentions “clusters of bananas.” The Arabic term used here is “talhin mandud,” which commentators have interpreted as banana trees bearing fruit arranged in neat, piled-up clusters. Bananas represent a fruit that is both delicious and satisfying, easy to eat, and comes in a form that is aesthetically pleasing. The fact that they are mentioned in clusters emphasizes abundance and easy accessibility. The believer does not need to search or work for a single fruit; the trees offer their produce in generous, stacked layers, ready to be picked. Some scholars have noted that the banana tree is a plant with broad, lush leaves that provide excellent shade, adding to the overall picture of a well-furnished garden. In a desert environment, the banana, a tropical fruit, would have been known as a luxury import, something rare and exotic, making its promise all the more appealing. On another level, the mention of specific fruits like lote berries and bananas reveals the generosity of Allah in describing Paradise. He could have simply said “there will be fruits,” but He named specific types so that the human mind could grasp the reality more concretely. The variety also shows that Paradise caters to all tastes; some may prefer the taste of lote fruit, others bananas, and all will find exactly what they desire. This meticulous care in describing the blessings demonstrates that Paradise is not a vague spiritual state but a tangible, physical reality where every sense will be engaged and delighted in the most perfect way.

=30=

Shade is one of the most emphasized blessings of Paradise in the Quran, and here it is described as “extended shade.” In the scorching climate of Arabia, shade was not a luxury but a necessity for survival. The blazing sun made any patch of shadow a precious refuge. The promise of extended shade, therefore, would resonate deeply with the first listeners. But in Paradise, this shade is not the temporary shadow of a cloud or a building that shifts and disappears with the movement of the sun. It is a permanent, stretched-out shade that never recedes, a canopy of comfort that covers the gardens continuously. There is no harsh sunlight to cause discomfort, no need to move from one spot to another to find relief. The shade is always there, deep and cool, creating an environment of perpetual pleasantness. On a symbolic level, shade represents divine protection and tranquility. In the Quran, the shade of Hell is described as the shadow of black smoke, offering no relief, whereas the shade of Paradise is the shadow of Allah’s mercy, encompassing the believers in safety and peace. The extended shade also hints at the vastness of Paradise; its canopies stretch as far as the eye can see, so that every inhabitant is always within this comfortable shelter. The believer who labored under the heat of the sun in this world, who faced hardships and toil, is rewarded with an eternity of perfect climate, where the external environment mirrors the inner peace of the soul.

=31=

Water is a recurring theme in the Quranic descriptions of Paradise, and here it is mentioned as “flowing water.” The beauty of this phrase lies in its evocation of movement, freshness, and life. Stagnant water becomes foul, but flowing water is constantly renewed, clear, and pure. In the dry and arid land of Arabia, a flowing stream was the ultimate image of abundance and luxury. Gardens in the desert are only possible where water flows, so the mention of flowing water confirms that Paradise is a place of verdant, thriving life. The water in Paradise is not just for drinking; it flows through the gardens, creating a soothing soundscape, watering the trees and plants, and providing visual delight with its sparkling surface. The believers will be able to enjoy its coolness, to see it run at their feet, and to direct its flow wherever they please. Islamic texts describe rivers in Paradise of water that is never stale, of milk that never sours, of wine that is delicious, and of purified honey. The flowing water mentioned here is part of that hydrological perfection. There is also a spiritual dimension: water is the source of physical life, and in Paradise, it symbolizes the eternal life that the believers have been granted. Just as water revives a dead land, the mercy of Allah revives the hearts of the believers and admits them into the everlasting gardens. The promise of flowing water would be especially comforting to those early Muslims who experienced thirst and hardship during their struggles in Mecca.

=32=

The description of Paradise continues with the mention of “abundant fruit.” This is not a repetition of earlier verses but rather an emphasis on the sheer quantity and variety of fruit available to the people of the right. While the foremost were described as having any fruit they choose, the people of the right are here assured that the fruits are abundant, never scarce, and always within reach.

The word “abundant” suggests that there is no rationing, no shortage, no waiting for a season. The trees are perpetually laden with fruit, and the branches are lowered in humility to make picking effortless. In the world, fruit is often subject to market forces; the poor may not be able to afford it, and even the wealthy must deal with seasonal availability. In Paradise, these constraints are abolished. Every believer has access to an overflowing abundance of the most delicious fruits. This speaks to the generosity of Allah, who gives without measure. The abundance of fruit also reflects the principle that in Paradise, there is no labor. One does not need to plant, irrigate, tend, and harvest; the fruits appear as a direct provision from the Lord. This effortless enjoyment is a reward for the effort the believers put into their worship and good deeds in the world. They toiled in obedience, and now they rest in provision.

The mention of fruit repeatedly throughout these verses also has a cumulative effect on the imagination, painting a picture of an orchard so rich that the mind struggles to encompass it all, a garden that is literally overflowing with every good thing.

=33=

This verse adds two crucial qualities to the abundant fruit of Paradise: it is never out of season and it is never forbidden. The first quality, “never out of season,” means that the fruits of Paradise are not subject to the cycles of nature that govern this world. On earth, each fruit has its specific season. Mangoes come in the summer, oranges in the winter, and if you desire a fruit outside its time, you simply cannot have it fresh. But in Paradise, the seasons are unified into a single eternal moment of perfect climate. Every fruit is always in season, always ripe, always at its peak of flavor. The believer can enjoy strawberries and pomegranates simultaneously, in any moment, without any decline in quality.

The second quality, “nor forbidden,” addresses a deeper concern. In this world, some things are forbidden to believers for their own good, such as pork, alcohol, and other prohibited items. But the fruit of Paradise is never subject to any prohibition. The believer can eat whatever fruit he sees, without any dietary restrictions, without any fear that something might be harmful or unlawful.

This is because Paradise is a realm of absolute purity, where nothing harmful exists. The absence of prohibition also signifies the removal of divine law’s restrictive aspect; what remains is pure permissibility and enjoyment. Together, these two qualities assure the believers that their desires will never be frustrated by either natural limitations or legal restrictions, and that the provision of Paradise is perfectly tailored to their complete and lasting satisfaction.

=34=

This verse completes the initial description of the people of the right’s environment by mentioning “elevated furnishings.” The Arabic word “furush” refers to what is spread out for sitting and reclining, such as mattresses, cushions, carpets, and couches. That these furnishings are “elevated” means they are raised high, both in terms of physical height and in terms of quality and luxury. In a physical sense, elevated couches allow the inhabitants to enjoy a view of the gardens around them, to see the rivers, trees, and companions from a position of comfort and honor. Psychologically, being raised up is associated with dignity and status. In the courts of kings, the throne is always elevated above the floor level, signifying the importance of the one seated. In Paradise, every believer is treated like royalty. The furnishings are not mere functional mats on the ground but plush, raised sofas and divans fitted with the finest materials. The elevation also has a practical aspect: it removes the sitter from any possibility of ground moisture, dust, or insects, though of course Paradise is free of such nuisances. It enhances the sense of cleanliness and refinement. On a spiritual level, the elevated furnishings symbolize the raising of the believers’ ranks. In the world, they may have been humble and lowly, sitting on the ground in poverty, but in the Hereafter, Allah lifts them up and seats them on high, honoring them before all creation.

This image of elevation and rest concludes the portrait of a garden that is physically comfortable, visually stunning, and emotionally reassuring.

=35=

The surah now turns to a deeply personal aspect of Paradise: the spouses and companions of the believing men and women. Allah declares with emphasis, “Indeed, We will have perfectly created their mates.

” The use of “Indeed, We” underscores the direct divine involvement and the certainty of this creation. The mates referred to here are the spouses that the believers will have in Paradise. For the believing men, these will include the hûr al-‘ayn, the pure maidens of Paradise, but also their believing wives from the worldly life, who will be transformed and perfected. For the believing women, the scholars explain that they too will have spouses in Paradise, and they will be the queens of their dwellings, satisfied and content in ways that far surpass any earthly marriage. The key point is that these mates are “perfectly created.” They are not born through a gradual process of growth and development but are directly brought into being by Allah’s creative power in a state of complete perfection. There is no flaw, no defect, no aging, no illness, no imperfection in their beauty or character. They are created specifically for the purpose of providing loving companionship in Paradise. This act of new creation also means that any negative history, any marital discord or sorrow that occurred in the world, is completely erased and replaced with a fresh, flawless relationship. The perfection of the mates is an essential element of the perfect happiness of Paradise, addressing the deep human need for intimate and harmonious companionship.

=36=

This verse describes one specific aspect of the perfection of the mates in Paradise: they will be made virgins. The Arabic word “abkaran” means those who have never been touched before, indicating purity, freshness, and exclusive devotion. This quality applies to the hûr al-‘ayn, the specially created maidens of Paradise, but it also applies to the believing women from the worldly life who enter Paradise, as they will be recreated in a new, perfected form, with all physical imperfections and past states transformed into pristine purity.

The concept of virginity here is not merely physical; it carries connotations of complete fidelity, undivided love, and a relationship that is entirely focused on one’s spouse without any previous attachments or comparisons. In Paradise, there is no jealousy, no sense of a partner’s past, no emotional baggage. The mates are created uniquely for their companions, and their love is pure and unchanging. For the believing women, Islamic scholars have explained that Allah’s justice and mercy ensure that they too will find complete satisfaction and that they will have spouses who are dedicated to them. The description of virginity also addresses the human desire for exclusive, special companionship. In this world, relationships can be complicated by histories and heartbreaks. In Paradise, Allah provides a fresh start, a bond that is perfect from its beginning and remains perfect for eternity.

This is part of the comprehensive comfort and joy that the believers will experience, a gift that caters to the emotional and psychological dimensions of human nature.

=37=

The perfection of the paradisal mates is further elaborated with two more qualities: they are loving and of equal age. The Arabic word “uruban” translated as “loving” conveys a sense of deep affection, tenderness, and passionate devotion. These are not cold or formal companionships but relationships filled with genuine love and warmth. The mates in Paradise are not merely beautiful; they are also emotionally compatible, expressing and inspiring love in a way that brings constant joy.

The phrase “of equal age” refers to the fact that all the inhabitants of Paradise, including the mates, will be of the same age of youthful perfection. Most scholars have said this age is around thirty-three years, the age of full physical maturity and peak vigor. No one in Paradise ages or changes; they remain in this state of ideal youth forever. This has profound implications. In this world, couples often grow old together, and while there is beauty in that, aging also brings physical decline, illness, and eventually separation through death. In Paradise, the companions are always at their prime, always equally youthful, and never face the sorrow of watching a loved one deteriorate. The equality of age also promotes harmony; there is no generational gap, no difference in energy levels or interests. Everyone is in a perfect state of being, and their love for one another is as fresh and vibrant on the millionth year as it was on the first.

This promise should inspire believers to work hard for Paradise, where the fleeting relationships of this world are replaced by eternal, perfect unions.

=38=

This brief verse ties all the preceding descriptions directly to the people of the right, confirming that these blessings are their specific share. The verse states “for the people of the right,” connecting the perfected spouses, the loving companions of equal age, to the group whose blessedness was exclaimed upon earlier. This explicit link serves several functions. First, it removes any ambiguity: the magnificent rewards being described are not for the foremost alone but for this larger body of believers as well. The people of the right, who form the majority of the inhabitants of Paradise, will also enjoy these refined pleasures. Second, it reassures the believers that their destiny is secure and detailed. Allah has not forgotten their need for companionship and love; He has specifically prepared these things for them. Third, it creates a sense of identity and solidarity. The listener who aspires to be among the people of the right now knows exactly what awaits them, making their hope more concrete and their motivation stronger.

The verse also serves as a structural marker, indicating that the description of their blessings is nearing completion and that the surah will soon move on to the next group. This concise statement is like a seal on a divine decree, making it personal and direct. It is as if Allah is saying: all these beautiful things, these gardens, fruits, shades, waters, furnishings, and perfect companions, are designated specifically for you, O people of the right, as a reward from your Lord.

=39=

The surah now provides demographic information about the people of the right, parallel to what was said about the foremost. A multitude of them will be from the earlier generations. This refers to the believers who lived from the beginning of humanity until the time of the final prophet. That they are described as a “multitude” indicates a large number, a throng, a great company.

This is comforting for several reasons. It shows that throughout history, despite the prevalence of ignorance and disbelief, there have always been many who believed and did righteous deeds. The path of truth has never been completely abandoned. It also gives a sense of community; the believer in later times is not alone but is part of a vast spiritual family that stretches across millennia.

The earlier generations had the advantage of receiving direct prophetic guidance, witnessing miracles, and being close to the wellsprings of revelation. Their faith, which often required tremendous sacrifices to establish the truth in the face of hostile environments, earned them this great reward. For the Meccan Muslims, who were few in number and felt isolated, this verse would have been a powerful reminder that the community of believers in the Hereafter is enormous, filled with the righteous from all ages. It expands the mind beyond the present struggle and places it within the grand narrative of salvation history, where Allah’s mercy has always embraced those who respond to His call.

=40=

This verse completes the description of the people of the right by stating that a multitude from the later generations will also be among them. Unlike the foremost, where the later generations were described as “a few,” for the people of the right, both the earlier and the later generations are described as multitudes.

This is a significant difference and a point of great hope. It means that the general body of the saved believers will be vast in number, drawn from all epochs of human history. The later generations, which include the Muslim community after the Prophet until the end of time, will contribute a large number to this group. This reflects the expansion of the Muslim nation, the spread of Islam to all corners of the earth, and the countless individuals who will believe, perform good deeds, and die in a state of faith. The mercy of Allah is vast, and His Paradise is spacious; it can accommodate a multitude upon multitude.

This verse also balances the earlier mention that the foremost among the later generations are few. While the highest ranks of nearness are attained by a minority due to their exceptional striving, the general ranks of Paradise are accessible to a much wider circle of believers. This should neither breed complacency nor despair. It encourages the believer to strive for excellence to be among the foremost, while also providing reassurance that sincere faith and obedience, even if not at the level of the spiritual elite, still lead to an eternally blissful destination. The presence of multitudes from both earlier and later generations creates a glorious assembly of the saved, a massive celebration of divine mercy that encompasses the faithful from the dawn of time to its very end.
