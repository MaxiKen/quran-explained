=1=

The surah opens with a powerful rhetorical question: “What are they asking one another about?” This is not a request for information but a device that arrests attention and draws the listener straight into the heart of the matter. The “they” here refers to the pagan Quraysh of Mecca, who, upon hearing the Prophet’s message about the resurrection and the Day of Judgement, began questioning one another in tones that mixed curiosity, scepticism and outright mockery. The verb “asking one another” shows that this was a widespread phenomenon—people were talking amongst themselves, exchanging doubts and half-mocking enquiries rather than seeking sincere answers. Some would say, “What is this news Muhammad is bringing? Is it sorcery, poetry or soothsaying?” Others would ask sarcastically, “Will we really be brought back to life after we have become dust and bones?

” The question the surah poses mimics their own question back to them, but with a profound shift: Allah is now asking about their question. This immediately puts them in the spotlight and makes their casual chatter an object of divine scrutiny. The very act of asking “what” implies that something enormous is being discussed, something that has unsettled them, and the surah will soon name it. By framing the opening in this way, the Qur’an engages the reader or listener directly and creates a sense of anticipation. We are made to wonder: what is this news that has stirred such intense debate?

The question also carries a subtle rebuke. It suggests that what they are casually asking about is far too serious to be reduced to idle talk or mutual wrangling.

The opening verse, therefore, sets the stage for the entire surah. It introduces the theme of disputation surrounding the “momentous news” and hints at the arrogance of those who treat the ultimate realities of existence as matters for entertainment or partisan argument.

The mention of “one another” underscores that the debate was not just private; it was a public, communal affair, and the entire society was caught up in it. This communal denial and confusion will be answered by a sequence of evidence and warning that leaves no room for doubt. So right from these first few words, the listener is positioned to take seriously what will follow, recognising that the question being bandied about in the streets of Mecca was, in truth, the most consequential question a human being can ever face.

=2=

The answer to the opening question comes immediately: “About the momentous news.” The Arabic term translated as “momentous news” is an-naba’ al-‘azīm, a phrase packed with gravity. Naba’ means important news, the kind of report that changes everything once it is known. ‘Azīm intensifies it to mean something tremendously weighty, awesome and profound. The earliest commentators of the Qur’an, among them the Companion Ibn ‘Abbās, explained that this momentous news refers primarily to the resurrection and the Day of Judgement, while others said it is the Qur’an itself, for the Qur’an carries the news of that Day and many other unseen realities. Both interpretations are compatible because the Qur’an is the vehicle of this ultimate news. What the Meccans were debating was precisely the message that there is life after death, that every soul will be held accountable, and that a day of absolute justice and recompense is inevitable. To the pagan mind, this was astonishing, even unbelievable. They saw death as final and could not conceive of their decayed bodies being brought back to life.

Yet the news was not a baseless claim; it came from the Lord of all worlds through a trustworthy messenger. The surah labels their topic of discussion “the momentous news” in order to jolt them out of their casual attitude. If they only grasped how immense this news truly is, they would not ask about it with smug condescension.

The phrase also prepares the ground for the threats and promises that fill the rest of the surah. Calling it “news” reminds us that it is information about a future event, information that one can either accept or reject, but which, once it comes to pass, will no longer be a matter of debate.

The word choice implies that the disbelievers themselves, by constantly talking about it, have testified to its importance. If it were trivial, they would have ignored it. Their intense disagreement itself proves that something monumental has been placed before them. The surah seizes on this reality and forces them to face the content of their own conversations, now described with the awe-inspiring epithet it truly deserves. This verse thus functions as a bridge: from the question about their talk, we move to the identity of that talk, and the remaining verses will first warn them that they will come to know its truth, then lay out the evidence in creation that makes the news entirely credible.

=3=

Having identified the subject as the momentous news, the surah adds: “over which they disagree.” The Quraysh could not settle on a single coherent objection to the Prophet’s message. Some called it “magic,” others “poetry,” others “ancient fables,” and still others claimed it was a fabrication taught to him by someone. This disunity of criticism is itself a sign of the weakness of their position. When truth is clear, those who oppose it often resort to contradictory accusations because they cannot find one solid basis for rejection. The Qur’an highlights this elsewhere, noting that their hearts are alike in denial even though their words differ. The disagreement was not only about how to label the message, but also about the nature of the resurrection itself. Some believed in a form of afterlife but distorted it; others flatly denied any life after death, thinking the very idea absurd. The verse captures that chaotic state of mind in which people argue passionately about something they have not sincerely investigated. Deep down, their disagreement arose from a shared desire to evade accountability. If they admitted the news was true, they would have to change their entire way of life, abandon idolatry, and prepare for a judgement they did not wish to face. So they preferred to keep the matter in the realm of endless debate, hoping that as long as they disagreed among themselves and with the Prophet, they could postpone any personal reckoning. The surah’s response is to state that this disagreement does nothing to alter the fact of the news itself. The day will come when all the quarrelling will be silenced, and everyone will see with certainty what they once disputed.

The placement of this verse right after the mention of the momentous news underscores a recurring Qur’anic pattern: humanity is given a clear message, yet instead of submitting, people often fall into contention. The believers are then comforted by the knowledge that this is not new; earlier nations also disputed with their prophets. The disagreement itself becomes a proof that the message is significant, for trivial matters do not provoke such sustained controversy.

Finally, the verse ties back to the first verse: their mutual questioning was not a calm search for truth but a state of confusion and conflict, and this is precisely what the warning “they will come to know” will answer.

=4=

This verse begins with the Arabic particle kallā, which is a strong, emphatic negation that interrupts the previous line of thought. Translated here as “But no!”, it sharply dismisses the disbelievers’ attitude of denial and mockery. It is as though Allah is saying: Their disagreement and scoffing are completely misplaced; reality is not defined by their opinions. Immediately after this forceful negation comes the threat: “They will come to know. ” The future tense carries absolute certainty. The knowledge referred to is not intellectual discovery but experiential realisation—they will taste the consequence of their denial when the punishment arrives. Scholars of tafsir explain that this knowing may take place in stages. The polytheists of Mecca would soon experience the truth of the Prophet’s warning in this world through the defeat at Badr and other events, and ultimately every human being will know the truth with certainty at the moment of death or on the Day of Resurrection. Some commentators mention that the first “they will come to know” in this verse points to the punishment of the grave, while the repetition in the next verse refers to the terrors of the Day of Judgement, but these are interpretive possibilities, and the verse itself leaves the timing intentionally broad so that it covers every level of realisation. The threat is not an arbitrary show of force; it is the logical outcome of rejecting clear truth after being given every opportunity to accept it. By stating “they will come to know” rather than specifying the punishment in detail right away, the surah creates a powerful sense of dread and anticipation. The listener understands that something inescapable is heading towards those who persist in their arrogance.

The verse also serves as a comfort to the Prophet and the believers, who were themselves mocked and harassed. It reassures them that the final word does not belong to the scoffers. Justice may be delayed, but it is never cancelled. The eloquence of the verse lies in its brevity and force; it is a thunderbolt in the middle of the dispute, cutting off all arguments and turning the tables on those who thought themselves safely entrenched in their denial. It prepares the heart for the detailed proofs of divine power that follow, because once the certainty of the outcome is established, the evidence for it can be calmly laid out.

=5=

The repetition of the warning in identical words—“Again, no! They will come to know”—intensifies the effect dramatically. In Arabic rhetoric, repetition is a device used to emphasise the absolute certainty of a statement and to leave no room for ambiguity.

The second kallā is like a double seal on the threat. It tells us that the matter is so serious that it deserves to be said twice, and that the coming knowledge is not a mere possibility but an iron-clad reality. If the first “they will come to know” breaks the disbelievers’ complacency, the second one shatters any lingering hope that the warning might be an exaggeration. Some scholars have noted that the repetition corresponds to two aspects of knowing: one in this life, when the signs of the Qur’an and the truth of the Prophet’s mission become undeniable, and the other in the Hereafter, when they face the full consequences. Others say it points to the repeated shocks of the Day of Judgement itself, where terrifying realisation will come in waves. Whatever the precise import, the overall message is that ignorance and denial have an expiry date.

The surah now pivots from warning to proof. The rhetorical questions that follow in the next verses are connected to this warning by a profound logic: the One who created the heavens and the earth with such precision and care is surely able to bring about the Day He has appointed.

Thus the threats in verses 4 and 5 act as a strong hinge. Before them, the surah identified the subject of dispute; after them, it will unfold the evidence that makes the news credible. This structure teaches a vital lesson about how the Qur’an addresses doubt: it first establishes the gravity of the message and the certainty of the outcome, and then it leads the listener gently through the observable signs of creation.

The repetition also has an emotional effect on the reciter and the attentive listener. It instils a healthy fear of the Hereafter and a sense of urgency to reflect before that knowledge comes in a way that no longer benefits the soul. By ending the verse with the same phrase, the surah engraves the warning into the heart, making it impossible to read the subsequent signs with a detached or indifferent mind.

=6=

With this verse, the surah begins a magnificent series of rhetorical questions, each starting with “Have We not…?” The first of these draws attention to the earth itself: “Have We not smoothed out the earth like a bed?

” The question expects an affirmative answer—indeed, every human being who walks upon the ground knows that the earth has been made spread out and stable. The word translated as “smoothed out” and “like a bed” comes from the Arabic root mahd, which refers to a cradle or a bed prepared for rest.

The image is tender and domestic. Just as a caring mother prepares a comfortable bed for her child, so has the Creator prepared the earth to be a place of dwelling, ease and rest for humanity. The earth is not a hostile, jagged surface that makes life impossible; it has been levelled and softened so that we can build, farm, travel and sleep upon it. The metaphor of a bed also connects directly to what comes a few verses later, when sleep itself is described as a form of rest. There is a profound harmony between the earth as a bed and sleep as a state of repose; the outer bed of the earth and the inner state of sleep are two gifts that work together. Beyond the immediate blessing, the verse carries a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who smoothed out the earth from a chaotic, uneven state and made it a suitable home is fully able to transform it again on the Day of Judgement into the plain of gathering. The Meccans themselves enjoyed the benefits of the smoothed-out earth every day, yet they denied the power of the One who prepared it for them. By asking the question, the surah invites them to reflect on what they already take for granted. It reminds them that the earth did not make itself habitable; a wise and powerful Maker stands behind it. This gentle interrogation is far more effective than a blunt statement because it forces the mind to concede the truth before it moves on to the next sign. The earth as a bed is thus the first stone in the foundation of evidence that will leave the deniers without any excuse.

=7=

The second sign follows naturally: “and made the mountains as its pegs.” After spreading the earth like a bed, the Creator anchored it with mountains that function as pegs. The imagery is drawn from the Bedouin tent, which is held firm by pegs driven into the ground. Without the pegs, the tent would collapse or be blown away by the wind. Similarly, mountains have deep roots that extend into the earth’s crust and stabilise the land, preventing it from shaking excessively. The Qur’an makes this function explicit in other verses, such as “He has cast into the earth firmly set mountains, lest it shift with you” (16:15). The early Arabs, living in a landscape dominated by towering mountains, could immediately grasp the analogy. But the verse goes beyond physical function. The mountains are not only functional; they are also majestic signs of the Creator’s power. They stand as silent witnesses to a design that balances the planet. The transition from the earth as a bed to the mountains as its pegs creates a complete picture of a stable, secure world prepared for human life. The bed needs its pegs, and the pegs serve the purpose of the bed. There are no useless elements in Allah’s creation. For the deniers of the resurrection, the mountains posed a particular challenge to their thinking. They saw them as symbols of permanence and solidity, yet the surah will later declare that on the Day of Judgement these very mountains will be blown away and become like a mirage. So the description here of mountains as pegs is deliberate: it establishes that these colossal formations, which seem eternal, are in fact placed and controlled by Allah. He who pegged them down can also uproot them.

The question “Have We not made…” again forces the listener to acknowledge the reality of the mountains’ stabilising role. No one can credibly claim that the mountains came into being by themselves or that their beneficial placement is the result of random chance. The verse humbles the human observer and points him towards the One who alone has the power to fashion the earth in this manner, and who therefore has the power to reshape it for the Day of Final Decision that the surah is building towards.

=8=

After the external signs on the earth, the surah turns to the human being himself: “and created you in pairs.” The term “pairs” translates the Arabic azwāj, which includes the meaning of male and female, spouses, and also more generally all complementary things. The immediate and most powerful implication is the creation of humanity as men and women, designed for companionship, love, procreation and mutual support.

The pairing is a sign of Allah’s wisdom and mercy. Through it, life continues across generations, and the human heart finds comfort in the bond of marriage, as described elsewhere in the Qur’an: “And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them” (30:21). The verse in this surah, however, does not only point to emotional and social benefits. It serves as a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who originated human beings from a single soul and then spread from them countless men and women is certainly capable of bringing them back together after death. If He could produce the first pair and then multiply them through the reproductive process, why would a second creation be difficult for Him? The Meccan pagans, who acknowledged Allah as the Creator, were trapped by the very logic they refused to follow to its conclusion. By mentioning “pairs” right after the earth and the mountains, the surah weaves humanity into the fabric of creation. Humans are not observers standing outside the universe; they are part of the same divinely designed order.

The pairing also subtly prepares for the later description of the rewards of Paradise, where the righteous will be joined with pure spouses. So the verse looks in two directions: back to the proofs in nature and forward to the fulfilment in the Hereafter.

The question format continues: “Have We not created you in pairs?” It is a question that every human being, regardless of belief, can answer with a simple yes. In that admission lies the seed of recognition that the Creator who designed this pairing for our benefit is also the One who has the right to call us to account on a day that is as real as the earth we walk upon.

=9=

The signs now shift to the rhythms of daily human experience: “and made your sleep for rest.” The Arabic word subāt denotes a cutting off or a state of deep repose where all activity ceases. Sleep is a universal human phenomenon that is so ordinary it is often forgotten as a sign, yet the Qur’an elevates it to a miracle. Every night, the human being enters a state that closely resembles death: consciousness is suspended, the body lies still, and the soul is taken in a manner described elsewhere in the Qur’an as a temporary taking by Allah. Then, by His mercy, the person wakes up to a new day, as if given a new life. This daily cycle of “death” and “resurrection” is a powerful, tangible proof that Allah can bring the dead back to life on the grand scale of the Hereafter. The verse subtly reminds the deniers that they experience a miniature version of resurrection every single morning, yet they deny the larger resurrection to come. Beyond its evidential value, sleep is described here as a gift and a mercy. The surah has already called the earth a bed; now it names sleep as the rest that that bed was made for. The two are linked in a chain of divine care. The One who spread the earth for you is the One who gave you the capacity to close your eyes and find renewal. Sleep is not a defect in human design; it is an intentional feature that heals the body, consolidates memory and restores the spirit. The verse invites reflection on what life would be like without the blessing of sleep. The continuous exhaustion would make existence unbearable. By framing it as a question—“and made your sleep for rest?”—the surah prompts the listener to feel gratitude and to recognise the intentional kindness behind this provision. The deep rest of sleep also contrasts starkly with the torment of Hell described later in the surah, where there will be no coolness, no drink and no respite.

Thus the verse functions on multiple levels: it is a sign, a mercy, a proof for the resurrection and a subtle warning that the comfort people now enjoy can be lost if they deny the Giver.

=10=

Continuing the pattern of everyday blessings, the surah asks: “and made the night as a cover.” The word libās normally means garment or clothing. So the night is a garment that wraps the world in darkness. A garment covers a person’s body, concealing it, protecting it and giving it a sense of privacy and security. Night performs these very functions for the earth and its inhabitants. It conceals people and their movements from the eyes of potential enemies; it provides the darkness necessary for restful sleep, as visual stimuli are dimmed; and it covers the landscape with a serene stillness that allows the tired mind and body to recuperate. The metaphor of clothing is rich with connotations. Just as a person instinctively seeks clothing for comfort and protection, so humanity instinctively welcomes the night as a covering that brings relief after the glare and heat of the day.

The verse also implies a moral and spiritual dimension: night is a cover for those who wish to pray in private, for lovers who seek lawful intimacy, and even for wrongdoers who try to hide their deeds. In all these cases, the night serves as a divinely appointed screen.

Yet the very fact that some misuse this cover does not negate the blessing itself; it only shows that human choice operates within the framework of divine provision. The surah has already mentioned the earth as a bed and sleep as rest; the night as a cover completes the picture of a perfect sleeping environment. The earth is the resting place, sleep is the state of repose, and the night is the blanket that envelops the sleeper. All three work together seamlessly.

The question “Have We not made the night as a cover?” expects the listener to acknowledge this obvious truth. It also prepares the ground for the immediate counterpart in the next verse—the day made for livelihood. The alternation of night and day is one of the most frequently cited signs in the Qur’an, and it serves here as a single coherent proof. The One who can spread the covering of night and then lift it to bring forth the day is the One who can bring the universe to an end and then recreate it for the Final Decision.

=11=

The counterpart to the covering of night is the day that has been “made for livelihood.” The Arabic ma‘āsh comes from a root meaning to live and to earn a living. The day is the time of activity, movement and work. When dawn breaks and light returns, people rise from their beds and go out to seek provision. Farmers tend their fields, traders open their markets, and all kinds of crafts and industries come to life. The bright light of day is not merely a physical phenomenon; it is a condition that enables human beings to see, to interact and to carry out the tasks that sustain their lives. The verse thus reminds the listener that the day is not an accident of cosmic mechanics but a deliberate gift from the Creator, tailored to human need.

The mention of “livelihood” is significant. It acknowledges that earning a living is a central part of human existence. Islam does not regard material work as something low or separate from spirituality; rather, it is part of the divine plan that humans should disperse through the earth and seek Allah’s bounty.

However, the verse also implies a test: the day is given for livelihood, but that livelihood is to be sought in ways that are lawful and mindful of the One who provided the day. The deniers of Mecca were busy in their trade and commerce, benefiting from the daylight, yet they attributed their success to their own efforts or to their idols, forgetting the One who made the day possible.

The pairing of night and day in these two verses forms a powerful argument. The One who alternates the night and day with perfect consistency, never allowing one to encroach permanently on the other, is clearly in control of time and the universe. If He can bring the day back after the death-like stillness of the night, He can just as easily bring life back after the stillness of the grave.

The question “and made the day for livelihood?” forces the listener to connect the daily rhythm he takes for granted with the greater rhythm of life, death and resurrection. It is a call to see the sign in the ordinary and to realise that the same Lord who gives the day for work has also appointed a Day for ultimate judgement and recompense.

=12=

After grounding the listener in the immediate blessings of earth, sleep, night and day, the surah lifts the gaze upwards: “and built above you seven mighty heavens.” The verb “built” (banaynā) evokes a strong, solid structure. The heavens are not mere empty space; they are a formidable construction, described as “mighty” (shidād), indicating their immense strength, vastness and perfection. The number seven is understood by the vast majority of scholars as literal, with each heaven being a distinct realm, as mentioned in the hadith of the Prophet’s night journey, where he passed through the seven heavens and met various prophets. The heavens are above us, yet they remain securely in place without visible pillars, as another verse states, “He raised the heavens without pillars that you see” (13:2).

This is a sign of Allah’s absolute power that surpasses human engineering. The Quraysh, who looked at the sky every night, could appreciate the grandeur of the celestial dome. By calling the heavens “mighty,” the surah emphasises that they are not fragile; they are part of an unshakeable cosmic order. This sets up a dramatic contrast with the later verses that describe the sky being split open and becoming gates on the Day of Judgement. The very structure that seems so permanent will one day be torn apart. The rhetorical question “Have We not built?” again demands an affirmative acknowledgment. The seven heavens are a standing proof that the Creator is capable of anything. If He could build such a towering, complex edifice without any support, what difficulty would He have in raising the dead?

The mention of seven heavens also links to the next verse, where within these heavens a shining lamp is placed. Thus the structure is not empty; it is populated with signs. For the human being standing on the smoothed earth, secured by the pegs of mountains, enveloped in the alternating garment of night and the brightness of day, and looking up at the seven mighty heavens, the entire universe becomes a temple of signs pointing to the One who created it all. The surah is methodically dismantling any excuse for denying the resurrection by placing the evidence in every direction.

=13=

Within those mighty heavens, the Creator “placed a shining lamp.” The “shining lamp” is the sun, described by the Arabic sirājan wahhājā, a lamp that is intensely glowing and radiant. The metaphor of a lamp is deeply instructive. A lamp is placed in a room to provide light, warmth and guidance. The sun does exactly that for the inhabitants of the earth. It illuminates the day, making livelihood possible, as mentioned earlier. It provides heat that drives the weather, nourishes plant life and sustains the entire ecosystem. Without this “lamp,” the earth would be a frozen, dark rock. The description wahhāj implies not just light but blazing brightness, indicating the sun’s tremendous energy. By calling it a lamp, the verse implicitly compares the sun to a smaller, familiar object that humans use in their homes, thereby making the vast and distant sun feel personal and directly beneficial.

The placement of the sun “in them”—meaning within the framework of the heavens—shows that it is part of the ordered system, not an independent deity as some pagans believed. The Quraysh were surrounded by cultures that worshipped the sun and the moon, and the Qur’an consistently reframes celestial bodies as signs and servants of Allah, not objects of worship. This verse is a quiet but firm correction of any such tendency. The lamp is a servant, set in its place by the Builder of the heavens. It has no will of its own; it runs its course by the command of its Lord.

The question “Have We not placed?” brings the listener back to the fundamental idea that all these blessings are given, not earned. The One who hung this brilliant lamp in the sky for human benefit will one day fold it up and bring the universe to an end. The sun that rises every morning with unfailing regularity is itself a daily reminder of the greater rising of the Day of Resurrection. Just as the lamp of the sun brings the dead earth to life with its light and warmth, so will the call of the Trumpet bring the dead out of their graves into the new dawn of eternity.

=14=

From the heights of the shining heavens, the surah now brings the gaze back to the interaction between sky and earth: “and sent down from rainclouds pouring water.” The word mu‘ṣirāt can refer to clouds that are heavy with rain, ready to burst, or to winds that press and squeeze the clouds until they release their load. In either understanding, the imagery is one of abundance and readiness. The water is described as thajjāj, meaning pouring in torrents, copious and flowing forcefully.

This is not a gentle drizzle but a generous, life-giving downpour that penetrates the earth, fills the rivers and replenishes the wells. The verse highlights the mechanism of rain as a deliberate act of sending down. Rain does not fall randomly; it is sent by the One who knows the needs of His creation. The pagan Arabs understood rain as a matter of life and death in their arid land. When the rains failed, famine followed.

Yet many of them attributed rainfall to natural forces or to the movements of stars, failing to acknowledge it as a direct blessing from Allah. The question “Have We not sent down?” reclaims the phenomenon as a divine gift. The descending water also becomes a central proof for the resurrection. The Qur’an repeatedly draws the analogy between rain reviving the dead earth and Allah reviving the dead on the Day of Judgement. The dry, barren land, when touched by the pouring water, trembles, swells and puts forth every kind of beautiful growth. Human beings see this miracle annually or seasonally, yet they doubt that their own dry bones can be clothed with life again. The surah is building a cumulative case: the earth as bed, the mountains as pegs, the alternation of night and day, and now the descent of life-giving water—all point to a Creator who initiates life, sustains it and can restore it. The pouring water is the vital link between the static structures of the universe and the vibrant life that will be described in the next verses, where grain, plants and orchards burst forth.

=15=

The purpose of the pouring rain is now made explicit: “producing by it grain and various plants.” The water that descends does not simply disappear into the ground; it is the direct cause of the vegetation that sustains human and animal life. The verse singles out “grain” (ḥabb) first. Grain refers to the edible seeds such as wheat, barley, corn, rice and all the staple crops that form the foundation of human food. Grain is the staff of life, and it is a marvel of design that a tiny, dry seed, when given water, germinates and multiplies into a plant bearing hundreds of new seeds. This cycle of death and rebirth is a miniature resurrection happening in every field and garden. After grain, the verse mentions “various plants” (nabāt), encompassing all kinds of herbs, grasses, vegetables and foliage that clothe the earth in green and provide food and medicine. The variety is a sign of Allah’s endless creativity. From the same water, the earth produces grapes and dates, olives and pomegranates, sweet and bitter, colourful and fragrant—a dazzling array that delights the senses and nourishes the body. The verse is a powerful argument for the resurrection. The One who causes a lifeless seed to split open and grow into a living plant, using nothing more than water and soil, is more than able to cause a human body to be resurrected from its scattered remains. The analogy is precise: the earth dies in drought and lives again with rain; the human body dies and will live again by the command of Allah. The Meccans, who depended on grain and plants for their survival, were being asked to look at their own dinner plates and see the signature of the Creator who can bring forth the dead.

The verse also teaches gratitude. Grain and plants are not automatic products of nature; they are gifts that flow from Allah’s mercy. Recognising this truth is the first step towards recognising the Giver and, subsequently, towards preparing for the day when all will be questioned about how they used these gifts.

=16=

The sequence of botanical signs reaches a beautiful climax with “and dense orchards?” The word jannāt means gardens, and alfāf means interwoven, thick and tangled in a lush way.

The image is of an orchard where the branches of fruit trees intertwine, creating a canopy of deep shade, abundant fruit and intoxicating fragrance. The density suggests a garden that is overflowing with life and fertility, far beyond a sparse, orderly row of trees. It is a place of delight, beauty and perfect provision. The choice of the word jannāt is deeply significant, because the same word is used throughout the Qur’an for the gardens of Paradise. The orchards of this world are thus a foretaste, a fleeting glimpse of the eternal gardens promised to the righteous. The surah later describes the reward of the believers in terms of gardens and vineyards, so this early mention of dense orchards creates a thematic thread that ties the signs in creation to the blessings of the Hereafter.

The question mark at the end reminds us that the entire passage from verse 6 to verse 16 has been one sustained rhetorical question. All these signs have been strung together under the overarching “Have We not…?” that demands a single answer: Yes, indeed, the Lord has done all this. The cumulative weight of these signs is now almost overwhelming. The listener has been led from the vast expanse of the smoothed earth, through the anchoring mountains, the intimacy of human pairs, the rest of sleep, the cover of night, the brightness of the day for livelihood, the mighty seven heavens, the blazing lamp of the sun, the pouring rain, the sprouting grain and plants, and finally into the cool shade of the intertwined orchard. Each sign built on the one before it. The conclusion is inescapable: the Creator who designed this magnificent, interconnected system is perfectly capable of bringing about the Day of Final Decision. The dense orchards also serve as a bridge. They are the last of the blessings mentioned, and they carry a hint of Paradise that prepares the heart for the stark contrast with the horrors of Judgement Day and Hell that the surah is about to depict. The scene is set for the transition from signs to the appointed time.

=17=

After the expansive list of signs, the surah returns with force to the main theme: “Indeed, the Day of Final Decision is an appointed time.” The word inna (“indeed”) underscores the absolute certainty of the statement. What follows is not a possibility or a myth; it is a fixed reality. The “Day of Final Decision” translates yawm al-faṣl. Faṣl means separation, distinguishing, and decisive judgement. On that Day, all the disputes of humanity will be settled. Truth will be separated from falsehood, the believer from the disbeliever, the obedient from the rebellious. The arguing that the Meccans engaged in regarding the momentous news will be brought to a definitive end. There will be no more room for disagreement.

The second part of the verse says that this Day is “an appointed time.” The word mīqāt refers to a time that has been precisely set, like the appointed times of prayer or the fixed seasons of pilgrimage. It is not a vague future possibility; it is a specific moment in Allah’s knowledge, determined before the creation of the heavens and the earth.

The use of mīqāt is comforting for the believers and deeply frightening for the deniers. For believers, it means that the injustice they suffer has a deadline; relief will come at the exact time Allah has decreed. For deniers, it means that the respite they enjoy is measured, and when the appointed time arrives, neither delay nor escape will be possible. The verse functions as a transitional hinge. The signs that were just presented prove that the One who created all this order in the universe is the One who has also appointed a fixed time for its conclusion and for the judgement of its inhabitants. The logic is watertight: a creation of such magnificent design cannot be left without a purpose and without a final accounting.

The verse also picks up the warning from verses 4 and 5. There, they were told they would come to know; now the exact name and nature of that coming event are disclosed. The “Day of Final Decision” gives a name to the dread that the earlier verses evoked. It is a day that will sort out every confusion and resolve every dispute, including the one with which the surah began.

=18=

The appointed time is now described in vivid terms: “the Day the Trumpet will be blown, and you will all come forth in crowds.” The Trumpet, or ṣūr, is a mighty horn that the angel Isrāfīl will blow by Allah’s command. According to authentic hadith, there will be two main blasts: the first will stun all creation and cause death, and the second will bring about the resurrection. This verse focuses on the second blast, the one that summons the dead from their graves.

The phrase “you will all come forth” addresses humanity directly and collectively. None will be left behind.

The word “crowds” translates afwāj, meaning groups after groups, waves upon waves of people surging forward. The imagery is that of a vast, unstoppable human tide rising from the earth and moving towards the plain of gathering. Every human being who ever lived will be present, from Adam to the last child born before the Hour. The verse brings the abstract idea of the Day of Decision into sharp sensory focus. The blowing of the Trumpet is a sound that will penetrate every corner of the universe, and it is a sound that no one can ignore. The coming forth in crowds shows that the resurrection is not a private, individual event; it is a public, universal, overwhelming spectacle. The disbelievers who used to ask mockingly, “When will this promise come to pass?” will see it with their own eyes. The crowds will include them as well, but they will be coming forth in terror, not in honour.

The verse also connects back to the earlier mention of pairs and the creation of human beings. The One who created people in pairs and scattered them across the earth is also the One who can gather them all in one place in a single moment. The apparent dispersion of humanity in life and in death is no obstacle to divine power. The scene is now set for the cosmic upheaval that will accompany this resurrection, which the next two verses will portray in startling language. The surah is moving quickly from the calm contemplation of creation’s signs to the thunderous reality of the end of the world.

=19=

As the dead rise from their graves, the very fabric of the universe begins to dissolve: “The sky will be split open, becoming many gates.” The sky, which was earlier described as one of the seven mighty heavens built securely above, will now be rent asunder. The passive verb indicates that this opening is not a gentle unfastening but a violent tearing apart. The solid, protective canopy that humanity has always known will break into fragments.

The image of the sky becoming “many gates” is both majestic and terrifying. Gates imply openings through which beings can pass. Many commentators explain that these gates will be the paths through which the angels descend, as mentioned later in verse 38 of the same surah, where the Spirit and the angels stand in ranks. The sky that once seemed like an impenetrable ceiling will become a series of portals, revealing the higher realms and making the presence of the angels unmistakably visible to all. The psychological impact on human beings who spent their lives oblivious to the unseen will be devastating. They will see what they denied and will have no place to hide.

The verse also recalls other Qur’anic descriptions of the Day of Judgement, such as “When the sky is split asunder” (84:1) and “And the heaven will be opened, becoming gates” (78:19). The consistent theme is that the destruction of the physical universe is not an act of chaos but a transformation that serves the purpose of judgement. The sky is split to allow the descent of the divine command and the arrival of the angels who will carry out their Lord’s decree. For the deniers, the splitting of the sky is the first sign that their world, which they thought was eternal and self-sufficient, is unravelling before their eyes. The very sky they looked at while mocking the news of the Hereafter will become a scene of overwhelming power.

The question from the beginning of the surah—what are they asking about?—finds its visual answer: they were asking about this Day when the sky itself will shatter into gates.

=20=

The transformation of the earth’s great anchors completes the scene of cosmic collapse: “and the mountains will be blown away, becoming like a mirage.” Earlier in the surah, the mountains were described as pegs that stabilise the earth. Now their apparent solidity is revealed to be entirely dependent on Allah’s command. The verb “blown away” (suyyirat) means they will be set in motion, uprooted and scattered. Other verses add that they will be crushed, ground to powder and then blown by the wind until no trace of them remains. After this complete disintegration, they will become “like a mirage.” A mirage, sarāb, is the optical illusion in the desert where distant sand appears as shimmering water. When a thirsty traveller approaches, he finds nothing. The mountains, which people trusted as the most permanent features of the landscape, will dissolve into nothingness in exactly that way. One moment they will be there, solid and immense; the next, they will be dust particles vanishing into the air, leaving behind a perfectly level plain.

The image is devastating for those who placed their sense of security in the material world. The disbelievers of Mecca were surrounded by mountains that had stood for millennia; they could not imagine them ever disappearing. By stating that these mountains will become like a mirage, the verse shatters the illusion of permanence that attaches people to this world. It also brings the argument for the resurrection full circle. The One who anchored the mountains as pegs can pull those pegs out. The earth that was smoothed like a bed will be turned into a completely flat arena for judgement. There is a stark contrast with the dense orchards mentioned earlier. The orchards were a picture of lush, life-filled stability; the mirage is a picture of barren emptiness. The verse marks the end of the world as the Meccans knew it and prepares the way for the next section, which will describe the dreadful destination that awaits the transgressors. The surah’s architecture is precise: from the question about the news, to the warning, to the signs of creation, and now to the overwhelming reality of the Day that will make everything clear.
