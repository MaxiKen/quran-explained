=41=

The surah now draws the Prophet’s attention, and through him all who will hear the Quran, to a future event of overwhelming certainty. The command *And listen!* is emphatic, urging a focused, receptive state of heart and mind.

This is not casual hearing but the kind of alertness that grips the soul when something of supreme importance is about to be declared. The listener is called to listen because what follows is the definitive answer to the doubts and mockeries of the deniers.

The verse then reveals *the Day the caller will call out from a near place*. The caller here, according to the majority of commentators, is the angel Israfil, who is entrusted with blowing the Trumpet. Others say the caller is an angel appointed to summon all creatures to the gathering place. The call itself is a tremendous summons that no one can ignore. The description *from a near place* has been understood in several complementary ways. Some explain that the angel will stand on a raised place, such as the rock of Jerusalem, and his voice will reach every being across the entire earth, making the call near to all. Others understand the nearness as the fact that the call will penetrate every ear as if it were coming from immediately beside each person. No one will have to strain to hear it; the summons will be unavoidably present. This nearness also symbolises the suddenness with which the affairs of the hereafter overtake humanity. Right now, the Day of Judgment might feel distant, a far-off concept, but when the caller cries out, the entire reality of the hereafter will feel closer than anything else. The verse transforms the abstract idea of resurrection into an audible event, a loud, clear call that shatters the silence of the graves and the illusion that this world would last forever. For the Prophet, peace be upon him, who was enduring the insults and denial of the Quraysh, this command to listen was a reassurance that the day of final settlement is real and approaching, and the voices of the mockers will soon be silenced by a far greater voice calling them to account.

=42=

This verse continues the description of the momentous day that the caller announces. *The Day all will hear the mighty Blast* refers to the tremendous sound of the Trumpet, the same instrument mentioned earlier in the surah. The word *mighty* conveys the overwhelming, terrifying nature of the sound. It will not be a distant rumble or a subtle signal; it will be a deafening, all-encompassing blast that no living or dead being can fail to perceive. The comprehensive nature of the hearing is emphasised by *all will hear*. This includes the earliest generations and the last, the powerful and the weak, the believer and the denier. There will be no exceptions, no one whose ears are shut, and no corner of the earth where the sound does not reach.

The phrase *in all truth* affirms that this blast carries the full weight of divine promise and reality. It is not a test or a drill; it is the actual, definitive summons that Allah promised repeatedly throughout the scriptures. Everything that was foretold about the day of resurrection is now happening, and there is no longer any room for doubt or revision.

The verse then names this event *the Day of emergence from the graves*. This ties directly back to the earlier argument of the surah where Allah compared resurrection to the revival of a lifeless land by rain. The dead earth bursts forth with vegetation, and similarly, the dead will burst forth from their graves at the sound of the blast. The emergence is a physical, bodily rising. Every human being, reconstructed from scattered particles by the perfect knowledge and power of Allah, will exit the earth and stand upright, hastening towards the place of gathering. The verse thus completes the picture that began with the individual moment of death and the blowing of the Trumpet. Now the universal resurrection is underway, and the next stage of judgment and eternal recompense follows inevitably.

For those who mocked the idea of rising from dust, this verse thunders the response: you will hear it, you will rise, and you will face what you denied.

=43=

Having described the Day of emergence, the surah now anchors the entire doctrine of resurrection in the continuous and observable reality of Allah’s power over life. *It is certainly We Who give life and cause death.* The statement is emphatic, removing any ambiguity. Every human being witnesses this truth daily. A child is born from nothing, a living soul breathed into a previously lifeless body. An old man dies, and the vitality that once filled him vanishes, leaving an empty shell. No one else claims the power to truly give life or to bring about death in its complete sense. Doctors may assist in delivery, and killers may end a life through violent means, but the actual spark of life and the moment of its extinguishing are solely in the hand of the Creator. Since Allah continuously gives life and causes death in this world, bringing the dead back to life for the final judgment is not a new or difficult task for Him. It is the same authority exercised once more.

The verse then adds, *And to Us is the final return.* The journey that began with life and passed through death does not end in the grave. There is a final return, a homecoming to the One who originated everything. This return is not an annihilation but a standing before Allah for accountability.

The word *final* emphasises that after this return, there is no further death, no transmigration, and no escaping the consequences of one’s earthly life. This verse also serves as a profound consolation for the believer who suffers injustice in this world. The oppressor who seems to escape punishment through death has not truly escaped, because death is not an exit from divine authority. It is merely a transition to the final return where perfect justice will be meted out. The Prophet, peace be upon him, is reminded that the deniers who trouble him will not evade their appointment. Their death, which they might see as the end, is merely the beginning of their journey back to the One who gave them life in the first place.

=44=

The surah continues to paint the scene of resurrection with vivid imagery, moving from the blast and the emergence to the physical rupture of the earth itself. *The Day the earth will split open* describes a violent and total transformation of the familiar ground. The solid, stable earth that people walk upon, build upon, and bury their dead within will crack and break apart from above them. This splitting is not a localised earthquake but a global rending of the earth's surface, opening pathways for the dead to rise. *Letting them rush forth* captures the frantic, hurried emergence of humanity from the graves.

The Arabic word conveys speed and agitation. People will pour out of the earth like scattered locusts, as described in other surahs, driven by the irresistible summons and the sheer terror of the moment. There will be no slow, dignified procession. Every soul will hasten towards the gathering place, overwhelmed by the magnitude of what is happening.

The verse then makes a statement that demolishes the core argument of the deniers: *That will be an easy gathering for Us.* The Meccan pagans had called the resurrection an astonishing thing, an impossibility. They thought that reassembling billions of decayed bodies from scattered dust was beyond any power. Allah responds that this gathering is *easy*. What human minds perceive as impossibly complex is for the divine will a simple affair, requiring no effort, causing no fatigue, and presenting no challenge whatsoever. This ease is tied back to the earlier declaration that Allah was not touched with fatigue in the creation of the heavens and the earth. The One who originated the universe with a word of command can reassemble its inhabitants with the same effortless authority. This verse instills both fear in the denier, who realises that nothing can prevent this easy gathering, and confidence in the believer, who knows that his Lord is never overwhelmed by any task, no matter how immense it seems to human estimation.

=45=

The surah concludes with a powerful reassurance to the Prophet and a clear delineation of his mission. *We know best what they say.* This is a balm for the heart of the Messenger who had endured mockery, accusations of sorcery and madness, and the constant rejection of his people. Allah tells him that He is fully aware of every insult, every argument, and every conspiracy whispered behind closed doors. Nothing of what they say escapes His knowledge, and therefore the Prophet does not need to respond to every taunt or become consumed by their words. Divine knowledge encompasses it all, and divine justice will handle it in due time.

The verse then clarifies the nature of the Prophet’s role with the words *you are not there to compel them to believe.* The Arabic term used for *compel* implies overpowering control, the kind of dominance that forces submission against one’s will. The Prophet is explicitly told that this is not his function. Faith, by its very nature, must be a voluntary act of the heart and mind, born of conviction and choice. If Allah had willed to compel belief, He could have done so directly, as the Quran states elsewhere. But He chose to test humanity through free will and to send messengers as warners and bearers of glad tidings, not as tyrants.

This principle becomes a foundational rule of Islamic outreach: no coercion in religion. The Prophet’s only task, and by extension the task of every caller to Islam, is *remind with the Quran only those who fear My warning.* The reminder is the Quran itself, the very book the disbelievers rejected. And the effective audience is those who have within their hearts a fear of Allah’s warning, a recognition that the threat of the hereafter is real and must be taken seriously. These are the hearts described earlier in the surah as mindful and attentive. The one who lacks this fear will not benefit from the reminder, not because the Quran is deficient, but because the receptacle is closed. The surah thus ends where it began, with the glorious Quran as the central instrument of guidance, and with a clear separation between those who will heed and those who will persist in their heedlessness until the caller cries out from a near place.
