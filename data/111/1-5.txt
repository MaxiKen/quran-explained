This opening verse is unlike any other in the Quran. It is a direct, unequivocal curse pronounced by Allah upon a specific individual named in the revelation. “May the hands of Abu Lahab perish, and he himself perish!

” The Arabic “tabbat yadâ abî Lahabin wa tabb” uses the verb “tabba,” meaning to be destroyed, to lose everything, to be cut off from all good, and to suffer utter ruin. The invocation begins with his hands because hands are the instruments of action and power. By cursing his hands to perish, the verse declares that all his evil deeds, all his hostile gestures, and all his strivings against the truth have ended in catastrophic failure and loss.

The second part, “wa tabb,” confirms that not only his actions but his entire being is engulfed in ruin. The man referred to is Abu Lahab, whose actual name was ‘Abd al-‘Uzzâ ibn ‘Abd al-Muṭṭalib, an uncle of the Prophet ﷺ. He earned the nickname “Abu Lahab,” meaning “Father of Flames,” either because of his ruddy complexion that glowed like fire or due to the prophecy of his fate in this surah. The historical context is striking. When the verse “And warn your closest kindred” was revealed, the Prophet ﷺ ascended Mount Ṣafâ and called the Quraysh clans. Abu Lahab was among the first to respond, and when the Prophet ﷺ warned them of a severe punishment if they did not believe, Abu Lahab shouted, “May you perish! Is this why you summoned us?” and he picked up stones to throw at the Prophet. In answer, Allah revealed this surah, turning Abu Lahab’s own curse back upon him, decreeing that it would be he who perishes, body and soul, while the Prophet ﷺ would be triumphant. The surah is an open miracle, for it prophesied that Abu Lahab would die a disbeliever. Although he lived several years after this revelation, witnessing the growing strength of Islam, he never even outwardly professed faith, proving the divine origin of the Quran.

Thus, the verse is a proclamation that the enmity of the most powerful and wealthy figures cannot harm the mission of truth; rather, the harm returns upon them. It also serves as a terrifying reminder that personal relationship to the Prophet—even being an uncle—avails nothing without faith. Abu Lahab’s hands perished in their efforts, and he himself perished in Hell, as the surah goes on to confirm.

=2=

The surah now explains the complete failure of all that Abu Lahab relied upon: “Neither his wealth nor worldly gains will benefit him.” The Arabic “mâ aghnâ ‘anhu mâluhu wa mâ kasab” uses the verb “aghnâ,” meaning to avail, to suffice, to give independence.

The question is rhetorical: what will his wealth do for him in the face of divine punishment? Absolutely nothing. Abu Lahab was one of the wealthiest men in the Quraysh, a prominent merchant whose riches were legendary. He relied on his fortune as a source of power, influence, and security, and he used it to oppose Islam. When the Prophet ﷺ began his public call, Abu Lahab would spend lavishly to discredit him and to support the pagan propaganda.

Yet this verse announces the utter bankruptcy of that wealth. It cannot delay his death, it cannot shield him from the angel of death, and it cannot buy him a moment of relief in the grave or on the Day of Judgment.

The phrase “wa mâ kasab” extends beyond monetary wealth. “Kasab” refers to everything a person earns: property, children, followers, reputation, and worldly achievements. The inclusion of children is particularly poignant because Abu Lahab, like others, took pride in his sons as his legacy and support. But even his children, some of whom initially opposed Islam, could not benefit him. One of his sons, ‘Utbah, eventually accepted Islam, but that did not retroactively save Abu Lahab. The verse is a precise deconstruction of the materialist worldview. The Quraysh measured success in terms of wealth and progeny, the very things that the previous surahs condemned as distractions. Abu Lahab was the living embodiment of the takâthur mentality, competing for more and hoarding it. Now the Quran declares his lifelong accumulation worthless.

This teaching echoes a profound hadith: the Prophet ﷺ said, “The son of Adam says, ‘My wealth, my wealth!’ But what is your wealth except that which you eat and consume, or wear and wear out, or give in charity and thus send ahead? ” Everything else belongs to the heirs. For Abu Lahab, nothing was sent ahead but hostility and kufr. The verse is a chilling reminder that on the Day when we stand before Allah, we will be utterly alone, stripped of everything we thought was ours. Abu Lahab’s wealth, which made him arrogant enough to curse the Prophet, became not a benefit but a witness against him, increasing his punishment because he used Allah’s blessings in rebellion against Allah. His story is a warning that the richest person in the world is truly a pauper if his wealth is not accompanied by faith and righteous deeds.

=3=

After describing his loss in this world, the surah turns to his fate in the Hereafter: “He will burn in a flaming Fire.” The Arabic “sa-yaṣlâ nâran dhâta lahab” uses the emphatic prefix “sa-” to indicate a certain future event. The verb “yaṣlâ” means to enter into a fire, to be roasted by it, to suffer its heat directly on the body. The Fire is described as “dhâta lahab,” meaning possessing fierce, leaping flames.

The word “lahab” is the very root of his name “Abu Lahab,” creating a devastating rhetorical connection. The man nicknamed “Father of Flames,” whether for his reddish face or his fiery temper, will now be embraced by flames that are real, eternal, and all-consuming. He is not a father of flames in any honorable sense; he is fuel for the Fire.

This is a classic example of divine justice manifesting as a fitting recompense: his name becomes his reality. The word “lahab” suggests flames that continuously flicker and leap upward, giving the image of a blaze that is alive and engulfing, not a quiet ember. The torment is tailored to the sin. Abu Lahab was inflamed with anger and pride against the truth, and now he will be surrounded by literal flames that burn from all sides. The verse is brief but terrifying, leaving no doubt about his final destination. In Makkah, the Quraysh would hear this surah recited and know that it directly named their leader and promised him Hell. This was an open challenge and a proof. If Abu Lahab could have disproved the Quran, he would have merely pretended to accept Islam, even hypocritically, to show that the prophecy was false. His failure to do so, despite his intense hatred and the passage of several years, is a sign of the Quran’s truth.

Moreover, the verse implicitly refutes the belief that noble lineage or clan protection can save a person from Allah’s judgment. Abu Lahab was from the Banu Hashim, the Prophet’s own clan, but this provided no shield. The fire he will enter is the same scorching Fire described in the earlier surahs, the Crusher, the Abyss. He will burn in it, experiencing the full force of Allah’s wrath. For the believers suffering persecution at his hands, this verse was a source of profound comfort, assuring them that the tormentor would face a torment infinitely worse, and that justice was inevitable.

=4=

The punishment is not limited to Abu Lahab alone; his wife shares in his fate because she shared in his crimes. “And so will his wife, the carrier of thorny kindling.

” The Arabic “wa-mra’atuhu ḥammâlata al-ḥaṭab” identifies his wife as his partner in punishment. Her name was Arwâ bint Ḥarb, the sister of Abu Sufyân, and she was commonly known as Umm Jamîl. She was a woman of high status in the Quraysh, known for her fierce enmity towards the Prophet ﷺ. While her husband cursed the Prophet publicly, Umm Jamîl had her own method of causing harm. Historical reports state that she would gather bundles of thorny wood, thistles, and prickly branches and secretly scatter them along the path where the Prophet ﷺ would walk at night, hoping to injure his bare feet.

The phrase “ḥammâlata al-ḥaṭab” literally means “the carrier of firewood” or “kindling. ” This is a direct reference to her vile actions, but it also carries a metaphorical meaning. Carrying firewood was an idiom in Arabic for one who spreads gossip, slander, and discord, for just as firewood fuels a fire, malicious words fuel enmity and strife between people. Umm Jamîl was notorious for carrying tales, backbiting, and stirring up hatred against the Prophet and the believers. So her punishment is a direct reflection of her crime. She carried physical thorns to harm the Prophet’s body, and she carried verbal thorns to harm his reputation and community. In Hell, she will literally carry firewood, but now it will be the fuel of her own torment.

The image is one of ironic justice: the woman who once carried bundles on her back out of spite will now carry the very material that kindles the flames engulfing her. The verse also emphasizes that women are not exempt from accountability; her high status as a chief’s wife does not save her. She was complicit in evil, and she faces the same fate. Some narrations recount that when she heard this surah being recited, she flew into a rage, grabbed a stone, and marched to the Ka‘bah to confront the Prophet, but Allah prevented her from seeing him even though he was present, and she had to retreat in humiliation. This further confirmed the divine protection and the truth of the revelation. The verse stands as a warning that those who aid and abet oppressors share in their guilt and their destiny.

=5=

The final verse of this surah adds a specific, horrifying detail to Umm Jamîl’s punishment: “around her neck will be a rope of palm-fibre.” The Arabic “fî jîdihâ ḥablun min masad” describes a rope twisted from “masad,” the tough, coarse fibers of the palm tree. In this life, Umm Jamîl was a wealthy woman who took pride in her jewelry. Reports say she wore a magnificent, expensive necklace of pearls and gold, and she once swore by her idols that she would sell this necklace and use the money to finance her enmity against the Prophet. In Hell, her necklace will be replaced by a rope of palm-fibre, a symbol of humiliation, servitude, and eternal punishment. The masad rope is also linked to her method of carrying thorns; such ropes were used to tie bundles of firewood. So the very tool she used in her worldly crime becomes the instrument of her eternal torment. The rope around the neck suggests strangulation, pulling, and dragging, a punishment both painful and degrading. It also indicates that she will be bound and dragged into the Fire, unable to escape. The ironic reversal is unmistakable: the proud noblewoman who adorned herself with precious gems is now adorned with a rope of harsh fibers. The imagery is vivid and shocking, and it serves to engrave the lesson in the mind.

The mention of “palm-fibre” also connects to the local environment of Makkah, where palm trees were common and the fibers were used for the most menial tasks. The surah, which began with a curse on Abu Lahab’s hands, ends with his wife bound by a rope made from the very fibers she might have used to tie her bundles of thorns. The chapter comes full circle. The couple who plotted and labored together in enmity against Allah’s Messenger are united in punishment, their schemes backfiring entirely. This final image reinforces the theme of divine justice: no one who makes enmity with the truth will escape, no matter their wealth, lineage, or worldly cleverness. For the believers, it provides closure and confidence, knowing that the oppressors will face a fitting and humiliating end, while for those who hear the Quran with an open heart, it serves as a powerful deterrent, urging them to abandon pride and enmity and to seek salvation before it is too late.
