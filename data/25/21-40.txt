=21=

This verse describes the arrogant demand of those who denied the resurrection: "Those who do not expect to meet Us say, 'If only the angels were sent down to us, or we could see our Lord!' They have certainly been carried away by their arrogance and have entirely exceeded all limits." The disbelievers, who did not believe in the meeting with Allah, demanded visible proof — that the angels be sent down to them directly, or that they see Allah with their own eyes — as a condition for belief. Their demand was not a sincere request for guidance but an expression of arrogance, for they had already been given ample signs and clear revelation.

The verse diagnoses their condition: "They have certainly been carried away by their arrogance." Their demand was rooted in pride, not in a genuine desire for truth. They wanted to dictate the terms of belief, demanding that Allah prove Himself on their terms, rather than humbly accepting the guidance He had already sent. This arrogance then led them to "entirely exceeded all limits," transgressing every boundary of decency and reason in their rejection. Their demand was not for guidance but for dominance, and it revealed the true state of their hearts.

The verse teaches the believer about the nature of true faith. Faith is not the result of seeing with the eyes but of submission with the heart. Those who demand visible proof before believing are often not seeking truth but avoiding submission, for if they truly sought the truth, the signs already given would have sufficed. The believer is reminded to approach Allah with humility, accepting His guidance on His terms, and to beware of the arrogance that demands proof while refusing to submit. The verse also reassures the believer that the day will come when every demand will be answered — but on that Day, belief will come too late.

=22=

This verse describes the outcome of the disbelievers' demand to see the angels: "˹But˺ on the Day they will see the angels, there will be no good news for the wicked, who will cry, 'Keep away! Away ˹from us˺!'" The disbelievers had demanded to see the angels as a condition of belief, but the verse warns them that the day they see the angels will not be a day of glad tidings but a day of terror. The angels will not come as a welcome sign but as harbingers of punishment, and the wicked will find no joy in their arrival.

The cry of the wicked — "Keep away! Away ˹from us˺!" — is a dramatic reversal of their earlier demand. They had asked to see the angels, but when the angels actually appear, the disbelievers will flee from them in terror, crying out for them to stay away. The word ḥijrā maḥjūrā conveys the desperate plea of those who, in this world, sought comfort in sin and now find that comfort turned to terror. The very sight they demanded has become the source of their dread.

The verse teaches a profound lesson about the Hereafter: the things people demand in arrogance often become the instruments of their own terror when reality arrives. The disbelievers' demand to see the angels was not sincere, and its fulfilment brings them no benefit, only fear. The believer is reminded to seek Allah and His guidance through faith, not through demands, and to prepare for the meeting with the angels in a state that will make their arrival a source of joy rather than terror. The verse contrasts the fate of the wicked with that of the righteous, for whom the angels will come as bearers of glad tidings.

=23=

This verse describes the fate of the disbelievers' good deeds on the Day of Judgment: "Then We will turn to whatever ˹good˺ deeds they did, reducing them to scattered dust." The verse addresses the deeds of those who disbelieved — the acts of charity, kindness, and generosity they may have performed in this world. On the Day of Judgment, Allah will turn to these deeds and nullify them, reducing them to "scattered dust" (habā'an manthūran) — dust particles scattered by the wind, without weight, without value, and without trace.

The image of scattered dust is powerful. Just as dust blown by the wind disappears without leaving any trace, so too will the good deeds of the disbelievers vanish on the Day of Judgment. The reason is that their deeds were not performed for the sake of Allah, nor built upon the foundation of faith. A deed is like a building: if its foundation is sound, it endures; if its foundation is corrupted, the whole building collapses. The disbelievers' deeds, however outwardly good, lacked the foundation of faith and sincerity, and so they could not withstand the test of the Hereafter.

The verse teaches the believer the crucial importance of faith and sincerity in every deed. A good deed is not measured by its outward form alone but by the intention and belief that underlie it. The believer is reminded to ensure that every act is done for the sake of Allah alone, for it is this sincerity that gives a deed its weight and permanence. The verse also serves as a sobering reminder that the Hereafter will reveal the true value of every action, and that only the deeds built upon faith and done for Allah will survive the Day of Judgment.

=24=

This verse contrasts the fate of the believers with that of the disbelievers: "˹But˺ on that Day the residents of Paradise will have the best settlement and the finest place to rest." While the disbelievers' deeds are being reduced to scattered dust, the believers will be enjoying the rewards of their faith. On the Day of Judgment, the residents of Paradise will have the best of all settlements and the finest of all resting places, a home of eternal comfort and peace that far surpasses anything the world could offer.

The verse uses two terms to describe the believers' reward: "settlement" (mustaqarr) and "place to rest" (maqīl). The first refers to the permanent dwelling place, while the second refers to the place of midday rest — a siesta, or the cool retreat of the afternoon. Together they convey that Paradise will provide both permanent residence and daily comfort, a home where the believer will find rest at every moment of the eternal day. There will be no toil, no fatigue, and no anxiety, only the continuous refreshment of Allah's grace.

The contrast in this verse is striking: the disbelievers' deeds vanish like dust, while the believers' deeds blossom into an eternal home of comfort. The verse teaches that the true measure of success is not the wealth or status one accumulates in this world, but the state of one's faith and the sincerity of one's deeds, for it is these that determine one's eternal settlement. The believer is encouraged to look forward to this reward with hope, and to strive in this world for the home that awaits them in the Hereafter, knowing that the best settlement and the finest rest are reserved for those who live in faith and obedience.

=25=

This verse turns to another scene of the Day of Judgment: "˹Watch for˺ the Day the heavens will burst with clouds, and the angels will be sent down in successive ranks." The verse describes the dramatic transformation of the heavens on that Day, when the sky will split open and clouds will pour through, and the angels will descend in vast, ordered ranks, filling the space between the heavens and the earth. The image is one of cosmic upheaval, as the familiar order of the universe is overturned and the unseen realm is made manifest.

The descent of the angels in successive ranks is a sign of the solemnity and grandeur of that Day. The angels, who had remained unseen during the life of this world, will now appear openly, descending rank upon rank to carry out the commands of their Lord. Their orderly descent contrasts with the chaos and terror that will grip the disbelievers, and it testifies to the complete control of Allah over His creation. Nothing will be hidden on that Day, and the realm of the unseen will be unveiled before all.

The verse serves as a powerful reminder of the reality and inevitability of the Hereafter. The heavens that seem so stable and permanent will one day be rent asunder, and the unseen world that seems so distant will be brought near. The believer is called to live with this reality in mind, to prepare for the Day when the angels will descend and the veil will be lifted, and to ensure that they are among those for whom that Day is a day of joy rather than terror. The verse also comforts the believer with the knowledge that the angels, who descended to protect and support the believers in this world, will descend on that Day to witness their reward.

=26=

This verse continues the description of the Day of Judgment: "True authority on that Day will belong ˹only˺ to the Most Compassionate. And it will be a hard day for the disbelievers." On that Day, all the false authorities of this world — the kings, the tyrants, the wealthy, and the powerful — will be stripped of their authority, and sovereignty will belong solely to Allah, the Most Compassionate. Every claim to power and every pretense of control will dissolve, and the absolute dominion of the One true Lord will be made manifest before all of creation.

The description of Allah as "the Most Compassionate" (ar-Raḥmān) is significant. It reminds us that the sovereignty that will be established on that Day is not the rule of a tyrant but the reign of the Most Merciful, whose justice is tempered by mercy and whose judgment is perfect. Yet even the mercy of Allah does not diminish the severity of that Day for the disbelievers, for the verse immediately adds: "And it will be a hard day for the disbelievers." The Day will be "hard" (ʿasīr) — difficult, severe, and crushing — for those who rejected their Lord, for they will face the full weight of His justice without the mercy they refused to seek.

The verse teaches the believer about the true nature of authority. In this world, authority appears to belong to many, but in reality, it belongs to Allah alone, and on the Day of Judgment, this reality will be unveiled for all to see. The believer is reminded to place their trust not in the fleeting authorities of this world but in the eternal authority of Allah, and to live in a way that will make the Day of Judgment a day of ease rather than a day of hardship. The verse is a call to seek the mercy of the Most Compassionate now, while the opportunity remains.

=27=

This verse describes the remorse of the wrongdoer on the Day of Judgment: "And ˹beware of˺ the Day the wrongdoer will bite his nails ˹in regret˺ and say, 'Oh! I wish I had followed the Way along with the Messenger.'" The image of biting the nails, or biting the hands in some renderings, conveys the depth of the wrongdoer's regret and anguish. In this world, he had rejected the Messenger and chosen his own path, but on that Day, he will be overwhelmed with remorse, wishing desperately that he had followed the Prophet and taken the straight path that leads to salvation.

The wrongdoer's wish — "I wish I had followed the Way along with the Messenger" — is the eternal cry of those who realize their error too late. In this world, the path of the Messenger seemed too difficult, too restrictive, or too demanding, and so they chose the easier path of their desires. But on the Day of Judgment, when the consequences of that choice are revealed, they will see that the path they rejected was the only path to safety, and their regret will be complete. The wish is futile, for the time of choice will have passed.

The verse is a powerful reminder of the value of the present moment. The believer is called to follow the Messenger now, while the opportunity remains, for the regret of the Hereafter cannot undo the choices of this world. The verse also highlights the mercy of Allah, who has made the path clear and sent a Messenger to guide humanity along it. The wise person is the one who heeds this guidance while there is still time, and who follows the Way along with the Messenger, so that on the Day of Judgment, they may have no cause for regret.

=28=

This verse continues the wrongdoer's lament on the Day of Judgment: "Woe to me! I wish I had never taken so-and-so as a close friend." The wrongdoer, already consumed with regret for rejecting the Messenger, now turns his remorse to the companions who led him astray. He wishes he had never befriended "so-and-so" — the person whose company drew him away from the truth and into sin. In this world, that friend seemed desirable, but on the Day of Judgment, the wrongdoer will see that this friendship was the cause of his ruin.

The verse highlights the profound influence of companionship on a person's destiny. The Prophet, peace be upon him, said that a person is upon the religion of their close friend, and this verse illustrates the consequence of choosing the wrong companions. A friend who encourages sin, mockery of the truth, and neglect of the Hereafter is not a friend but an enemy in disguise, for they lead a person towards ruin while appearing to be a source of pleasure. The wrongdoer's wish — "I wish I had never taken so-and-so as a close friend" — is the recognition, too late, of this truth.

The verse teaches the believer to choose their companions wisely, for companionship shapes the heart and determines the direction of a life. The believer is encouraged to seek the company of the righteous, who remind them of Allah and encourage them towards good, and to avoid the company of those who lead them away from their Lord. The verse also warns that the friendships of this world will not survive into the Hereafter unless they are rooted in faith, and that the companions who lead one astray will become a source of eternal regret.

=29=

This verse completes the wrongdoer's lament by identifying the true nature of his lost friend: "It was he who truly made me stray from the Reminder after it had reached me." The wrongdoer now recognises that his friend was the one who turned him away from the Qur'an — the "Reminder" (dhikr) — after it had reached him. The truth had come to him, but his companion, through persuasion, mockery, and companionship in sin, had drawn him away from it. The friend who seemed loyal in this world was, in reality, the agent of his misguidance.

The verse then adds a universal truth: "And Satan has always betrayed humanity." The word "betrayed" (khadhūl) describes Satan as the one who abandons and betrays his followers at the moment of their greatest need. Satan promises, entices, and leads people astray, but when the consequences arrive, he deserts them, leaving them to face their fate alone. This is the eternal pattern of Satan's relationship with humanity: he is a betrayer who never fulfils his promises and never shares the burden of the ruin he causes.

The verse teaches the believer to be vigilant against the two sources of misguidance: the bad companion and Satan himself. Both operate by making sin appear attractive and the truth appear burdensome, and both abandon their victims when the consequences arrive. The believer is reminded to cling to the Reminder of Allah — the Qur'an — as the surest protection against both, and to recognise that the only friend who will never betray them is Allah, and the only companionship that endures is the companionship of the righteous. The verse is a call to choose one's path and one's friends with the Hereafter in view.

=30=

This verse records the complaint of the Prophet to his Lord: "The Messenger has cried, 'O my Lord! My people have indeed received this Quran with neglect.'" This is one of the most poignant verses in the Qur'an, for it captures the grief of the Prophet as he witnessed his people turning away from the revelation he had brought them. The word "neglect" (mahjūran) conveys the idea of abandonment and desertion — they did not merely reject the Qur'an, but they turned their backs on it, left it unread and unheeded, treating it as something to be abandoned.

The complaint is not born of personal grievance but of prophetic sorrow. The Prophet had devoted his life to conveying the Qur'an, enduring persecution and hardship for its sake, yet his people responded with neglect — some with outright rejection, others with indifference, and still others with a superficial acceptance that did not reach their hearts. The verse reveals the deep pain of a messenger whose mission was met with such a response, and it stands as an eternal testimony to the neglect that the Qur'an has suffered at the hands of many who claim to follow it.

The verse is a powerful admonition to every believer. It invites each reader to examine their own relationship with the Qur'an: Is it recited with reflection, or left neglected on a shelf? Is it a guide for daily life, or a text honoured in form but ignored in substance? The believer is called to avoid the neglect for which the Prophet will complain on the Day of Judgment, and to make the Qur'an a living companion — read, understood, and acted upon — so that they may not be among those who received the Reminder with neglect. The verse transforms the Prophet's complaint into a personal challenge for every soul.

=31=

This verse offers consolation to the Prophet in the face of the opposition he faced: "Similarly, We made enemies for every prophet from among the wicked, but sufficient is your Lord as a Guide and Helper." The verse assures the Prophet that the hostility he experienced was not unique to him but was the common lot of every prophet. Throughout history, every messenger of Allah faced enemies from among the wicked — those who opposed the truth out of arrogance, self-interest, and stubbornness. The Prophet's trials were part of the pattern of prophethood, not a sign of failure or abandonment.

The verse then provides the ultimate reassurance: "but sufficient is your Lord as a Guide and Helper." The Prophet did not stand alone against his enemies, for Allah was with him as his Guide (hādī) and Helper (naṣīr). The guidance of Allah would keep him firm upon the truth, and the help of Allah would protect him from his enemies and grant him victory. The presence of enemies was not a cause for despair but an opportunity to rely upon Allah, whose sufficiency makes every enemy insignificant.

The verse teaches the believer a profound lesson about opposition and reliance. Those who stand for the truth will inevitably face enemies, for truth always challenges falsehood, and falsehood always resists. But the believer is never alone, for Allah is sufficient as a guide and helper for all who turn to Him. The believer is encouraged to face opposition with patience and trust, knowing that the same Lord who guided and helped the prophets will guide and help them. The verse transforms the reality of opposition from a source of fear into an occasion for deeper reliance upon Allah.

=32=

This verse records another objection raised by the disbelievers: "The disbelievers say, 'If only the Quran had been sent down to him all at once!' ˹We have sent it˺ as such ˹in stages˺ so We may reassure your heart with it. And We have revealed it at a deliberate pace." The disbelievers objected that the Qur'an was revealed gradually over many years rather than as a single complete book. They imagined that a single, sudden revelation would have been more convincing, and they used the gradual nature of the revelation as a pretext for doubt.

The verse answers this objection by explaining the wisdom of gradual revelation: "so We may reassure your heart with it." The Qur'an was revealed in stages to strengthen and steady the heart of the Prophet and his followers. Each new revelation brought fresh guidance, fresh reassurance, and fresh strength to face the challenges of the moment. A single revelation would not have served this purpose, for the believers needed ongoing divine support through the trials they faced over twenty-three years. The gradual revelation was not a defect but a mercy.

The verse adds: "And We have revealed it at a deliberate pace," referring to the careful, measured, and well-arranged manner of the revelation. The word tartīl conveys the sense of deliberate, unhurried recitation and arrangement, indicating that the Qur'an was sent down with wisdom and order, each part in its proper place and time. This verse teaches the believer to appreciate the wisdom of Allah's methods, to trust that He knows best how to guide His servants, and to find in the gradual revelation of the Qur'an a source of reassurance for their own gradual growth in faith. The believer is also reminded that the Qur'an is meant to be absorbed slowly and deliberately, not rushed through.

=33=

This verse assures the Prophet that every objection raised against him will be answered: "Whenever they bring you an argument, We come to you with the right refutation and the best explanation." The disbelievers repeatedly brought arguments, doubts, and objections against the Prophet and his message, seeking to undermine the truth with their cleverness and rhetoric. The verse promises that for every argument they bring, Allah will provide the truth that refutes it and the explanation that surpasses it. The Prophet was never left without an answer to his opponents.

The verse describes Allah's response in two ways: "the right refutation" (al-ḥaqq) and "the best explanation" (aḥsan tafsīr). The first refers to the truth itself, which refutes falsehood by its very nature, for truth and falsehood cannot coexist without the latter being exposed. The second refers to the clarity and excellence of the explanation, for the Qur'an not only refutes falsehood but also explains the truth in a manner more beautiful and convincing than anything the disbelievers could produce. The Qur'an answers objections not merely by negation but by illumination.

This verse is a timeless reassurance for every believer and every caller to the truth. In every age, the truth will be challenged by arguments and doubts, but the Qur'an remains the ultimate answer, containing within it the refutation of every falsehood and the explanation of every truth. The believer is encouraged to deepen their knowledge of the Qur'an, to study it and reflect upon it, so that they may be equipped to answer the objections they encounter with wisdom and clarity. The verse also teaches that the believer need not fear the arguments of the skeptics, for the truth has its own compelling power, and Allah stands ready to support those who uphold it.

=34=

This verse describes the fate of those who rejected the truth: "Those who will be dragged into Hell on their faces will be in the worst place, and are ˹now˺ farthest from the ˹Right˺ Way." The image of being dragged on one's face is one of utter humiliation and degradation. Those who walked arrogantly upon the earth, refusing to bow to their Lord, will be dragged face-down into the Fire, their pride turned to abject shame. The position of the face — the most honoured part of the body — being used to drag them conveys the completeness of their humiliation.

The verse then delivers a double verdict: they will be "in the worst place" and "farthest from the ˹Right˺ Way." In this world, they believed themselves to be upon the right path and considered the believers to be astray. But the verse reveals the truth: they were the ones farthest from guidance, and their destination will be the worst of all places. The reversal is complete, for those who walked proudly in error will find themselves in the lowest and most degraded of positions on the Day of Judgment.

The verse teaches the believer about the consequence of arrogance and the true nature of honour. Honour does not lie in walking proudly upon the earth but in humble submission to Allah, for it is this humility that leads to elevation in the Hereafter, just as arrogance leads to humiliation. The believer is reminded to guard against pride, to keep their face — the symbol of their dignity — devoted to the worship of their Lord in prostration, and to remember that the true measure of a person's path is not how they appear in this world but where their path leads in the next. The verse is a warning against the arrogance that leads to the worst of destinations.

=35=

The sûrah now turns to the lessons of history, beginning with Moses: "We certainly gave Moses the Book and appointed his brother Aaron as his helper." This verse recalls the story of Moses, who was given the Torah as a Book of guidance, and whose brother Aaron was appointed as his helper and partner in prophethood. The mention of Moses serves as a reminder that the Prophet Muhammad was not the first messenger, and that his mission followed the pattern of the prophets who came before him, each of whom was sent with a Book and supported by divine help.

The appointment of Aaron as Moses' helper illustrates a recurring theme in the Qur'an: the prophets were not sent alone but were supported by their Lord and, in some cases, by fellow prophets and righteous companions. Moses had asked Allah to appoint Aaron to share his burden, and Allah granted his request. This detail serves as a consolation to the Prophet Muhammad, reminding him that the support of Allah is sufficient, and that the pattern of prophethood includes both divine assistance and human companionship.

The verse teaches the believer to draw lessons from the stories of the prophets, for these stories are not mere history but living examples of faith, patience, and divine support. The believer is reminded that those who carry the truth may face great burdens, but they are never alone, for Allah provides help in ways both seen and unseen. The verse also encourages the believer to value the companionship of the righteous, for just as Aaron supported Moses, so too do the believers support one another in the path of Allah, lightening each other's burdens and strengthening each other's resolve.

=36=

This verse records the command given to Moses and Aaron: "We had ordered ˹them˺, 'Go to the people who would deny Our signs.' Then We annihilated the deniers entirely." Moses and Aaron were commanded to go to Pharaoh and his people, who had denied the signs of Allah and oppressed the Children of Israel. Despite the power and arrogance of Pharaoh, the two brothers were sent with the command to deliver the message, and their mission was ultimately vindicated when Allah destroyed the deniers who refused to believe.

The verse highlights a consistent pattern in the Qur'an: the prophets delivered their message to people who denied the signs of Allah, and when those people persisted in their denial, they were destroyed. This pattern is repeated throughout history as a lesson for humanity, demonstrating that the rejection of the truth has consequences, and that no power — not even the might of Pharaoh — can protect a people from the judgment of Allah when they persist in denial.

The verse teaches the believer about the certainty of divine justice and the ultimate triumph of the truth. The messengers were sent to warn, and the deniers were given every opportunity to believe, but when they chose denial, the decree of Allah was fulfilled against them. The believer is reminded that the truth will ultimately prevail, that the opponents of the truth will not succeed in the end, and that the faithful delivery of the message is itself a victory, regardless of the immediate response. The verse is a source of confidence for those who uphold the truth, for the outcome of every struggle between truth and falsehood is already determined.

=37=

This verse recalls the fate of the people of Noah: "And when the people of Noah rejected the messengers, We drowned them, making them an example to humanity. And We have prepared a painful punishment for the wrongdoers." The people of Noah rejected not only Noah himself but, by implication, all the messengers, for the rejection of one messenger is the rejection of the message they all shared. Their rejection was met with the flood, in which they were drowned, and their fate became an enduring example for all of humanity.

The verse describes their destruction as "an example" (āyah) — a sign and a lesson. The story of Noah is not merely a historical account but a divine sign, preserved in the Qur'an and passed down through the generations, warning humanity of the consequences of rejecting the truth. Every nation that hears the story of Noah is meant to reflect upon it and to recognise in it the pattern of divine justice that governs the fate of all who deny their Lord.

The verse concludes with a warning that extends beyond the people of Noah: "And We have prepared a painful punishment for the wrongdoers." The punishment of the flood was a punishment in this world, but a greater punishment has been prepared in the Hereafter for all who do wrong. The verse teaches the believer that the accounts of past nations are not entertainment but instruction, meant to awaken the heart to the reality of divine justice and to inspire a life of faith and obedience. The believer is urged to take heed of these examples and to avoid the path that led to their destruction.

=38=

This verse enumerates further examples of destroyed nations: "Also ˹We destroyed˺ ’Ād, Thamûd, and the people of the Water-pit, as well as many peoples in between." The verse lists three specific peoples — ’Ād, Thamûd, and the people of the Water-pit (aṣḥāb ar-Rass) — along with many other peoples who lived between them, all of whom were destroyed for their rejection of the truth. These names would have been familiar to the Arabs, who passed by the ruins of these ancient civilisations and knew their stories.

The mention of "many peoples in between" indicates that the examples given are not exhaustive but representative. History is filled with nations that rose and fell, and the Qur'an presents a selection of them as signs for humanity. Each of these nations received its messenger, was given the opportunity to believe, and chose instead to deny and rebel, and each met the same end. The repetition of this pattern across history is itself a proof of the divine law that governs the fate of nations.

The verse teaches the believer to study history through the lens of faith, seeing in the rise and fall of nations the working of divine justice. The ruins of ’Ād, the fate of Thamûd, and the forgotten people of the Water-pit all testify to a truth that transcends their time: the rejection of Allah's guidance leads to ruin, while faith and obedience lead to preservation. The believer is urged to travel through the earth and reflect upon the traces of these nations, drawing from them the lesson that the path of denial is a path of destruction, and that the only secure path is the path of faith.

=39=

This verse summarises the lesson of the destroyed nations: "For each We set forth ˹various˺ lessons, and We ultimately destroyed each." The verse states that for every nation, Allah set forth lessons, parables, and clear warnings — through the messengers, through the signs in creation, and through the examples of those who came before. No nation was destroyed without first being warned, and no nation was left without guidance. The destruction that followed was not arbitrary but the consequence of persistent denial after every opportunity had been given.

The phrase "We ultimately destroyed each" conveys the finality and inevitability of the divine judgment. The word tatbīran, translated as "destroyed each," carries the sense of utter and complete destruction, leaving nothing behind. Each nation that rejected the truth was brought to a complete end, their power, wealth, and numbers proving useless against the decree of Allah. The repetition of this pattern across history leaves no room for doubt about the certainty of divine justice.

The verse teaches the believer about the mercy and the justice of Allah. His mercy is shown in the lessons and warnings He sends before destruction, giving every people the chance to repent and be saved. His justice is shown in the destruction of those who, after every opportunity, persist in denial. The believer is reminded that they too are recipients of these lessons, and that the warnings of the Qur'an are a mercy meant to guide them away from the fate of the destroyed nations. The verse calls for gratitude for the guidance that has been given and heedfulness of the warning that accompanies it.

=40=

This verse draws the attention of the disbelievers to a lesson from their own surroundings: "They have certainly passed by the city ˹of Sodom˺, which had been showered with a dreadful rain ˹of stones˺. Have they not seen its ruins? But they do not expect to be resurrected." The verse refers to the city of the people of Lot, which the Arabs of Mecca would pass by on their trade journeys to Syria. That city had been destroyed by a rain of stones sent down upon its inhabitants for their sins, and its ruins remained as a visible sign of divine punishment.

The verse asks a pointed question: "Have they not seen its ruins?" The ruins of Sodom were not hidden or distant; they lay along the very routes the Quraysh travelled. The disbelievers had seen with their own eyes the evidence of what happens to those who defy Allah, yet they failed to take heed. The verse explains why: "But they do not expect to be resurrected." Their failure to learn from the ruins was rooted in their denial of the Hereafter, for if a person does not believe in resurrection and accountability, the destruction of past nations holds no lesson for them.

The verse teaches the believer about the relationship between belief in the Hereafter and the ability to learn from signs. The person who believes in the Hereafter sees in the ruins of the past a warning for the future, while the person who denies it sees nothing but ancient stones. The believer is encouraged to travel, observe, and reflect, drawing lessons from the signs of Allah in the world around them. The verse also warns against the heedlessness that comes from denying the Hereafter, for it is this denial that blinds a person to the most obvious of lessons.
